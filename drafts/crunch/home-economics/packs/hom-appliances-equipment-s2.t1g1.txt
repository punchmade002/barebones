TARGET hom-appliances-equipment-s2: 2. Selecting a large and a small household appliance
[hom-appliances-equipment-s2.0.b] Before buying, compare the cost, size and capacity of each model against what the household needs. A good choice is value for money, which means quality against price, not simply the lowest price.
[hom-appliances-equipment-s2.0.0] Initial cost: compare the purchase price, including delivery and installation, across several shops and brands, so you know the true cost of getting the appliance working in your home.
[hom-appliances-equipment-s2.0.1] Running cost: this is the energy and water the appliance uses over its life. Compare it with the purchase price, since a cheap appliance can cost more to run, and the energy label shows this.
[hom-appliances-equipment-s2.0.2] Size and space: the appliance must fit the available space and allow room for doors to open, air to circulate (ventilation) and access to plumbing and power points.
[hom-appliances-equipment-s2.0.3] Capacity: match it to household size, such as litres of fresh and frozen food for a fridge or kilograms of laundry for a washing machine, and litres for a kettle.
[hom-appliances-equipment-s2.0.4] Payment method: compare cash, debit or credit card and credit agreements, and the total amount payable under any credit, because credit adds to the final cost.
[hom-appliances-equipment-s2.1.b] Cost and size are not the whole story. A buyer also checks how long the appliance will last, who will fix it, and how easy and safe it is to use every day.
[hom-appliances-equipment-s2.1.0] Brand reputation and reliability: established brands with a record of durable products, good reviews and available spare parts are less likely to need early repair.
[hom-appliances-equipment-s2.1.1] Guarantee and after-sales service: check the length of the guarantee, what it covers, and how quickly repairs, servicing, spare parts, delivery and installation are available.
[hom-appliances-equipment-s2.1.2] Ease of operation and cleaning: clear controls, removable parts and filters, smooth surfaces, dishwasher-safe parts and cord storage reduce the time and effort of use.
[hom-appliances-equipment-s2.1.3] Safety features: automatic cut-outs, interlocks, child lock, cool-touch surfaces and recognised safety marks reduce the risk of injury or fire.
[hom-appliances-equipment-s2.1.4] Family needs and modern features: choose features the household will actually use, such as programmes, timers and smart controls, rather than paying for extras that stay unused.
[hom-appliances-equipment-s2.2.b] The same criteria apply to every appliance, but how much each one matters depends on the size and price of what you are buying. Apply them to one large and one small example.
[hom-appliances-equipment-s2.2.0] Large appliances (cooker, fridge, washing machine, dishwasher, tumble dryer) are expensive and long-lived and usually delivered and installed, so cost, energy efficiency, size and servicing weigh most.
[hom-appliances-equipment-s2.2.1] Small appliances (kettle, toaster, iron, food processor, mixer) are cheaper and portable, so storage, cleaning and cord length weigh more.
[hom-appliances-equipment-s2.2.2] Examples to use for the individual studies: a washing machine, fridge-freezer or dishwasher as the large appliance, and a kettle, toaster, food processor or iron as the small one.
[hom-appliances-equipment-s2.3.b] Design is how an appliance looks and works, and construction is what it is made of and how well it is built. Together they decide how safe, durable and easy to clean it is.
[hom-appliances-equipment-s2.3.0] Design affects safety, durability, ease of use and cleaning, and how well the appliance fits the style and colour of the kitchen.
[hom-appliances-equipment-s2.3.1] Controls should be clearly labelled, easy to reach and simple to read; large, simple controls suit older users and children.
[hom-appliances-equipment-s2.3.2] Weight, handles and grip should suit the person using the appliance, so that lifting and pouring are safe, and non-slip feet give stability.
[hom-appliances-equipment-s2.3.3] Robust construction and good-quality materials, such as stainless steel for surfaces and blades, increase durability. Other materials used include heat-resistant or cool-touch plastic, glass and enamelled steel.
[hom-appliances-equipment-s2.3.4] Smooth, sealed surfaces and removable parts are easier to clean and give fewer places for food and bacteria to collect. Sealed joints and a strong flex and plug add durability.
[hom-appliances-equipment-s2.3.5] Design for safety and efficiency: insulation, interlocks, automatic cut-off, energy-saving settings and quiet running make the appliance safer and cheaper to use.
[hom-appliances-equipment-s2.3.6] Examples of safe design: an interlock stops a machine working while its door is open, so fingers cannot reach moving parts.
[hom-appliances-equipment-s2.4.b] The energy label lets buyers compare the energy efficiency of appliances at a glance, on a common scale. An efficient appliance costs less to run and does less harm to the environment.
[hom-appliances-equipment-s2.4.0] The EU energy label rates an appliance from A to G, where A is the most efficient and G the least efficient. This scale applies to fridges, freezers, washing machines, washer-dryers and dishwashers from 1 March 2021, and to tumble dryers from 1 July 2025.
[hom-appliances-equipment-s2.4.1] The label is displayed on refrigerating appliances (fridges, freezers and fridge-freezers), washing machines, washer-dryers, dishwashers and tumble dryers. Two named appliances are enough if asked to name two.
[hom-appliances-equipment-s2.4.2] A-rated appliances use the least electricity, so they cost least to run and cause the least harm to the environment; G-rated appliances use the most.
[hom-appliances-equipment-s2.4.3] The label gives annual energy consumption in kilowatt-hours (kWh), which indicates likely running cost and environmental impact. Washing machine and dishwasher labels give energy per 100 cycles.
[hom-appliances-equipment-s2.4.4] It also gives, depending on the appliance, the noise level in decibels, capacity and water use. A refrigeration label shows the fresh and frozen capacity in litres.
[hom-appliances-equipment-s2.4.5] The current label carries a QR code that links to the product's entry in the EU product database (EPREL) for more detail.
[hom-appliances-equipment-s2.4.6] Energy in kWh equals power in kW multiplied by hours used. Higher Level answers can use this to compare the running costs of two appliances.
[hom-appliances-equipment-s2.4.7] Efficiency in use is also the consumer's job: use full loads, eco programmes and lower temperatures, switch off standby, and choose the correct size.
[hom-appliances-equipment-s2.5.b] A star rating on a fridge-freezer or freezer tells you how cold the frozen-food compartment is and how long frozen food keeps in it.
[hom-appliances-equipment-s2.5.0] The star rating applies to the frozen-food compartment of fridges and fridge-freezers and to freezers, not to the fresh-food section.
[hom-appliances-equipment-s2.5.1] One star: the compartment holds frozen food at −6°C for up to one week.
[hom-appliances-equipment-s2.5.2] Two stars: the compartment holds frozen food at −12°C for up to one month.
[hom-appliances-equipment-s2.5.3] Three stars: the compartment holds frozen food at −18°C for up to three months.
[hom-appliances-equipment-s2.5.4] Four stars: the compartment holds frozen food at −18°C to −25°C for up to one year, and fresh food can be frozen in it. Only four stars allows this.
[hom-appliances-equipment-s2.5.5] The star rating measures storage performance, not energy efficiency, so the energy label is used to compare running costs.
[hom-appliances-equipment-s2.6.b] Marks on an appliance or its packaging tell the buyer that a standard has been met. Each mark means something different, so know what each one does and does not show.
[hom-appliances-equipment-s2.6.0] The CE mark is the manufacturer's declaration that the product meets the health, safety and environmental requirements of the European Union. It is not independent testing.
[hom-appliances-equipment-s2.6.1] The double insulation symbol, a square inside a square, shows that the appliance has two layers of insulation (a Class II appliance), so it needs no earth wire and its plug has no earth connection. It is common on plastic-bodied small appliances.
[hom-appliances-equipment-s2.6.2] The Irish Standard mark of the National Standards Authority of Ireland (NSAI) shows that the product has been certified to a standard.
[hom-appliances-equipment-s2.6.3] An ISO 9000 quality mark shows that the maker's quality-management system has been assessed.
[hom-appliances-equipment-s2.6.4] Marks should appear on the appliance or its packaging, and a mark that cannot be found on the product does not show certification.
[hom-appliances-equipment-s2.6.5] Safety marks show that a product has been tested to a standard, but they do not guarantee its performance or reliability.
[hom-appliances-equipment-s2.6.6] A manufacturer's guarantee or a retailer's own-brand label is not an independent mark.
[hom-appliances-equipment-s2.7.b] Before buying, a consumer gathers information from several sources. Some are independent, some come from sellers and may be biased, and some are personal experience.
[hom-appliances-equipment-s2.7.0] Independent sources: consumer magazines and websites that test and compare products publish independent results on appliance models.
[hom-appliances-equipment-s2.7.1] The Competition and Consumer Protection Commission (CCPC), the body for consumer protection and information, publishes guidance for consumers on their rights when buying appliances.
[hom-appliances-equipment-s2.7.2] Citizens Information offers free, independent information on consumer rights and on buying goods and services. The European Consumer Centre Ireland helps with cross-border purchases, and libraries also hold information.
[hom-appliances-equipment-s2.7.3] Manufacturers' leaflets and brochures give specifications, guidelines for use and guarantee terms, but they are promotional documents.
[hom-appliances-equipment-s2.7.4] Product labels and packaging show the energy rating, safety marks, capacity and guarantee details, so you can compare models for example in the shop.
[hom-appliances-equipment-s2.7.5] Sales staff can explain features, but their advice may be influenced by the stock and margins of the shop. Advertisements highlight strengths, so check their claims against independent sources.
[hom-appliances-equipment-s2.7.6] Word of mouth, online user reviews and in-shop demonstrations reflect other people's experience; read them critically because they can be unreliable.
[hom-appliances-equipment-s2.7.7] Benefits of getting information before buying: compare price, installation and running cost; find the most energy-efficient model; compare features, design, size and ease of operation; reduce the influence of advertising; avoid a costly mistake.
[hom-appliances-equipment-s2.8.b] An appliance affects the environment at three points: when it is chosen, while it is used and when it is thrown out. A responsible consumer thinks about all three.
[hom-appliances-equipment-s2.8.0] Choosing: pick the best energy class you can afford and the correct size, and prefer repairable, durable appliances with eco and low-water programmes and little packaging.
[hom-appliances-equipment-s2.8.1] Using: run full loads, use eco and short cycles and lower temperatures, switch off standby, defrost freezers, and do not leave fridge doors open, because each wastes energy.
[hom-appliances-equipment-s2.8.2] Maintaining: descale and maintain appliances so that they keep working efficiently.
[hom-appliances-equipment-s2.8.3] Disposing: repair, sell or donate an appliance while it still works, so it is not wasted.
[hom-appliances-equipment-s2.8.4] Maintaining also helps because limescale and ice build-up make an appliance work harder and use more energy.
[hom-appliances-equipment-s2.8.5] Under the European Union (Waste Electrical and Electronic Equipment) Regulations 2014, a retailer must take back an old appliance of similar type free of charge, one for one, when a new one is bought, so old appliances do not end up in landfill.
[hom-appliances-equipment-s2.8.6] Collection is on delivery if notice was given, and larger shops must accept very small items without a purchase.
[hom-appliances-equipment-s2.8.7] Electrical waste can also go to a civic amenity site. Never put it in the household bin, which the crossed-out wheelie bin symbol warns.
[hom-appliances-equipment-s2.9.b] A washing machine shows how the selection criteria apply to a large appliance, where cost, efficiency, size and installation all matter.
[hom-appliances-equipment-s2.9.0] Capacity: the drum size, stated in kilograms of dry laundry, should match the number of people in the household.
[hom-appliances-equipment-s2.9.1] Spin speed, measured in revolutions per minute (rpm), affects how dry clothes are after washing and so how much drying is needed.
[hom-appliances-equipment-s2.9.2] Eco and low-temperature programmes reduce water and energy use, and the energy label shows the rating for the model.
[hom-appliances-equipment-s2.9.3] Installation needs: a water supply and drain, a suitable power point, and a level floor must all be available where the machine is placed.
[hom-appliances-equipment-s2.10.b] A kettle shows how the criteria apply to a small appliance, where capacity, cleaning and safety features matter most.
[hom-appliances-equipment-s2.10.0] Capacity is stated in litres; buy a kettle that holds the amount the household needs, not more, because heating extra water wastes energy.
[hom-appliances-equipment-s2.10.1] A concealed element in the base is easier to clean than an exposed element, where limescale collects.
[hom-appliances-equipment-s2.10.2] Automatic cut-off switches the kettle off when the water boils, and boil-dry protection switches it off if the water level is too low.

CANDIDATES hom-consumer-rights-s1: 1. Consumer choices
[hom-consumer-rights-s1.0.b] Every purchase starts with how much a household can spend. Income sets the limit, and it also decides whether a household buys what it needs or what it merely wants.
[hom-consumer-rights-s1.0.0] Household income sets the limit on spending, so a household on a tight budget compares unit prices (the price per kilo or per item), buys own-brand and bulk items, and delays non-essential purchases.
[hom-consumer-rights-s1.0.1] Disposable income is the money left after tax and essential bills. It decides how much is free for discretionary spending (spending that is a choice, not a necessity) such as eating out or branded clothing.
[hom-consumer-rights-s1.0.2] When income rises, households often trade up to branded or higher-quality goods; when it falls, they trade down to cheaper alternatives.
[hom-consumer-rights-s1.0.3] Essential needs such as food, heating and shelter are bought first, and luxury wants are the first to be cut when income falls.
[hom-consumer-rights-s1.0.4] A needs-based decision is guided by necessity and income. A wants-based decision is more open to advertising and impulse buying.
[hom-consumer-rights-s1.0.5] Credit and easy-pay arrangements can allow spending above current income, and the debt they create reduces future choice because part of later income is already committed.
[hom-consumer-rights-s1.1.b] Beyond money, choices reflect who the buyer is, who influences them and what the product offers. For a large purchase such as a washing machine, buyers weigh several of these together.
[hom-consumer-rights-s1.1.0] Personal preference, and values and lifestyle (health, convenience, ethical concerns, and cultural or religious practice), all influence what is bought.
[hom-consumer-rights-s1.1.1] Household composition narrows choice: family size, life-cycle stage and special diets, such as a coeliac or diabetic member or an allergy, determine which goods are suitable.
[hom-consumer-rights-s1.1.2] Time pressure pushes busy households towards convenience foods, takeaway meals and online shopping, because these save time.
[hom-consumer-rights-s1.1.3] Other people influence choice through word of mouth, sales staff, peers and current trends.
[hom-consumer-rights-s1.1.4] For a large purchase, buyers weigh the energy rating, reliability of the brand, ease of use and design, size or capacity for the family, purchase and running cost, quality marks, after-sales service and guarantee, and whether to pay cash or use credit.
[hom-consumer-rights-s1.1.5] The environmental impact of a product or its packaging is a modern factor that some consumers weigh when choosing.
[hom-consumer-rights-s1.1.6] Impulse buying is an unplanned purchase triggered by a display, an offer or an item at the checkout. It results from several of the factors above, such as advertising, values and income, working together.
[hom-consumer-rights-s1.2.b] Shoppers often choose by name rather than by comparing every product. Brands and own-brand goods are the two main kinds of product on the shelf, and they differ in price and in how they are promoted.
[hom-consumer-rights-s1.2.0] A brand is a name, logo or design that identifies a manufacturer's product and distinguishes it from competing goods.
[hom-consumer-rights-s1.2.1] Branded goods are made by a manufacturer and are usually advertised nationally, so they tend to cost more than equivalent goods.
[hom-consumer-rights-s1.2.2] Own-brand goods are sold under the retailer's own name. They are often cheaper because the retailer avoids the cost of national advertising.
[hom-consumer-rights-s1.2.3] Brand loyalty is habitual repeat buying of one brand, which saves the shopper the effort of comparing alternatives.
[hom-consumer-rights-s1.3.b] Advertising is communication that informs consumers about goods and services and tries to create a want for them. It happens mostly outside the shop, in the media a consumer meets every day.
[hom-consumer-rights-s1.3.0] Advertising makes people aware of new products and builds brand loyalty, so that consumers ask for the product by name.
[hom-consumer-rights-s1.3.1] It also informs consumers about features, price and offers.
[hom-consumer-rights-s1.3.2] Media used include television, radio, print, outdoor billboards, websites, social media, and sponsorship of sport and events.
[hom-consumer-rights-s1.3.3] Celebrity endorsement transfers the image or popularity of a well-known person to the product.
[hom-consumer-rights-s1.3.4] Product differentiation: advertising highlights a feature, such as a special ingredient, that sets one brand apart from similar goods.
[hom-consumer-rights-s1.3.5] Advertising can make people feel they need products they cannot afford, and it can encourage impulse buying and the use of credit.
[hom-consumer-rights-s1.3.6] Advertising aimed at children and teenagers is especially influential. It can prompt impulse purchases and family purchases, because young consumers have less experience of judging value.
[hom-consumer-rights-s1.3.7] The advantage to the consumer is awareness of choice and information. The disadvantages are that the cost of advertising is added to the price, claims can exaggerate, and there is pressure to buy.
[hom-consumer-rights-s1.4.b] Merchandising is the arrangement and presentation of goods in a shop to encourage consumers to buy more, and to buy more often. It is what the retailer does inside the shop, in contrast to advertising.
[hom-consumer-rights-s1.4.0] Store layout: goods are arranged in clearly identifiable departments with space to browse, and essentials such as bread and milk are placed at the back so that shoppers pass other products on the way to them.
[hom-consumer-rights-s1.4.1] Route design: aisles are arranged to keep shoppers moving through the store, so that they see more products and spend longer browsing.
[hom-consumer-rights-s1.4.2] Product placement: complementary goods are shelved together, such as biscuits beside tea, so that buying one reminds the shopper of the other. Sweets and magazines are placed at the checkout as impulse items.
[hom-consumer-rights-s1.4.3] Shelf position: premium or luxury goods are placed at eye level, where they are seen first, while cheaper products are often placed on lower or higher shelves.
[hom-consumer-rights-s1.4.4] Special offers displayed at the end of aisles draw attention to promotional stock.
[hom-consumer-rights-s1.4.5] In-store stimuli: background music that slows the pace of shopping, aromas such as freshly baked bread, good lighting and warmth, and colourful displays.
[hom-consumer-rights-s1.4.6] Sampling and demonstrations let shoppers taste or try a product before buying it, which makes a purchase more likely.
[hom-consumer-rights-s1.5.b] Pricing techniques make a price look lower, or make a purchase look more valuable, than it is. Discount offers and discount stores do lower the cost, but the consumer should still compare total value.
[hom-consumer-rights-s1.5.0] Charm pricing: a price set just below a round figure, so that the item appears cheaper than it is.
[hom-consumer-rights-s1.5.1] Multi-buy offers, such as three items for a set price, encourage consumers to buy more than they would at a single-item price.
[hom-consumer-rights-s1.5.2] Clearance and reduction labels suggest extra value and can prompt impulse buying of goods the shopper did not plan to buy.
[hom-consumer-rights-s1.5.3] Loss leaders: a well-known product is sold at or below cost to draw shoppers into the store, who then buy other items at normal prices.
[hom-consumer-rights-s1.5.4] Coupons and money-off vouchers reward a specific purchase and are often distributed through loyalty schemes or advertising.
[hom-consumer-rights-s1.5.5] Discount offers, and discount stores, lower the cost of goods, but a lower price is not always better value, so the consumer should still compare total value.
[hom-consumer-rights-s1.6.b] Loyalty schemes reward repeat custom with points or discounts, and give retailers information about what customers buy. They are one of the shopping systems named in the syllabus.
[hom-consumer-rights-s1.6.0] Customers earn points or tokens for each amount spent. The points are exchanged for money off goods, vouchers or goods after a set period.
[hom-consumer-rights-s1.6.1] Examples in Ireland include the Tesco Clubcard and the Dunnes Stores ValueClub card.
[hom-consumer-rights-s1.6.2] Schemes encourage saving for special occasions, such as Christmas, by building up points over time.
[hom-consumer-rights-s1.6.3] Shoppers may spend more to reach a reward target, and points can take priority over price when they compare shops, so they return to the same shop.
[hom-consumer-rights-s1.6.4] Card use gives retailers data on buying patterns, which helps them target offers and plan stock.
[hom-consumer-rights-s1.6.5] Disadvantages: the scheme discourages shopping around, can lead to unplanned purchases, and ties the shopper to one shop. It also requires the card to be carried.
[hom-consumer-rights-s1.7.b] Easy-pay arrangements spread the cost of a purchase over time, so goods can be bought without paying the full price at once. This is a form of credit, which has both benefits and costs.
[hom-consumer-rights-s1.7.0] Easy-pay schemes: a retailer or utility company lets the consumer pay for goods or a bill in instalments over a set period.
[hom-consumer-rights-s1.7.1] Hire purchase: the consumer uses the goods while paying instalments under an agreement, and ownership passes only when the final instalment is paid.
[hom-consumer-rights-s1.7.2] Hire purchase is governed by the Consumer Credit Act 1995, which requires a written agreement showing the cash price, the cost of credit and the APR (annual percentage rate, the yearly cost of the credit), and gives the consumer a cooling-off period of ten days.
[hom-consumer-rights-s1.7.3] Advantages of credit: goods can be used immediately, the cost is spread over time, larger items become affordable, and essentials can be bought in an emergency when cash is not available.
[hom-consumer-rights-s1.7.4] Disadvantages: the total cost is higher than the cash price because of interest and charges, easy-pay can encourage buying of non-essential goods and overspending, and the debt reduces future choice.
[hom-consumer-rights-s1.8.b] Both allow shopping from home, but the consumer cannot inspect the goods before buying them.
[hom-consumer-rights-s1.8.0] Television buying: goods are demonstrated on screen and ordered by phone or online, often with a limited-time offer that creates urgency.
[hom-consumer-rights-s1.8.1] Mail order: goods are chosen from a catalogue, leaflet or website and delivered by post or courier.
[hom-consumer-rights-s1.8.2] Advantages of mail order: it is convenient for people without time to shop, a wide range is available, there is no travel, and orders can be placed by post or phone.
[hom-consumer-rights-s1.8.3] Disadvantages of mail order: goods cannot be seen, touched or tried on, delivery can take weeks, and postage adds to the cost.
[hom-consumer-rights-s1.8.4] Distance purchases (those made without meeting the seller) carry a statutory right to cancel within a cooling-off period under the Consumer Rights Act 2022, because the buyer could not see the goods before ordering.
[hom-consumer-rights-s1.9.b] Online shopping offers convenience and easy price comparison, but adds risks around payment, delivery and personal data.
[hom-consumer-rights-s1.9.0] Advantages: access at any hour from home, easy comparison of prices across retailers, a wider range, customer reviews, and delivery to the door.
[hom-consumer-rights-s1.9.1] Disadvantages: goods cannot be seen or handled before purchase, and delivery can be delayed or carry extra charges.
[hom-consumer-rights-s1.9.2] Payment risk: card details can be taken through phishing emails (fake messages that trick people into giving details) or fake websites, so pay only on secure sites that show a padlock symbol and an address beginning https.
[hom-consumer-rights-s1.9.3] Keep records of the order, so that there is proof of what was bought and paid for if something goes wrong.
[hom-consumer-rights-s1.9.4] Overspending risk: stored card details and one-click ordering make impulse purchases quick and easy.
[hom-consumer-rights-s1.9.5] Retailers collect data on browsing and buying, which can be used to target advertising at the individual consumer.
[hom-consumer-rights-s1.10.b] Packaging does more than wrap a product. It protects the goods, carries information, and helps to sell the product.
[hom-consumer-rights-s1.10.0] Packaging protects and preserves the product, so food stays safe and goods are not damaged on the way to the shop or home.
[hom-consumer-rights-s1.10.1] It informs the shopper, because the pack carries the name, instructions and other details.
[hom-consumer-rights-s1.10.2] It attracts attention on the shelf and conveys quality through colour, shape and design.
[hom-consumer-rights-s1.10.3] It gives convenience, such as portion size and easy-open features, and it suggests quality.
[hom-consumer-rights-s1.10.4] Care labels and safety or hazard symbols on non-food goods, for example on household cleaning agents, explain how to use the product and prevent accidents.
[hom-consumer-rights-s1.11.b] Labels on pre-packed foods are a legal requirement, so shoppers can choose safely and compare products. The detail is kept short here and is consumer-focused.
[hom-consumer-rights-s1.11.0] Mandatory food labels show the name of the food, ingredients listed in descending order of weight (the heaviest first), net quantity, a use-by or best-before date, storage conditions and instructions for use, and the name and address of the food business.
[hom-consumer-rights-s1.11.1] Use-by dates apply to highly perishable foods and the food is unsafe after the date; best-before dates indicate quality, and the food may still be safe after them.
[hom-consumer-rights-s1.11.2] The 14 major allergens must be emphasised in the ingredients list, for example in bold or capitals, so that people with allergies can spot them quickly.
[hom-consumer-rights-s1.11.3] A nutrition declaration gives energy and the amounts of fat, saturates, carbohydrate, sugars, protein and salt. The per 100 g (or 100 ml) figure is mandatory, while the per-portion figure is optional.
[hom-consumer-rights-s1.11.4] The country of origin must be shown where the law requires it.
[hom-consumer-rights-s1.11.5] Claims such as low fat, light or high fibre may be made only where the product meets defined criteria.
[hom-consumer-rights-s1.11.6] Quality marks and symbols help consumers choose. The Bord Bia Quality Mark for Irish food shows that a product meets a defined standard, and the gluten-free symbol and allergen emphasis help people with special diets.
[hom-consumer-rights-s1.11.7] EU food information rules are set out in Regulation (EU) No 1169/2011, given effect in Ireland by the European Union (Provision of Food Information to Consumers) Regulations 2014.

CANDIDATES hom-consumer-rights-s3: 3. Consumer responsibility: information and rights
[hom-consumer-rights-s3.0.b] A purchase involves several parties, and each has a different relationship to the consumer. Knowing who is who tells you where to go when something goes wrong.
[hom-consumer-rights-s3.0.0] The consumer is an individual who buys goods or services for personal use, not for business, so a shopper buying a kettle for the kitchen is a consumer.
[hom-consumer-rights-s3.0.1] The retailer or trader sells goods or services in the course of business and is the consumer's first point of contact for a complaint, because the consumer's contract is with the seller.
[hom-consumer-rights-s3.0.2] Under consumer law the retailer is responsible for faults in goods, even when the manufacturer made the fault, and must deal with the complaint.
[hom-consumer-rights-s3.0.3] The manufacturer makes the product and is the other party to any manufacturer's guarantee (a written promise to repair or replace a faulty product).
[hom-consumer-rights-s3.0.4] A guarantee is a contract between the manufacturer and the consumer. It is usually offered in addition to the consumer's statutory rights (rights set by law), not instead of them.
[hom-consumer-rights-s3.0.5] The supplier or distributor moves goods from the manufacturer to the retailer, so the consumer rarely deals with this party directly.
[hom-consumer-rights-s3.1.b] Informed decisions rest on reliable information. It comes from the product itself, from the seller and from independent sources, and each should be checked against the others.
[hom-consumer-rights-s3.1.0] Labels, instructions and care labels give the contents or ingredients, how to use and care for the product, and warnings about hazards, so they should be read before buying and using.
[hom-consumer-rights-s3.1.1] Unit pricing shows the price per kilogram or per litre, which allows comparison between different pack sizes.
[hom-consumer-rights-s3.1.2] Advertising and sales material is useful but should be checked against the product label and independent information, because the seller wants to make the sale.
[hom-consumer-rights-s3.1.3] Independent consumer publications and testing reports compare products on quality and value for money. The Consumers' Association of Ireland publishes comparative findings in Consumer Choice.
[hom-consumer-rights-s3.1.4] Citizens Information gives free, confidential information on consumer rights, so a consumer can ask about a problem without cost.
[hom-consumer-rights-s3.1.5] The Competition and Consumer Protection Commission publishes guidance on consumer rights and on unfair trading.
[hom-consumer-rights-s3.1.6] Terms and conditions, the returns policy and guarantee wording should be read before purchase, as they set out what the seller offers.
[hom-consumer-rights-s3.1.7] Marks and symbols are information too: quality marks such as the Bord Bia Quality Mark, the CE mark (the manufacturer declares the product meets EU safety requirements), the energy rating label on appliances, and safety and hazard symbols.
[hom-consumer-rights-s3.1.8] Reviews, word of mouth and online comparison sites are useful but may be biased, so check more than one source.
[hom-consumer-rights-s3.2.b] A consumer's part in a transaction is to be informed, careful and honest. These are the consumer's duties, which are different from the rights the consumer is entitled to.
[hom-consumer-rights-s3.2.0] Know your rights and the laws that protect consumers: the basic rights and remedies under consumer law apply whether or not the seller mentions them.
[hom-consumer-rights-s3.2.1] Be informed about goods and services before buying, and compare value, not only price, by weighing price against quality, durability and the cost of running or maintaining the product.
[hom-consumer-rights-s3.2.2] Read and understand labels and terms before buying, including ingredients, dates, guarantee conditions and cancellation terms.
[hom-consumer-rights-s3.2.3] Follow the manufacturer's instructions and care labels; a fault caused by misuse is unlikely to be the trader's responsibility.
[hom-consumer-rights-s3.2.4] Keep receipts and proof of purchase, since they show when, where and what was bought, and are needed for a claim.
[hom-consumer-rights-s3.3.b] The consumer's duties continue after the sale. A responsible consumer also acts quickly when something goes wrong and plans spending so that problems are less likely.
[hom-consumer-rights-s3.3.0] Report faults promptly, stop using a faulty or unsafe product, and complain where appropriate, as delay can make a problem harder to prove.
[hom-consumer-rights-s3.3.1] Give accurate, truthful information when complaining, including a clear description of the problem and the redress (the remedy) wanted.
[hom-consumer-rights-s3.3.2] Plan purchases and set a budget: check whether a product is needed before buying.
[hom-consumer-rights-s3.3.3] Resist pressure selling and impulse buying, and do not be rushed by sales talk, because a quick decision leaves no time to compare or check.
[hom-consumer-rights-s3.3.4] Check the total cost, including interest and charges, before agreeing to credit or easy-pay arrangements, because the instalments can add up to far more than the cash price.
[hom-consumer-rights-s3.3.5] Use resources responsibly and consider the environment: reduce waste, and reuse and recycle packaging.
[hom-consumer-rights-s3.4.b] Traditional buying principles still shape how a consumer should approach a purchase, even though the law now gives buyers more protection.
[hom-consumer-rights-s3.4.0] Caveat emptor is a Latin principle meaning 'let the buyer beware': it places the duty of checking goods on the buyer, so the buyer had to inspect goods before buying.
[hom-consumer-rights-s3.4.1] Modern consumer law limits caveat emptor by giving statutory rights that apply automatically, but inspecting goods and asking questions before purchase remains the best protection.
[hom-consumer-rights-s3.4.2] A needs-based decision is guided by necessity and income; a wants-based decision is more open to advertising and impulse buying.
[hom-consumer-rights-s3.4.3] Vague claims such as 'the best' or 'new' that lack supporting evidence should be questioned before they influence a purchase, because they sound impressive but promise nothing that can be checked.
[hom-consumer-rights-s3.5.b] Consumer rights are what a buyer is entitled to expect. Papers list them in slightly different ways, so learn the rights below as the common set.
[hom-consumer-rights-s3.5.0] Right to safety: goods and services must not endanger health or life.
[hom-consumer-rights-s3.5.1] Right to honest and truthful information about goods and services, so that buyers can decide on accurate facts.
[hom-consumer-rights-s3.5.2] Right to choice: a range of goods and services is available to choose from.
[hom-consumer-rights-s3.5.3] Right to value for money: goods and services should be of a quality that matches the price paid, and goods should be of satisfactory quality.
[hom-consumer-rights-s3.5.4] Right to redress: a consumer whose goods or service are faulty can seek repair, replacement or a refund.
[hom-consumer-rights-s3.5.5] Right to services completed to a high standard, by a qualified person, using good materials.
[hom-consumer-rights-s3.5.6] There is no right to a refund simply for a change of mind in a shop purchase; a retailer may offer an exchange or a credit note as goodwill.
[hom-consumer-rights-s3.5.7] Rights and responsibilities are linked: each right is only as useful as the consumer's knowledge and action, so a right to information works only if labels are read.

CANDIDATES hom-environmental-issues-s2: 2. Recycling and waste management
[hom-environmental-issues-s2.0.b] Sustainable waste management means reducing waste first, then reusing, recycling and recovering value, and disposing of only what is left. The waste hierarchy ranks the options from most to least preferred, so that as little waste as possible reaches disposal.
[hom-environmental-issues-s2.0.0] The order of preference is prevention, minimisation, reuse, recycling, recovery (including energy recovery) and disposal. Marking schemes phrase it as reduce, reuse, recycle, recover, dispose.
[hom-environmental-issues-s2.0.1] Prevention and minimisation come first: buying only what is needed and avoiding excess packaging stops waste being created in the first place, so there is nothing to collect or treat.
[hom-environmental-issues-s2.0.2] Reuse means using an item again for its original or another purpose, such as refilling a container, reusing a jar for storage, repairing, donating or buying second-hand. The item stays whole, which is why reuse is not recycling.
[hom-environmental-issues-s2.0.3] Recycling processes used materials into new products, such as paper, glass, metals, some plastics and textiles. It saves raw materials and usually energy, and keeps waste out of landfill.
[hom-environmental-issues-s2.0.4] Recovery means burning residual waste to generate heat or electricity (energy from waste); gas collected from landfill can also be used for energy. Energy recovery is not recycling, because the material is not made into a new product.
[hom-environmental-issues-s2.0.5] Disposal, mainly landfill, is the least preferred option because it uses up land, and food and other organic waste decomposes without oxygen (anaerobically), releasing methane, a potent greenhouse gas.
[hom-environmental-issues-s2.0.6] In Irish law the hierarchy is applied through the Waste Management Act 1996 (as amended). It sets up controls on waste collection and movement, and gives local authorities and the EPA roles in promoting waste reduction, recycling and reuse.
[hom-environmental-issues-s2.1.b] Much household waste is biodegradable, meaning it can be rotted down by living things. Composting turns it into something useful instead of sending it to landfill.
[hom-environmental-issues-s2.1.0] Biodegradable material such as food scraps, tea leaves and garden waste is broken down by micro-organisms into a nutrient-rich soil conditioner called compost. Composting differs from recycling because organic matter rots down rather than being reprocessed into a new product.
[hom-environmental-issues-s2.1.1] Composting keeps biodegradable waste out of landfill, so less methane is produced as it would otherwise rot without oxygen.
[hom-environmental-issues-s2.1.2] Compost returns nutrients and organic matter to the soil, so gardeners need less peat-based compost, and it is cheap for households to make.
[hom-environmental-issues-s2.1.3] In Irish households food and garden waste is collected in the brown bin where a collection is provided. The three bins are green (dry recyclables), brown (food and garden waste) and black (general waste).
[hom-environmental-issues-s2.2.b] Most waste can be cut at home by what a household buys and how it sorts what is left. Each action below moves waste up the hierarchy, away from disposal.
[hom-environmental-issues-s2.2.0] Plan meals and write a shopping list, so food is not bought and then thrown away.
[hom-environmental-issues-s2.2.1] Buy concentrated products, such as concentrated detergents, which need less packaging per wash. Buy in bulk where it cuts packaging per unit, and use refill stations and refillable containers.
[hom-environmental-issues-s2.2.2] Use reusable bags, bottles and containers instead of single-use ones, so fewer items are made and thrown away. Reuse jars, tubs and shopping bags.
[hom-environmental-issues-s2.2.3] Separate waste at home into recyclables, food and garden waste, and general waste, so that each stream can be treated in the right way.
[hom-environmental-issues-s2.2.4] Choose products in recyclable, recycled or biodegradable packaging, and products with the EU Ecolabel. Repair items rather than replacing them.
[hom-environmental-issues-s2.2.5] Take paint and other household chemicals to a civic amenity site (the local authority's recycling and waste centre), not the bin, because they can harm the environment and the people handling waste. Do not put paint, oil, chemicals or batteries in the bin.
[hom-environmental-issues-s2.3.b] Packaging carries symbols that tell the consumer how it can be treated after use. The recycling symbol is the most common, and it says less than many people think.
[hom-environmental-issues-s2.3.0] The recycling symbol (three arrows forming a loop) shows that the packaging is made of material that can be recycled. It does not guarantee that a local collection accepts it, so do not say it is certainly accepted in the household recycling bin.
[hom-environmental-issues-s2.3.1] A percentage inside the arrows shows the proportion of recycled content in the packaging.
[hom-environmental-issues-s2.3.2] A number inside a triangle on plastic is a plastic-type code: 1 is PET, used for drinks bottles, and 2 is HDPE, used for milk bottles and detergent containers.
[hom-environmental-issues-s2.4.b] Two other symbols are often confused with the recycling loop. They tell the consumer about who paid for recovery and about a product's whole environmental effect, not whether the pack can be recycled.
[hom-environmental-issues-s2.4.0] The Green Dot shows that the producer of the packaging has contributed financially to the recovery and recycling of packaging waste.
[hom-environmental-issues-s2.4.1] In Ireland the Green Dot is shown by members of Repak, the packaging recovery scheme, so a producer who pays into Repak can put it on their packs.
[hom-environmental-issues-s2.4.2] The Green Dot does not mean the item itself is recyclable or recycled, which is why it must not be treated as a recycling symbol.
[hom-environmental-issues-s2.4.3] The EU Ecolabel, a flower symbol, is an audited, voluntary label certifying that a product or service causes minimum damage to the environment across its life cycle.
[hom-environmental-issues-s2.4.4] The Ecolabel assessment considers the energy and materials used in making the product, the pollution produced, and how far it can be reused or recycled.
[hom-environmental-issues-s2.5.b] Packaging makes up a large share of household waste, so government, producers, local authorities and consumers all act to reduce it. A strong answer names one initiative and gives two details about it.
[hom-environmental-issues-s2.5.0] The levy on single-use plastic shopping bags, charged at the point of sale since 2002, discouraged their use and increased the use of reusable bags.
[hom-environmental-issues-s2.5.1] The landfill levy is a charge per tonne on waste sent to landfill, which makes landfill more expensive and so encourages recycling and recovery.
[hom-environmental-issues-s2.5.2] Packaging producers must meet national recovery and recycling targets under Irish packaging regulations, and most meet them by funding the Repak scheme (the Green Dot).
[hom-environmental-issues-s2.5.3] Local authority recycling schemes gather glass, cans, paper and plastics through kerbside collections and bring banks or civic amenity centres.
[hom-environmental-issues-s2.5.4] The Re-turn deposit return scheme began on 1 February 2024. A refundable deposit is paid on plastic drinks bottles and aluminium and steel cans, which are returned in shops or to reverse vending machines. Glass and dairy containers are not included.
[hom-environmental-issues-s2.5.5] Consumers can refuse over-packaged goods, choose loose produce and bring their own containers to refill counters.
[hom-environmental-issues-s2.5.6] Manufacturers reduce excess packaging by using less material per product and lighter packaging.
[hom-environmental-issues-s2.6.b] Each packaging material has strengths for the product and a different fate as waste. Knowing both lets you judge how sustainable a material is.
[hom-environmental-issues-s2.6.0] Flexible films are thin plastic films that are light, low in cost, fit the shape of the food closely, can be printed on and can be heat-sealed (closed by melting the edges together).
[hom-environmental-issues-s2.6.1] Flexible films give a good barrier against moisture and gases, which extends shelf life and reduces spoilage, and they are suitable for frozen food.
[hom-environmental-issues-s2.6.2] Their drawback is that many multi-layer films are hard to recycle, so they are often sent to landfill or energy recovery.
[hom-environmental-issues-s2.6.3] Glass can be washed and reused, or recycled repeatedly without loss of quality, but it is heavy and costly to transport.
[hom-environmental-issues-s2.6.4] Steel and aluminium cans are highly recyclable, and aluminium can be recycled repeatedly without loss of quality.
[hom-environmental-issues-s2.6.5] Paper and card are recyclable, though their fibres shorten with each recycling cycle. They also come from a renewable raw material, are biodegradable, are light and take printing well.
[hom-environmental-issues-s2.6.6] Biodegradable packaging breaks down naturally through the action of micro-organisms. Examples are paper or card, and compostable starch-based packaging.
[hom-environmental-issues-s2.7.b] WEEE is waste electrical and electronic equipment, and it is governed by its own collection and take-back rules. It must be kept out of the general bin because of what it contains.
[hom-environmental-issues-s2.7.0] Electrical goods contain hazardous substances such as lead, mercury, cadmium and brominated flame retardants, and also valuable materials that can be recovered, so they must not go into general household waste.
[hom-environmental-issues-s2.7.1] The crossed-out wheeled bin symbol means the item must be collected separately from household waste.
[hom-environmental-issues-s2.7.2] Route 1: repair, sell or donate a working appliance first, because keeping it in use is the top of the waste hierarchy.
[hom-environmental-issues-s2.7.3] Route 2: when a consumer buys a new equivalent item, the retailer must take back the old one free of charge, including on home delivery.
[hom-environmental-issues-s2.7.4] Route 3: a civic amenity site or recycling centre accepts small appliances, batteries and other electrical items. Larger electrical shops must accept very small electrical items without a purchase, and batteries can be returned to shops without buying anything.
[hom-environmental-issues-s2.7.5] Take batteries out of appliances and dispose of them separately before the appliance is handed in. Erase personal data from smart appliances.
[hom-environmental-issues-s2.7.6] Old fridges and freezers contain refrigerant and insulating foam, which must be removed by an authorised treatment facility. Make the appliance safe before disposal.
[hom-environmental-issues-s2.7.7] The legal basis is the WEEE Directive, applied in Ireland by the European Union (Waste Electrical and Electronic Equipment) Regulations 2014.

CANDIDATES hom-environmental-issues-s4: 4. Environmentally responsible practices in the home
[hom-environmental-issues-s4.0.b] Everyday household activities use energy, water and materials, and they produce emissions and waste. How big the impact is depends on how much energy an activity uses and which fuel supplies that energy.
[hom-environmental-issues-s4.0.0] Space heating and hot water account for the largest share of energy use in most homes, so savings on heating usually matter more than savings elsewhere.
[hom-environmental-issues-s4.0.1] Electricity used in the home is generated partly by burning fossil fuels, which releases carbon dioxide at the power station, so using less electricity cuts emissions.
[hom-environmental-issues-s4.0.2] Water used in the home must be pumped and treated, and hot water also needs energy to heat it, so saving water saves energy as well.
[hom-environmental-issues-s4.0.3] Packaging, food waste and discarded appliances all add to the waste that is landfilled (buried in a landfill site) or recovered (reused or recycled).
[hom-environmental-issues-s4.1.b] Heating is the biggest energy use in most homes, so the best savings come from stopping heat escaping and from heating only when and where it is needed. Heat leaves through the roof, walls, floor, windows and gaps.
[hom-environmental-issues-s4.1.0] Insulation laid on the floor of the roof space (attic) is one of the most effective measures for an existing house, because warm air rises and escapes through the roof.
[hom-environmental-issues-s4.1.1] Cavity wall insulation fills the gap between the two leaves of a masonry wall, reducing heat loss through the walls.
[hom-environmental-issues-s4.1.2] Draught-proofing seals gaps around doors, windows, letterboxes and floorboards, which stops warm air escaping and cold air entering. Keep controlled ventilation where fuel is burned.
[hom-environmental-issues-s4.1.3] Double or triple glazing reduces heat loss through windows, and closing thick curtains at night adds a further layer of insulation.
[hom-environmental-issues-s4.1.4] A lagging jacket on the hot water cylinder and insulated (lagged) pipes reduce heat lost while water is stored and moved.
[hom-environmental-issues-s4.1.5] Reflective panels or foil behind radiators on external walls send heat back into the room rather than into the wall.
[hom-environmental-issues-s4.1.6] An annual boiler service keeps combustion efficient and safe, so less fuel is burned for the same heat.
[hom-environmental-issues-s4.2.b] Heating only when and where it is needed saves fuel, and renewable sources reduce reliance on fossil fuels. Controls and cleaner fuels also cut the emissions released when fuel is burned at home.
[hom-environmental-issues-s4.2.0] A room thermostat and a programmer ensure the heating runs only when it is needed, and timers on the immersion heater do the same for hot water. Lowering the set temperature, even slightly, reduces fuel use.
[hom-environmental-issues-s4.2.1] Thermostatic radiator valves control the temperature of individual radiators, so unused rooms can be kept cooler.
[hom-environmental-issues-s4.2.2] Renewable heat sources, such as heat pumps, solar thermal panels and stoves burning wood from sustainably managed forests, reduce reliance on fossil fuels.
[hom-environmental-issues-s4.2.3] Burning fuels at home releases carbon dioxide, sulphur dioxide, nitrogen oxides, carbon monoxide and smoke, which harm the environment.
[hom-environmental-issues-s4.2.4] Emissions can be reduced by using smokeless fuel, an efficient boiler, heating controls and renewable heat.
[hom-environmental-issues-s4.3.b] A washing machine's energy use is mostly about heat, so the choices you make about temperature, load size and drying matter far more than the machine itself.
[hom-environmental-issues-s4.3.0] Heating the water accounts for most of the energy used on a hot wash, so lower temperatures save energy.
[hom-environmental-issues-s4.3.1] A cold or 30 °C wash uses far less energy than a 60 °C or 90 °C wash, and modern detergents clean well at lower temperatures, so a hotter wash is not usually needed.
[hom-environmental-issues-s4.3.2] A full load uses almost the same energy and water as a part load, so run the machine only when it is full.
[hom-environmental-issues-s4.3.3] An eco programme runs at lower temperatures or with less water, using less energy per load.
[hom-environmental-issues-s4.3.4] A higher spin speed removes more water before drying, which reduces the time a dryer must run.
[hom-environmental-issues-s4.3.5] A tumble dryer is one of the most energy-intensive appliances in the home, so line drying saves significant energy.
[hom-environmental-issues-s4.3.6] Using the correct dose of detergent avoids residue and reduces the amount of product that enters drains.
[hom-environmental-issues-s4.3.7] Wash clothes only when needed, and repair and reuse them rather than replacing them.
[hom-environmental-issues-s4.4.b] Food has an environmental cost before it reaches the plate and after it is thrown away. Wasting food wastes everything used to produce it and creates a greenhouse gas in landfill.
[hom-environmental-issues-s4.4.0] Food waste sent to landfill decomposes without oxygen (anaerobically) and releases methane, a greenhouse gas.
[hom-environmental-issues-s4.4.1] Food production uses land, water, fertiliser and energy, so wasted food wastes all the resources used to grow, transport and package it.
[hom-environmental-issues-s4.4.2] Plan meals, shop with a list, check dates and stock, and buy only what will be eaten. Use leftovers and freeze surplus food promptly.
[hom-environmental-issues-s4.4.3] Store food correctly: keep the fridge at or below 5 °C and freeze food at -18 °C or below, which slows bacterial growth and spoilage and so cuts waste.
[hom-environmental-issues-s4.4.4] Compost food and garden waste or use the brown bin, so it is not sent to landfill.
[hom-environmental-issues-s4.5.b] What you choose to buy and eat, and how it is packaged and transported, also affects the environment. Small changes in the weekly shop add up.
[hom-environmental-issues-s4.5.0] Vegetables, pulses and cereals generally need less land, water and energy to produce than meat and dairy, so eating more plant-based foods lowers the environmental impact of the diet.
[hom-environmental-issues-s4.5.1] Seasonal and local food avoids the energy used for long-distance transport and for heated or refrigerated growing and storage.
[hom-environmental-issues-s4.5.2] Buy loose fruit and vegetables and products with minimal packaging, and buy in bulk to reduce packaging.
[hom-environmental-issues-s4.5.3] Use reusable bags and containers for bulk and counter purchases, so fewer single-use bags are thrown away.
[hom-environmental-issues-s4.5.4] Buy refills and products in recycled or reusable packaging, and choose products with the Ecolabel.
[hom-environmental-issues-s4.5.5] Cutting the number of shopping trips reduces the fuel used for travel.
[hom-environmental-issues-s4.6.b] The energy label lets you compare appliances before you buy, so you can choose the one that will use the least energy over its life.
[hom-environmental-issues-s4.6.0] The EU energy label rates an appliance from A (most efficient) to G (least efficient) for energy efficiency, and this rescaled scale replaced the older A+++ to D scale in 2021, so do not compare old and new ratings.
[hom-environmental-issues-s4.6.1] The label also shows the appliance's annual or per-cycle energy consumption and its noise level.
[hom-environmental-issues-s4.6.2] For washing machines and dishwashers, the label shows water use as well.
[hom-environmental-issues-s4.6.3] Choose a high rating and the right capacity for the household, because an oversized appliance uses more energy than its load needs.
[hom-environmental-issues-s4.6.4] Other criteria to look for are appliances that are repairable and durable, have little packaging, and offer eco and low-temperature programmes.
[hom-environmental-issues-s4.7.b] Even an efficient appliance wastes energy if it is used badly. Most savings come from small habits, which are easy to name in an exam answer.
[hom-environmental-issues-s4.7.0] Standby is not off: the appliance still draws electricity continuously, so switch appliances off at the socket, or use a switched power strip.
[hom-environmental-issues-s4.7.1] LED bulbs use much less power than incandescent bulbs and last much longer. Older schemes say CFL for the energy-saving bulb.
[hom-environmental-issues-s4.7.2] Use the oven only when needed, avoid preheating for longer than necessary, and use a microwave, slow cooker or air fryer for small quantities. Use lids on pans.
[hom-environmental-issues-s4.7.3] Boil only the water needed in a kettle, so no energy is spent heating water you do not use.
[hom-environmental-issues-s4.7.4] Clogged filters and dirty condenser coils make fridges and dryers use more energy, so keep them clean and keep door seals sound.
[hom-environmental-issues-s4.7.5] A fridge set colder than necessary wastes energy. Keep the door closed and do not overpack it, and a freezer that is not kept full uses more energy to stay cold.
[hom-environmental-issues-s4.7.6] Use full loads and eco or cold cycles in washing machines and dishwashers.
[hom-environmental-issues-s4.8.b] Making an appliance take longer to wear out avoids the energy and materials needed to manufacture a new one. When it is finally finished with, how you get rid of it matters too.
[hom-environmental-issues-s4.8.0] Keeping a working appliance in use through servicing, repair or second-hand sale avoids the energy and materials needed to manufacture a replacement.
[hom-environmental-issues-s4.8.1] Sell or donate a working appliance rather than replacing and discarding it.
[hom-environmental-issues-s4.8.2] Dispose of old appliances through the retailer take-back or a recycling centre, rather than putting them in the bin.
[hom-environmental-issues-s4.9.b] Saving water saves the energy used to pump, treat and heat it. Most savings come from fixing leaks and changing habits in the bathroom and garden.
[hom-environmental-issues-s4.9.0] Showers use less hot water than baths, so short showers save both water and the energy needed for heating.
[hom-environmental-issues-s4.9.1] Turning off the tap while brushing teeth and fixing dripping taps and leaks save water that would otherwise be wasted. Aerator or sensor taps and efficient showerheads reduce flow further.
[hom-environmental-issues-s4.9.2] A dual-flush toilet allows the smaller flush to be used for liquid waste, saving water with each flush.
[hom-environmental-issues-s4.9.3] Collecting rainwater in a butt provides free water for the garden, and grey water (used water from baths, sinks and washing machines) can be reused.
[hom-environmental-issues-s4.9.4] Insulating the cylinder and pipes and timing the immersion save water-heating energy, and solar thermal heating can supply hot water.
