MODE: cost and links
TARGET hom-food-additives-s6: 6. Evaluation and sensory analysis
[hom-food-additives-s6.0.b] Evaluation means judging a dish or meal against stated criteria, using evidence from the dish itself rather than just saying whether you liked it. A good evaluation always ends with a reason and an improvement.
[hom-food-additives-s6.0.0] Criteria include nutritional value (energy, fibre, fat, sugar and salt), cost per portion, sensory qualities (appearance, aroma, flavour and texture), time and skill required, suitability for the household or special diets, safety and hygiene, and packaging and waste.
[hom-food-additives-s6.0.1] Method: 1. State the criterion. 2. Give evidence from the recipe, label or tasting. 3. Make a judgement and give the reason for it. 4. Suggest an improvement. Evidence is what stops a judgement being just an opinion.
[hom-food-additives-s6.0.2] Recipes are modified to make them more nutritious, to suit special diets such as coeliac (a condition where gluten must be avoided), to change the quantity made, or to save cost.
[hom-food-additives-s6.0.3] Setting: evaluate a family meal against healthy-eating guidelines, such as the Department of Health Food Pyramid, and suggest changes to the portions or ingredients so that it matches the recommended balance.
[hom-food-additives-s6.1.b] A home-made dish and its shop-bought equivalent can be compared on the same set of criteria, usually in a table, before you reach an overall judgement. Neither is always better, because the result depends on the dish and the quantities.
[hom-food-additives-s6.1.0] Cost: home-made food from basic ingredients is often cheaper per portion, whereas commercial products carry the costs of labour, packaging and distribution.
[hom-food-additives-s6.1.1] Nutrition: home-made food lets the cook control salt, sugar, fat and fibre, while the commercial nutrition declaration (the nutrition information on the label) allows products to be compared.
[hom-food-additives-s6.1.2] Additives: home-made food usually contains none of the preservatives, colourings or emulsifiers that many commercial products contain.
[hom-food-additives-s6.1.3] Time and skill: commercial products need little time or skill, which saves effort, while home-made food needs preparation, cooking and cleaning time.
[hom-food-additives-s6.1.4] Sensory quality: home-made food may taste fresher, while a commercial product gives the same taste and texture from one batch to the next.
[hom-food-additives-s6.1.5] Shelf life: commercial products are processed to last longer, while home-made food usually needs refrigeration and is eaten within a short time.
[hom-food-additives-s6.1.6] Packaging and waste: home-made food made from loose ingredients may create less packaging waste than a pre-packed commercial equivalent, which comes in more packaging.
[hom-food-additives-s6.1.7] Method: compare the same product on a set of criteria in a table, then reach a judgement that weighs them up.
[hom-food-additives-s6.2.b] Sensory analysis is the scientific evaluation of food using the senses, carried out under controlled conditions. Food companies use it so that decisions about a product are based on what consumers actually perceive, not on guesses.
[hom-food-additives-s6.2.0] Product development: testing samples to find which version consumers prefer, and how they respond to a new product, before it is launched.
[hom-food-additives-s6.2.1] Modifying an existing product: for example reducing the salt in a product, or changing an ingredient or supplier, with difference tests used to confirm that consumers cannot perceive the change.
[hom-food-additives-s6.2.2] Matching an existing product: testing so that an own-label food can be made to taste like the leading brand, copying a well-known brand.
[hom-food-additives-s6.2.3] Evaluation and quality control: checking that the final product meets its original specification, such as the shortness of a biscuit.
[hom-food-additives-s6.2.4] Comparing an own-brand product with a leading brand, and supporting marketing claims about taste or texture.
[hom-food-additives-s6.3.b] The first of three categories of sensory test is the preference test. It asks whether people like a product, not whether they can tell products apart.
[hom-food-additives-s6.3.0] Preference tests show whether consumers like or dislike a product, or prefer one product to another.
[hom-food-additives-s6.3.1] Paired preference test: the tester chooses the preferred of two coded samples.
[hom-food-additives-s6.3.2] Hedonic rating scale: the tester rates how much they like a product on a numbered scale from dislike extremely to like extremely, so it measures liking.
[hom-food-additives-s6.3.3] Food action rating: the tester states how likely they are to buy or eat the product, so it measures willingness to eat or buy.
[hom-food-additives-s6.3.4] Preference ranking: the tester ranks several samples from most to least preferred.
[hom-food-additives-s6.4.b] The other two categories of sensory test ask different questions. Difference tests ask whether people can tell samples apart, and descriptive tests ask what the samples are like.
[hom-food-additives-s6.4.0] Difference tests show whether a difference exists between samples and, if so, how noticeable it is, which tells a company whether consumers would notice a change.
[hom-food-additives-s6.4.1] Simple difference paired comparison: the tester says whether two coded samples, which may be the same or different, are different.
[hom-food-additives-s6.4.2] Directional paired comparison: the tester identifies which of two samples has a greater intensity of a stated characteristic, such as sweetness.
[hom-food-additives-s6.4.3] Triangle test: the tester identifies which of three coded samples is different from the other two. By chance one in three would be right.
[hom-food-additives-s6.4.4] Duo-trio test: the tester is given a reference sample and two coded samples, and identifies which coded sample is the same as the reference, so the other is different. By chance one in two would be right.
[hom-food-additives-s6.4.5] Descriptive tests describe the perceived sensory characteristics of products, such as the effect of processing, packaging or storage on quality.
[hom-food-additives-s6.4.6] Descriptive ranking ranks samples by the intensity of a characteristic; descriptive rating scores that intensity on a scale.
[hom-food-additives-s6.5.b] The triangle test checks whether testers can detect a difference between two products, for example an established product and a lower-salt version. Each tester gets three samples and must pick out the odd one.
[hom-food-additives-s6.5.0] Aim: to find out whether testers can identify the odd sample among three coded samples, and so whether a difference between the two products can be detected.
[hom-food-additives-s6.5.1] Independent variable: the samples, two identical and one different.
[hom-food-additives-s6.5.2] Dependent variable: the tester's choice of the odd sample.
[hom-food-additives-s6.5.3] Controlled variables: temperature, amount, shape, colour and container of each sample, timing of the test and the room conditions, so that only the product itself can explain a correct choice.
[hom-food-additives-s6.5.4] Method: 1. Set up a tray with three coded samples (random three-digit codes) in a balanced, randomised order. 2. Each tester tastes the samples in the order given, with water between samples and no discussion.
[hom-food-additives-s6.5.5] Method, continued: 3. The tester identifies the sample that is different. 4. Record the responses on a sheet. 5. Reveal the codes and count the correct answers.
[hom-food-additives-s6.5.6] Diagram: a tray with three identical, coded containers, one marked as the odd sample for the investigator's records only.
[hom-food-additives-s6.6.b] Once the testers have responded, the answers must be read against chance, because a tester who simply guesses will still be right some of the time. The serving order must also be planned so that position does not bias the answers.
[hom-food-additives-s6.6.0] Serving order: there are six possible orders (AAB, ABA, BAA, ABB, BAB, BBA, where A and B are the two products), and a balanced or randomised order means no sample is favoured by where it sits on the tray.
[hom-food-additives-s6.6.1] Chance: testers guess correctly one time in three, so the number of correct answers is compared with this chance level, using a significance table.
[hom-food-additives-s6.6.2] Expected result: if enough testers, compared with chance, identify the odd sample, a difference is detected.
[hom-food-additives-s6.6.3] A triangle test shows only whether a difference can be detected. A preference test would then show whether testers like the change.
[hom-food-additives-s6.7.b] Sensory tests measure human perception, which is easily disturbed. The room and the testers must therefore be controlled so that the results reflect the food and nothing else.
[hom-food-additives-s6.7.0] Location: a quiet, clean, odour-free room with testing booths or enough space between testers, and no talking so that information is not shared and opinions are not influenced.
[hom-food-additives-s6.7.1] Lighting and ventilation: bright, even, preferably natural, light so that samples are seen clearly, and ventilation that removes lingering odours.
[hom-food-additives-s6.7.2] Timing: mid-morning or mid-afternoon, when taste sensitivity is best, with no strongly flavoured food or drink, smoking or gum for at least 30 minutes before the test.
[hom-food-additives-s6.7.3] Testers who are ill should not take part, because illness dulls taste and smell.
[hom-food-additives-s6.7.4] Organisers: the people who set up the test should not take part as testers, because they already know which sample is which.
[hom-food-additives-s6.7.5] Instructions: clear, identical instructions are given to every tester, and one characteristic is judged at a time.
[hom-food-additives-s6.8.b] The samples themselves must be identical in every way except the feature being tested. Otherwise a tester could be reacting to something other than what the test is measuring.
[hom-food-additives-s6.8.0] Samples: all samples at the same temperature, of uniform shape, colour and amount, and in identical containers, so that nothing but the product differs.
[hom-food-additives-s6.8.1] Number of samples: it is limited so that testers do not become tired or confused.
[hom-food-additives-s6.8.2] Coding and sequence: samples are coded so that the codes do not cause bias, and the order of presentation is planned and balanced, as is the set-up of the trays.
[hom-food-additives-s6.8.3] Palate cleansing: water, or a dry or plain cracker where suitable, is used for rinsing between samples so that one taste does not carry over into the next.
[hom-food-additives-s6.8.4] Hygiene and dietary needs: strict hygiene and food safety are maintained, and special dietary conditions, such as coeliac disease, are catered for.
[hom-food-additives-s6.9.b] We choose, cook and eat food with all of our senses, not just taste. Each sense gives information about whether food is fresh, safe and appetising.
[hom-food-additives-s6.9.0] Sight: appearance, colour, size, shape, gloss and presentation are used to judge whether food looks acceptable and fresh.
[hom-food-additives-s6.9.1] Expectations of colour matter: mould is acceptable on Stilton cheese but not on bread, and lemon is associated with yellow and mint with green.
[hom-food-additives-s6.9.2] Smell: aroma and odour help to judge freshness, and a rancid or off smell leads to rejection, while a good smell makes food appetising.
[hom-food-additives-s6.9.3] Taste: the taste buds detect sweet, salt, sour and bitter, and umami is the fifth taste, savoury and given by glutamate.
[hom-food-additives-s6.9.4] Touch and mouthfeel: nerves in the mouth detect texture (such as crisp, creamy or gritty), temperature (such as ice cream) and chemical heat, such as the burn of chilli.
[hom-food-additives-s6.9.5] Hearing: the sound of food, such as sizzling sausages, crunchy vegetables or fizzy drinks, influences choice and eating.
[hom-food-additives-s6.9.6] Cooking changes the senses: colour changes, flavours intensify, an aroma develops and textures change, making cooked food more appetising than raw.

CANDIDATES hom-food-additives-s1: 1. Processed foods and three processed-food profiles
[hom-food-additives-s1.0.b] A processed food is any food changed from its raw state by one or more processes, such as milling, heating, freezing, drying, canning or adding ingredients. Processing makes a raw food safer, longer-lasting, more convenient or more valuable.
[hom-food-additives-s1.0.0] Main reasons for processing: to extend shelf life, make food safe to eat, cut down on waste, provide a wide choice of good-quality food all year round and create new food products, which varies the diet.
[hom-food-additives-s1.0.1] The range of processed foods includes frozen, canned, dried, instant or ready-to-eat and cook-chill foods, as well as preserved or pickled foods and fortified foods.
[hom-food-additives-s1.0.2] Minimal processing includes washing, cutting, bagging salad leaves or freezing vegetables, with no ingredient added, so the food stays close to its raw state.
[hom-food-additives-s1.0.3] Extensive processing involves many stages and ingredients, so the finished product is far removed from its raw material.
[hom-food-additives-s1.1.b] Flour is the standard example of extensive processing: wheat grain is turned into a fine powder that looks and behaves nothing like the raw grain, and it then becomes the base of many other foods.
[hom-food-additives-s1.1.0] Flour is made by cleaning and blending wheat grain, then milling it between rollers (break rolls and smooth rolls) and sieving it. This separates the bran and germ from the starchy endosperm (the white inner part of the grain), and white flour is mainly endosperm.
[hom-food-additives-s1.1.1] Removing the bran and germ lowers fibre and some vitamins and minerals, so white flour has fewer of these than wholemeal flour. Nutrients lost in milling, for example B vitamins and iron, may be added back, and flour is a food named as fortified.
[hom-food-additives-s1.1.2] White flour keeps longer than wholegrain flour because the germ, which contains oils that can turn rancid (go off), has been removed.
[hom-food-additives-s1.1.3] Improvers (additives that help flour perform when baked) or added nutrients may be incorporated at the end of milling.
[hom-food-additives-s1.1.4] Flour is weighed into paper bags. The label gives the brand, product name, ingredients, best-before date, quantity, nutrition information and storage.
[hom-food-additives-s1.1.5] Flour is then an ingredient in bread, cakes, pasta and breakfast cereals, each of which passes through further stages such as mixing, baking or extrusion (forcing through a shaped opening), then packaging.
[hom-food-additives-s1.1.6] Setting: a visit to a local mill shows these stages in practice.
[hom-food-additives-s1.2.b] Milk spoils quickly because micro-organisms and enzymes act on it. Preservation methods slow or stop the micro-organisms and enzymes that spoil food, so milk goes through several treatments at the creamery.
[hom-food-additives-s1.2.0] A creamery receives milk from farms, tests its quality, cools it, separates cream from skimmed milk, standardises the fat content, pasteurises it, cools it again, packs it and keeps it in chill storage.
[hom-food-additives-s1.2.1] Pasteurisation heats milk (to about 72 °C for 15 seconds) to destroy pathogens and most spoilage organisms, then cools it rapidly. The milk still needs refrigeration and keeps for days.
[hom-food-additives-s1.2.2] UHT (ultra-high temperature) treatment heats milk to about 135 °C for a few seconds. Sealed in aseptic (germ-free) packaging, it keeps for months at room temperature until opened. Sterilised milk is heated even more severely in the sealed bottle.
[hom-food-additives-s1.2.3] Homogenisation forces milk under pressure through tiny openings, breaking fat globules so that cream does not rise to the top. This improves consistency but is not a preservation step and does not kill bacteria.
[hom-food-additives-s1.2.4] Canning heats food in sealed containers to destroy micro-organisms; freezing and storage at or below -18 °C stops most micro-organisms from growing.
[hom-food-additives-s1.2.5] Drying removes moisture so micro-organisms cannot grow, and vacuum packing removes air to slow spoilage.
[hom-food-additives-s1.2.6] Setting: a visit to a local creamery or cheese plant shows milk being pasteurised and separated in practice.
[hom-food-additives-s1.3.b] Added value means processing a raw material into a product with greater economic value, not a more nutritious one. The product is worth more to the manufacturer and is better matched to consumer taste, convenience or health.
[hom-food-additives-s1.3.0] For example, cook-chill soups and readymade meals, cheese and grated cheese, flavoured yoghurts, sliced cooked ham, herb-flavoured oils and vinegars, jams, pickles and fruit sauces.
[hom-food-additives-s1.3.1] Cook-chill foods are cooked, chilled rapidly (to 3 °C within 30 minutes and fully chilled within 90 minutes), stored and transported at 1-3 °C, and reheated thoroughly before serving.
[hom-food-additives-s1.3.2] Milk is the raw material for cheese and yoghurt; each involves culturing, coagulating or otherwise changing the milk into a new product with a higher selling price.
[hom-food-additives-s1.3.3] Cheese: pasteurised milk is inoculated (given a starter) with lactic acid bacteria, which turn lactose into lactic acid. Rennet coagulates it into curds and whey, the curds are cut and the whey drained, then the curds are heated, cheddared, salted, moulded and pressed.
[hom-food-additives-s1.3.4] The cheese is then ripened for months, graded, cut and sealed.
[hom-food-additives-s1.3.5] The manufacturer gains a higher selling price, while the consumer gains convenience, taste or a health benefit.
[hom-food-additives-s1.4.b] Yoghurt is a worked example of added value: ordinary milk is fermented into a thicker, sour product that sells for more. Follow the stages in order.
[hom-food-additives-s1.4.0] 1. Milk is tested on reception and its fat content is standardised (adjusted to a set level); milk solids may be added to give body.
[hom-food-additives-s1.4.1] 2. Milk is homogenised under pressure so that fat globules stay evenly dispersed.
[hom-food-additives-s1.4.2] 3. Milk is pasteurised to destroy pathogens, then cooled to the temperature the starter culture needs.
[hom-food-additives-s1.4.3] 4. Lactic acid bacteria, and any probiotic culture, are added and the milk is incubated; the bacteria ferment lactose to lactic acid.
[hom-food-additives-s1.4.4] 5. The falling pH (it becomes more acidic) coagulates the casein (the main milk protein), giving yoghurt its thick texture and sour flavour.
[hom-food-additives-s1.4.5] 6. Once the correct acidity is reached, the yoghurt is chilled quickly to slow fermentation.
[hom-food-additives-s1.4.6] 7. Fruit purée, permitted sugar, stabiliser or flavouring may be blended in; the yoghurt is filled, sealed, coded and kept refrigerated in the chill chain (the unbroken cold storage from factory to shop).
[hom-food-additives-s1.5.b] Some foods are chosen for more than their basic nutrients. A functional food has a health benefit on top of ordinary nutrition.
[hom-food-additives-s1.5.0] A functional food gives health benefits beyond its basic nutritional value because of an added or naturally present ingredient.
[hom-food-additives-s1.5.1] Live yoghurt with probiotic bacteria supports the digestive system, because the added bacteria are the beneficial ingredient.
[hom-food-additives-s1.5.2] Plant sterol spreads reduce blood cholesterol, so they are chosen by people wanting to lower it.
[hom-food-additives-s1.5.3] Omega-3 enriched foods are promoted for heart health.
[hom-food-additives-s1.5.4] Folic acid enriched foods reduce the risk of neural tube defects (birth defects of the brain and spine) in a developing baby.
[hom-food-additives-s1.6.b] Fortification is a way of putting nutrients into foods that people eat every day. It is different from food supplements, which are tablets.
[hom-food-additives-s1.6.0] Fortification adds nutrients so that their level in the food is higher than the level naturally present.
[hom-food-additives-s1.6.1] Fortification replaces nutrients lost in processing, so the food is not poorer, and addresses public health needs.
[hom-food-additives-s1.6.2] Breakfast cereals are often fortified with iron and vitamins, fruit juices with vitamin C, and margarine and dairy spreads with vitamins A and D.
[hom-food-additives-s1.6.3] Other foods named as fortified include milk, flour, bread and biscuits.
[hom-food-additives-s1.7.b] Convenience foods are processed foods that are ready to eat or quick to prepare. They are popular because they solve real problems for busy households.
[hom-food-additives-s1.7.0] They save preparation, cooking and cleaning time and need little cooking skill, which suits busy households and people with limited mobility.
[hom-food-additives-s1.7.1] Long shelf life gives a wide choice of food all year round, including out-of-season produce.
[hom-food-additives-s1.7.2] They give consistent quality, taste and portion size, and clear portions aid meal planning.
[hom-food-additives-s1.7.3] Processing reduces waste, so less food is thrown out, and frozen or canned foods keep many of their nutrients.
[hom-food-additives-s1.7.4] They are easy to store, so they are useful as an emergency meal.
[hom-food-additives-s1.8.b] The same foods have drawbacks for health, cost and the environment, which is why they should not make up the whole diet.
[hom-food-additives-s1.8.0] Many are high in salt, sugar and saturated fat and low in fibre.
[hom-food-additives-s1.8.1] They are often more expensive per portion than home-prepared food of similar size, because the maker's processing and packaging costs are added to the price.
[hom-food-additives-s1.8.2] Many contain additives, a minority of consumers react to particular substances.
[hom-food-additives-s1.8.3] Packaging adds waste and environmental impact.
[hom-food-additives-s1.8.4] Reliance on ready meals reduces cooking skills, because meals are no longer made from scratch, and can unbalance the diet.
[hom-food-additives-s1.8.5] Taste, colour and texture can be inferior to the fresh equivalent, because heating and storage change them.
[hom-food-additives-s1.9.b] Ultra-processed foods are at the far end of processing. They are made in factories from refined ingredients and additives rather than from whole foods.
[hom-food-additives-s1.9.0] Ultra-processed foods are industrial formulations made largely from refined ingredients and additives, with little or no whole food. They are classed as group 4 in the NOVA system.
[hom-food-additives-s1.9.1] Examples, such as packaged snacks, sugary drinks, instant noodles and many ready meals.
[hom-food-additives-s1.9.2] They are designed for convenience, palatability (how pleasant they taste) and long shelf life, and often contain flavourings, colourings, emulsifiers and sweeteners that are not used in home cooking.
[hom-food-additives-s1.9.3] They are less healthy than fresh food.
[hom-food-additives-s1.10.b] Two other labels on food describe how it was produced: genetic modification changes the plant or animal itself, while organic farming limits the chemicals used to grow it.
[hom-food-additives-s1.10.0] Genetic modification alters the DNA of a plant or animal to change a characteristic such as size, speed of ripening or resistance to disease.
[hom-food-additives-s1.10.1] Soya and maize are examples of crops widely grown as genetically modified varieties.
[hom-food-additives-s1.10.2] EU law requires foods containing genetically modified ingredients, including foods made from genetically modified soya or maize, to be labelled.
[hom-food-additives-s1.10.3] Organic produce is grown without synthetic chemical fertilisers, pesticides and preservatives.
[hom-food-additives-s1.10.4] Organic farming is less intensive, so it gives lower yields, and must follow rules on fertiliser use and on pest, weed and disease control.
[hom-food-additives-s1.10.5] Organic certification is given by inspection bodies recognised by the Department of Agriculture, Food and the Marine, and certified products carry the symbol of the certifying body.
[hom-food-additives-s1.10.6] Lower yields and more labour make organic food more expensive.
