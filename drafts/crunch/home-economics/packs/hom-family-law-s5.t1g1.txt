TARGET hom-family-law-s5: 5. The Child Care Act 1991
[hom-family-law-s5.0.b] The Child Care Act 1991 makes provision for the care and protection of children (a child is a person under 18). It matters because it gives the State, through Tusla, the Child and Family Agency, the power to act where a child is not safe at home.
[hom-family-law-s5.0.0] The Act places a duty on Tusla, the Child and Family Agency, to promote the welfare of children who are not receiving adequate care and protection. Tusla has done this job since 2014.
[hom-family-law-s5.0.1] Tusla's duties include providing child care and family support services and investigating where a child may be at risk, so it helps families as well as stepping in.
[hom-family-law-s5.0.2] A child is at risk where they are assaulted, neglected or sexually abused, or where their physical, emotional or educational needs are not met. Ill-treatment also counts.
[hom-family-law-s5.0.3] Tusla has power to intervene in a family situation where a child is deemed to be at risk. More widely, this means the child's health, development or welfare is likely to be harmed and the child is unlikely to get the care needed without Tusla's help.
[hom-family-law-s5.0.4] Only Tusla, not individuals, applies to the District Court for the orders in this Act, so the agency acts on behalf of the child and the court decides.
[hom-family-law-s5.0.5] Care is not always compulsory: where parents ask for it or agree to it, Tusla can take a child into care without a court order (voluntary care).
[hom-family-law-s5.0.6] The Act balances the family's right to bring up their child with the child's right to protection, so Tusla acts only where a child is at risk and the court decides on the evidence.
[hom-family-law-s5.0.7] The Act's protections support children in the family alongside the protection orders available under the Domestic Violence Act 2018, and Tusla can also apply for protection orders on behalf of a child.
[hom-family-law-s5.1.b] Some children need protection at once, before a court can hear a full case. The Act therefore provides short-term measures, ordered by urgency, which give way to a longer order only if the court decides one is needed.
[hom-family-law-s5.1.0] Where there is an immediate and serious risk to a child, a Garda can remove the child to safety and place them in Tusla's care. Tusla must then seek a court order if it intends to keep the child in care.
[hom-family-law-s5.1.1] An emergency care order is a short-term court order that allows a child to be removed to a safe place where there is an immediate risk. It allows Tusla to take the child into care at once.
[hom-family-law-s5.1.2] An emergency care order can be made without notice to the parents, because waiting to tell them could leave the child in danger.
[hom-family-law-s5.1.3] An interim care order is a court order made while the court considers a full application, so that the child is protected in the meantime.
[hom-family-law-s5.1.4] Emergency and interim orders are temporary, and the court must decide whether a longer order is needed.
[hom-family-law-s5.2.b] A care order is the serious step in the Act: the child is removed from the family home. It is made by the court, not by Tusla itself, and it is for a child who needs care and protection they are unlikely to get at home.
[hom-family-law-s5.2.0] A care order is made by the court on application by Tusla, where a child's welfare requires that they be placed in care.
[hom-family-law-s5.2.1] A care order can place a child in care for a long or short term, away from the family home.
[hom-family-law-s5.2.2] Once a care order is made, Tusla takes responsibility for the child's care until the order ends and decides where the child is placed, such as with foster carers or in a residential unit.
[hom-family-law-s5.2.3] Removal from the family home under a care order is the most serious step the Act allows, so the court must be satisfied that the child's welfare requires it.
[hom-family-law-s5.3.b] Not every child at risk needs to leave home. A supervision order lets the child stay with the family while Tusla keeps a close watch, so it is the less intrusive option.
[hom-family-law-s5.3.0] A supervision order is made by the court on application by Tusla, so that the child can stay at home.
[hom-family-law-s5.3.1] Under a supervision order, Tusla personnel can visit the child in the home periodically to monitor the child's health and welfare.
[hom-family-law-s5.3.2] A supervision order allows Tusla to support the family, and to give support or directions to it, and keep the child at home while monitoring the risk.
[hom-family-law-s5.3.3] A supervision order is the less intrusive option, because the child is not removed from the family home.
[hom-family-law-s5.3.4] The court makes the decision on the evidence put before it by Tusla, including whether the child's welfare requires removal or monitoring.

CANDIDATES hom-family-caring-s2: 2. Child–parent relationships
[hom-family-caring-s2.0.b] Young children depend entirely on adults to meet their basic needs for growth and survival. If a physical need is missed, the child's health and development suffer, so each one matters.
[hom-family-caring-s2.0.0] Food: a balanced diet in suitable amounts supports growth, and new foods are introduced as the child grows so that the body gets all the nutrients it needs.
[hom-family-caring-s2.0.1] Shelter: a warm, dry, safe home protects children from weather and hazards, because young children cannot regulate their body temperature or avoid danger for themselves.
[hom-family-caring-s2.0.2] Clothing: suitable clothing protects against cold and injury and allows free movement, so the child can play and explore.
[hom-family-caring-s2.0.3] Sleep and rest: regular sleep supports growth, concentration and the repair of the body.
[hom-family-caring-s2.0.4] Health care: regular check-ups, immunisation (vaccination against disease) and prompt treatment of illness prevent disease and support healthy development.
[hom-family-caring-s2.0.5] Hygiene: washing, care of teeth and toilet training prevent infection, because germs spread easily where hygiene is poor.
[hom-family-caring-s2.0.6] Safety and protection: supervision and safe surroundings prevent accidents and abuse, since young children cannot recognise danger.
[hom-family-caring-s2.1.b] Children need more than food and shelter. Their emotional and mental needs shape how confident they feel and how well they relate to others, now and as adults.
[hom-family-caring-s2.1.0] Love and affection: warm physical and verbal affection helps children feel valued, so they grow up believing they matter.
[hom-family-caring-s2.1.1] Security and trust: a dependable, consistent caregiver teaches children that the world is predictable and safe.
[hom-family-caring-s2.1.2] Attachment: a strong bond with a consistent caregiver forms the basis for later relationships, because the child learns what a trusting relationship feels like.
[hom-family-caring-s2.1.3] Praise and encouragement: recognising effort builds self-esteem (belief in your own worth) and motivation.
[hom-family-caring-s2.1.4] Stimulation and learning: play, conversation, reading and exploration develop language and thinking.
[hom-family-caring-s2.1.5] Routine and boundaries: consistent limits help children learn self-control and understand expectations.
[hom-family-caring-s2.1.6] Social contact: interaction within the family teaches children to form friendships and relate to others.
[hom-family-caring-s2.2.b] How well a child's needs are met in the early years affects the adult they become.
[hom-family-caring-s2.2.0] When physical and psychological needs are met, children grow into well-adjusted, confident adults who are able to contribute to society.
[hom-family-caring-s2.2.1] When needs go unmet, children are more likely to grow up insecure, with poor self-esteem and difficulty forming relationships.
[hom-family-caring-s2.3.b] Adolescents are changing quickly in body and mind. They still need care, but they need it in a different form from young children, with more freedom and respect.
[hom-family-caring-s2.3.0] Growth: rapid growth (the growth spurt) increases the body's need for energy, protein, calcium and iron, and adequate physical activity supports healthy development.
[hom-family-caring-s2.3.1] Sleep: adolescents need extended sleep because they are growing, and changes in their body clock can make early starts difficult.
[hom-family-caring-s2.3.2] Independence: adolescents need increasing freedom to make choices, with parents gradually reducing direct control while keeping limits.
[hom-family-caring-s2.3.3] Identity and self-esteem: adolescents build a personal identity through relationships, interests and feedback from others.
[hom-family-caring-s2.3.4] Peer acceptance: friendships become central, so a safe place at home to meet friends supports them.
[hom-family-caring-s2.3.5] Privacy: adolescents need personal space and private time, which parents should respect, because it helps them develop their own identity.
[hom-family-caring-s2.3.6] Emotional support: adolescents need to be listened to without judgement when problems arise, so they feel able to turn to parents.
[hom-family-caring-s2.3.7] Guidance on risks: adolescents benefit from advice on the risks they meet, so they can make safe choices as they gain independence.
[hom-family-caring-s2.4.b] The family is where children's needs are met day to day, mostly through ordinary routines and the way adults behave.
[hom-family-caring-s2.4.0] Daily routines: regular meal, sleep and homework times give structure and security, so the child knows what to expect.
[hom-family-caring-s2.4.1] Modelling: children learn behaviour, language and values by watching how adults act.
[hom-family-caring-s2.4.2] Teaching: parents teach self-care, manners, safety and social skills during everyday activities.
[hom-family-caring-s2.4.3] Health monitoring: parents arrange medical care, watch for illness and promote healthy eating and exercise.
[hom-family-caring-s2.4.4] Responsiveness: a prompt response to a child's distress builds attachment and trust.
[hom-family-caring-s2.4.5] The home is where language, values and early skills are first learned, before formal schooling begins. This first learning within the family is called primary socialisation.
[hom-family-caring-s2.5.b] Families do not meet every need alone. The State also helps with the cost and care of rearing children.
[hom-family-caring-s2.5.0] Education through schools, for example, gives every child the chance to learn and develop.
[hom-family-caring-s2.5.1] Child Benefit is a regular payment to parents for each child, which helps with the cost of rearing children.
[hom-family-caring-s2.5.2] Medical cards give access to medical care for families who qualify, so health needs are not left untreated because of cost.
[hom-family-caring-s2.5.3] Supports for disadvantaged pupils in school help children from poorer backgrounds to take part and do well.
[hom-family-caring-s2.5.4] Child-protection services through Tusla, the Child and Family Agency, protect children from harm.
[hom-family-caring-s2.6.b] Children are not just dependants. They have rights, which are things every child is entitled to, and these are linked to the needs children have.
[hom-family-caring-s2.6.0] Right to life, and to a name and nationality, so the child has an identity and belongs to a country.
[hom-family-caring-s2.6.1] Right to be cared for by parents and to live with them, unless this would be contrary to the child's interests.
[hom-family-caring-s2.6.2] Right to protection from abuse and neglect, because children cannot defend themselves.
[hom-family-caring-s2.6.3] Right to education and to develop physically, mentally, socially, morally and spiritually.
[hom-family-caring-s2.6.4] Right to rest, play, leisure, culture and recreation, because play is how children learn and relax.
[hom-family-caring-s2.6.5] Right to protection from discrimination, so every child is treated fairly.
[hom-family-caring-s2.6.6] Right to maintenance, meaning financial support from parents. The duty of parents to maintain their children is in the Family Law (Maintenance of Spouses and Children) Act 1976.
[hom-family-caring-s2.6.7] Right to express an opinion and be heard in matters affecting the child, in line with age and maturity. Where parents live apart, children also have the right to see both parents.
[hom-family-caring-s2.7.b] Children's rights come from international and Irish law, and they are balanced with the duties of parents.
[hom-family-caring-s2.7.0] The UN Convention on the Rights of the Child was adopted in 1989 and ratified by Ireland in 1992.
[hom-family-caring-s2.7.1] The Irish Constitution, since the Children Amendment, states the State's duty to protect children's rights.
[hom-family-caring-s2.7.2] Rights come with parents' duties, and the State steps in only where a child's welfare requires it.
[hom-family-caring-s2.7.3] The Child Care Act 1991 sets out the State's duty to protect children whose health, development or welfare is at risk.
[hom-family-caring-s2.7.4] The Child Care Act 1991 gives the State, through Tusla, the Child and Family Agency, powers to protect children at risk. These include care orders and emergency care orders, which can place the child outside the home, for example with foster carers.
[hom-family-caring-s2.8.b] Conflict is common in adolescence because young people are moving towards adulthood while parents are still responsible for them. Most causes come back to the wish for independence.
[hom-family-caring-s2.8.0] Adolescents seek independence and may feel their parents are out of touch with them.
[hom-family-caring-s2.8.1] Peer and media influence can bring adolescents' values into conflict with the values of their parents.
[hom-family-caring-s2.8.2] Adolescents accept adult responsibilities but are not yet allowed adult freedoms, which causes frustration.
[hom-family-caring-s2.8.3] Relationships with peers and partners can take priority over schoolwork and family commitments, leading to disagreement.
[hom-family-caring-s2.8.4] Parents may struggle to let go gradually and trust young people, such as by allowing more freedom, while still needing to set limits.
[hom-family-caring-s2.8.5] Common flashpoints include curfews (a time to be home by), technology and money.
[hom-family-caring-s2.9.b] Conflict is easier to resolve when both sides stay calm and work towards a solution together instead of trying to win.
[hom-family-caring-s2.9.0] Firm but fair discipline: rules are enforced consistently and, where possible, explained and agreed.
[hom-family-caring-s2.9.1] Listening and defining the issue: each side states its view and the problem is named clearly rather than made personal.
[hom-family-caring-s2.9.2] Negotiation and compromise: possible solutions are weighed together and reviewed after a trial period.
[hom-family-caring-s2.9.3] Home atmosphere: adolescents are more likely to resolve conflict when they feel free to talk to parents.
[hom-family-caring-s2.9.4] Parents can draw on their greater life experience while still hearing the young person's view.
[hom-family-caring-s2.9.5] Parents need to give trust gradually, so the young person has the chance to show responsibility.
[hom-family-caring-s2.10.b] Role-play lets you practise handling a disagreement, such as a curfew, in a safe way.
[hom-family-caring-s2.10.0] 1. Choose a conflict, for example an adolescent who wants a later curfew, and give one person the part of the adolescent and one the parent.
[hom-family-caring-s2.10.1] 2. Each person states their view without interrupting, so each side feels heard.
[hom-family-caring-s2.10.2] 3. Use "I" statements, such as "I feel left out when I have to leave early", so the other person is not blamed.
[hom-family-caring-s2.10.3] 4. Name the problem clearly and suggest a compromise, for example a later curfew at weekends.
[hom-family-caring-s2.10.4] 5. Agree a trial period and a time to review it, then discuss how it went.
[hom-family-caring-s2.11.b] Communication is how family members share what they need and feel. Good communication keeps relationships strong and helps problems get solved.
[hom-family-caring-s2.11.0] Communication lets members express needs, feelings and opinions, so that each person feels heard, valued and secure.
[hom-family-caring-s2.11.1] Problems discussed openly are more likely to be solved before they become serious conflict.
[hom-family-caring-s2.11.2] Bottled-up feelings, such as anger kept hidden, can lead to resentment, stress and withdrawal.
[hom-family-caring-s2.11.3] Discussion of spending, meals, childcare and the care of vulnerable members allows household work to be shared fairly.
[hom-family-caring-s2.11.4] Explaining the reasons for rules, rather than simply imposing them, makes them more likely to be accepted.
[hom-family-caring-s2.11.5] Children learn language, empathy and negotiation by taking part in conversation with adults.
[hom-family-caring-s2.11.6] Older members who encourage and praise achievement build confidence in younger members.
[hom-family-caring-s2.11.7] Secrecy or aggressive criticism creates mistrust, loneliness and conflict.
[hom-family-caring-s2.12.b] Good communication is a skill, and it can be blocked by everyday habits.
[hom-family-caring-s2.12.0] Active listening means listening without interrupting, using calm language and checking understanding before replying.
[hom-family-caring-s2.12.1] Taking turns to speak gives everyone a chance to be heard, so no one person dominates.
[hom-family-caring-s2.12.2] Family meetings give a regular, planned time to discuss plans and problems, so issues are raised before they grow.
[hom-family-caring-s2.12.3] Barriers to communication include lack of time, screens, differences in age and tone of voice.
