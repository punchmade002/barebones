TARGET hom-dairy-eggs-s4: 4. Cheese
[hom-dairy-eggs-s4.0.b] Cheese is made by coagulating (setting) milk protein and draining off the liquid whey. Cheeses are grouped by how firm and moist they are and by how they are ripened, so each class has typical examples that exams ask you to name.
[hom-dairy-eggs-s4.0.0] Hard cheese is low in moisture, firm and matured for longer, so it keeps well. Examples are Cheddar, Parmesan, Cheshire and Emmenthal.
[hom-dairy-eggs-s4.0.1] Semi-hard or semi-soft cheese sits between hard and soft in firmness. Examples are Gouda, Edam and Halloumi.
[hom-dairy-eggs-s4.0.2] Soft cheese is high in moisture. Cottage cheese, cream cheese, feta and mozzarella are fresh (eaten young, with feta also kept in brine), while Brie and Camembert are ripened by a mould on the surface.
[hom-dairy-eggs-s4.0.3] Blue-veined cheese is ripened by a mould that grows through the cheese, giving the blue veins. Examples are Cashel Blue (an Irish cheese), Stilton, Roquefort and Gorgonzola.
[hom-dairy-eggs-s4.0.4] Processed cheese is natural cheese melted and blended with emulsifying salts and other ingredients, then moulded into a uniform product, so it has a longer shelf life and melts easily. Examples are cheese slices, cheese spreads and processed cheddars.
[hom-dairy-eggs-s4.0.5] Smoked cheese is cheese flavoured by smoking, often sold as a processed product, so it is grouped with processed cheese.
[hom-dairy-eggs-s4.0.6] Diagram: draw a table with the columns class, description, examples and uses, with one row for each class of cheese.
[hom-dairy-eggs-s4.1.b] Cheese is concentrated milk: most of the water and lactose are drained away, leaving the protein, fat and minerals. That is why it is rich in some nutrients and has none of others.
[hom-dairy-eggs-s4.1.0] Protein is about 10 to 27% of cheese, mostly casein. It is high biological value (HBV) protein, containing all the essential amino acids (the ones the body cannot make), for growth, repair and the making of enzymes, hormones and antibodies.
[hom-dairy-eggs-s4.1.1] Protein content varies by type, because soft cheese contains less protein than hard cheese.
[hom-dairy-eggs-s4.1.2] Fat is about 4 to 35%, mostly saturated, with cholesterol, so full-fat cheese supplies concentrated energy. Cottage cheese is about 4% fat.
[hom-dairy-eggs-s4.1.3] The more water in a cheese, the less fat it has, and cheese made from skimmed milk is lower in fat.
[hom-dairy-eggs-s4.1.4] Carbohydrate is 0 to about 1.5%, because most of the lactose drains away in the whey and the rest changes to lactic acid.
[hom-dairy-eggs-s4.1.5] Vitamin A and beta-carotene support healthy skin, vision and membranes. Riboflavin (B2) and B12 are the main B group vitamins supplied.
[hom-dairy-eggs-s4.1.6] Calcium and phosphorus support bones and teeth, and some vitamin D is supplied to help calcium absorption. Total minerals are about 1.5 to 4%.
[hom-dairy-eggs-s4.1.7] Sodium comes mainly from the salt added during production, so cheddar and processed cheese are high in salt. Cheese is deficient in vitamin C, iron and fibre, and its water content varies by type.
[hom-dairy-eggs-s4.2.b] Dietetic value means how suitable cheese is for different people and diets. It has strong points as a food and some limits that matter for health.
[hom-dairy-eggs-s4.2.0] Cheese is a relatively cheap protein food with no waste, needs no cooking, and suits packed lunches and snacks.
[hom-dairy-eggs-s4.2.1] Protein and calcium support growth in children and adolescents, and are needed in pregnancy and by nursing mothers.
[hom-dairy-eggs-s4.2.2] Cheese is a high-energy food because of its fat, so it suits active people.
[hom-dairy-eggs-s4.2.3] Cottage cheese and low-fat varieties suit low-kilocalorie, low-cholesterol and weight-watching diets. Full-fat cheeses are high in saturated fat and salt, so people with heart disease or high blood pressure should limit them.
[hom-dairy-eggs-s4.2.4] Some cheeses are difficult to digest because they are high in fat.
[hom-dairy-eggs-s4.2.5] Pregnant women are advised to avoid mould-ripened soft cheeses such as Brie and Camembert, blue cheeses and soft cheeses made from unpasteurised milk, because of the risk of listeria (a food poisoning bacterium). Hard cheese, cottage cheese, cream cheese and pasteurised processed cheese are considered safe.
[hom-dairy-eggs-s4.2.6] Cheese lacks carbohydrate and fibre, so it should be eaten with foods such as bread, crackers, pasta or potatoes, and with vegetables or fruit for fibre and vitamin C.
[hom-dairy-eggs-s4.2.7] Cheese is versatile and comes in wide variety, which helps variety in the diet. It sits on the milk, yoghurt and cheese shelf of the Food Pyramid, where it should be eaten in small amounts and in lower-fat forms.
[hom-dairy-eggs-s4.3.b] Two ingredients turn liquid milk into cheese: bacteria that sour it, and an enzyme that sets it. Each does a different job, and exams often ask you to keep them apart.
[hom-dairy-eggs-s4.3.0] Lactic acid bacteria are added to pasteurised milk as a starter culture. They convert lactose (milk sugar) into lactic acid, which preserves the cheese, adds flavour and helps the curd to form.
[hom-dairy-eggs-s4.3.1] Rennet contains the enzyme rennin, which changes caseinogen (the soluble milk protein) into casein (the insoluble form). Rennet traditionally comes from calf stomach, while vegetarian rennet is made from microbes or plants.
[hom-dairy-eggs-s4.3.2] The casein coagulates, so the milk proteins bond and set, and the milk separates into curds and whey.
[hom-dairy-eggs-s4.3.3] Curds are the solid part. Whey is the liquid part, which holds most of the lactose and water, so little lactose stays in the cheese.
[hom-dairy-eggs-s4.4.b] Making cheese follows a fixed order, from adding the bacteria to ripening. Learn the stages in sequence, with what each one achieves.
[hom-dairy-eggs-s4.4.0] 1. A culture of lactic acid bacteria is added to pasteurised milk, to turn lactose into lactic acid.
[hom-dairy-eggs-s4.4.1] 2. The milk is heated to about 30°C and rennet is added. Left to stand for about 30 minutes, it separates into curds and whey.
[hom-dairy-eggs-s4.4.2] 3. The curds are cut or chopped to release more whey, and the whey is drained off.
[hom-dairy-eggs-s4.4.3] 4. The curds are heated to 35 to 40°C (called scalding), which squeezes out more whey and gives the correct consistency.
[hom-dairy-eggs-s4.4.4] 5. Cheddaring (for Cheddar and similar cheeses): the curds are cut into blocks and piled on each other, and turned, to complete drainage of the whey.
[hom-dairy-eggs-s4.4.5] 6. The blocks are milled (cut) and about 2% salt is added for flavour and preservation.
[hom-dairy-eggs-s4.4.6] 7. The salted curds are placed in moulds and pressed. The moulds may be sprayed with hot water so that a protective rind forms.
[hom-dairy-eggs-s4.4.7] 8. The cheese is removed from the moulds, date stamped, and stored for about 3 to 12 months to ripen (mature), then graded. Ripening develops flavour and texture, and longer maturing gives a stronger, drier cheese.
[hom-dairy-eggs-s4.5.b] The steps above give a hard cheese such as Cheddar. Ripened cheeses use a mould to get their character, which is the contrast to know.
[hom-dairy-eggs-s4.5.0] Blue cheese has a mould added, and the cheese is pierced to let air in, so the mould can grow through it.
[hom-dairy-eggs-s4.5.1] Soft ripened cheeses such as Brie and Camembert have a mould growing on the surface.
[hom-dairy-eggs-s4.6.b] Packaging protects the cheese, and labelling tells the buyer what it is and how to store it. Both carry marks in the production question.
[hom-dairy-eggs-s4.6.0] Cheese is vacuum packed in polythene (plastic) zip-lock bags, which extends shelf life. The pack protects it from mould and drying.
[hom-dairy-eggs-s4.6.1] Waxed paper or wax coating and plastic tubs are used, with tubs suiting smaller portions.
[hom-dairy-eggs-s4.6.2] The label gives the type of cheese, brand, quantity and nutritional information.
[hom-dairy-eggs-s4.6.3] The label also gives the date stamp, storage instructions and allergen information (milk).
[hom-dairy-eggs-s4.6.4] Labels must comply with Regulation (EU) No 1169/2011, given effect in Ireland by the European Union (Provision of Food Information to Consumers) Regulations 2014.
[hom-dairy-eggs-s4.7.b] Heat changes both the protein and the fat in cheese. Knowing these changes explains how to cook it well.
[hom-dairy-eggs-s4.7.0] Colour changes as the cheese browns.
[hom-dairy-eggs-s4.7.1] The protein coagulates and shrinks.
[hom-dairy-eggs-s4.7.2] The fat melts, and if the cheese is overheated the fat separates from it and makes it oily.
[hom-dairy-eggs-s4.7.3] Overcooked cheese becomes stringy, tough and hard to digest, and overcooking can carbonise (burn) it.
[hom-dairy-eggs-s4.7.4] Little loss of nutrients occurs when cheese is cooked.
[hom-dairy-eggs-s4.7.5] To avoid these faults, grate or slice the cheese, cook it at a moderate temperature for a short time, and add it at the end of a sauce.
[hom-dairy-eggs-s4.8.b] Cheese is versatile because it can be eaten cold, melted, grated or cooked into dishes. Match the type of cheese to the dish.
[hom-dairy-eggs-s4.8.0] Sandwiches and snacks, including cheese slices in sandwiches.
[hom-dairy-eggs-s4.8.1] Baking and pastry, such as cheese scones, cheese pastry and cheese straws.
[hom-dairy-eggs-s4.8.2] Savoury and sweet fillings, and sauces such as white cheese sauce and macaroni cheese.
[hom-dairy-eggs-s4.8.3] Desserts, such as cheesecake made with cream cheese, and cheeseboards.
[hom-dairy-eggs-s4.8.4] Main ingredient in pizza, pasta, potato and salad dishes.
[hom-dairy-eggs-s4.8.5] Dips, fondue, and toppings and garnishes such as gratin.
[hom-dairy-eggs-s4.8.6] Choosing the cheese: Parmesan, a hard cheese, is grated over pasta carbonara; cream cheese is used in lemon cheesecake; cheddar slices are used in a cheese sandwich; mozzarella goes on pizza; grated hard cheese suits toppings that brown.
[hom-dairy-eggs-s4.9.b] Irish farmhouse cheese is a speciality (artisan) food that has grown in popularity. Exams ask for reasons, each explained.
[hom-dairy-eggs-s4.9.0] Farmhouse cheese is made on the farm in small quantities, mainly by hand, usually from the farm's own cow's, goat's or sheep's milk.
[hom-dairy-eggs-s4.9.1] Examples are Cashel Blue (Co Tipperary, blue), Gubbeen (West Cork, washed rind), Durrus (West Cork) and Coolea (Co Cork, Gouda style).
[hom-dairy-eggs-s4.9.2] Its distinctive flavours, textures and degrees of maturity differ from mass-produced cheese, and there is a wide variety, including soft, hard, blue, flavoured, organic and reduced-fat cheeses.
[hom-dairy-eggs-s4.9.3] It is seen as high quality, hygienic, less processed and more natural.
[hom-dairy-eggs-s4.9.4] Consumers value traceable local food, with fewer food miles, and support for local employment and rural family farm businesses.
[hom-dairy-eggs-s4.9.5] It is promoted and sold at farmers' markets, specialist shops, restaurants, food tourism, and by celebrity chefs and the media, and it suits the cheeseboard and entertaining.
[hom-dairy-eggs-s4.9.6] Awards and quality assurance strengthen its premium image.

CANDIDATES hom-food-safety-s4: 4. Uses of micro-organisms in food production
[hom-food-safety-s4.0.b] Not all micro-organisms spoil food. Some are added on purpose, because their feeding changes the food in useful ways, and this process is called fermentation.
[hom-food-safety-s4.0.0] Fermentation is the breakdown of organic substances, such as sugars, by bacteria or yeast, usually in the absence or shortage of oxygen. It is used to make many foods.
[hom-food-safety-s4.0.1] The by-products of fermentation include carbon dioxide, energy, acids (such as lactic acid and acetic acid) and alcohol, which is why fermented foods taste and behave differently from the raw ingredients.
[hom-food-safety-s4.0.2] In lactic acid fermentation, bacteria turn sugars such as lactose (the sugar in milk) into lactic acid. This is the basis of yoghurt and cheese.
[hom-food-safety-s4.0.3] In alcoholic fermentation, yeast turns sugars into alcohol and carbon dioxide. This is the basis of bread, beer and wine.
[hom-food-safety-s4.1.b] Milk contains the sugar lactose, which lactic acid bacteria feed on. Their acid changes the milk's flavour and texture, which is how milk becomes cheese or yoghurt.
[hom-food-safety-s4.1.0] Starter cultures are lactic acid bacteria added to milk to begin fermentation. They ferment the lactose in milk into lactic acid, giving cheese and yoghurt their sour flavour and texture.
[hom-food-safety-s4.1.1] In cheese-making, the lactic acid works together with rennet to curdle the milk and set the curd, so acid on its own is not enough. Ripening by further bacteria or moulds then develops the flavour.
[hom-food-safety-s4.1.2] Yoghurt is made by fermenting milk, typically with Lactobacillus bulgaricus and Streptococcus thermophilus. The lactose becomes lactic acid, so the milk thickens and tastes sour. Some yoghurts add other strains.
[hom-food-safety-s4.1.3] Probiotic (functional) yoghurts and drinks contain live bacteria that may benefit the intestinal flora (the helpful bacteria living in the gut) when eaten, so they are examples of functional foods.
[hom-food-safety-s4.2.b] Moulds are fungi that grow as fine threads. In a few foods a particular mould is added deliberately, and each gives the food its own flavour.
[hom-food-safety-s4.2.0] Blue-veined cheese: the mould Penicillium roqueforti grows through the cheese, producing blue-green veins and a sharp flavour. Cashel Blue, made in Co. Tipperary, is an Irish example; Roquefort and Stilton are others.
[hom-food-safety-s4.2.1] Surface-ripened soft cheese: Penicillium camemberti grows on the outside and gives the white rind of Camembert and Brie.
[hom-food-safety-s4.2.2] Soy sauce is produced by the mould Aspergillus oryzae, which breaks down the protein and starch in soya beans by fermentation, giving the sauce its savoury flavour.
[hom-food-safety-s4.3.b] Yeast is a single-celled fungus, not a bacterium. It ferments sugar into alcohol and carbon dioxide, and different foods make use of different parts of that result.
[hom-food-safety-s4.3.0] In bread-making (including sourdough), yeast ferments the sugars in the dough and produces carbon dioxide, which makes the dough rise. This is why yeast is called a biological raising agent.
[hom-food-safety-s4.3.1] The alcohol produced in the dough evaporates during baking, so bread is not alcoholic.
[hom-food-safety-s4.3.2] In brewing, yeast ferments the sugars in malted barley (barley that has been germinated) to produce alcohol and carbon dioxide, giving beer.
[hom-food-safety-s4.3.3] In wine-making, yeast ferments the sugars in grape juice into alcohol. Spirits are distilled from fermented liquids.
[hom-food-safety-s4.3.4] Vinegar is made by acetic acid bacteria, such as Acetobacter, which oxidise (add oxygen to) alcohol to form acetic acid.
[hom-food-safety-s4.4.b] Micro-organisms are also used in meat products and as foods in their own right. Some provide protein or vitamins rather than flavour.
[hom-food-safety-s4.4.0] Salami, pepperoni and chorizo are made by adding bacterial starter cultures to cured meat. The bacteria ferment its sugars to lactic acid, which flavours the product and helps to preserve it.
[hom-food-safety-s4.4.1] Quorn is a meat substitute made from mycoprotein, which comes from the fungus Fusarium venenatum grown by fermentation in large fermenters. It is high in protein and fibre and low in saturated fat.
[hom-food-safety-s4.4.2] Mycoprotein is a novel protein food, meaning a new kind of food produced by fermentation rather than from animals. It is from a fungus, not from yeast, bacteria or plants.
[hom-food-safety-s4.4.3] Mushrooms and truffles are large fungi that are eaten directly as food.
[hom-food-safety-s4.4.4] Yeast extracts and yeast-based food supplements are used as sources of B vitamins, and bacteria are also used in food supplements.
[hom-food-safety-s4.5.b] Using micro-organisms on purpose gives foods qualities that the raw ingredients do not have.
[hom-food-safety-s4.5.0] Fermentation adds flavour and changes texture, for example the sourness of yoghurt, the veins of blue cheese and the rise of bread dough.
[hom-food-safety-s4.5.1] Fermented foods can have a longer keeping time, because acids such as lactic acid and acetic acid are produced, for example in salami and vinegar.
[hom-food-safety-s4.5.2] Micro-organisms improve nutritional value, since yeast extracts supply B vitamins and mycoprotein supplies protein.
[hom-food-safety-s4.5.3] They add variety to the diet, from cheeses, yoghurts and breads to meat substitutes such as Quorn.

CANDIDATES hom-proteins-s4: 4. Sources
[hom-proteins-s4.0.b] Protein comes from both animal and plant foods, so everyone can meet their needs whatever they eat. Exam answers earn marks for naming a variety of foods from each group.
[hom-proteins-s4.0.0] Animal protein sources are meat, poultry, fish, eggs, milk, cheese and yoghurt. Cheese and yoghurt belong in the list because they are made from milk.
[hom-proteins-s4.0.1] Plant protein sources are soya beans and soya products, pulses (lentils, peas and beans), cereals (wheat, rice, maize and oats), nuts and seeds.
[hom-proteins-s4.0.2] Cereals such as wheat, rice and maize supply protein as well as carbohydrate, so everyday foods like wheat, bread and pasta are plant protein sources.
[hom-proteins-s4.0.3] Potatoes and vegetables contain only small amounts of protein, so they are not counted as main protein sources.
[hom-proteins-s4.1.b] Albumin is the protein most people meet first, in the white of an egg. It is a good example of a protein that is tied to one particular food.
[hom-proteins-s4.1.0] Albumin is the main protein of egg white (ovalbumin), and it is also found in lean meat.
[hom-proteins-s4.1.1] Lactalbumin, a related albumin, is found in the whey of milk, the watery part left when the casein is removed.
[hom-proteins-s4.1.2] Albumin is a soluble globular protein. It dissolves in water and coagulates (sets) when heated, which is why raw egg white turns solid and white on cooking.
[hom-proteins-s4.2.b] Casein is the main protein of milk, so it follows milk into every food made from milk. Cheese-making depends on changing it.
[hom-proteins-s4.2.0] Casein (the older term is caseinogen) is the main protein of milk.
[hom-proteins-s4.2.1] Casein is present in cheese and yoghurt, the products made from milk.
[hom-proteins-s4.2.2] Casein is coagulated by the enzyme rennin, which is the first step in making cheese because it forms the curd. Acid also coagulates it, which is why milk curdles when it sours.
[hom-proteins-s4.3.b] Gelatine is the protein behind jelly, but it is not found in raw meat. It is made when another protein, collagen, is heated.
[hom-proteins-s4.3.0] Gelatine is not present in raw meat; it forms when collagen from animal bones, skin and connective tissue is heated in moisture.
[hom-proteins-s4.3.1] Gelatine sets to a gel when it cools, so it is used in jelly, aspic, gelatine-set mousses and desserts, marshmallows and some sweets.
[hom-proteins-s4.3.2] Gelatine comes from animal sources, so foods set with it are not vegetarian.
[hom-proteins-s4.4.b] Gluten is the protein that gives wheat bread its structure. It does not exist in dry flour; it is formed when the flour is wet.
[hom-proteins-s4.4.0] Gluten is the protein of wheat and wheat flour.
[hom-proteins-s4.4.1] Gluten forms when the proteins gliadin and glutenin, both already present in the flour, are mixed with water. Flour itself contains gliadin and glutenin, not formed gluten.
[hom-proteins-s4.4.2] Gluten is elastic and stretchy, and kneading develops it. It gives bread dough its elastic, stretchy texture and traps the carbon dioxide made by yeast, so the dough rises.
[hom-proteins-s4.4.3] Foods that depend on wheat gluten include bread, pasta, cakes, pastry and other flour products.
[hom-proteins-s4.5.b] Each of these proteins is tied to a food or food group. Pairing every named protein with one food is the quickest way to answer a naming question.
[hom-proteins-s4.5.0] Albumin: found in egg white and meat, and it sets when heated.
[hom-proteins-s4.5.1] Casein: found in milk, cheese and yoghurt, and it is coagulated by rennin.
[hom-proteins-s4.5.2] Gelatine: found in set desserts such as jelly, and it is made from collagen.
[hom-proteins-s4.5.3] Gluten: found in wheat flour and bread, and it traps gas in dough.
[hom-proteins-s4.6.b] Meat and fish are made of muscle and connective tissue, and each contains its own proteins. Higher Level questions ask you to name them.
[hom-proteins-s4.6.0] Myosin and actin are the contractile proteins of muscle, which means they contract to move the muscle. They are found in the flesh of meat and fish.
[hom-proteins-s4.6.1] Collagen is the main protein of connective tissue and is found in meat, fish and bones.
[hom-proteins-s4.6.2] Collagen in meat and fish is the source of gelatine when it is heated in moisture; this is why cheap tough cuts of meat tenderise on slow, moist cooking.
[hom-proteins-s4.6.3] Elastin in meat and globulin in egg are also acceptable answers when asked for proteins in meat or egg.

CANDIDATES hom-proteins-s5: 5. Properties
[hom-proteins-s5.0.b] Cooking and food preparation change protein in two linked steps. First the folded protein chain unfolds (denaturation), then the unfolded chains join up and set (coagulation). Both are permanent, and together they explain most of what happens to eggs, meat, fish and milk.
[hom-proteins-s5.0.0] Denaturation is the irreversible unfolding of the secondary and tertiary structure of a protein (the folded shape of its chain), without breaking its peptide bonds (the links between amino acids), so the chain keeps its amino acids but changes shape.
[hom-proteins-s5.0.1] Denatured chains lose the original shape and functional properties of the protein, because the function of a protein depends on its shape.
[hom-proteins-s5.0.2] Denatured chains can then link to one another, and this linking is coagulation.
[hom-proteins-s5.0.3] Coagulation is the setting of denatured protein into a solid or semi-solid mass, which is why a runny raw egg becomes firm when cooked.
[hom-proteins-s5.0.4] Denaturation changes the texture and appearance of a food, and denatured protein is more digestible, so cooked protein foods are easier to digest than raw ones.
[hom-proteins-s5.0.5] Denaturation can be caused by heat, mechanical action (such as whisking), chemicals (such as acid and salt) and enzymes.
[hom-proteins-s5.0.6] Egg white coagulates from about 60 degrees C, turning from transparent to opaque and firm; the yolk sets at about 68 degrees C. These are approximate temperatures because eggs set over a range.
[hom-proteins-s5.0.7] Fish flesh changes from translucent to opaque as its protein coagulates during cooking, which is how you can tell fish is cooked.
[hom-proteins-s5.0.8] Examples of coagulation in cookery: omelette, quiche, boiled egg, meringue, soufflé and lemon meringue pie.
[hom-proteins-s5.1.b] A foam is a mass of air bubbles held in a thin film. Egg white makes a good foam because its protein, albumen, can be stretched into a film around each bubble and then set by heat.
[hom-proteins-s5.1.0] Whisking or beating stretches and partly denatures the albumen chains, which unfold and arrange themselves around the air bubbles. This is mechanical action, not heat, that creates the foam.
[hom-proteins-s5.1.1] The protein film around each bubble holds the trapped air, forming a foam.
[hom-proteins-s5.1.2] Gentle heat in cooking then coagulates the albumen around the bubbles, setting the foam permanently, which is why a baked meringue stays firm.
[hom-proteins-s5.1.3] Over-whisking denatures the protein too far and breaks the film, so the foam becomes grainy and collapses.
[hom-proteins-s5.1.4] Examples: meringue, pavlova, soufflé and whisked sponge cake.
[hom-proteins-s5.2.b] A gel is a liquid held in a soft solid. Some proteins, especially gelatine, can do this: they dissolve in hot liquid and set into a network as it cools.
[hom-proteins-s5.2.0] A gel forms when protein chains (gelatine) dissolved in a hot liquid cool and link into a network that traps the liquid, such as water.
[hom-proteins-s5.2.1] Gelatine is the main protein used to make gels in cookery.
[hom-proteins-s5.2.2] A firmer gel results from a higher concentration of gelatine, because more chains form a denser network.
[hom-proteins-s5.2.3] Examples: jelly, aspic, mousses and cheesecake.
[hom-proteins-s5.3.b] Dry heat cooks food without added liquid. It acts mainly on the surface protein, which is why roasted and grilled foods firm up and brown.
[hom-proteins-s5.3.0] Dry heat (roasting, baking, grilling and frying) denatures and coagulates the surface protein, causing shrinkage and firmness.
[hom-proteins-s5.3.1] Prolonged dry heat dries the muscle protein and makes meat tough and dry, so overcooked meat is chewy.
[hom-proteins-s5.3.2] Dry heat at high temperatures produces the Maillard reaction, which browns the surface of meat, bread and biscuits.
[hom-proteins-s5.4.b] The golden-brown crust on bread and roast meat is not caused by sugar alone or by enzymes. It comes from protein and sugar reacting together under dry heat.
[hom-proteins-s5.4.0] The Maillard reaction is non-enzymic browning, which happens when the amino acids of protein react with a reducing sugar (a sugar such as glucose) on heating.
[hom-proteins-s5.4.1] It needs high heat and a dry surface with little moisture; it does not need enzymes.
[hom-proteins-s5.4.2] It produces brown colour and roasted, savoury flavour, as in a toasted bread crust, roast meat, grilled or toasted cheese and biscuits.
[hom-proteins-s5.5.b] Moist heat cooks food in liquid or steam at lower temperatures. For meat it does something dry heat cannot: it changes the tough connective tissue into a soft jelly.
[hom-proteins-s5.5.0] Moist heat (boiling, stewing, poaching and braising) converts the collagen (the tough protein in connective tissue) to gelatine, which tenderises meat.
[hom-proteins-s5.5.1] Collagen conversion needs long, slow cooking in moisture, so cheaper cuts of meat, which contain a lot of connective tissue, become tender.
[hom-proteins-s5.5.2] Prolonged high heat toughens muscle protein, so gentle moist heat is used for tender results.
[hom-proteins-s5.5.3] Heat breaks the weak bonds that hold the folded structure, which is why heat denatures protein.
[hom-proteins-s5.6.b] Whisking, beating and mincing physically stretch and break protein, so they change texture without any heat.
[hom-proteins-s5.6.0] Whisking, beating and mincing unfold protein chains; whisking egg white is the example that forms a foam.
[hom-proteins-s5.6.1] Kneading bread dough develops the gluten (the protein of wheat flour) into an elastic network that traps the carbon dioxide from yeast, so the dough rises.
[hom-proteins-s5.6.2] Beating meat with a mallet breaks up the muscle fibres and tenderises it.
[hom-proteins-s5.6.3] Excessive agitation denatures protein so far that it becomes tough or curdled.

[hom-dairy-eggs-s1.0.b] Milk is a complete food for young mammals, meaning it supports a growing baby animal, and a useful source of most nutrients for people. It is widely available, so it is a staple of the Irish diet.
