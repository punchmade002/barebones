TARGET hom-clothing-selection-s4: 4. Elements and principles of design in clothing
[hom-clothing-selection-s4.0.b] The elements are the visual building blocks of a garment, and each can be changed to alter the look of the outfit. There are five: line, shape, colour, texture and pattern.
[hom-clothing-selection-s4.0.0] Line: a visible edge or direction in a garment, created by seams, necklines, hemlines, trims, pleats, fastenings and pattern. Where the eye is led along a line, the garment seems to go in that direction.
[hom-clothing-selection-s4.0.1] Shape: the outline of a garment or of an area within it, such as a neckline, sleeve or pocket. The outline of the whole garment is its silhouette.
[hom-clothing-selection-s4.0.2] Colour: hue (the actual colour), tone (how light or dark it is) and intensity (how bright or dull it is). Warm colours such as red and orange appear to advance, while cool colours such as blue and green appear to recede.
[hom-clothing-selection-s4.0.3] Colour schemes can be monochrome or tonal (one colour in different shades), contrasting or complementary. Colour should also suit the wearer's skin, hair and eye tones.
[hom-clothing-selection-s4.0.4] Texture: the surface quality of a fabric, which can be smooth, rough, shiny or matt, and which can be seen or felt.
[hom-clothing-selection-s4.0.5] Pattern: the repetition of line, shape or colour over a surface, such as stripes, checks, herringbone, zig-zag, polka dots, florals or geometric prints.
[hom-clothing-selection-s4.1.b] Because the eye follows lines, the direction of the lines in a garment changes how tall, wide or soft the wearer appears. Designers use this to flatter the figure.
[hom-clothing-selection-s4.1.0] Vertical lines give the impression of length, so they make the figure look slimmer and taller.
[hom-clothing-selection-s4.1.1] Horizontal lines draw the eye across the body, which makes the figure look wider and shorter.
[hom-clothing-selection-s4.1.2] Diagonal lines add contrast and movement, and they lead the eye across the garment.
[hom-clothing-selection-s4.1.3] Curved lines soften an outfit and add a feminine feel, such as the curve of a rounded neckline.
[hom-clothing-selection-s4.1.4] A princess seam runs from the shoulder or armhole to the hem. It shapes the garment to the body without a waist seam, and the long unbroken line also helps the wearer look taller.
[hom-clothing-selection-s4.2.b] Colour and texture change how much light a garment reflects or absorbs, and that changes how big the wearer appears. Remember that these are effects on appearance only.
[hom-clothing-selection-s4.2.0] Dark colours appear to reduce the size of the figure because they absorb light, while light and bright colours appear to enlarge it. Dark navy and black also define and slim the silhouette.
[hom-clothing-selection-s4.2.1] One colour worn from head to toe, or tonal shades of one colour, creates a longer, unbroken line, so the figure looks taller. Tonal shades also add depth.
[hom-clothing-selection-s4.2.2] Contrasting or complementary colours at the top and bottom divide the outfit and can balance the shape of the figure, for example when the shoulders and hips are different widths.
[hom-clothing-selection-s4.2.3] Shiny fabrics reflect light and increase the apparent size of the figure, while matt fabrics absorb light and reduce it.
[hom-clothing-selection-s4.2.4] Rough, thick or heavily textured fabrics look bulkier, while smooth fabrics give a neater, more streamlined finish.
[hom-clothing-selection-s4.3.b] Pattern and shape work in the same way as line and colour: they decide where the eye goes and how wide or narrow the figure looks.
[hom-clothing-selection-s4.3.0] Small patterns suit small figures, and a larger figure can carry a large pattern well.
[hom-clothing-selection-s4.3.1] Vertical stripes add height, and horizontal stripes add width and draw attention to the middle of the figure.
[hom-clothing-selection-s4.3.2] A patterned area draws the eye to the place where it is worn, for example near the face or neckline for the upper body or at the hem.
[hom-clothing-selection-s4.3.3] Narrow rectangular shapes slim the figure, while triangular or A-line shapes widen the hem or shoulders and so can balance the hips or shoulders.
[hom-clothing-selection-s4.3.4] A fitted bodice with a full skirt gives an hourglass shape, and a fitted top with straight trousers gives a streamlined shape.
[hom-clothing-selection-s4.4.b] The principles describe how the elements are arranged to create a unified garment. There are five: proportion, balance, emphasis, harmony and rhythm.
[hom-clothing-selection-s4.4.0] Proportion: the relationship between the sizes of the parts of a garment, to each other and to the wearer, such as pockets sized to the garment, a collar sized to the neckline, or jacket length to skirt length.
[hom-clothing-selection-s4.4.1] Balance: an even distribution of visual weight. It can be vertical, symmetrical (both sides match) or asymmetrical (the sides differ but still feel even).
[hom-clothing-selection-s4.4.2] Emphasis: a centre of interest that draws the eye to one area, created by a brooch, contrasting trim, a belt, a bright accessory, a bold hemline or a neckline. There should be only one centre of emphasis in an outfit.
[hom-clothing-selection-s4.4.3] Harmony (unity): the sense that all parts of a design work together and complement one another, for example colours, fabrics and style that suit each other, or one colour repeated.
[hom-clothing-selection-s4.4.4] Rhythm or repetition: the repeated use of a colour, line or shape, such as a repeated button or stripe, which moves the eye through the garment.
[hom-clothing-selection-s4.4.5] Diagram: when asked to sketch an outfit, label the elements and principles used, such as line, shape, proportion, harmony and emphasis.
[hom-clothing-selection-s4.5.b] Choosing the elements well lets a wearer look taller, slimmer or better balanced, and draw attention to a feature or away from one. This is the use of the elements that exam questions ask about directly.
[hom-clothing-selection-s4.5.0] To look taller or slimmer, use vertical lines, one colour head to toe or tonal shades, dark colours such as navy and black that absorb light, V-necks, princess seams, a single long line from shoulder to hem and narrow shapes.
[hom-clothing-selection-s4.5.1] To balance or add width, use horizontal stripes, light or bright colours and bulky textures. Contrasting top and bottom colours balance the shoulders and hips.
[hom-clothing-selection-s4.5.2] To draw attention to a feature, put pattern or a bright colour near it, such as near the face for the upper body, or use a belt to define the waist.
[hom-clothing-selection-s4.5.3] To draw attention away from an area, wear dark colour over it, because dark colours appear to reduce size.
[hom-clothing-selection-s4.5.4] Colour also flatters the wearer's hair, eyes and skin; for example, blues can brighten the complexion.
[hom-clothing-selection-s4.5.5] Shape, fit and proportion matter too: choose lines that suit the figure. A short jacket with slim trousers lengthens the leg line.
[hom-clothing-selection-s4.6.b] The way a garment is made creates its line, shape and emphasis. Here the focus is on the effect of each feature on the design.
[hom-clothing-selection-s4.6.0] Seams join the parts of a garment, and their placement creates line and can add shaping to the body.
[hom-clothing-selection-s4.6.1] Darts remove excess fabric to shape a garment around the bust, waist or hips. Pressing defines the darts.
[hom-clothing-selection-s4.6.2] Princess seams shape the garment without a waist seam.
[hom-clothing-selection-s4.6.3] Pleats, tucks and gathers add fullness and movement, and are used at the waist, sleeve or skirt, where they can add visual interest.
[hom-clothing-selection-s4.6.4] Pockets, collars, buttons, trims and fastenings add line and emphasis, and they must be positioned in proportion to the garment.
[hom-clothing-selection-s4.6.5] Fabric choice affects the finished design: crisp fabrics hold a shape, as in tailoring, while soft fabrics drape. The fabric's texture and pattern must also suit the design lines.
[hom-clothing-selection-s4.6.6] Design and colour can be applied to fabric by dyeing, screen, roller, stencil or transfer printing, embroidery and appliqué (fabric shapes stitched on).
[hom-clothing-selection-s4.7.b] A garment is evaluated against three criteria: how well its design serves its function, its comfort and its aesthetic appeal. Examiners often set these as headings for an outfit shown in a picture.
[hom-clothing-selection-s4.7.0] Function: the garment must suit the purpose, activity, climate, weather and occasion it is intended for, such as freedom of movement for sport. Durability, easy care, useful pockets and fastenings that work also count.
[hom-clothing-selection-s4.7.1] Comfort: the fabric (soft, absorbent, breathable or stretch), fit and ease of movement, weight, versatility in different weather and footwear determine whether the wearer is comfortable.
[hom-clothing-selection-s4.7.2] Aesthetic appeal: the colour scheme, line, shape, texture, proportion, balance and emphasis, and any accessories, must work together to produce an attractive and pleasing overall look.
[hom-clothing-selection-s4.7.3] A well-designed garment shows a clear relationship between its design features and the function they serve, for example a drawcord hood, reflective trim or a zip opening.
[hom-clothing-selection-s4.7.4] Using colour and design to flatter the body: choose colours and lines that emphasise the wearer's best features and balance any areas they wish to de-emphasise.
[hom-clothing-selection-s4.7.5] Other headings that appear are shape, proportion, design features, current fashion trend, lifestyle, cost, care and suitability for the occasion.
[hom-clothing-selection-s4.7.6] Method: for each heading make a point from the picture, then give the reason, such as the fabric, cut or colour.
