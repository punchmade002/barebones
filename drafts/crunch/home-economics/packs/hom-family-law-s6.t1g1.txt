TARGET hom-family-law-s6: 6. Making a will
[hom-family-law-s6.0.b] A will is a legal document, governed by the Succession Act 1965, in which a person (the testator) sets out how their property and money, known as the estate, are to be shared after their death. Making one means your wishes are followed instead of leaving the decision to the law.
[hom-family-law-s6.0.0] Without a will (called intestacy), the estate passes under fixed rules in the Succession Act that set the shares, and these may not match the person's wishes.
[hom-family-law-s6.0.1] Under those fixed rules a spouse with no children takes the whole estate, and a spouse with children takes two thirds while the children share one third. Other relatives inherit only if there is no spouse or children, and the State inherits only if there are no relatives.
[hom-family-law-s6.0.2] A will allows the testator to choose who receives each item of property and in what proportions, so the property passes to the people they want.
[hom-family-law-s6.0.3] A will names an executor (the person who carries out the will), who collects the estate, pays the debts and distributes the property as the will directs.
[hom-family-law-s6.0.4] A will can protect a spouse and dependants, and it can state the testator's funeral or burial wishes.
[hom-family-law-s6.0.5] A clearly drawn will reduces the likelihood of disputes among family members over the estate, and it gives the testator peace of mind.
[hom-family-law-s6.0.6] Parents can use a will to appoint a guardian for young children and to make suitable provision for them.
[hom-family-law-s6.0.7] Divorced parents who make a will can ensure that property passes to their children as they intend, can choose an executor, and can change the will later if circumstances alter, such as remarriage or new children.
[hom-family-law-s6.0.8] Careful planning of a will, with a solicitor's advice, may reduce the inheritance tax burden on those who inherit.
[hom-family-law-s6.1.b] The law sets simple rules so that a will really reflects what the testator wanted. If a rule is broken, the will may not be valid.
[hom-family-law-s6.1.0] The testator must be 18 or over (or be or have been married), because the law treats them as old enough to make this decision.
[hom-family-law-s6.1.1] The testator must be of sound mind, meaning able to understand what a will is and what property they own, so that the will reflects their real wishes.
[hom-family-law-s6.1.2] The will must be in writing, and it must be signed by the testator or by someone signing for them in their presence and at their direction.
[hom-family-law-s6.1.3] The signature must be made, or acknowledged, in the presence of two witnesses who are both present at the same time.
[hom-family-law-s6.1.4] The witnesses then sign the will in the presence of the testator.
[hom-family-law-s6.1.5] A witness, or the witness's spouse, who is also a beneficiary (someone who is given a gift in the will) loses the gift made to them under the will, which is why witnesses should be people with nothing to gain. The executor, however, can be a beneficiary.
[hom-family-law-s6.1.6] Good practice is to date the will, store it safely (for example with the solicitor or in a bank) and tell the executor where it is kept, so that it can be found after the death.
[hom-family-law-s6.2.b] Making a will is a matter of working out what you own and who should get it, then putting that in a form the law accepts. It follows six steps.
[hom-family-law-s6.2.0] Step 1: list all assets, such as property, savings, investments, policies and personal possessions, with their value and location, and any debts or liabilities, so you know what the estate contains.
[hom-family-law-s6.2.1] Step 2: decide who the beneficiaries are, starting with the nearest relatives, and record their full names and addresses and the item or share each is to receive.
[hom-family-law-s6.2.2] Step 3: appoint an executor, a trusted person who is willing to act and who will collect the estate, pay the debts and carry out the terms.
[hom-family-law-s6.2.3] Step 4: decide how the estate is divided, including any specific gifts, provision for children and dependants, any guardian, and any funeral or burial arrangements the testator wishes to make. Check this against the legal restrictions in the Succession Act.
[hom-family-law-s6.2.4] Step 5: have the will drawn up. A person may write their own will, but a solicitor is advised so that it is clear and valid.
[hom-family-law-s6.2.5] Step 6: sign the will before two witnesses, store the signed will safely, and tell the executor where it is kept.
[hom-family-law-s6.2.6] After the death, the executor applies for a grant of probate, which confirms the will and gives the executor legal authority to deal with the estate.
[hom-family-law-s6.3.b] A will can be changed at any time while the testator is of sound mind, because it takes effect only on death. The law allows several ways of changing or cancelling it.
[hom-family-law-s6.3.0] A change to an existing will can be made by a codicil, a short document added to the will, which must be signed and witnessed in the same way as the original will.
[hom-family-law-s6.3.1] A new will that expressly revokes (cancels) an earlier will replaces it entirely.
[hom-family-law-s6.3.2] A will can also be revoked by destroying it with that intention.
[hom-family-law-s6.3.3] A will is revoked by the testator's marriage (or civil partnership), unless the will was made in contemplation of that marriage. This is why remarrying parents must make a new will.
[hom-family-law-s6.4.b] A testator is free to choose who inherits, but not completely free. The law protects a surviving spouse, and it gives the executor a formal step to carry out.
[hom-family-law-s6.4.0] A surviving spouse (or civil partner) has a legal right to a share of the estate that the will cannot remove: one-half where there are no children, and one-third where there are children.
[hom-family-law-s6.4.1] Children cannot be left out without thought: a child may ask the court for fair provision if the parent failed in their moral duty to them. Where there is a will, children have no automatic fixed share.
[hom-family-law-s6.4.2] Executors apply for a grant of probate, which confirms the will and gives the executor authority to deal with the estate.
