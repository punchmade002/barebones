CANDIDATES hom-cooking-methods-s6: 6. Recipe balance, presentation and dishes for different needs
[hom-cooking-methods-s6.0.b] A recipe is balanced when a dish or meal supplies the right proportions of nutrients for the people who will eat it. The Department of Health Healthy Eating Guidelines and the Food Pyramid are the checks a cook uses.
[hom-cooking-methods-s6.0.0] A balanced main meal usually includes a protein food, a starchy food and vegetables, so that protein, carbohydrate, vitamins, minerals and fibre are all present, with little added fat, sugar and salt.
[hom-cooking-methods-s6.0.1] On the Food Pyramid, vegetables, salad and fruit form the base, because they should be eaten most. Next come wholemeal cereals and breads, potatoes, pasta and rice, then milk, yogurt and cheese.
[hom-cooking-methods-s6.0.2] Higher up are meat, poultry, fish, eggs, beans and nuts, then a small shelf of fats, spreads and oils, which are used sparingly. Foods and drinks high in fat, sugar and salt sit at the top and are not needed for health.
[hom-cooking-methods-s6.0.3] Wholegrain or wholemeal versions of starchy foods, and pulses, add fibre, B vitamins and minerals to a dish, so they improve balance.
[hom-cooking-methods-s6.0.4] Oily fish, lean meat, pulses and low-fat dairy supply protein with less saturated fat than fatty cuts of meat and full-fat dairy.
[hom-cooking-methods-s6.0.5] Fresh, frozen or tinned fruit and vegetables in a main course or dessert supply vitamin C, folate and fibre, and extra vegetables add vitamins and minerals. Using less fat, sugar and salt also improves balance.
[hom-cooking-methods-s6.0.6] Balance also covers colour, texture, flavour and cost, so that a meal is appealing as well as affordable.
[hom-cooking-methods-s6.1.b] Modifying (adapting) a recipe means changing its ingredients, quantity or method so that it suits the people eating it. There are six main reasons.
[hom-cooking-methods-s6.1.0] To make a dish more nutritious, by adding fibre or reducing fat, sugar or salt, in line with healthy-eating guidelines.
[hom-cooking-methods-s6.1.1] To cater for a person on a special diet, such as a coeliac (someone who cannot eat gluten) who needs gluten-free flour.
[hom-cooking-methods-s6.1.2] To increase or decrease the number of portions, because standard recipes are written for a set number of servings, usually about four adults.
[hom-cooking-methods-s6.1.3] To add variety to a basic recipe, for example by adding nuts to a vegetable stir-fry.
[hom-cooking-methods-s6.1.4] To economise, by substituting an expensive ingredient with a cheaper one, for example margarine for butter in a sponge.
[hom-cooking-methods-s6.1.5] To suit the likes and dislikes of those eating, for example by leaving out an ingredient that a family member avoids.
[hom-cooking-methods-s6.2.b] Making a dish healthier usually means swapping a few ingredients or changing the cooking method. Each change below targets one nutrient.
[hom-cooking-methods-s6.2.0] Reduce fat by grilling, baking, steaming or poaching instead of frying, which adds fat. Also trim visible fat from meat, skim fat from stews, and use a vegetable oil or low-fat spread instead of butter.
[hom-cooking-methods-s6.2.1] Reduce salt by omitting added salt and stock cubes, which are high in salt. Flavour with herbs, spices, garlic or lemon juice instead, and choose reduced-salt products.
[hom-cooking-methods-s6.2.2] Reduce sugar by using less of it, by adding fruit that gives natural sweetness, and by avoiding sugary toppings.
[hom-cooking-methods-s6.2.3] Increase fibre by replacing white flour with wholemeal flour, using brown rice and pasta, and adding oats, pulses and vegetables. Leave the skins on fruit and vegetables, since the skin is a source of fibre.
[hom-cooking-methods-s6.3.b] Other changes alter who the dish suits or how many it feeds. After any change, the result still has to taste right.
[hom-cooking-methods-s6.3.0] Gluten-free cooking replaces wheat flour with a gluten-free flour, such as rice or maize flour, and checks that other ingredients are gluten-free.
[hom-cooking-methods-s6.3.1] Vegetarian versions replace meat with pulses, tofu, eggs or mushrooms, while keeping a source of iron and protein in the dish, because meat is normally where those come from.
[hom-cooking-methods-s6.3.2] Scale a recipe by multiplying or dividing every ingredient by the same factor, so the proportions stay right. Then adjust the tin or dish size and the cooking time, since larger quantities usually take longer, and check the seasoning.
[hom-cooking-methods-s6.3.3] Adapt flavour and texture by substituting herbs and spices, or by using a different fruit or vegetable in season.
[hom-cooking-methods-s6.3.4] Taste and adjust the seasoning after modifying a recipe, because reduced salt or sugar changes the flavour.
[hom-cooking-methods-s6.4.b] Aesthetic awareness is a general reaction to how food looks, smells and feels, not a formal test. Sight, smell and texture help a cook judge food before it is bought and while it is prepared.
[hom-cooking-methods-s6.4.0] Sight judges appearance, colour, size, shape and surface. Colour and appearance show freshness or ripeness, for example a firm, bright, unbruised apple.
[hom-cooking-methods-s6.4.1] Certain colours are linked to flavours, such as yellow with lemon, and people expect particular appearances: mould is accepted on Stilton cheese but not on bread.
[hom-cooking-methods-s6.4.2] Smell detects aroma through receptor cells in the nasal cavity (the nose), which tell us whether food is fresh or rancid (gone off, as fat does).
[hom-cooking-methods-s6.4.3] Texture is judged by mouth feel and by sight, and a variety of textures, such as a crisp salad served with a soft dish, makes a meal more interesting.
[hom-cooking-methods-s6.4.4] The shape and size of cut pieces affect appearance; uniform cuts look neater and cook evenly.
[hom-cooking-methods-s6.5.b] Food that looks and smells good is more appealing and is eaten with more enjoyment. It also helps appetite, which matters especially for children, older people and the ill, and dishes are judged on presentation.
[hom-cooking-methods-s6.5.0] Cut food evenly so it looks neat and cooks evenly. Avoid overcooking, which dulls colour and softens texture; green vegetables cooked briefly in fast-boiling water keep their colour.
[hom-cooking-methods-s6.5.1] A garnish adds colour and contrast, for example parsley on soup, parmesan shavings or grated cheese on pasta, or icing sugar on a sponge or cake.
[hom-cooking-methods-s6.5.2] A garnish should be edible, in keeping with the dish and small enough not to overpower it.
[hom-cooking-methods-s6.5.3] Hot food should be served hot and cold food cold, on warmed plates for hot food and chilled plates for cold food, so the dish stays at its best temperature.
[hom-cooking-methods-s6.5.4] Choose contrasting colours on the plate, such as a red pepper beside green beans, and vary shape and texture.
[hom-cooking-methods-s6.5.5] Arrange food with height and balance, keep the rim of the plate clean and portion each item in proportion to the dish. A neat table setting also adds to the effect.
[hom-cooking-methods-s6.6.b] The same recipe is not right for everyone. The number of people, their ages and activity levels, and the budget all shape what is cooked.
[hom-cooking-methods-s6.6.0] For one person, plan a single-serving dish such as a filled jacket potato, or cook a batch that can be frozen in single portions, which avoids waste.
[hom-cooking-methods-s6.6.1] Someone eating alone, such as an older person or a student, should still make sure the meal supplies enough nutrients.
[hom-cooking-methods-s6.6.2] For a family group, plan meals to suit the ages, tastes, activity levels and budget of the family, use lower-cost cuts and ingredients, and involve the family in choices.
[hom-cooking-methods-s6.6.3] A one-pot or batch dish, such as a casserole, feeds several people at low cost with little washing-up.
[hom-cooking-methods-s6.6.4] Cook a dish in advance and reheat it on a busy day, so that the meal is ready at a convenient time.
[hom-cooking-methods-s6.6.5] Young children need small, soft pieces that are easy to chew, with little added salt and sugar.
[hom-cooking-methods-s6.6.6] Teenagers and active adults need larger portions and energy-rich starchy foods, and teenagers need extra iron and calcium.
[hom-cooking-methods-s6.6.7] Older people often need smaller, nutrient-dense portions (a lot of nutrition in a small amount), with soft textures if chewing is difficult.
[hom-cooking-methods-s6.7.b] A modified diet changes the choice of food and the cooking methods, but the meal must still be balanced and attractive. For each diet, the cook changes particular ingredients.
[hom-cooking-methods-s6.7.0] Coeliac: wheat, barley and rye contain gluten, so gluten-free flours and starches, such as rice and maize, replace them in bread, pastry, sauces and coatings, and labels are checked. Oats must be labelled gluten-free.
[hom-cooking-methods-s6.7.1] Diabetic: carbohydrate portions are controlled and added sugar is reduced, with wholegrain starches and high-fibre vegetables preferred and less fat used.
[hom-cooking-methods-s6.7.2] Low-fat or heart-healthy: lean meat, oily fish and low-fat dairy are used, with grilling, steaming and baking because they need little added fat.
[hom-cooking-methods-s6.7.3] Low-salt: no added salt or stock cubes, processed meats and salted snacks are avoided, and dishes are flavoured with herbs and spices.
[hom-cooking-methods-s6.7.4] Vegetarian and vegan: pulses, tofu, nuts and seeds replace meat, and eggs and dairy foods do so for vegetarians only. Include sources of iron and vitamin B12, use plant-based milks, and check for hidden animal ingredients.
[hom-cooking-methods-s6.7.5] Lactose intolerance: lactose-free milk or a fortified plant alternative replaces milk, and labels are checked for hidden milk ingredients.
[hom-cooking-methods-s6.7.6] High-fibre: wholemeal flour, pulses, vegetables and fruit are used.

CANDIDATES hom-cooking-methods-s5: 5. Food preparation and cooking equipment
[hom-cooking-methods-s5.0.b] Good equipment makes cooking easier and safer, but only if it suits the job. Choose for the quantity of food, the cooking method, the type of hob, durability, ease of cleaning and cost, and check how the material conducts heat (passes heat through it).
[hom-cooking-methods-s5.0.0] Buy equipment you will use often and can store easily, because a gadget used once a year wastes money and cupboard space.
[hom-cooking-methods-s5.0.1] A heavy, flat, thick base spreads heat evenly and reduces hot spots that scorch food and make it stick; a thin base heats unevenly.
[hom-cooking-methods-s5.0.2] Aluminium and copper conduct heat quickly and evenly, so they respond fast when the heat changes.
[hom-cooking-methods-s5.0.3] Stainless steel is durable and does not react with acidic foods, but it conducts heat less well, so good pans have a base bonded to copper or aluminium. It is not the best conductor.
[hom-cooking-methods-s5.0.4] Cast iron retains heat well and suits slow, even cooking such as braising, but it is heavy and rusts if left damp. Glass and ceramic dishes suit oven cooking and conduct heat slowly.
[hom-cooking-methods-s5.0.5] A non-stick coating allows frying with little fat, but it is scratched by metal utensils and damaged by overheating, for example heating an empty pan.
[hom-cooking-methods-s5.0.6] A close-fitting lid retains steam, so food simmers with less energy and in less time. The pan base should match the ring size, so that the full base makes contact with the heat source.
[hom-cooking-methods-s5.0.7] Induction hobs heat the pan by magnetism, so they need a pan with a magnetic (ferrous) base; aluminium and copper pans without one will not heat.
[hom-cooking-methods-s5.0.8] Handles should be firm, heat-resistant and fixed securely; plastic and wooden handles are not suitable for the oven unless the manufacturer states it.
[hom-cooking-methods-s5.1.b] A practical kitchen needs a core set of tools, and recipes only work if ingredients are measured accurately.
[hom-cooking-methods-s5.1.0] Pans and dishes: saucepans of different sizes, a frying pan, a roasting tin, a casserole, and baking tins and trays.
[hom-cooking-methods-s5.1.1] Preparation tools: mixing bowls, a sieve, a colander, wooden spoons, a spatula, a whisk, a grater, a rolling pin, a peeler and a tin opener.
[hom-cooking-methods-s5.1.2] Measuring and checking tools: a measuring jug, scales, measuring spoons, a timer and a thermometer.
[hom-cooking-methods-s5.1.3] Weigh and measure accurately with scales, jugs and spoons, because too much or too little of an ingredient can change how a dish turns out.
[hom-cooking-methods-s5.1.4] Ingredients are measured in metric, and spoon measures are level, not heaped, so that every cook gets the same amount.
[hom-cooking-methods-s5.2.b] Knives are the most dangerous everyday tool in the kitchen, so choosing the right one and handling it correctly prevents most cuts. Chopping boards need care too, because they can pass germs between foods.
[hom-cooking-methods-s5.2.0] A cook's knife is used for general chopping, a paring knife for small, detailed work and peeling, a serrated (bread) knife for bread and soft-skinned foods, and a carving knife for carving cooked meat.
[hom-cooking-methods-s5.2.1] A sharp knife is safer than a blunt one because it cuts with less force and is less likely to slip.
[hom-cooking-methods-s5.2.2] The claw grip curls the fingertips of the guiding hand under, so the knuckles guide the blade and the fingertips stay clear of it.
[hom-cooking-methods-s5.2.3] Place the chopping board on a damp cloth or non-slip mat so that it cannot move while cutting.
[hom-cooking-methods-s5.2.4] Colour-coded boards (for raw meat, fish, vegetables and cooked foods) keep them apart, preventing cross-contamination (germs spreading from raw to ready-to-eat food).
[hom-cooking-methods-s5.2.5] Wash boards well after raw meat, and replace badly scored boards because germs can lodge in the cuts.
[hom-cooking-methods-s5.2.6] Carry a knife by its handle with the blade pointing down and away from the body; never leave a knife in a sink of washing-up water, where it cannot be seen.
[hom-cooking-methods-s5.2.7] Store knives in a block, on a rack or on a magnetic strip, so that the edges are protected and the blades are not loose in a drawer.
[hom-cooking-methods-s5.3.b] Most kitchen accidents are burns, scalds, cuts and electric shocks, and most are avoidable with a few habits. Each habit below removes a specific danger.
[hom-cooking-methods-s5.3.0] Hob: turn pan handles inwards over the hob so they cannot be knocked or pulled, use a ring that matches the pan size, and never leave hot oil or fat unattended.
[hom-cooking-methods-s5.3.1] Keep tea towels and flexes away from the hob, where they can catch fire or melt.
[hom-cooking-methods-s5.3.2] Lift lids away from the face so that escaping steam does not scald.
[hom-cooking-methods-s5.3.3] Oven: place the shelves before heating, open the door carefully, and use a stable tray for hot dishes.
[hom-cooking-methods-s5.3.4] Use dry oven gloves or a dry cloth to handle hot dishes; a damp cloth conducts heat and causes scalds.
[hom-cooking-methods-s5.3.5] Electrical: keep the flex of appliances away from hot surfaces and water, never handle an appliance with wet hands, and don't overload sockets.
[hom-cooking-methods-s5.3.6] Switch off and unplug an appliance before cleaning its blades or removing food from them, and do not use one with a damaged flex or plug; it must be repaired or replaced before use.
[hom-cooking-methods-s5.3.7] Pressure cooker: check that the sealing ring and valve are clean and undamaged, never force the lid, and open it only after the pressure has fallen. Microwave: use microwave-safe containers, never put metal in the cavity, and cover and stir food.
[hom-cooking-methods-s5.4.b] Beyond individual appliances, a few habits keep the whole kitchen safe, especially when something goes wrong.
[hom-cooking-methods-s5.4.0] Clean up spills at once, because a wet or greasy floor causes slips and falls.
[hom-cooking-methods-s5.4.1] Keep a fire blanket in the kitchen and know how to use it, so a small pan fire can be smothered quickly.
[hom-cooking-methods-s5.4.2] Never use water on a fat fire, because it causes the burning fat to flare up and spread.
[hom-cooking-methods-s5.5.b] Looking after equipment makes it last longer and keeps food safe. The right care depends on the material, so follow the manufacturer's care instructions.
[hom-cooking-methods-s5.5.0] Wash equipment in hot soapy water or in a dishwasher, then rinse, dry thoroughly and store it clean and dry, so that bacteria cannot grow and metal does not rust.
[hom-cooking-methods-s5.5.1] Do not run cold water into a hot pan; the sudden change in temperature can warp the base and crack a non-stick or ceramic surface. Soak burnt pans before scrubbing.
[hom-cooking-methods-s5.5.2] Cast iron must be dried straight after washing and wiped with a thin film of oil to prevent rust.
[hom-cooking-methods-s5.5.3] Use wooden or silicone utensils on non-stick surfaces, whereas metal utensils scratch them.
[hom-cooking-methods-s5.5.4] Use abrasive pads only on surfaces that tolerate them; stainless steel and cast iron tolerate them better than non-stick coatings.
[hom-cooking-methods-s5.5.5] Wooden items are not left to soak, because the wood absorbs water.
[hom-cooking-methods-s5.5.6] Dishcloths, sponges, tea towels and brushes can carry bacteria and should be changed or washed regularly.
[hom-cooking-methods-s5.5.7] Store knives safely, and stack pans with protection between them so non-stick surfaces are not scratched.
[hom-cooking-methods-s5.6.b] In the practical coursework, equipment is part of what you are marked on, because it shows you can plan and work like a competent cook.
[hom-cooking-methods-s5.6.0] Area B assignments ask you to describe new equipment or a new skill, and the planning section lists the key equipment you will use.
[hom-cooking-methods-s5.6.1] You should be able to name the equipment for a dish, say why it was chosen, and explain how it was used safely and hygienically.

CANDIDATES hom-food-safety-s6: 6. Safe food preparation
[hom-food-safety-s6.0.b] Almost every rule for handling food comes from two facts about food poisoning bacteria: they multiply fastest in warm food, and they travel easily from one food to another. Knowing these makes the individual rules easy to remember.
[hom-food-safety-s6.0.0] Food poisoning bacteria multiply fastest in the danger zone, 5°C to 63°C. This is why safe preparation keeps food cold, hot, or moving quickly through that range.
[hom-food-safety-s6.0.1] Cross-contamination is the transfer of bacteria from raw food to cooked or ready-to-eat food, directly or by hands, chopping boards, cloths, utensils or meat juices. This is why raw and cooked foods are kept apart.
[hom-food-safety-s6.0.2] Freezing does not kill bacteria, it only stops them multiplying, and thawing lets them start multiplying again. So frozen food must be handled with care once it thaws.
[hom-food-safety-s6.1.b] Storage aims to keep food cold or frozen so bacteria cannot multiply, and to stop bacteria passing from one food to another. Short questions usually ask for three to six storage guidelines.
[hom-food-safety-s6.1.0] Cover all food and store it in sealed containers, which prevents drying out, odour transfer and cross-contamination.
[hom-food-safety-s6.1.1] Keep perishable food (food that spoils quickly) in the fridge at 5°C or below, which slows the growth of bacteria. Check the fridge and freezer temperature with a thermometer.
[hom-food-safety-s6.1.2] Store raw meat, poultry and fish on the lowest shelf, on a plate and loosely covered with the shop wrapping removed, so that juices cannot drip onto cooked or ready-to-eat food.
[hom-food-safety-s6.1.3] Keep raw and cooked foods apart, covered or in sealed containers, so bacteria cannot pass between them.
[hom-food-safety-s6.1.4] Cool cooked food quickly before refrigerating or freezing it, and do not overpack the fridge, so that cold air can circulate around the food.
[hom-food-safety-s6.1.5] Check the use-by or best-before date of each food and follow the storage instructions on its label. Use-by is a safety date for highly perishable food, while best-before is a quality date.
[hom-food-safety-s6.1.6] Remove fruit and vegetables from plastic wrapping, which encourages mould, and check them regularly for mould.
[hom-food-safety-s6.1.7] Store dry foods in a cool, dry, well-ventilated cupboard, in vermin-proof containers, and keep storage areas clean.
[hom-food-safety-s6.1.8] Keep perishable food out of the fridge for the shortest possible time while it is being prepared, because food left in warm air soon enters the danger zone.
[hom-food-safety-s6.2.b] Freezing keeps food safe for a long time, but only if it is frozen and thawed properly. Bacteria are not killed by freezing, so the care taken when thawing matters.
[hom-food-safety-s6.2.0] Freeze food quickly at −25°C, then store it at −18°C.
[hom-food-safety-s6.2.1] Defrost frozen food completely before cooking, preferably in the fridge on the bottom shelf, so that it thaws slowly and any drips cannot reach other food.
[hom-food-safety-s6.2.2] Never refreeze food that has thawed, because bacteria multiply while it thaws.
[hom-food-safety-s6.3.b] Cooking and reheating must heat food right through to kill bacteria, and hot food must then stay hot or be eaten promptly. Otherwise bacteria multiply again in the danger zone.
[hom-food-safety-s6.3.0] Cook food thoroughly, using the right time and temperature, and turn or stir it so that heat reaches the centre. Cook meat, poultry and minced meat right through, to a core temperature of at least 72°C for poultry.
[hom-food-safety-s6.3.1] Reheat food quickly until it is above 100°C throughout, and reheat it only once.
[hom-food-safety-s6.3.2] High-risk foods such as stews and gravies should be brought to boiling when reheated, because their moist, protein-rich nature suits bacteria.
[hom-food-safety-s6.3.3] Cover food when reheating. In a microwave, stir or turn the food and allow standing time, so that heat reaches all parts evenly, and follow the appliance's instructions.
[hom-food-safety-s6.3.4] Serve cooked food immediately. If holding it hot, keep it above 63°C, because bacteria multiply rapidly between 5°C and 63°C.
[hom-food-safety-s6.3.5] Eat food that has been held hot within about 90 minutes (1.5 hours).
[hom-food-safety-s6.3.6] Do not keep food in the danger zone. Cool leftovers quickly, refrigerate them and use them soon.
[hom-food-safety-s6.4.b] People carry food poisoning bacteria on their hands, hair, skin and clothes, so the person preparing food is a major source of contamination. Examiners usually ask for four to six personal hygiene points.
[hom-food-safety-s6.4.0] Wash hands in hot water with liquid soap, and a nailbrush for the nails, before handling food, because hands carry food poisoning bacteria. Dry them on a clean towel or paper towel.
[hom-food-safety-s6.4.1] Wash hands after the toilet, sneezing or coughing, changing nappies, handling pets, handling rubbish and handling raw meat.
[hom-food-safety-s6.4.2] Keep nails short and clean and remove jewellery before preparing food, because dirt and bacteria collect under nails and around jewellery.
[hom-food-safety-s6.4.3] Tie hair back or cover it. Wear a clean apron or clothing, and change it if it becomes soiled.
[hom-food-safety-s6.4.4] Cover cuts, boils and sores with a brightly coloured waterproof plaster, so that infection cannot pass to food and the plaster can be seen if it falls into the food.
[hom-food-safety-s6.4.5] Do not cough or sneeze over food, and do not taste food with your fingers or lick your fingers.
[hom-food-safety-s6.4.6] Do not prepare or serve food for others while suffering from diarrhoea or vomiting, because the illness can pass to the people eating it.
[hom-food-safety-s6.5.b] A clean kitchen with sensible habits stops bacteria building up on surfaces and equipment and passing to food. Four kitchen hygiene practices is a common exam question.
[hom-food-safety-s6.5.0] Clean and disinfect work surfaces often, and wash equipment in hot water and detergent. Clean as you go.
[hom-food-safety-s6.5.1] Use colour-coded chopping boards and separate utensils for raw and cooked foods, to prevent cross-contamination.
[hom-food-safety-s6.5.2] Never prepare raw and cooked foods on the same surface.
[hom-food-safety-s6.5.3] Handle food as little as possible during preparation, and keep it covered, because every touch can pass on bacteria.
[hom-food-safety-s6.5.4] Floors, walls and work surfaces should be easy to clean, and the kitchen should have good lighting.
[hom-food-safety-s6.5.5] Effective ventilation reduces condensation, which encourages the growth of micro-organisms.
[hom-food-safety-s6.5.6] The kitchen should have a clean water supply and no access for pets or pests.
[hom-food-safety-s6.5.7] Keep a covered waste bin and empty it regularly, and change clean cloths and dishtowels often.
