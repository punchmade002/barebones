TARGET hom-home-services-s8: 8. Lighting
[hom-home-services-s8.0.b] Light is a form of energy that travels in straight lines as waves, and white light is made of the colours of the spectrum. How a surface or material treats light decides how a room looks, so each property has a practical use in the home.
[hom-home-services-s8.0.0] Materials treat light in three ways: transparent materials let light through, translucent materials scatter it, and opaque materials block it.
[hom-home-services-s8.0.1] Reflection: light rays bounce back from shiny surfaces such as mirrors, glazed tiles, gloss paint and white ceilings, which brightens a room and makes it look larger. Fitting reflectors behind lamps use the same idea.
[hom-home-services-s8.0.2] Refraction: light rays bend as they pass into thick or ridged glass, such as glass bricks or cut crystal, which spreads light through a room and gives decorative effects.
[hom-home-services-s8.0.3] Diffusion (scattering): light scatters when it passes through translucent material such as frosted glass, net curtains, fabric or opal shades, so the light is soft and glare is reduced.
[hom-home-services-s8.0.4] Absorption: dark, matt surfaces take in light rather than reflecting it, so a room with dark walls looks darker, appears smaller and cosier, and needs more light. Pale surfaces absorb less, so choose wall, ceiling and floor colours with the light level in mind.
[hom-home-services-s8.0.5] Dispersion: a prism or crystal splits white light into its component colours, as in a crystal chandelier.
[hom-home-services-s8.0.6] Colour of light: warm light is yellowish and cool light is bluish, and this is described by the lamp's colour temperature. Coloured surfaces and shades also change how colours look in a room.
[hom-home-services-s8.1.b] Good lighting is planned, not added at the end. A plan matches the light to how each room is used, mixes natural and artificial light, and keeps the system safe and easy to look after.
[hom-home-services-s8.1.0] Plan the lighting at the design or building stage, so that outlets, switches, wiring and fittings are positioned before construction begins, which also keeps the cost lower.
[hom-home-services-s8.1.1] Match the lighting to the function of the room and its users: a kitchen and a study need bright task lighting at work surfaces, a living room needs a softer, flexible mix of light, a bedroom needs general and bedside light, and a bathroom needs safe, moisture-protected fittings.
[hom-home-services-s8.1.2] Think about who uses the room, such as children, elderly people, reading or homework, because their needs decide how bright and how safe the lighting must be.
[hom-home-services-s8.1.3] Combine general (ambient) light for the whole room, task light for an activity such as reading, cooking or desk work, and accent light to highlight a picture or feature. Use one or more focal points to set the atmosphere.
[hom-home-services-s8.1.4] Balance natural and artificial light. The aspect of the room (a south-facing room gets most daylight) and the size and position of its windows decide how much daylight it receives. Daylight saves electricity and helps wellbeing.
[hom-home-services-s8.1.5] The colour and size of the room matter: pale walls and ceilings reflect light while dark colours absorb it, and the size and shape of the room decide how many fittings are needed.
[hom-home-services-s8.2.b] Once the right light is chosen for each room, the plan must also be comfortable to live with, safe and easy to look after.
[hom-home-services-s8.2.0] Avoid glare and shadows by placing fittings away from the normal line of sight (for example TV and screens), using shades or indirect light that spread the light, and using several light sources.
[hom-home-services-s8.2.1] Allow flexibility and mood with dimmers, timers, movable lamps, switching in groups and two-way switches on stairs, so the light can suit different activities and times of day.
[hom-home-services-s8.2.2] Safety: use the correct voltage and fuse rating, have fittings installed correctly by an electrician, leave adequate space between the bulb and the shade, avoid trailing flexes, and choose bathroom fittings suited to the zone.
[hom-home-services-s8.2.3] Maintenance: fittings should be easy to clean, bulbs and any replaceable parts should be easy to reach and replace, and switches in sensible places.
[hom-home-services-s8.3.b] Daylight is free and pleasant, so a good plan makes the most of it before adding lamps. A room gets the most daylight when light can enter easily and reflect around it.
[hom-home-services-s8.3.0] Use large windows, skylights and glass bricks, which let in more daylight, and keep clear glass in windows because frosted glass scatters light.
[hom-home-services-s8.3.1] Leave out net curtains and heavy blinds, cut back trees outside the window, and place furniture so it does not block the light.
[hom-home-services-s8.3.2] Use pale colours and mirrors, which reflect daylight deeper into the room.
[hom-home-services-s8.4.b] Fittings are grouped by the job they do. Name the type of lighting first, then give a fitting that does that job.
[hom-home-services-s8.4.0] General lighting lights the whole room: central ceiling fittings such as pendants, semi-flush and flush fittings, chandeliers, rise-and-fall fittings, recessed downlights, and track lights and spotlights.
[hom-home-services-s8.4.1] Task lighting lights a specific activity: table and desk lamps, floor lamps with a pivot or flexible stem, under-cabinet and under-counter lights, pelmet lighting, and recessed downlights over a work area.
[hom-home-services-s8.4.2] Accent lighting highlights a feature or focal point: picture lights, wall brackets, uplighters and downlighters, swivel lights, track lights and spotlights.
[hom-home-services-s8.4.3] Outdoor fittings include sensor lights, solar lights, and column, step and decking lights.
[hom-home-services-s8.5.b] A lamp is energy efficient when most of the electricity it uses becomes light rather than heat. To explain this, compare the old filament bulb with the compact fluorescent lamp (CFL) and the light-emitting diode (LED).
[hom-home-services-s8.5.0] A filament bulb produces light when current heats a tungsten filament until it glows. Most of the energy it uses is given off as heat, not light, so it is inefficient, and it has been phased out of sale in the EU under efficiency rules.
[hom-home-services-s8.5.1] CFL principle: electric current heats electrodes at each end of a coiled (looped) glass tube containing argon gas and mercury vapour. The mercury vaporises and the gas gives off ultraviolet light.
[hom-home-services-s8.5.2] A phosphor coating on the inside of the CFL tube converts the ultraviolet light into visible light.
[hom-home-services-s8.5.3] CFLs use much less energy and last much longer than filament bulbs, but they cost more to buy, are slow to reach full brightness, may cause glare without a shade, and contain mercury, so they must be recycled and disposed of safely.
[hom-home-services-s8.5.4] The EU exemption that allowed mercury in compact fluorescent lamps ended on 24 February 2023, so CFLs are being phased out in favour of LEDs.
[hom-home-services-s8.5.5] LED principle: electricity passes through a semiconductor chip, and electrons combine with gaps called holes and release energy as light, a process called electroluminescence. The colour depends on the semiconductor material.
[hom-home-services-s8.5.6] LEDs use far less electricity than filament bulbs, start at full brightness instantly, give off little heat, have a very long life and come in many shapes and colours. They can be dimmable with a compatible dimmer, and although they cost more to buy, they save money on bills.
[hom-home-services-s8.5.7] Energy-saving use: dimmers, timers, motion sensors, task lighting instead of lighting a whole room, using daylight and switching off unneeded lights reduce the energy used by any lamp.
[hom-home-services-s8.6.b] When you buy a lamp, the watts on the box do not tell you how bright it is. Several labelled details help you choose well.
[hom-home-services-s8.6.0] Brightness is measured in lumens, not watts. Watts measure the power a lamp uses, so a lower-wattage LED can be as bright as a higher-wattage filament bulb.
[hom-home-services-s8.6.1] Check the colour temperature, to get warm (yellowish) or cool (bluish) light that suits the room.
[hom-home-services-s8.6.2] Check that the lamp is compatible with the fitting and with any dimmer, and check its life.
[hom-home-services-s8.6.3] Look for a high rating on the energy label. The EU energy label for light bulbs has used an A to G scale since September 2021.
[hom-home-services-s8.7.b] Modern lighting is smaller, smarter and more efficient than older types, and developments are often judged on energy saving, convenience and mood.
[hom-home-services-s8.7.0] LED strips, bulbs, rope lights and compact fittings come in many colour temperatures, can be dimmable, have a long life and give off little heat, and they can be fitted into small or awkward spaces.
[hom-home-services-s8.7.1] Sensor or motion-activated and timer lighting switches on only when someone is present, saving energy and improving security in halls, stairs, porches and outdoor areas.
[hom-home-services-s8.7.2] Smart lighting can be controlled from a phone, app or voice assistant to set schedules, dim the lights or change their colour.
[hom-home-services-s8.7.3] Solar (photovoltaic) outdoor lights use a small panel to charge a battery during the day for garden and path lights, which light up at night.
[hom-home-services-s8.7.4] Colour-changing LEDs and LED rope lighting create ambience and can be used as accent lighting.
[hom-home-services-s8.7.5] Recessed and under-counter lights suit kitchens, and low-voltage and fibre-optic lighting are further developments.
[hom-home-services-s8.7.6] Dimmable LEDs need a compatible dimmer, and cheap LED lamps may flicker or give poor colour rendering (colours looking unnatural).
[hom-home-services-s8.8.b] This practical work builds the skill of judging lighting for a real room, using examples you collect yourself.
[hom-home-services-s8.8.0] Collect examples of contemporary lighting from magazines, brochures and shop visits, and record the type of fitting and its lamp, such as LED or CFL.
[hom-home-services-s8.8.1] For each example, note whether it provides general, task or accent lighting, and what its packaging states about brightness and colour temperature.
[hom-home-services-s8.8.2] Evaluate each example for energy efficiency, safety, maintenance and suitability for a named room.

CANDIDATES hom-home-services-s3: 3. Electricity
[hom-home-services-s3.0.b] Electricity in the home is described by three quantities: how hard the supply pushes, how much charge flows and how fast an appliance uses energy. The relationship between them lets a student calculate the current an appliance draws.
[hom-home-services-s3.0.0] Voltage (V) is the electrical pressure that pushes current around a circuit, a bit like water pressure in a pipe. The standard Irish domestic supply is 230 V at 50 Hz (50 cycles per second).
[hom-home-services-s3.0.1] Current (I), measured in amperes or amps (A), is the rate of flow of electric charge through a conductor, such as the copper wire in a flex.
[hom-home-services-s3.0.2] Wattage (W) is the rate at which an appliance uses electrical energy, so it measures power. 1 kilowatt (kW) equals 1000 watts.
[hom-home-services-s3.0.3] Power relationship: wattage = voltage × current (W = V × A), so current = wattage ÷ voltage.
[hom-home-services-s3.0.4] Worked example: a 2300 W kettle on a 230 V supply draws 2300 ÷ 230 = 10 A.
[hom-home-services-s3.0.5] A higher wattage appliance uses more electricity per hour, so a 3 kW heater uses three times the energy of a 1 kW appliance running for the same time.
[hom-home-services-s3.0.6] Appliance rating plates give the voltage and wattage, and appliances rated for 230 V must be used on the Irish supply to avoid damage.
[hom-home-services-s3.0.7] The same calculation chooses the plug fuse: a 3 A fuse suits appliances up to about 690 W and a 13 A fuse suits appliances up to about 3000 W, so each plug carries a fuse of 3, 5 or 13 A matched to the appliance.
[hom-home-services-s3.1.b] Every appliance connects to the supply through a plug, and each wire inside it has a separate job. Knowing the jobs explains why the plug is built the way it is.
[hom-home-services-s3.1.0] The live wire (brown) carries current to the appliance, the neutral wire (blue) returns it to complete the circuit, and the earth wire (green and yellow) is a safety wire that does nothing in normal use.
[hom-home-services-s3.1.1] A three-pin plug has one pin for each wire, so live, neutral and earth are each connected to their own pin.
[hom-home-services-s3.1.2] The plug holds a cartridge fuse of 3 A, 5 A or 13 A, matched to the appliance's wattage, so the fuse melts before an overloaded flex can overheat.
[hom-home-services-s3.1.3] The flex is gripped by a cord grip, so a pull on the flex tugs on the grip and not on the wire connections.
[hom-home-services-s3.2.b] Electricity is generated at a distance from the home and reaches it in stages, each changing the voltage for efficient transmission.
[hom-home-services-s3.2.0] Stage 1: generation at a power station, where a generator is turned by steam, water or wind.
[hom-home-services-s3.2.1] Stage 2: a step-up transformer (a device that raises voltage) raises the voltage for transmission on the national grid. High voltage means low current, which reduces the energy lost as heat in the cables.
[hom-home-services-s3.2.2] Stage 3: step-down substations reduce the voltage for distribution on local networks.
[hom-home-services-s3.2.3] Stage 4: a final transformer near the home reduces the voltage to 230 V, the voltage used in homes.
[hom-home-services-s3.2.4] Stage 5: a mains service cable carries the supply into the house, through the sealed meter box, which holds the main fuse and the electricity meter.
[hom-home-services-s3.2.5] Stage 6: the consumer unit (fuse board) with its main switch and circuit breakers distributes the supply to circuits serving sockets, lighting, cookers, immersion heaters and showers.
[hom-home-services-s3.2.6] The consumer unit divides the supply into separate circuits, each with its own protective device, so a fault on one circuit disconnects only that circuit.
[hom-home-services-s3.2.7] Diagram: flow chart of the six stages from power station to consumer unit, with a labelled transformer at each change of voltage.
[hom-home-services-s3.3.b] Households pay for the energy they use, not the power of their appliances, and the unit of energy on the bill is the kilowatt-hour. Once you know it, you can cost any appliance.
[hom-home-services-s3.3.0] A kilowatt-hour (kWh), or unit, is the energy used by a 1000-watt (1 kW) appliance running for one hour. It is energy (power × time), not a rate.
[hom-home-services-s3.3.1] Energy in kWh = power in kW × time in hours. For example, a 2 kW heater running for 3 hours uses 2 × 3 = 6 kWh.
[hom-home-services-s3.3.2] Cost of running an appliance = energy in kWh × the unit rate charged by the supplier. In a worked example, use a made-up rate and label it as an example.
[hom-home-services-s3.3.3] Costing a household's use: add up each appliance's kW × hours, then identify the biggest users, such as heating, the shower, the cooker and the immersion heater.
[hom-home-services-s3.3.4] Reading the meter: the difference between two readings taken a set period apart (new reading minus old reading) gives the number of units used in that period.
[hom-home-services-s3.3.5] The bill has four charges. The standing charge is a fixed daily charge that covers maintenance of the network, meter reading and billing, and is payable whether or not electricity is used.
[hom-home-services-s3.3.6] The unit charge is the price for each kWh used, and appears on the bill as the number of units consumed multiplied by the rate.
[hom-home-services-s3.3.7] The PSO levy is a charge on all customers, set each year by the energy regulator (CRU), that funds support for renewable energy. VAT is added to the bill as well.
[hom-home-services-s3.4.b] A tariff is the pricing plan a supplier uses, and choosing one means matching when you use electricity to when it is cheapest.
[hom-home-services-s3.4.0] A standard tariff charges one rate all day.
[hom-home-services-s3.4.1] A night-rate or day-and-night tariff, also called a time-of-use tariff, charges less for electricity used at night or off-peak, so storage heaters, immersion heaters and dishwashers can be run then.
[hom-home-services-s3.4.2] A smart meter records use in intervals, so it supports time-of-use pricing and helps a household shift use to cheaper periods.
[hom-home-services-s3.5.b] Faults can cause fires or fatal shocks, so each circuit has devices that cut the supply. Fuses and MCBs react to too much current, while an RCD reacts to current leaking to earth, so they do different jobs.
[hom-home-services-s3.5.0] A fuse is a deliberate weak link: a thin wire inside the fuse heats and melts when the current exceeds its rating, breaking the circuit and preventing fire. It breaks once and must be replaced, and is found in plugs and old fuse boards.
[hom-home-services-s3.5.1] A miniature circuit breaker (MCB) is a switch that trips (switches off) when the current exceeds its rating, in an overload or short circuit, or when a fault occurs. It can be reset once the fault is repaired, and is found in the consumer unit.
[hom-home-services-s3.5.2] A residual current device (RCD) compares the current in the live and neutral wires. A difference signals current leaking to earth through a person or fault, and it trips within milliseconds.
[hom-home-services-s3.5.3] Socket circuits are commonly protected by RCDs with a sensitivity of 30 mA, the usual domestic setting, and 30 mA RCDs are required on socket circuits. They can trip before a fatal shock is delivered, protection that fuses and MCBs do not give.
[hom-home-services-s3.5.4] The earth wire, usually green and yellow, is a safe path for fault current to flow to earth, for example if a fault makes the metal casing of a kettle, cooker or washing machine live.
[hom-home-services-s3.5.5] The surge of current along the earth wire blows the fuse or trips the MCB, so the metal casing cannot stay live to shock the user.
[hom-home-services-s3.5.6] Appliances with double insulation have a plastic casing, shown by the symbol of a square within a square, and no earth wire, so the appliance cannot become live through a metal part.
[hom-home-services-s3.5.7] Each circuit has its own protective device in the consumer unit, so a fault on one circuit disconnects only that circuit.
[hom-home-services-s3.6.b] A ring circuit is a single circuit that supplies many sockets in a room or floor from one protective device.
[hom-home-services-s3.6.0] The live, neutral and earth conductors run from the consumer unit, out through each socket in turn, and return to the same breaker in the consumer unit, forming a continuous loop.
[hom-home-services-s3.6.1] Each socket is supplied from two directions, so the current divides between two paths and the cable can be smaller than a single radial cable (one run out to the sockets) carrying the same load.
[hom-home-services-s3.6.2] Many sockets can be added to the ring, and a fault cuts off only that ring.
[hom-home-services-s3.6.3] Upstairs and downstairs ring circuits are often wired separately, usually one ring per floor, so a fault on one floor does not disconnect the other. Lighting and the cooker are on separate circuits.
[hom-home-services-s3.6.4] A spur is a short branch from a socket in the ring, which can supply one additional socket or one fixed appliance.
[hom-home-services-s3.6.5] The ring has its own protective device, an MCB or fuse, in the consumer unit, which trips if the total current on the loop exceeds its rating.
[hom-home-services-s3.6.6] Diagram: a wiring circuit showing the consumer unit, live, neutral and earth conductors forming a loop, sockets along the loop and one spur labelled.
[hom-home-services-s3.7.b] These guidelines each remove one danger: water near electrical equipment, damaged flexes or appliances, and overloaded sockets.
[hom-home-services-s3.7.0] Never mix water and electricity: do not handle electrical equipment with wet hands, and keep water and electricity apart in the bathroom and kitchen, because water conducts current through the body. Bathroom switches are pull-cords, and the only appliance normally used there is a shaver socket.
[hom-home-services-s3.7.1] Do not overload sockets or use multiple adaptors or long extension leads; each plug and socket should be used within its rating, because too much current overheats the wiring and starts fires. Avoid trailing flexes.
[hom-home-services-s3.7.2] Check flexes, plugs and sockets regularly for damage, replace damaged flexes, and never pull a plug out by its flex.
[hom-home-services-s3.7.3] Unplug appliances before cleaning or repairing them, and never repair an appliance yourself while it is connected to the supply.
[hom-home-services-s3.7.4] Keep flexes away from hobs, sinks and heat sources, and never cover a heater or drape clothes over it.
[hom-home-services-s3.7.5] Use only appliances with a recognised safety mark, fit the right fuse and follow the instructions. Do not use portable heaters in bathrooms.
[hom-home-services-s3.7.6] Electric blankets should be checked for damage and wear, and not folded or left switched on under a sleeping person.
[hom-home-services-s3.7.7] Outdoor sockets and appliances need an RCD and weatherproof fittings; repairs and new circuits must be carried out by a qualified, registered electrician.
[hom-home-services-s3.7.8] Unplug all appliances, except a fridge or freezer, before going to bed to reduce the risk of an electrical fire, and fit smoke alarms.
[hom-home-services-s3.8.b] Gas can leak, and burning it in the wrong conditions makes a poisonous gas. Safe use means knowing what to do about a leak, about carbon monoxide, and about ventilation and servicing.
[hom-home-services-s3.8.0] Natural gas and LPG are odourless without the added odorant, so a gas smell is an emergency. The three immediate actions are to turn off the gas supply at the meter, open doors and windows, and not use switches, matches or other flames.
[hom-home-services-s3.8.1] Then call the gas emergency number, or leave the house and call from outside.
[hom-home-services-s3.8.2] Carbon monoxide is produced by incomplete combustion, when gas, oil or solid fuel burns without enough air, for example from a blocked flue or a faulty boiler. It has no smell and causes headache, dizziness and nausea.
[hom-home-services-s3.8.3] A carbon monoxide alarm gives early warning of the gas, and should be fitted in rooms containing fuel-burning appliances.
[hom-home-services-s3.8.4] Gas appliances need adequate ventilation for the air needed to burn the gas, so they must not be blocked by furniture or closed vents, and flues and vents must be kept clear.
[hom-home-services-s3.8.5] Do not use a cooker or oven to heat the room.
[hom-home-services-s3.8.6] Gas appliances should be installed by a registered installer (on the Register of Gas Installers of Ireland) and serviced at regular intervals so the flue and burner stay safe and efficient.
[hom-home-services-s3.9.b] Electrical services must match the use of the room, and the layout must keep sockets and switches safe and convenient.
[hom-home-services-s3.9.0] Lighting: plan layers of light, with ambient lighting from ceiling fittings, task lighting over work areas and accent lighting for display.
[hom-home-services-s3.9.1] Switches should be placed at the door, within easy reach, and sockets at a safe height and away from water sources.
[hom-home-services-s3.9.2] Heating options: electric storage heaters charged on night rate, panel heaters on a thermostat, or electric underfloor heating, each controlled by a thermostat or timer.
[hom-home-services-s3.9.3] Ventilation: a fan with a timer or humidity switch, fitted in a kitchen or bathroom, removes moisture and cooking smells from the room.
[hom-home-services-s3.9.4] Circuits and sockets: enough double sockets on the ring or a separate circuit so no extension lead is needed for regular appliances.
[hom-home-services-s3.9.5] Safety: the fittings for a bathroom must suit its zone rating, and any new work must be done by a registered electrician and certified.

CANDIDATES hom-home-services-s2: 2. Emissions, energy inefficiency and energy efficiency
[hom-home-services-s2.0.b] Heat is the largest energy use in a home, so wasted heat costs money and adds to emissions. Heat moves from warm to cool by conduction, convection and radiation, and in a house most is lost through the fabric (roof, walls, windows, doors, floors) and through air leakage.
[hom-home-services-s2.0.0] Conduction: heat passes through solid walls, roof, floor and glazing from the warm inside to the cold outside, so any uninsulated surface is a route for loss.
[hom-home-services-s2.0.1] Convection: warm air rises and escapes through gaps, while cold air enters at lower levels, creating draughts.
[hom-home-services-s2.0.2] Air leakage: gaps around doors, windows, floorboards, letterboxes and chimneys let warm air out and draw cold air in.
[hom-home-services-s2.0.3] Heat rises, so an uninsulated roof or attic is one of the largest routes for heat loss in a typical house.
[hom-home-services-s2.0.4] Single glazing loses heat much faster than an insulated wall; double glazing traps a layer of still air between two panes, which slows heat transfer.
[hom-home-services-s2.0.5] Thermal bridges are parts of the structure, such as lintels and junctions between walls and floors, where heat escapes faster than through the surrounding fabric.
[hom-home-services-s2.0.6] Uninsulated hot water cylinders and pipes lose heat continuously to the surrounding air, even when no water is drawn off.
[hom-home-services-s2.0.7] Heating that runs with no timer, room thermostat or radiator valves heats rooms that are not in use, so energy is paid for but wasted.
[hom-home-services-s2.1.b] Beyond the building fabric, energy is wasted by how heating, hot water, electrical appliances and cooking are used. Each habit below is a fault you would record in a home survey.
[hom-home-services-s2.1.0] Heating: an old or unserviced boiler, no timer, room thermostat or thermostatic radiator valves, rooms heated when not in use, a thermostat set too high, an open fire losing heat up the chimney, and radiators blocked by furniture or curtains.
[hom-home-services-s2.1.1] Hot water: an uninsulated cylinder and pipes, an immersion (electric heating element in the cylinder) left on, long hot showers or baths, water heated at the wrong time, and dripping hot taps.
[hom-home-services-s2.1.2] Electricity and appliances: old filament bulbs, lights left on, appliances left on standby, half loads in washing machines and dishwashers, old or low-rated appliances, a tumble dryer used instead of line-drying, and a fridge door left open.
[hom-home-services-s2.1.3] Cooking: using more water or heat than needed, no lids on pans, a large pan on a small ring (or the reverse), opening the oven door, preheating too long, and cooking one dish in a large oven.
[hom-home-services-s2.1.4] Cooking: using an oven where a microwave, toaster or slow cooker would do, and boiling too much water in the kettle.
[hom-home-services-s2.2.b] Burning a carbon fuel (gas, oil, coal, turf or wood) in boilers, stoves and fires gives off gases and particles. What comes out depends on how completely the fuel burns.
[hom-home-services-s2.2.0] Complete combustion happens with plenty of air and a well-adjusted burner, and gives carbon dioxide and water vapour. For methane: CH₄ + 2O₂ → CO₂ + 2H₂O.
[hom-home-services-s2.2.1] Incomplete combustion, caused by too little air, a blocked flue or a dirty or faulty burner, also produces carbon monoxide (CO) and soot or smoke.
[hom-home-services-s2.2.2] Carbon monoxide is a colourless, odourless and poisonous gas that binds to haemoglobin (the oxygen-carrying protein in red blood cells), so less oxygen is carried around the body. Effects include headache, dizziness, nausea and death.
[hom-home-services-s2.2.3] Carbon monoxide is prevented by servicing appliances, ensuring ventilation, keeping flues clear and fitting a CO alarm, because you cannot smell or see the gas.
[hom-home-services-s2.2.4] Water vapour is also given off when fuels burn, and it can cause condensation in the home.
[hom-home-services-s2.2.5] Diagram: a flow chart from fuel burned in the boiler or stove to the pollutants released (CO₂, CO, SO₂, nitrogen oxides, particulates) and their environmental effects.
[hom-home-services-s2.3.b] Each gas given off by burning fuels does its own kind of damage. Match each gas to its effect and do not swap them.
[hom-home-services-s2.3.0] Carbon dioxide is a greenhouse gas that builds up in the atmosphere and traps heat, contributing to global warming and climate change: rising temperatures and sea levels, extreme weather, and changes to crops and species.
[hom-home-services-s2.3.1] Sulphur dioxide (SO₂) comes from the sulphur in coal, oil and turf. It dissolves in rainwater to form acid rain, which damages trees and crops, acidifies lakes and kills fish, corrodes metal and wears limestone buildings and statues. It also irritates the lungs.
[hom-home-services-s2.3.2] Nitrogen oxides form when the air is heated in a burner or engine at high temperatures. They contribute to acid rain and smog and irritate the lungs.
[hom-home-services-s2.3.3] Smoke and particles come from coal, turf, wood and badly adjusted oil burners. They cause smog, dirty buildings and breathing problems such as asthma and bronchitis; smokeless fuel and efficient stoves reduce them.
[hom-home-services-s2.3.4] Per unit of heat, coal and turf give the most carbon dioxide and smoke, oil gives less, and natural gas gives the least. Electricity gives no emissions in the house but may do at the power station.
[hom-home-services-s2.3.5] Wood is a renewable fuel, but burning it still releases carbon dioxide and, if the stove is poor, smoke.
[hom-home-services-s2.4.b] Aim: to identify where energy is wasted in a home and to recommend changes that improve efficiency.
[hom-home-services-s2.4.0] Step 1: walk through the house with a checklist and note draughts, uninsulated attic space, single glazing, missing draught seals and any unlagged cylinder or pipes.
[hom-home-services-s2.4.1] Step 2: check for draughts by holding the back of the hand or a smoke pencil near gaps, noting where air movement shows leakage.
[hom-home-services-s2.4.2] Step 3: record the heating controls: whether there is a timer, a room thermostat and radiator valves, and the thermostat setting in each room.
[hom-home-services-s2.4.3] Step 4: record the room temperatures at the same time on two occasions, and the hours the heating runs each day.
[hom-home-services-s2.4.4] Step 5: identify how hot water is heated (boiler, immersion or electric shower), how long the immersion runs, and whether the cylinder is insulated.
[hom-home-services-s2.4.5] Step 6: observe cooking practices, such as opening the oven door often, preheating for longer than needed, using large pans on small rings, leaving pans uncovered and boiling more water than needed in the kettle.
[hom-home-services-s2.4.6] Step 7: compare meter readings or fuel deliveries over a set period, and note appliances left on standby.
[hom-home-services-s2.4.7] Safety: keep naked flames away from gas and oil appliances and do not use a lit taper to check for leaks from fuel pipes.
[hom-home-services-s2.5.b] The cheapest way to cut energy use is to stop heat leaving the house. These measures target the fabric and air leaks found in a survey.
[hom-home-services-s2.5.0] Insulation: quilt laid between the joists in the attic, cavity wall insulation, internal or external wall insulation, and floor insulation.
[hom-home-services-s2.5.1] Glazing: double or triple glazing, with lined curtains closed at night, and low-emissivity coatings on glass.
[hom-home-services-s2.5.2] Draught-proofing of doors, windows, letterboxes and floorboards with seals and brushes, which stops warm air escaping and cold air entering.
[hom-home-services-s2.5.3] Ventilation balanced with insulation: an airtight fabric needs a controlled ventilation system so that moisture and stale air are removed without wasting heat.
[hom-home-services-s2.5.4] Improvements are ranked by cost and effect: draught-proofing and attic insulation are cheap and effective, while a heat pump or external wall insulation costs much more. SEAI grants help with costs.
[hom-home-services-s2.6.b] Heating and hot water are the biggest energy users, so controlling when and where heat is made saves the most. Cleaner heating also cuts emissions.
[hom-home-services-s2.6.0] Heating controls: a programmer or timer, a room thermostat, thermostatic radiator valves on each radiator, and a cylinder thermostat with a time switch for hot water, so heat is only produced when and where it is needed. Zoning (heating separate areas independently) and closing doors also help.
[hom-home-services-s2.6.1] Efficient boilers: a condensing boiler recovers heat from its flue gases, and an annual service keeps the burner adjusted so combustion stays complete.
[hom-home-services-s2.6.2] Heating systems: a stove in place of an open fire, and background heating where it suits.
[hom-home-services-s2.6.3] Hot water: lagging the cylinder and pipes with jackets, using the immersion only when required on a timer, fixing dripping taps, and setting the cylinder thermostat correctly.
[hom-home-services-s2.6.4] Choosing a shower over a bath saves water only if the shower is short, because a long or power shower can use as much as a bath.
[hom-home-services-s2.6.5] Renewable heat: solar thermal collectors, a heat pump or a well-managed wood-burning stove with a flue and an air supply controlled for complete combustion.
[hom-home-services-s2.7.b] Everyday electricity and cooking habits waste energy too, and changing them costs little. Each fix answers a fault from the home survey.
[hom-home-services-s2.7.0] Lighting and appliances: LED lamps, switching off unused lights, sensors and timers, and appliances with the highest rating on the energy label.
[hom-home-services-s2.7.1] Laundry and standby: full loads on washing machines and dishwashers, eco programmes, switching off standby, and line-drying clothes instead of using a tumble dryer.
[hom-home-services-s2.7.2] Cooking: using lids, matching pan size to the ring, boiling only the water needed, and filling the oven when it is on.
[hom-home-services-s2.7.3] Cooking: using the microwave, a pressure cooker or a slow cooker for small or long cooking, avoiding opening the oven door, and cooking several items together.
[hom-home-services-s2.8.b] Emissions fall when less fuel is burned and when what is burned burns cleanly. These steps answer the "way to cut it" part of an emissions question.
[hom-home-services-s2.8.0] Burn less fuel overall, through insulation, controls and efficient appliances, because less fuel burned means fewer pollutants.
[hom-home-services-s2.8.1] Change from coal or oil to gas or smokeless fuel, and choose a renewable electricity supplier, because these produce less carbon dioxide, sulphur dioxide and smoke.
[hom-home-services-s2.8.2] Service boilers so combustion stays complete, keep flues clear, and fit a CO alarm, which warns of carbon monoxide that you cannot smell.
[hom-home-services-s2.8.3] Do not burn rubbish, and use smokeless fuel and efficient stoves to reduce smoke and particles.
[hom-home-services-s2.8.4] Renewable options such as solar thermal and PV (photovoltaic panels that make electricity), and heat pumps, burn no fuel in the home.
[hom-home-services-s2.9.b] It is far cheaper to build efficiency in than to add it later. A new house can be designed to lose little heat and to gain free heat from the sun.
[hom-home-services-s2.9.0] A compact shape with few corners has less outside wall area through which heat escapes.
[hom-home-services-s2.9.1] South-facing living rooms with large windows gain free heat from the sun (passive solar gain), while small north windows lose little heat.
[hom-home-services-s2.9.2] High levels of insulation in the attic, walls and floor, with double or triple glazing, and airtight construction with controlled ventilation.
[hom-home-services-s2.9.3] A porch or lobby cuts draughts when the door is opened, and orientation and shelter from wind cut heat loss.
[hom-home-services-s2.9.4] Zoned heating with controls, and an efficient or renewable heating system.
[hom-home-services-s2.9.5] Rainwater use, and LED and sensor lighting.
[hom-home-services-s2.10.b] A BER is a certificate that rates a dwelling's energy performance, like the energy label on appliances. It tells owners and buyers how efficient a home is.
[hom-home-services-s2.10.0] The BER assessment is carried out by an assessor registered with SEAI, and SEAI runs the system.
[hom-home-services-s2.10.1] It rates a dwelling on a scale from A (most efficient) to G (least efficient). From 24 May 2026 the scale is A0 and A to G, and the sub-bands such as B1 to B3 were removed.
[hom-home-services-s2.10.2] A valid BER is required when a home is sold or rented, it must appear in adverts, and it has been required since 2009. BERs issued before 24 May 2026 stay valid for sale or rent for ten years from issue.
[hom-home-services-s2.10.3] It shows the energy efficiency of a house, including windows, walls, insulation and heating, and where improvements would have most effect.
[hom-home-services-s2.10.4] The EU energy label for appliances and bulbs was rescaled to A to G (appliances in March 2021, lamps in September 2021), so few products now reach A.

CANDIDATES hom-home-interior-s1: 1. Elements and principles of design and factors in interior design
[hom-home-interior-s1.0.b] The elements of design are the visual building blocks of a room, the things you can actually see and touch when you walk in. The marking schemes list colour, line, shape, pattern and texture, and form (the 3-D version of shape) may be added.
[hom-home-interior-s1.0.0] You should be able to name three elements and explain two of them, each with an example of how it is used in a room.
[hom-home-interior-s1.0.1] Each element does a job: line leads the eye, shape and form give a room its feel, colour sets mood, pattern adds interest and texture adds warmth or a clean look.
[hom-home-interior-s1.1.b] Line and shape are the structure of a room. They decide whether it feels tall or wide, soft or crisp, before any colour is added.
[hom-home-interior-s1.1.0] Line is the path the eye follows; horizontal lines make a room feel wider and calmer, while vertical lines make it feel taller.
[hom-home-interior-s1.1.1] Lines come from skirting boards, picture rails, curtain edges, cabinet edges and the joints between tiles or floorboards, so a room always contains them.
[hom-home-interior-s1.1.2] Shape is the two-dimensional (flat) outline of an object or area; form is its three-dimensional volume, such as the bulk of a sofa.
[hom-home-interior-s1.1.3] Rounded shapes look soft and informal, while angular shapes look crisp and formal.
[hom-home-interior-s1.2.b] Colour is the element with the biggest effect on how a room feels. To use it well you need the colour wheel vocabulary and to know how warm and cool colours behave.
[hom-home-interior-s1.2.0] Primary colours are red, yellow and blue; they cannot be made by mixing other colours.
[hom-home-interior-s1.2.1] Secondary colours are orange, green and violet, each made by mixing two primaries.
[hom-home-interior-s1.2.2] Tertiary colours are made by mixing a primary with a neighbouring secondary colour.
[hom-home-interior-s1.2.3] Warm colours (red, orange and yellow) advance visually, which means they seem to come towards you, so a room feels cosy and smaller. Cool colours (blue, green and violet) recede, so a room feels spacious and calm.
[hom-home-interior-s1.2.4] Pastels are pale, soft colours that feel restful, and neutrals are colours such as white and cream that go with almost anything.
[hom-home-interior-s1.2.5] Pale colours reflect more light and make a room appear larger; dark colours absorb light and make a room appear smaller and more intimate.
[hom-home-interior-s1.2.6] Pale ceilings look higher than dark ones.
[hom-home-interior-s1.3.b] Changing how light or dark a colour is gives a range from one hue (a pure colour such as blue). Choosing which colours sit together then gives a colour scheme.
[hom-home-interior-s1.3.0] A tint is a colour with white added, a tone has grey added, and a shade has black added.
[hom-home-interior-s1.3.1] A monochromatic scheme uses tints, tones and shades of a single colour.
[hom-home-interior-s1.3.2] A complementary (contrasting) scheme pairs colours opposite each other on the colour wheel, such as blue and orange, for contrast.
[hom-home-interior-s1.3.3] A harmonious (analogous) scheme uses colours that sit next to each other on the wheel, such as green, blue and violet, for a restful effect.
[hom-home-interior-s1.3.4] An accent colour is a strong colour used in small amounts, such as cushions or a single feature wall, to draw the eye.
[hom-home-interior-s1.4.b] Colour can do four jobs in a room, and the marking schemes list all four. Which colours you choose also depends on the way the room faces.
[hom-home-interior-s1.4.0] Colour can link rooms, so that a repeated colour makes one space flow into the next.
[hom-home-interior-s1.4.1] Colour can alter the apparent proportions of a room, for example pale colours to make it look larger and dark colours to make it feel smaller and cosier.
[hom-home-interior-s1.4.2] Colour can highlight a room's good features, or disguise poor ones.
[hom-home-interior-s1.4.3] Colour can create atmosphere, such as pastels for a restful bedroom or warm colours for a cosy sitting room.
[hom-home-interior-s1.4.4] Aspect (the direction a room faces) matters: a north-facing room receives little daylight, so warm colours suit it, while cool colours suit a south-facing room.
[hom-home-interior-s1.5.b] Pattern adds variety and interest, but the amount and scale you use decide whether a room feels lively or fussy.
[hom-home-interior-s1.5.0] Pattern is a repeated or decorative design, such as stripes, checks, florals or geometric motifs.
[hom-home-interior-s1.5.1] Large-scale or bold patterns dominate a room and make it feel busy and can shrink it; small-scale patterns recede and create a calmer effect.
[hom-home-interior-s1.5.2] Several patterns can work together if they are linked by a shared colour, texture or motif, which ties the room together.
[hom-home-interior-s1.5.3] Too much pattern makes a room fussy, while too little makes it bland.
[hom-home-interior-s1.6.b] Texture is how a surface feels, or looks as if it feels. It affects both the comfort of a room and how easy it is to keep clean.
[hom-home-interior-s1.6.0] Texture is the surface quality of a material, perceived by touch and by the way light falls on it.
[hom-home-interior-s1.6.1] Smooth textures such as glass, mirror, glazed tile, paint and polished steel look cool and are easy to keep hygienic, so they suit kitchens and bathrooms.
[hom-home-interior-s1.6.2] Rough or soft textures such as carpet, upholstery, brick and textured wallpaper or paint add warmth and comfort to a sitting room or bedroom.
[hom-home-interior-s1.6.3] Matt surfaces absorb light and hide surface flaws, while gloss surfaces reflect light and show marks.
[hom-home-interior-s1.6.4] Contrasting textures, such as smooth glass beside rough wool, add interest without adding colour.
[hom-home-interior-s1.7.b] The principles of design are the rules for putting the elements together. The four the marking schemes use are proportion, balance, emphasis and rhythm.
[hom-home-interior-s1.7.0] Proportion is the size relationship between the parts of a room, such as furniture, windows, doors and wall areas, and between those parts and the room itself.
[hom-home-interior-s1.7.1] Scale is the size of one object relative to the room around it; oversized furniture in a small room makes the room look crowded.
[hom-home-interior-s1.7.2] Proportion guides furniture choice: a large sofa suits a large room, while a small room suits low, slim pieces.
[hom-home-interior-s1.8.b] Balance is about sharing visual weight around a room so that it feels settled rather than lopsided.
[hom-home-interior-s1.8.0] Balance creates harmony, so that no single feature dominates and colour, pattern and texture are shared out evenly.
[hom-home-interior-s1.8.1] Formal balance is symmetrical, with matching items placed either side of a central point, such as a pair of lamps beside a fireplace.
[hom-home-interior-s1.8.2] Informal balance places items of different shapes but similar visual weight on either side of a room.
[hom-home-interior-s1.9.b] Emphasis gives the eye somewhere to go first. That place is called the focal point, and it stops a room looking like a jumble of equal features.
[hom-home-interior-s1.9.0] Emphasis creates a focal point: the feature the eye goes to first, such as a fireplace, a window view, a feature wall or a large painting.
[hom-home-interior-s1.9.1] A focal point can be created with colour, lighting, a strong pattern or the position of furniture.
[hom-home-interior-s1.9.2] A room works best with one focal point; two or more features competing for attention make the room feel disjointed.
[hom-home-interior-s1.10.b] Rhythm is what makes the eye travel smoothly round a room instead of stopping and starting.
[hom-home-interior-s1.10.0] Rhythm is created by repeating a colour, shape, style, line, pattern or texture so that the eye moves easily around the room.
[hom-home-interior-s1.10.1] Repeating a cushion colour on the sofa and in a rug, for example, links the two areas and carries the eye across the room.
[hom-home-interior-s1.11.b] Evaluate a room by naming each element used, then checking whether each principle is achieved and where it fails. Designing one follows a sensible order, starting from what the room is for.
[hom-home-interior-s1.11.0] Start from the room's function, such as a living room for relaxing and watching television, and choose a colour scheme that suits that function.
[hom-home-interior-s1.11.1] Place the focal point first, then arrange seating and storage so that the focal point is visible from the main entrance.
[hom-home-interior-s1.11.2] Use proportion to choose furniture: a large sofa suits a large room, while a small room suits low, slim pieces.
[hom-home-interior-s1.11.3] Then arrange furniture and traffic routes, and check each principle in turn.

[hom-home-interior-s1.14.b] Ergonomics is about the fit between people and the layout of a room, so that they can move, reach and work with least effort and strain. It is not the same as how the room looks.
[hom-home-interior-s1.14.0] Ergonomics is the design of a room so that its layout suits how people move, reach and work.
