QUESTION homeeconomics-pp-2026-HI-sectionc-housing-Q1 (LC Home Economics Higher 2026 Paper BC — Q1; filed under hom-housing)
PART (a)(i) [20 marks]
  Q: Discuss the adequacy of housing provision in Ireland.
  A: Irish housing is provided through owner occupation, private renting and social housing supplied by local authorities, approved housing bodies, voluntary organisations and co-operatives. The stock includes detached and semi-detached houses, terraces, apartments, rural one-off houses, supported accommodation and homes adapted for people with disabilities or older people. This variety can meet different household sizes, locations and stages of the family life cycle. Building regulations and BER requirements have also improved safety and energy efficiency in newer housing.

However, adequacy means more than the total number of dwellings. Housing must be affordable, secure, suitable, accessible and located near employment, transport, schools, healthcare and shops. Provision is uneven: strong demand in cities and commuter areas can produce high purchase prices and rents, overcrowding and long social-housing waiting lists, while some rural areas have vacant housing but fewer jobs and services. Low-income families, lone parents, homeless people, older people and people with disabilities may not obtain housing suited to their needs. Emergency accommodation does not provide the stability of a permanent home. Older stock may be damp, poorly insulated or costly to heat, creating fuel poverty.

Local-authority building, cost-rental and voluntary/co-operative schemes improve supply, and grants or adaptation supports help particular groups. Renovating vacant property and planning mixed, serviced developments can also help. Overall, Ireland has a broad range of provision, but it is not fully adequate where cost, regional distribution, homelessness and access to appropriate social and special-needs housing remain barriers.
PART (a)(ii) [15 marks]
  Q: Analyse the importance of local amenities and services in housing developments.
  A: Amenities and services determine whether a housing development supports daily family life rather than merely supplying dwellings. Schools, childcare and playgrounds serve children and reduce long journeys. Shops, post offices, banks and community centres provide necessities and social contact. Accessible healthcare, pharmacies and services for older people or those with disabilities support independence. Parks, sports grounds and safe walking/cycling routes promote exercise, leisure and community interaction.

Reliable water, sewage, drainage, waste collection, electricity, broadband and emergency services are essential to hygiene, work and safety. Frequent public transport and road links give access to employment and education, reduce isolation and can lower traffic congestion and pollution. Street lighting, paths, crossings and open-space supervision improve personal safety. If these facilities are absent, households become car-dependent, costs rise, commuting time reduces family time, and schools or medical services may become overcrowded. Property may be less attractive and residents can feel isolated. Amenities should therefore be planned at the same time as housing, be accessible on foot and include the needs of children, teenagers, older residents and disabled people. A mixed, well-serviced development is more sustainable and develops a stronger sense of community.
PART (a)(iii) [15 marks]
  Q: Outline the historical development of housing styles in Ireland.
  A: In nineteenth-century rural Ireland, many families lived in simple single-storey thatched cottages with stone or mud walls, small windows, a kitchen and bedroom, half-door and open fire. Prosperous farmers had larger two-storey slated houses, while wealthy landowners occupied estate houses. In towns, Georgian terraces had two to four storeys, basements, high ceilings and prominent doorways; workers occupied small cottages or overcrowded tenements, and shopkeepers often lived above shops.

During the twentieth century, rural electrification and rising standards modernised homes, while slate or tiles replaced much thatch. Local-authority estates, private estates, detached and semi-detached houses, bungalows and some high-rise blocks expanded in urban areas. Building regulations, indoor sanitation and new materials such as concrete, PVC, fibreglass and double glazing improved comfort.

Twenty-first-century housing is more varied: apartments, townhouses, gated or mixed-density schemes and suburban developments coexist with architect-designed rural houses. Period homes are renovated; attics, extensions, sunrooms and home offices adapt existing stock. Modern design gives greater attention to open-plan living, accessibility, security, insulation, low-emissivity glazing, renewable energy and compact sustainable construction. Housing styles therefore reflect changing materials, technology, regulation, family size, land availability and environmental awareness.
PART (b)(i) [15 marks]
  Q: Analyse how the following factors influence house design and construction:
• family requirements
• technological developments
• aesthetic and environmental factors.
  A: Family requirements determine size and layout. The design should allow sufficient bedrooms, storage and shared space for present and future household members. Young children need safe supervision and outdoor play; teenagers need privacy and study space; disability or ageing may require step-free access, wide doors, an adapted bathroom and a ground-floor bedroom. Dual employment or home working may require an office, while an open-plan area can support communication but needs acoustic and functional zoning.

Technological developments influence both planning and use. CAD and virtual models help test designs; smart security, sensor lighting, intelligent appliances, broadband, zoned heating, thermostats and remote controls increase convenience and efficiency. Technology must be safe, affordable to maintain and capable of upgrading.

Aesthetic factors include proportion, balance, colour, materials, roof line and harmony with neighbouring buildings. Aspect and windows should maximise useful daylight and attractive views. Environmental factors include site gradient and drainage, local planning rules, ecological impact, durable local materials, insulation, low-E glazing and renewable energy. A successful design balances appearance with family function, construction budget, energy use and the visual character of its setting rather than treating any one factor in isolation.
PART (b)(ii) [15 marks]
  Q: Give details of the professional services available to assist in designing and building a home.
  A: An architect translates the client’s brief and budget into drawings, advises on site, layout, materials and planning permission, coordinates consultants and may inspect construction. A structural or site engineer assesses ground and structural requirements, designs foundations or load-bearing work and advises on technical problems. A surveyor examines the site or existing building, checks boundaries and defects, estimates work and suggests solutions; a quantity surveyor controls construction costs.

A solicitor checks title and planning matters, handles purchase contracts and registration, protects the client’s legal interests and may lodge deeds with a lender. The builder prepares the site, organises trades, follows drawings, agrees cost and completion terms and corrects items on the snag list. Specialist services include an electrician and plumber for safe certified installations, an interior designer for functional finishes and furniture, and a landscape architect for external space and drainage. A BER assessor evaluates energy performance and recommends improvements. Planning officers and building-control professionals check compliance with development plans and building regulations. Clear contracts, professional qualifications, insurance and communication among these services reduce defects, unexpected costs and delay.
PART (c)(i) [18 marks]
  Q: Discuss one renewable energy source you have studied.
Refer to:
• name
• advantages
• disadvantages.
  A: Solar energy is renewable energy obtained from sunlight. Solar thermal collectors absorb heat and transfer it to fluid that heats domestic water. Photovoltaic panels use semiconductor cells to convert light directly to electricity; a battery may store surplus. Passive solar design positions the house and large areas of glazing towards the south or south-west so that winter sunlight contributes heat and daylight.

Advantages are that sunlight is renewable and produces no smoke or carbon dioxide at the point of use. A suitable system reduces purchased electricity or fuel, lowers running costs after installation and can improve a home’s BER. Solar thermal energy provides much domestic hot water, while photovoltaic electricity can power household appliances. Systems have few moving parts and relatively low routine maintenance.

Disadvantages include high initial purchase and installation cost. Output varies with daylight, weather, season, roof aspect, angle and shading, so a backup supply or storage is required. Batteries add cost and eventually require replacement; panels occupy roof space and may be considered visually intrusive. Manufacture and disposal also use materials and energy. On balance, a correctly sized, well-oriented solar system is a sustainable long-term contribution, especially when the house first reduces demand through insulation and efficient appliances; it cannot alone guarantee continuous supply.
PART (c)(ii) [12 marks]
  Q: Describe how levels of thermal comfort in the home can be controlled.
  A: Thermal comfort depends on air temperature, the temperature of surrounding surfaces, humidity, air movement, clothing and activity. A suitable, efficient heating system should be zoned so occupied rooms are heated to the required level. Timers prevent heating empty rooms, thermostats maintain the selected temperature and thermostatic radiator valves control individual spaces. Insulation in roof, walls, floors and hot-water pipes, together with low-emissivity double/triple glazing, lined curtains and draught proofing, reduces heat loss and cold surfaces. Controlled ventilation removes moist, stale air and prevents condensation without excessive draughts; extractor fans are useful in kitchens and bathrooms. Sensible humidity, sunshine through appropriate glazing, furniture placement away from radiators and suitable clothing also improve comfort. Regular maintenance ensures heaters and controls work safely and efficiently.

PART (b) [15 marks]
  Q: Write a profile of one natural fabric.
Refer to:
• fibre production
• fibre identification test
• fabric properties.
  A: Cotton is a natural cellulosic fibre obtained from the seed bolls of the cotton plant. When the mature bolls open, the white staple fibres are picked by hand or machine. Ginning separates fibre from seed; the cotton is cleaned, graded by length, colour and fineness, compressed into bales, carded or combed, drawn and spun into yarn. The yarn may then be woven or knitted into fabric.

In a burning identification test, a small fibre sample is held safely with tongs near a flame. Cotton ignites readily, continues to burn after removal from the flame, smells like burning paper and leaves a soft, light-grey, feathery ash rather than a hard bead.

Cotton is strong, and stronger when wet, absorbent, cool, breathable, comfortable and easy to wash. It dyes well and tolerates relatively high ironing temperatures. Its disadvantages are that it creases easily, has limited elasticity, may shrink unless treated, dries more slowly than many synthetics and can mildew when stored damp. These properties make cotton suitable for shirts, underwear, sports tops, dresses, towels, sheets and curtains.
PART (c)(i) [6 marks]
  Q: Describe, giving one example, how function influences our clothing choices.
  A: Function determines the protection, comfort, movement, fabric and design features required from clothing. For example, a waterproof outdoor jacket needs a water-repellent closely constructed fabric, sealed or covered openings, a hood, secure pockets and adjustable cuffs. It should be light enough for movement but provide wind protection. These functional requirements are more important than decoration, although colour and style can still make the garment attractive.
PART (c)(ii) [9 marks]
  Q: Analyse how social influences impact the design of clothing.
  A: Designers respond to the values and behaviour of society. Celebrities, musicians, athletes, bloggers and social-media influencers expose large audiences to silhouettes, colours and brands, so trends spread quickly and firms may design ranges around their endorsements. Peer groups influence teenagers’ desire for acceptance and brand identity. Travel and migration introduce motifs, embroidery, modest dress and other cultural features, while historical crafts such as Aran knitting, Irish linen, lace and crochet are reinterpreted in modern garments. Work patterns and more active lifestyles increase demand for smart-casual clothing and athleisure. Environmental concern encourages recycled fibres, durable construction, repair, vintage and upcycled designs. Social change therefore affects both how clothing looks and the ethical, practical requirements it is expected to meet.

QUESTION homeeconomics-pp-2026-HI-sectionc-personalfinance-Q3 (LC Home Economics Higher 2026 Paper BC — Q3; filed under hom-unemployment-poverty)
PART (a)(i) [20 marks]
  Q: Analyse the extent and distribution of unemployment across Ireland.
  A: Unemployment exists when people of working age are without paid work, available for work and actively seeking it. Its extent changes with the economic cycle. A recession reduces consumer demand, output and recruitment, producing cyclical unemployment; recovery normally reverses some of this. Structural and technological unemployment persist when automation, globalisation or changing industry makes existing skills or occupations obsolete. Frictional unemployment occurs while people move between jobs, and seasonal unemployment affects tourism, agriculture and construction at particular times.

Distribution is uneven. Urban areas offer larger and more varied labour markets, public transport, education and multinational employers, but may still contain unemployment blackspots where poverty, low qualifications and industrial decline are concentrated. Rural and remote areas have fewer large employers and training services; agricultural mechanisation and closure of a major local business can have a disproportionate effect. Seasonal tourism may produce insecure employment. People may commute or migrate, and their departure further reduces demand for local shops and services.

Young people and early school leavers are vulnerable because they lack qualifications and experience. Workers whose skills no longer match technology, people with disabilities, carers, lone parents without affordable childcare and some older workers may face additional barriers. Unemployment is therefore not distributed simply by county: education, skills, transport, health, family responsibilities and local industrial structure matter. National figures can hide intense local and household disadvantage, so training, childcare, transport and regionally balanced job creation must accompany general economic growth.
PART (a)(ii) [20 marks]
  Q: Discuss the effects of unemployment on individuals and society.
  A: For an individual, unemployment removes earnings and lowers living standards. Rent or mortgage, energy, food and debt become harder to meet, and participation in leisure and normal social activities may fall. Loss of occupational role can reduce status, identity, self-esteem and purpose. Repeated rejection may cause stress, anxiety, depression, boredom and isolation; health and family relationships can deteriorate, and some people turn to alcohol, drugs or antisocial behaviour. Skills and confidence decline during long unemployment, making re-entry harder. Young adults may delay leaving home, further education, relationships or starting a family, while emigration separates them from support networks.

Families experience financial pressure, conflict and reduced opportunities for children. Difficulty providing books, technology, nutrition or quiet study can affect educational achievement and continue a cycle of disadvantage. At community level, spending falls, so shops and services close and further jobs disappear. Areas can lose population, investment and reputation; crime, vandalism, homelessness and inequality may rise.

Society also loses productive skill and tax/PRSI revenue while State expenditure on Jobseeker payments, housing and health support increases. A black economy may develop, undermining employment rights and revenue. However, welfare and active labour-market measures are essential protection, not a cause to blame individuals. Effective responses combine income support with education, retraining, work experience, childcare, transport and enterprise development so that people can obtain sustainable employment rather than remain excluded.
PART (a)(iii) [10 marks]
  Q: Name and discuss one statutory response to creating employment.
  A: SOLAS, the Further Education and Training Authority, is a statutory response that improves employment options by funding, planning and coordinating further education and training with Education and Training Boards. It supports apprenticeships, vocational courses, literacy and numeracy, digital upskilling, CV and job-search preparation and community-based training. These programmes help unemployed people update skills that no longer match industry, gain recognised qualifications and move into sectors where vacancies exist. Work-based learning also gives experience sought by employers. By linking training provision to labour-market needs, SOLAS tackles structural unemployment rather than only paying income support, although success still depends on accessible local courses, childcare, transport and real job opportunities.
PART (b)(i) [15 marks]
  Q: Explain how an individual can benefit personally by participating in voluntary work.
  A: Voluntary work gives an individual purposeful use of time and the satisfaction of contributing to other people’s welfare. Achieving a shared goal builds self-esteem, confidence and a sense of belonging, while contact with clients and other volunteers reduces loneliness and broadens friendships. It develops empathy, patience, tolerance and awareness of social problems.

The volunteer also acquires transferable skills: communication, teamwork, leadership, planning, budgeting, problem-solving, punctuality and responsibility. A role in a charity shop can develop retail and cash-handling experience; coaching develops organisation and leadership; visiting older people develops listening and interpersonal skill. Training in first aid, safeguarding or administration may be provided. These experiences can be included on a CV, supply a reference, clarify career interests and improve employability, particularly for a young or unemployed person. Volunteering can therefore support physical, social, emotional and intellectual development. The individual must still choose a suitable, well-managed organisation and balance the commitment with study, work and family responsibilities so that the role remains reliable and beneficial.
PART (b)(ii) [15 marks]
  Q: Discuss the benefits of voluntary work to the community.
  A: Volunteers extend the range and speed of services available to a community. They may deliver meals, visit isolated older people, coach children, staff charity shops, fundraise, provide homework clubs, support homeless people or improve local amenities. This complements statutory provision and gives a personal local response, particularly where public funding or staffing is limited. Early support can stop a difficulty becoming a larger social problem.

Community projects bring people of different ages and backgrounds together, building trust, friendship, cooperation and civic responsibility. Tidy Towns work, sports clubs and environmental projects improve safety, appearance and recreation; support organisations reduce poverty, isolation and disadvantage. Volunteers contribute time and skills without a full wage cost, allowing scarce funds to reach clients, although appropriate training, insurance and safeguarding remain essential.

Voluntary organisations can identify unmet needs, collect local evidence and act as pressure groups. Their campaigns may influence public opinion, policy and social reform. Fundraising circulates support towards housing, health or education projects, while successful activities make an area more attractive and cohesive. Thus voluntary work does not replace the State’s responsibilities, but it strengthens resilience, participation and the quality of life of the whole community.
PART (c)(i) [20 marks]
  Q: Analyse the role of education in child development.
Refer to:
• physical development
• emotional development
• intellectual development
• moral development.
  A: Education supports holistic development from the home and preschool through formal schooling. Physical development occurs through play, physical education and sport, which strengthen bones and muscles, fitness, balance and gross motor control. Art, writing, construction toys, practical subjects and laboratory work refine hand–eye coordination and fine motor skill; health education encourages safe, active habits.

Emotional development is supported by secure relationships, praise, achievable challenges and opportunities to express feelings. Children gain independence from parents, confidence, resilience and self-esteem. Working and playing with others teaches empathy, cooperation, conflict resolution and respect for different needs, while trusted adults can identify and support emotional difficulty.

Intellectual development comes through language, literacy, numeracy, science, creative subjects, questioning and problem-solving. Teachers and resources stimulate curiosity, memory, imagination and critical thinking. Assessment gives feedback, and differentiated teaching, learning support, SNAs and assistive technology help children reach their potential.

Moral development begins through family values and is extended by school rules, discussion and example. Children learn right from wrong, fairness, honesty, responsibility, respect for people and property and the consequences of actions. Religious, ethical and civic education can encourage informed conscience and active citizenship. Education also socialises the child into cultural norms while teaching tolerance rather than blind conformity. Its effect is greatest when home and school communicate, facilities are safe and inclusive, and opportunities are available regardless of income or additional need.
PART (c)(ii) [10 marks]
  Q: Name and give details of one statutory education initiative provided for pre-school children.
  A: The Early Childhood Care and Education (ECCE) programme is a State-funded preschool initiative. It provides eligible children with a free preschool place for a set programme before they begin primary school, delivered through participating early-years services. The State pays the provider, reducing the direct cost to parents and widening equality of access. Through supervised play, stories, art, music, movement and group routines, children develop language, creativity, fine and gross motor skills, independence, cooperation and confidence. Regular attendance also helps the transition to primary school. Participating services must meet relevant quality and staffing requirements, and inclusion supports can help children with additional needs take part. ECCE therefore combines childcare support for families with planned early education and socialisation.

QUESTION homeeconomics-pp-2026-HI-sectionc-cerealsgrains-Q4 (LC Home Economics Higher 2026 Paper BC — Q4; filed under hom-cereals-grains)
PART (a)(i) [16 marks]
  Q: Evaluate the contribution of wholegrain cereals to the diet.
  A: Wholegrain cereals retain the bran, endosperm and germ, so they contribute more nutrients than highly refined cereals. The starchy endosperm supplies carbohydrate for heat and energy and some low-biological-value protein. The bran is rich in dietary fibre, B-group vitamins and minerals; the germ contributes protein, unsaturated fat, vitamin E and B vitamins. Wholemeal bread, oats, brown rice and wholegrain breakfast cereals therefore add iron, phosphorus and other micronutrients as well as energy.

Fibre absorbs water, adds bulk to faeces and stimulates peristalsis, helping prevent constipation and bowel disorders. It promotes fullness and can assist weight control; soluble fibre in oats can help lower blood cholesterol and slow glucose absorption. Wholegrains are versatile, economical staple foods.

Their contribution must still be evaluated in the whole diet. Cereal protein is generally low in lysine, so combining it with pulses—for example beans on wholemeal toast—improves biological value. Wholegrains contain gluten and are unsuitable for coeliac disease unless the grain is naturally gluten-free and protected from contamination. Phytic acid and very high fibre intakes can reduce mineral absorption. Choose unsweetened, low-salt wholegrain products because a ‘wholegrain’ label does not cancel excessive sugar, fat or salt.
PART (a)(ii) [18 marks]
  Q: Give an informative account of the production of flour.
Refer to:
• stages of production
• packaging and labelling.
  A: At the mill, wheat is sampled, graded and blended to obtain the required flour quality. It is cleaned by sieves, air currents and magnets to remove dust, stones, chaff and metal. During conditioning, a controlled amount of water is added and the grain rests; this toughens the bran and softens the endosperm for separation.

In roller milling, corrugated break rollers open the kernels rather than crushing them completely. Plansifters separate the bran, germ and endosperm particles by size. The endosperm passes repeatedly through smoother reduction rollers and sieves until it becomes fine white flour. Wholemeal flour recombines or retains the appropriate bran, germ and endosperm; extraction rate therefore determines how much of the original grain remains. Flour may be blended for consistent gluten strength, treated as permitted and fortified where required, then tested for moisture and quality.

It is packed hygienically in strong, sealed, food-grade bags that protect it from moisture, insects, contamination and odours while allowing practical handling. The label states the type of flour, ingredient list, net quantity, nutrition information, allergen emphasis for wheat/gluten, manufacturer or packer, batch/date information and storage instructions. Cooking or recipe guidance and any fortification may also be shown. Correct labelling supports safe choice, traceability and comparison.
PART (a)(iii) [16 marks]
  Q: Discuss the measures taken by manufacturers of cereal products in order to cater for those with diet-related problems and specific dietary requirements.
  A: For people with coeliac disease or gluten intolerance, manufacturers produce cereal foods from rice, maize/corn and other gluten-free ingredients, use controlled production to prevent cross-contamination, test products and display a recognised gluten-free declaration. Clear emphasis of wheat, barley, rye and other allergens on ingredients lists protects consumers. Nut-free or milk-free recipes and prominent allergen information cater for additional allergies.

Wholegrain, high-fibre and bran-enriched breads, cereals and pasta support consumers at risk of constipation or seeking greater satiety. Lower-sugar or unsweetened breakfast cereals and reduced-salt breads assist weight control, diabetes meal planning and hypertension; nutrition panels and portion guidance help comparison. Manufacturers may reduce saturated fat, avoid hydrogenated fats and use more unsaturated oils. Fortification with iron, calcium, folic acid or B vitamins can address low micronutrient intake, while vegan products omit animal ingredients.

Convenient formats—individual portions, resealable packs and clear cooking instructions—help older people, small households and those with limited time. However, a specialist claim should not disguise an otherwise unbalanced product: gluten-free biscuits may still be high in sugar or fat. Reformulation, safe segregation, accurate labels and sensible portion sizes must operate together.
PART (b)(i) [18 marks]
  Q: Write a detailed account of one difference test used in the food industry.
Refer to:
• name of test
• aim
• implementation.
  A: The triangle test is a difference test used to discover whether a detectable sensory difference exists between two products, for example an established breakfast cereal and a version made with less salt. It does not ask which sample is preferred; its purpose is to determine whether panellists can distinguish them.

Each assessor receives three samples at the same time. Two are identical and one is different. The samples are presented in identical containers, in equal quantities and at the same temperature. Each is labelled with a random three-digit code so brand or treatment is concealed. The six possible serving orders are balanced or randomised among panellists to prevent position bias. In a quiet, odour-free, evenly lit test area, each person tastes from left to right, rinsing the mouth with water between samples, and identifies the odd sample. The assessor should not discuss answers with others.

A sufficient number of independent responses is collected. Correct identifications are totalled and compared with the number that could occur by chance, using the appropriate significance table. If enough assessors identify the odd sample correctly, the manufacturer concludes that the formulation change is perceptible; otherwise there is insufficient evidence of a detectable difference. Further preference or hedonic testing would then be needed to decide whether consumers like the changed product.
PART (b)(ii) [12 marks]
  Q: Outline the conditions that should be controlled for sensory analysis testing.
  A: Testing should take place in a clean, quiet, odour-free room with comfortable temperature and neutral, even lighting; coloured light may be used only when appearance must be masked. Individual booths prevent discussion and visual influence. Samples must be identical in amount, shape, serving container and temperature, prepared by the same method and served promptly. Random three-digit codes conceal identity, and serving order should be randomised or balanced. Assessors need clear, identical instructions and enough time, but should not smoke, chew gum, wear strong perfume or test while ill, hungry or immediately after strongly flavoured food. Water and, where appropriate, a plain cracker cleanse the palate between samples. Equipment must be hygienic, and one sensory characteristic should be judged at a time to reduce fatigue and bias.
PART (c)(i) [18 marks]
  Q: Discuss the challenges a family may experience when a member has special needs.
  A: A special need may require continuous supervision, personal care, therapy, medical appointments or help with communication, mobility and learning. Parents must locate appropriate health, education, respite and transport services and advocate for entitlements; waiting, travel and complex administration cause stress. The home may need ramps, wide doors, an adapted bathroom, specialist equipment or assistive technology.

Costs can rise while income falls if a parent reduces hours or leaves work to become a carer. Childcare may be difficult to obtain, family holidays and social activities may be restricted, and disturbed sleep produces fatigue. Parents can experience worry, grief, guilt or anxiety about their child’s future and who will provide care in adulthood. Disagreement about duties or discipline can strain a couple’s relationship.

Siblings may receive less attention, take on extra tasks or feel embarrassment, resentment or protectiveness; honest age-appropriate communication is important. The member with special needs may face exclusion, inaccessible facilities or overprotection, while the family may become socially isolated. At the same time, families often develop patience, cooperation and resilience. Fair distribution of tasks, respite, financial support, inclusive schooling, counselling, support groups and encouragement of the person’s independence help the family meet these challenges without allowing one member’s needs to erase everyone else’s.
PART (c)(ii) [12 marks]
  Q: Name and give details of one voluntary organisation that supports families when a member has special needs.
  A: Enable Ireland is a voluntary organisation supporting children and adults with disabilities and their families. Its services aim to maximise independence, participation and quality of life rather than defining a person only by disability. Depending on assessed need and local provision, multidisciplinary supports may include physiotherapy, occupational therapy, speech and language therapy, psychology, assistive technology, education or training and family support. It provides information and practical guidance so parents can understand services and advocate for their family member. Respite, community participation and support networks can reduce isolation and pressure on carers, while programmes help the person develop communication, mobility, self-care, social and employment-related skills. Enable Ireland also raises awareness and funds services through voluntary activity and retail shops, complementing statutory health and education provision.

QUESTION homeeconomics-pp-2023-HI-sectiona-cookingmethods-Q5 (LC Home Economics Higher 2023 Paper A — Q5; filed under hom-cooking-methods)

PART (whole) [6 marks]
  Q: Outline two benefits of including high fibre foods in the diet of adults.
  A: Slows glucose release; lowers cholesterol/CHD risk; adds bulk and fullness; speeds peristalsis and helps prevent bowel disorders.

QUESTION homeeconomics-pp-2023-HI-sectiona-cookingmethods-Q7 (LC Home Economics Higher 2023 Paper A — Q7; filed under hom-cooking-methods)
PART (whole) [6 marks]
  Q: Describe two chemical changes that occur when food is cooked.
  A: Maillard reaction: amino acids react with sugar under dry heat, browning food. Caramelisation: sugar melts to brown caramel. Dextrinisation: dry heat converts starch to short-chain dextrins, causing browning.

QUESTION homeeconomics-pp-2023-HI-sectiona-foodadditives-Q8 (LC Home Economics Higher 2023 Paper A — Q8; filed under hom-food-additives)
PART (whole) [6 marks]
  Q: Name one sensory analysis test used to determine a preferred product from two or more samples.
Describe two different uses of sensory analysis in the food industry.
  A: Paired-preference, hedonic-rating or preference-ranking test. Uses: develop new products; detect differences between original and modified foods; compare own-brand and leading brands; quality assurance.
