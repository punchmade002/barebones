QUESTION homeeconomics-pp-2026-HI-sectionb-proteins-Q1 (LC Home Economics Higher 2026 Paper BC — Q1; filed under hom-proteins)
PART (a) [20 marks]
  Q: Using the information provided and having regard to current healthy eating guidelines, compare and evaluate the nutritional contribution of protein bars and high protein fruit yogurts to the diet.
  A: Both products can make a useful protein-rich snack, but their complete food labels and place in the day’s diet must be considered. A protein bar commonly contains concentrated milk/whey or soya protein. This can supply all essential amino acids and support growth and tissue repair, which is valuable for teenagers or active adults. It is portable and convenient, and some bars supply fibre from oats, nuts or seeds. However, concentration does not automatically make it healthier: a bar may also be energy-dense and contain added sugar, saturated fat, salt, chocolate coatings or several additives. It should not replace balanced meals.

A high-protein fruit yoghurt supplies HBV milk protein for growth and repair together with calcium and phosphorus for bones and teeth and B-group vitamins. Its high water content usually makes it less energy-dense, and a live yoghurt may contain beneficial bacteria. Fruit adds flavour and may contribute some micronutrients, but a sweetened fruit preparation can raise the free-sugar content. Full-fat varieties contribute more saturated fat, while low-fat versions may contain more added sugar.

Therefore, compare protein per serving, portion size, energy, saturated fat, sugar, fibre and salt rather than relying on the words ‘high protein’. Under healthy-eating guidance, either can be occasional and useful, but plain high-protein yoghurt with fresh fruit is generally the less processed choice; the bar is most valuable where genuine portability is required.
PART (b) [8 marks]
  Q: Differentiate between essential amino acids and non-essential amino acids and give one example of each type of amino acid.
  A: Essential, or indispensable, amino acids cannot be manufactured by the body in adequate amounts and must be supplied by food. Lysine is one example. A protein’s biological value depends on whether it provides all essential amino acids in the correct proportions. Non-essential amino acids can be synthesised in the body from other amino acids and suitable nitrogen-containing compounds; alanine is an example. ‘Non-essential’ does not mean unimportant—it means that a dietary supply is not normally essential.
PART (c) [14 marks]
  Q: Describe the deamination of protein.
  A: Deamination is the process used when amino acids are present in excess of the body’s needs. Unlike fat and carbohydrate, surplus amino acids cannot be stored as protein, so they are transported to the liver. There, the nitrogen-containing amino group (–NH₂) is removed from each amino acid. This produces ammonia, which is highly toxic. The liver rapidly combines ammonia with carbon dioxide to form the much less toxic compound urea. Urea enters the bloodstream, is filtered from the blood by the kidneys and is excreted in urine.

The remainder of the amino acid is a carbon skeleton. It can be oxidised during respiration to release energy. Depending on the body’s energy balance, it may also be converted to glucose and stored as glycogen, or converted to fat and stored in adipose tissue. Deamination therefore allows useful energy to be recovered from surplus dietary protein while the potentially harmful nitrogen is safely removed. The process shows why excess protein is not stored as extra body protein and why adequate fluid is needed to excrete urea.
PART (d) [18 marks]
  Q: Explain the denaturation of protein. In your answer refer to:
• heat
• mechanical action
• enzymes.
  A: Denaturation is the irreversible unfolding of a protein’s secondary and tertiary structure without breaking its peptide bonds. The altered chains lose their original shape and properties; they may then link together and coagulate. This changes the food’s texture, appearance and digestibility.

Heat breaks the weak bonds holding the folded structure. Egg white, for example, changes from transparent and liquid to opaque and firm as albumen denatures and coagulates from about 60°C. Meat proteins set and shrink; with gentle moist heat, collagen changes to gelatine and becomes tender, whereas excessive heat makes muscle protein tough.

Mechanical action also denatures protein. Whisking egg white stretches the albumen chains and introduces air. The chains arrange around the air bubbles and form a foam. Heat during baking then coagulates the protein and fixes the foam permanently, as in a meringue or soufflé.

Enzymes cause characteristic protein changes. Rennin coagulates the milk protein casein in the stomach and in cheese-making. Proteolytic enzymes such as papain break down proteins and can tenderise meat. Acids, alkalis and heavy-metal salts can also denature proteins. Thus denaturation may be desirable and controlled in cooking, but excessive treatment damages texture.
PART (e) [20 marks]
  Q: Devise a set of strategies to follow when selecting protein foods to ensure safety and value for money.
  A: Plan meals and make a list so that the quantity bought matches the household’s needs; this prevents impulse buying and protein-rich food waste. Compare unit prices and the usable portion, not merely pack price. Eggs, milk, pulses, beans, lentils, tinned fish and complementary combinations such as beans on wholemeal toast can provide good-value protein. Choose lean meat and poultry, use cheaper cuts with slow moist cooking, buy fish in season and compare own-brand products. Special offers or bulk packs are worthwhile only if the food can be used or frozen safely.

Buy from a clean, reputable retailer. Check use-by dates, quality-assurance information, allergen declarations, storage instructions and the nutrition panel. Packaging must be sealed and undamaged; cans must not be bulging. Chilled meat, fish and dairy food should be below 5°C and frozen food should be solid at about −18°C. Fresh fish should have firm flesh, bright eyes and red gills; meat should have a fresh colour and smell. Pick chilled food near the end of the trip and transport it in a cool bag.

At home, refrigerate promptly, rotate stock, separate raw animal foods from cooked or ready-to-eat foods, and follow use-by dates. Defrost in the fridge, cook thoroughly, avoid cross-contamination, divide bulk food into portions and reheat only once. These measures combine safety, nutrition and genuine value for money.

QUESTION homeeconomics-pp-2026-HI-sectionb-vitaminsminerals-Q2 (LC Home Economics Higher 2026 Paper BC — Q2; filed under hom-vitamins-minerals)
PART (a) [20 marks]
  Q: Evaluate the nutritional significance of vegetables in the diet.
  A: Vegetables make an important, varied contribution to a balanced diet. Most contain about 1–8% mainly low-biological-value protein, although pulses are richer and soya provides HBV protein. They are generally very low in fat; olives, avocado and soya are exceptions and supply mainly unsaturated fat. Carbohydrate ranges from small amounts of sugar in carrots and onions to starch in potatoes, roots and pulses. Their non-starch polysaccharide or dietary fibre increases stool bulk, stimulates peristalsis, prevents constipation and may lower the risk of bowel disease; it also promotes fullness and can assist weight control.

Dark-green, orange, yellow and red vegetables provide beta-carotene, which the body converts to vitamin A for rhodopsin, growth and healthy epithelium. Leafy greens, peppers, tomatoes and potatoes contribute vitamin C for collagen, immunity and iron absorption. Pulses and green vegetables provide B vitamins and folate; green vegetables also supply vitamin K. Mineral contributions include non-haem iron, calcium, potassium, magnesium and small amounts of zinc and iodine. Their 70–95% water content supports hydration.

Vegetables are therefore valuable in low-energy, high-fibre, vegetarian and heart-healthy diets, and their antioxidant nutrients help protect cells. However, they do not provide vitamin B12 and are poor in vitamin D, so a diet cannot rely on vegetables alone. Vitamin C and B vitamins are easily lost during storage, cutting and prolonged boiling; eating a varied range, including fresh, lightly cooked and seasonal vegetables, maximises their value.
PART (b) [12 marks]
  Q: Outline four guidelines to follow when preparing and cooking vegetables in order to maximise retention of nutrients.
  A: Choose fresh vegetables and store them for the shortest possible time, because vitamin C is lost during storage. Prepare immediately before cooking: wash before cutting, peel thinly or leave edible skins on, avoid soaking, and cut into large rather than small pieces to reduce the surface exposed to water and oxygen. Do not leave prepared vegetables standing in water or exposed to air. Cook rapidly in the minimum boiling water with a tight lid, or steam, microwave or stir-fry; do not add bicarbonate of soda or use copper/brass pans. Serve immediately and keep vegetables slightly crisp. If water is used, retain the vitamin- and mineral-containing cooking liquid for soup, sauce or gravy.
PART (c) [18 marks]
  Q: Discuss the causes of food spoilage in vegetables.
Refer to:
• enzymes
• moisture loss
• microbial contamination.
  A: Enzymes remain active after harvesting. They continue normal ripening until vegetables become over-ripe, soft and eventually decay. When cells are cut or bruised, oxidase is released and reacts with oxygen, causing enzymic browning and destroying some vitamin C and carotene. Unblanched vegetables may continue to deteriorate even in a freezer; blanching denatures these enzymes before freezing.

Moisture loss occurs through respiration and evaporation, especially in warm, dry, uncovered storage. Cells lose turgor, so leafy vegetables wilt, roots shrivel and skins wrinkle. The vegetable becomes limp, loses mass and has poorer texture and appearance. Refrigerated, humid storage and suitable packaging slow this loss, although sealed plastic must not encourage condensation.

Microbial contamination by bacteria, yeasts and moulds comes from soil, dirty hands, equipment, pests, damaged produce or cross-contamination. Bruising and cuts give organisms access to nutrients and moisture. Warmth, time and a suitable pH allow rapid growth; bacteria cause soft, slimy rot and unpleasant odours, while mould appears as coloured fuzzy growth and may produce harmful mycotoxins. Hygiene, separation from raw meat, removal of damaged items, storage below 5°C where suitable, and prompt use all delay spoilage.

QUESTION homeeconomics-pp-2026-HI-sectionb-foodadditives-Q3 (LC Home Economics Higher 2026 Paper BC — Q3; filed under hom-food-additives)
PART (a) [16 marks]
  Q: Discuss the advantages and disadvantages of including processed/convenience foods in the diet.
  A: Processed and convenience foods save preparation, cooking and cleaning time and require less skill, so they are useful for busy households, older people or those with limited dexterity. Processing extends shelf life, improves safety, provides out-of-season variety and reduces waste. Clear portions can help planning, while freezing and canning preserve many nutrients; fortification may add calcium, iron or vitamins. They are easy to store and can provide an emergency meal.

However, highly processed products may contain excessive salt, free sugar, saturated fat and energy while providing little fibre. Repeated use can unbalance the diet and increase risks associated with obesity, hypertension and coronary heart disease. They may contain many additives, and a minority of consumers react to particular substances. They are often dearer than equivalent home-prepared food, use more packaging and create environmental waste. Large portions and attractive marketing can encourage overconsumption, and reliance on ready meals reduces cooking skills. The best judgement is to use them selectively, compare labels and combine them with fresh vegetables, wholegrains and other minimally processed foods.
PART (b) [18 marks]
  Q: Write an informative account of artificial sweeteners.
Refer to:
• description of types/classes
• examples
• uses in food.
  A: Artificial sweeteners are food additives that give sweetness while replacing some or all sucrose. They are useful where manufacturers want a sweet product with less sugar or energy, but approved sweeteners must be safety-tested, used within legal limits and identified on the label by name or E-number.

Intense, non-nutritive sweeteners are many times sweeter than sugar, so only a very small amount is needed and they contribute negligible energy. Examples include saccharin, aspartame, acesulfame K and sucralose. Because the quantity is tiny, they do not supply sugar’s bulk, browning, preservation or texture, so a bulking agent may also be needed. They are used in low-energy soft drinks, tabletop sweeteners, yoghurts, desserts and foods intended to reduce sugar intake.

Bulk sweeteners, or polyols, include sorbitol and mannitol. They are less intensely sweet and are used in larger quantities, giving bulk and texture as well as sweetness. They occur in products such as sugar-free confectionery and chewing gum and can act as humectants, helping food retain moisture. Artificial sweeteners are useful in slimming and diabetic products because they reduce sucrose, although the whole nutrient profile and portion still matter. They do not perform all the culinary functions of sugar, and excessive consumption of some polyols can have a laxative effect.
PART (c) [16 marks]
  Q: Analyse how labelling can assist consumers in their choice of processed/convenience foods.
  A: Labelling allows a consumer to compare convenience foods rather than being guided by brand, pictures or health claims. The nutrition declaration gives energy, fat, saturates, carbohydrate, sugars, protein and salt, normally per 100 g and often per portion. This supports comparison of products and selection for low-salt, low-saturated-fat, high-fibre or energy-controlled diets, provided the consumer checks the stated serving size.

Ingredients are listed in descending order by weight, showing whether sugar, fat or refined starch is a major component. Additives are identified by function and name or E-number. Allergens must be emphasised, protecting people with coeliac disease, milk, egg, nut or other allergies. Quantity, origin where required, use-by or best-before date, storage conditions and cooking/reheating instructions support safety, value and reduced waste. The manufacturer’s details and lot information assist traceability and complaints. Quality-assurance, organic, fair-trade or gluten-free marks can guide particular ethical or dietary choices. A good consumer also distinguishes a regulated nutrition statement from vague marketing and reads the complete label, because ‘low fat’ does not necessarily mean low sugar, salt or energy.

QUESTION homeeconomics-pp-2026-HI-sectionb-personalfinance-Q4 (LC Home Economics Higher 2026 Paper BC — Q4; filed under hom-personal-finance)
PART (a) [20 marks]
  Q: Analyse the recent changes in consumer shopping patterns.
  A: Shopping has shifted from frequent dependence on small local shops towards a mixture of large supermarkets, discount multiples, convenience stores and online retailing. Many households complete a planned weekly supermarket shop and use local or convenience outlets for top-ups. Extended opening hours, self-service, contactless payment, home delivery and click-and-collect make shopping fit around dual employment and irregular family schedules. Online shopping widens choice and simplifies price comparison, but delivery costs, fraud risk and the inability to inspect goods remain disadvantages.

Consumers have become more price-conscious. Discount retailers, own brands, loyalty cards, coupons, multibuy offers and comparison sites influence where they shop. Bulk buying can reduce unit cost but only provides value if products are used before waste occurs. Retailers use product placement, attractive displays, music, pricing just below a round number and checkout promotions to stimulate unplanned purchases.

At the same time, health and ethical concerns increasingly affect decisions. Shoppers compare nutrition and allergen labels, seek low-salt, low-sugar, gluten-free, vegetarian or fortified products, and may choose local, seasonal, organic, fair-trade, free-range or recyclable-packaged goods. Prepared ingredients, ready meals and food-to-go have grown with time pressure, while farmers’ markets and small producers appeal through quality and traceability. These patterns increase convenience and choice, but informed consumers still need a list, budget and label checks to avoid overspending and food waste.
PART (b) [18 marks]
  Q: Discuss how the following impact on household spending:
• income
• family stage
• personal values.
  A: Income sets the total resources available. A high, secure dual income permits more discretionary spending, saving and credit repayment; a low, irregular or reduced income means essentials such as housing, food, heat and transport must take priority. Inflation, tax, welfare, debt and job security alter disposable income, while access to credit may bring purchases forward but increases future commitments.

Family stage changes both the type and amount spent. A young single adult may spend more on rent, travel and leisure. A couple establishing a home faces deposits, mortgage, furniture and appliances. Families with babies require childcare and equipment; school-age children add food, clothing, education and activities; adolescents may increase transport, technology and education costs. An empty-nest or retired household may spend less on children but more on health, heating or home adaptation, often from a fixed pension.

Personal values decide how income is allocated within these limits. A family that values education may prioritise books or courses; environmental values may favour durable, efficient appliances, local food and public transport; health values influence food and leisure spending. Status or strong brand loyalty may increase spending, while thrift, saving, repair and reuse reduce it. Effective management balances these values with needs, goals and available resources rather than allowing advertising or peer pressure to dictate expenditure.
PART (c) [12 marks]
  Q: Describe the advantages of household budgeting.
  A: A household budget lists expected net income and allocates it among fixed, irregular and discretionary expenditure before money is spent. It ensures essential bills are paid, exposes overspending and prevents impulse buying, debt and excessive reliance on credit. Regular allowance for savings creates an emergency fund and supports short- and long-term goals such as holidays, education or a home. Budgeting reduces stress because the family knows where its money is going and can plan for annual costs and unexpected events. It encourages communication, shared decision-making and responsible use of resources. Comparing actual spending with the plan also provides feedback, so allocations can be revised when income, prices or family circumstances change.

QUESTION homeeconomics-pp-2026-HI-sectionb-personalfinance-Q5 (LC Home Economics Higher 2026 Paper BC — Q5; filed under hom-social-change)
PART (a) [20 marks]
  Q: Analyse how economic and social changes affect families in Ireland.
  A: Economic change affects both family resources and roles. Growth in female employment and dual-income households can improve living standards and independence, but childcare costs, commuting and dual roles create time pressure and role conflict. Flexible work, job-sharing and working from home can support work-life balance, while unemployment or insecure, low-paid work reduces income, strains relationships and can cause poverty, debt or migration. High housing and living costs may delay leaving home, marriage and parenthood; adult children often depend on parents for longer.

Social change has produced smaller and more diverse family structures, including lone-parent, blended, same-sex-parent and childless families as well as nuclear and extended families. Separation and divorce can lead to financial and emotional adjustment, new childcare arrangements and step-family relationships. Increased life expectancy means more active grandparents may provide childcare and financial or emotional support, while families may also care for dependent older relatives.

Greater gender equality has encouraged shared earning, housework, decision-making and childcare rather than rigid segregated roles. Improved education and contraception have widened women’s choices and contributed to later childbearing and smaller families. Urbanisation and geographic mobility may weaken daily extended-family support, although communication technology maintains contact. Social media and home technology offer connection, information and efficiency but can also reduce face-to-face communication. Families therefore remain responsible for physical, economic, emotional and social needs, but the way these functions are carried out has become more negotiated and varied.
PART (b) [16 marks]
  Q: Discuss how the roles of parents and children evolve throughout the life cycle of the family.
  A: At the beginning of family life, partners establish the home, earn income, share decisions and negotiate domestic tasks. With a baby, parents assume almost total responsibility for physical care, protection, affection, socialisation and financial support; sleep loss and childcare can intensify dual roles. During the preschool and school years, they set boundaries, model behaviour, support education and gradually assign simple tasks. Children move from dependence towards responsibility by tidying, helping at home, respecting rules and cooperating with siblings.

In adolescence, parental control should increasingly become guidance. Teenagers seek privacy, peer contact and independence, but remain responsible for schoolwork, appropriate behaviour, a fair share of household duties and possibly helping younger or older relatives. Clear communication and negotiated limits reduce conflict. In the launching stage, adult children leave for education, work or relationships; parents offer advice and sometimes financial support while accepting independent decisions. During the empty-nest and retirement stages, roles may reverse: adult children may support ageing parents physically, emotionally or financially. Grandparents may assist with childcare and transmit family values. Thus healthy family roles change from direct care and authority towards cooperation, independence and mutual support.
PART (c) [14 marks]
  Q: State why it is important for parents to make a will and outline the procedure involved.
  A: A will ensures parents’ property passes to the people they choose rather than being distributed only by intestacy rules. It can appoint a trusted guardian for dependent children, name an executor, make suitable provision for dependants, reduce disputes, express funeral wishes and, with advice, reduce inheritance-tax difficulties. This gives security and peace of mind.

The parents should list all assets, liabilities and relevant policies, and record each beneficiary’s full details. They decide how the estate is to be allocated, make appropriate provision for children, appoint an executor and guardian, and state funeral or burial wishes. Although a person may draft a will, using a solicitor helps ensure that it is clear and valid. The testator signs at the end in the presence of two independent witnesses, who are present together and then sign; beneficiaries should not act as witnesses. The dated will is stored safely, its location is made known to the executor, and it should be reviewed after births, deaths, marriage, separation or major changes in property.
