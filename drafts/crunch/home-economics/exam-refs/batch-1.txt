PART 2(a)(ii) [15 marks]
  Q: In relation to the outfit give details of: how three principles of design have been applied; how the outfit is suitable for purpose.
  A: Balance is created by the symmetrical shirt collar, V-neck, sleeves and evenly arranged skirt pleats on either side of the centre line. This gives a calm, formal appearance and ensures that no feature makes one side look visually heavier.

Proportion is shown by the relationship of garment lengths and details. The jumper finishes near the natural hip so the upper and lower sections are in pleasing proportion; the skirt ends at the knee, and the tie, collar, pocket and pleats are sized to suit the wearer rather than dominate the outfit.

Rhythm and harmony are achieved by repeating the school colour from the jumper in the tie stripe and skirt check. Repeated vertical pleat and tie lines lead the eye through the outfit, while the limited palette and similar practical textures unite all parts.

PART 2(b) [15 marks]
  Q: Write a profile of one fabric manufactured from natural fibres. Refer to: fibre production; fibre properties; identification of fibres using the burning test.
  A: Natural fabric: woven wool.

Fibre production begins when the fleece is shorn from a sheep, usually in one piece. It is sorted and graded according to fineness, length, colour and the part of the fleece. Scouring in warm water and detergent removes grease, suint and dirt; lanolin may be recovered. The clean fibres are teased and carded into a web. For a smooth worsted yarn they are also combed so long fibres lie parallel and short fibres are removed. Drawing evens the sliver, and spinning adds twist to form yarn. Yarns are then woven or knitted and the fabric may be fulled and finished.

Wool is warm because its crimp traps insulating air. It is absorbent but does not feel wet immediately, elastic and resilient, so it has good crease recovery and drape. It dyes well and is naturally flame-resistant/self-extinguishing. However, it is relatively weak when wet, may pill, can be damaged by moths and may felt or shrink through heat, moisture and agitation, so care is required. These properties make it suitable for tailored uniforms, suits, coats and knitwear.

Burning test: remove finishes where possible and hold a small fibre sample with forceps over a non-flammable surface. Bring it slowly towards a flame. Wool shrinks/curls away, burns slowly with a small sputtering flame and tends to self-extinguish when removed. It smells like burning hair or feathers because it is a protein fibre. It leaves a dark, irregular, brittle bead or crushable black ash. Compare the result with a known wool control, ventilate the room and keep water/fire blanket nearby. The test is indicative, so a blend may give mixed results.

  A: Smaller/later families; male unemployment; economic need for two incomes; women's higher education and desire to use qualifications; childcare support; flexible, part-time/job-share/parental/term-time arrangements; equality in work and education.
PART 3(c)(ii) [10 marks]
  Q: Give two examples of how legislation protects the rights of people in employment.
  A: Equality Act 2004 prohibits discrimination on gender, marital/family status, age, race, religion, sexuality and Traveller membership. Organisation of Working Time Act 1997 protects hours/breaks. Other laws cover minimum wage, health/safety, holidays and family leave, and young-person working conditions.

  Q: State two causes of protein denaturation and give one example in each case.

QUESTION homeeconomics-pp-2021-HI-sectiona-lipids-Q2 (LC Home Economics Higher 2021 Paper A — Q2; filed under hom-lipids)
PART (whole) [6 marks]
  Q: In relation to lipids, explain each of the following:
Oxidative rancidity
Hydrolytic rancidity
  A: Oxidative rancidity: unsaturated fatty acids react with oxygen at their double bonds, producing aldehydes/ketones and a rancid smell. Hydrolytic rancidity: lipases from food or micro-organisms hydrolyse fat to fatty acids and glycerol, producing unpleasant taste/smell; may occur in freezers if enzymes were not destroyed.

Vitamin D

QUESTION homeeconomics-pp-2021-HI-sectiona-energynutrition-Q4 (LC Home Economics Higher 2021 Paper A — Q4; filed under hom-energy-nutrition)

  Q: What is Basal Metabolic Rate (BMR)?
Give two factors that determine a person’s basal metabolic rate.
  A: BMR is the minimum energy needed to sustain the body at complete rest, maintain temperature and keep internal organs functioning. Factors include age, gender, body weight/size, health, hormones, pregnancy/lactation, occupation, activity and climate.

QUESTION homeeconomics-pp-2021-HI-sectiona-foodpreservation-Q7 (LC Home Economics Higher 2021 Paper A — Q7; filed under hom-food-preservation)
PART (whole) [6 marks]
  Q: Explain the effect of each of the following in relation to the processing of vegetables and fruit:
Canning
Dehydration
  A: Canning: may lose water-soluble vitamins and change colour, texture and flavour; destroys micro-organisms/enzymes; syrup, brine or oil may increase sugar, salt or fat. Dehydration: may lose B vitamins, A and E and alter colour, texture and flavour; prevents microbial/enzyme activity; dried fruit may be high in sugar.

QUESTION homeeconomics-pp-2021-HI-sectiona-cookingmethods-Q9 (LC Home Economics Higher 2021 Paper A — Q9; filed under hom-cooking-methods)
PART (whole) [6 marks]
  Q: Explain the following chemical changes that occur during food preparation/cooking:
Enzymic browning
Non-enzymic browning
  A: Enzymic browning: cut/damaged fruit or vegetables release oxidase, which reacts with air and oxidises the food, e.g. apple turns brown. Non-enzymic: Maillard reaction between amino acids and carbohydrate under heat; caramelisation when sugar melts to brown caramel; dextrinisation when dry heat forms dextrins on carbohydrate foods such as toast.
