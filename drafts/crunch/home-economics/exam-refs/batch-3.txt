QUESTION homeeconomics-pp-2019-HI-sectionc-housing-Q1 (LC Home Economics Higher 2019 — Q1; filed under hom-housing)
PART (a)(i) [20 marks]
  Q: Outline the historical development of housing styles in Ireland.
  A: 19th century: rural thatched cottages and larger two-storey farm/estate houses; urban Georgian terraces, shop-houses, workers’ cottages and tenements. 20th century: rural upgrades, slate/tile roofs and electrification; urban council/private estates, detached/semi-detached houses, bungalows, high-rises and improved materials/regulations. 21st century: varied developments, renovated period homes, architect-designed one-offs, extensions and outdoor living; urban gated complexes, townhouses/apartments, suburban expansion and high-density housing.
PART (a)(ii) [18 marks]
  Q: Discuss the economic and environmental factors that influence the choice of housing styles.
  A: Economic factors: income, building-material and professional costs, investment potential, BER and grants. Environmental factors: building regulations, energy efficiency, ecological principles, aspect and fitting into the surrounding environment.
PART (a)(iii) [12 marks]
  Q: Give details of the professional services available to consumers to assist in the design and building of a home.
  A: Architect advises on site/design and oversees construction; engineer oversees construction and advises on structural problems; surveyor surveys the site and identifies solutions; solicitor handles purchase, title and contracts; builder agrees costs/completion, prepares the site, subcontracts and resolves snags. Other services include interior designer, landscape architect, electrician, plumber and BER assessor.
PART (b)(i) [16 marks]
  Q: Outline four factors that should be considered when selecting wall finishes for a home.
  A: Aesthetics; cost; environmental impact; room requirements and aspect; durability; washability; finish and texture; ease of application/cleaning; coverage; light reflectance.
PART (b)(ii) [14 marks]
  Q: Name and describe two types of wall finishes suitable for a kitchen/living room.
State two properties of each finish.
  A: Paint: water-based emulsion, oil/solvent gloss or satin, or specialised textured/flame-retardant paint; may be washable, water-resistant, durable, easy to clean, quick/slow drying, odorous/low-odour, coloured and light-reflective. Wallpaper: general, embossed, woodchip, vinyl or flock; may withstand condensation, be washable/durable, camouflage faults, add texture, be paintable and come in many designs. Tiles: ceramic/porcelain; hard-wearing, waterproof, easy to maintain and available glazed/unglazed in many colours and sizes.
PART (c)(i) [12 marks]
  Q: In relation to household electricity supply describe each of the following:
• ring circuit
• minature circuit breaker (MCB).
  A: Ring circuit: cable runs from the consumer unit through sockets and returns to it; separate upstairs/downstairs loops may be used, each earthed and fused. MCB: a safety trip switch that interrupts and disconnects current during a fault/overload and can be reset after repair; RCDs may add protection.
PART (c)(ii) [18 marks]
  Q: Compile a set of guidelines for the safe use of electricity in the home.
  A: Buy safety-approved appliances; wire and maintain plugs/flexes correctly; take care outdoors; never handle electricity with wet hands; keep flexes from hobs/sinks; do not overload sockets; do not repair appliances yourself; unplug before cleaning; check electric blankets; never cover heaters; remove dust from heaters; use pull cords/enclosed shades and avoid portable heaters in bathrooms.

QUESTION homeeconomics-pp-2019-HI-sectionc-personalfinance-Q3 (LC Home Economics Higher 2019 — Q3; filed under hom-work-leisure)
PART (a)(i) [12 marks]
  Q: In relation to the food industry, comment on the contribution made by the primary, secondary and tertiary sectors.
  A: Primary: resource-based food industries such as agriculture and fishing. Secondary: manufacture/processing of primary raw materials into finished food goods. Tertiary: services such as tourism, leisure, healthcare, transport, media, food outlets and education.
PART (a)(ii) [16 marks]
  Q: Analyse the positive and negative effects of technology on work and employment.
  A: Positive: interesting skilled jobs, removal of repetitive tasks, safer/cleaner workplaces, productivity, leisure, home working, quality/consistency and more work. Negative: job loss for unskilled workers, relocation to cheap-labour countries, deskilling, loss of craftsmanship/job satisfaction, isolation and decline of primary/secondary employment.
PART (a)(iii) [10 marks]
  Q: Discuss how educational qualifications can lead to greater employment options.
  A: The Leaving Certificate is the minimum for many jobs; qualifications widen employment choice; most jobs require FETAC-level qualifications; further qualifications enable career advancement.
PART (a)(iv) [12 marks]
  Q: Explain, giving examples, how legislation protects people in employment.
  A: Safety, Health and Welfare at Work Act 2005 protects health and safety; National Minimum Wage Order sets minimum pay; Employment Equality Acts prohibit discrimination; Organisation of Working Time Act 1997 regulates hours and breaks; employee legislation provides leave/benefits; Protection of Young Persons (Employment) Act 1996 regulates young workers’ hours, breaks and days off.
PART (b)(i) [12 marks]
  Q: Summarise the principal factors that influence a family’s requirements for childcare.
  A: Both or a lone parent working; availability of relatives; cost; after-school and holiday care; availability, convenience, hours and flexibility; proximity; child’s age or special needs; reliability.
PART (b)(ii) [18 marks]
  Q: Evaluate two different childcare options available to parents.
  A: Two childcare options are a centre-based crèche and a childminder.

A crèche cares for groups of children in premises designed for childcare. Its strengths include trained staff, planned routines and a range of age-appropriate indoor/outdoor resources. Group play encourages sharing, language, independence and friendships; structured activities support physical, intellectual, emotional and social development. Registered services should meet requirements for adult-child ratios, space, hygiene, food safety, equipment and record-keeping. Several staff members give continuity if one adult is absent, and full-day services may include meals, sleep, preschool learning and after-school care.

Its limitations are cost, fixed collection times and sometimes long waiting lists. A large group gives less one-to-one attention, can be noisy or tiring and allows infections to spread. Staff turnover may make attachment difficult. A service may close for holidays, and parents may pay even when a child is absent. The centre must therefore be evaluated for staff qualifications, turnover, warm interaction, inspection/registration, ratios, settling-in policy, allergy/medication procedures, emergency plans, outdoor space, food and travel time.

A childminder provides care for a small number of children, normally in the minder's home or occasionally the child's home. The setting is homelike and can follow the child's normal meals, naps and school routine. Fewer children allow individual attention and close attachment; siblings can stay together, and the minder may offer flexible early starts, shift-work hours, school collection or holiday care. Parents and minder can communicate directly, and fewer group contacts may reduce exposure to infection. For some families it is cheaper than paying separate centre fees for siblings.

Disadvantages are variation in training, resources and regulation. A home may have limited outdoor/equipment provision, fewer peers for socialisation and no second adult to observe practice. If the minder becomes ill, takes leave or ends the arrangement, care can fail immediately. Fees, paid holidays, meals, transport, overtime and notice may cause disputes unless written clearly. Parents should check references, insurance, first aid, Garda vetting/registration where applicable, safe sleep, hygiene, pets, smoking, car restraints, discipline and emergency backup.

The better option depends on the child's age, temperament, disability/allergy, need for routine or group contact, parents' work hours, distance, affordability and availability. A sociable preschool child may benefit from a well-run crèche; a baby, shy child or family with irregular hours may suit a carefully chosen childminder. Neither is automatically superior. Parents should visit while children are present, observe relationships, verify standards and agree a written contract. High-quality, stable and responsive care is more important than the label attached to the service.

(ii)

This occurs when enzymes (lipases) hydrolyse fats, breaking them down into fatty acids and glycerol;

and smell; occurs in freezers when enzymes are not destroyed, etc.

QUESTION homeeconomics-pp-2013-HI-sectionb-foodsafety-Q3 (LC Home Economics Higher 2013 — Q3; filed under hom-food-safety)
PART (a) [15 marks]
  Q: Discuss the causes of food spoilage in relation to the action of enzymes.
  A: 3 points @ 5 marks each 

Enzymes bring about spoilage in 3 ways – over-ripening, browning and enzyme 
deterioration 

Over-ripening: enzymes cause food to over- ripen e.g. hard under-ripe banana becomes softer and

easier to digest; enzymes cause over-ripeness / spoilage e.g. black banana, etc. 

Enzymic browning: when certain foods are cut, e.g. apples, bananas and potatoes, the cut surface 

releases oxidase and is exposed to the air, causing them to turn brown, etc. 
Enzymic deterioration (rotting): enzymes in fish cause deterioration even at low temperatures; 
vegetables may be spoiled in the freezer particularly if they were not blanched before freezing; 
the enzyme oxidase present in the cell walls of plants is activated by oxygen and can destroy thiamine, 
Vit C and carotene, etc.

PART (c) [15 marks]
  Q: Assess irradiation as a method of food preservation.
  A: 3 points @ 5 marks each 

Advantages: destroys food poisoning bacteria and other parasites; longer shelf life; slows ripening 

and sprouting in fruit and vegetables; little effect on colour, flavour, texture of food; eliminates 

need for chemical preservatives, etc. 

Disadvantages: not suitable for high fat food, causes rancidity; loss of vitamins; high levels of radiation

QUESTION homeeconomics-pp-2013-HI-sectionb-personalfinance-Q4 (LC Home Economics Higher 2013 — Q4; filed under hom-personal-finance)
PART (a) [20 marks]
  Q: Discuss four factors that can influence the management of family resources.
  A: 4 points @ 5 marks each 

Composition of family: number of people in the family, presence of persons with special needs, 

one parent or two parent families, etc. 

Stages in life cycle: priorities are different for a family with or without children, as children get older 

they become part of the decision making process, etc. 

Employment patterns: number of family members working, whether employment is inside or outside 

the home, type of employment, hours of employment, etc. 

Socio-economic status: people from different socio economic backgrounds have different needs, 
lower socio economic backgrounds place emphasis on needs whilst people from higher socio economic 
backgrounds place emphasis on luxuries, etc. 
Culture: the culture of a country can affect management of family e.g. dress code, food eaten, 
religious practices, etc. 
Management of dual roles: this refers to where both parties work outside the home and extra planning 
is required; single parent families - extra demand being the earner and parent, etc. 
Gender roles - roles or behaviour expected by men and women, more equal partnership and shared 
roles nowadays, etc. 
Values and standards: affect management as they determine decision taken; when circumstances 
change in a family so too can values e.g. illness of a family member, etc.
