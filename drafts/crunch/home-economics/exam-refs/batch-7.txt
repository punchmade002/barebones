 fibre production
 fabric properties
 uses.
  A: Name of fabric: 3 marks 
Cotton 
Fibre production: comes from the boll or seed head of the cotton plant, with each fibre being 
between 2-3cm long; fibres are white and fluffy; the bolls are picked, by either hand or machine; 
the fibres are separated from the seeds; they are then pressed into bales; the cotton is then graded 
according to the length of the fibres; these fibres can be combed or spun into yarn; etc. 
Properties: strong; absorbent; cool; washes and dries easily; dyes easily; shrinks; creases; 
affected by mildew; weakened by sun; etc. 
Uses: clothes, towels, sheets and curtains; etc.

Linen 
Fibre production: made from the stem of the flax plant; flax stems are pulled and stems are retted, 
soaked until outer stem rots; stems are crushed and combed; fibres are spun into yarn; yarn is 
bleached and dyed; woven into a textured fabric; etc. 
Properties: absorbent; cool; strong; washes well; creases easily; shrinks easily; difficult to dye; 
damaged by mildew; etc. 
Uses: dresses; suits; soft furnishings; towels; napkins; tablecloths; handkerchiefs; etc. 

Wool 
Fibre production: comes from the soft hair of sheep; the soft hair, which is called its fleece, is 
removed from the sheep; it is then collected and graded to the length of the fibres and also the 
colour and fineness of the wool; it is then cleaned and combed which is sometimes known as 
carding; it is then spun into yarn and can then be used to produce textiles; etc. 
Properties: warm; absorbent; does not burn easily; hardwearing; resilient; shrinks if washed at 
too high a temperature or if tumble dried; coarse, can irritate the skin; etc. 
Uses: jumpers; blankets; carpets; soft furnishings; etc. 

Silk 
Fibre production: silkworm feeds on mulberry leaves; it spins a cocoon of silk around itself; 
cocoons are soaked in water; silk threads are unwound from cocoons onto reels; several of these 
thin silk threads are spun or twisted together to make thicker thread; these are then woven into 
fabric; etc. 
Properties: absorbent; crease resistant; strong; smooth; light; drapes well; expensive; damaged by 
careless washing, moths, sunshine, chemicals; etc. 
Uses: blouses; shirts; dresses; suits; ties; paintings; cushion covers; curtains; etc.

  A: The menu excludes meat, fish, poultry, eggs, milk and all other animal-derived ingredients, so it meets the basic requirement of a vegan diet. PART (c) [21 marks]
  Q: Give an account of vitamin B12 (cobalamin) and refer to: sources in the diet; properties; biological functions.
  A: Sources: milk/dairy, eggs, fish, offal, meat and poultry. Properties: water-soluble, heat-stable and generally stable in light, acids and alkalis, but may be destroyed by UV rays. Functions: red-blood-cell manufacture; metabolism of fatty acids and folic acid; formation of the myelin sheath and a healthy nervous system; and normal growth.

QUESTION homeeconomics-pp-2009-HI-sectionb-foodsafety-B2 (LC Home Economics Higher 2009 — B2; filed under hom-food-additives)
PART (a) [16 marks]
  Q: Discuss the role of small businesses and home enterprises within the Irish food industry.
  A: They create employment, often in rural areas; diversify changing markets and meet consumer demand; identify and enter old/new niche markets; offer new exclusive food ranges; promote quality ingredients and innovation; and strengthen Ireland's reputation for high-quality food and drink.
PART (b) [26 marks]
  Q: Write a profile of an 'added value' food you have studied. Give details of: stages of production; packaging; labelling.
  A: Product studied: low-fat probiotic strawberry yoghurt. It is an added-value food because ordinary milk has been processed into a convenient product with extended shelf life, flavour, a smooth texture and live probiotic bacteria; the reduced-fat formulation also targets health-conscious consumers.

Production begins with reception and quality testing of milk. The fat content is standardised and milk solids may be increased to give body. The milk is homogenised under pressure so fat globules remain evenly dispersed. It is pasteurised to destroy pathogens and most spoilage organisms, then cooled to the temperature needed by the starter culture. Lactic-acid bacteria, and the specified probiotic culture, are added. During incubation they ferment lactose to lactic acid. The falling pH coagulates casein and produces the characteristic sour flavour and thick texture. Once the correct acidity is reached, the yoghurt is rapidly chilled to slow fermentation. Prepared pasteurised strawberry purée may be blended in, with permitted sugar, stabiliser or flavouring. The yoghurt is then filled hygienically, sealed, coded, refrigerated and distributed through the chill chain.

Packaging is a food-grade plastic pot with a heat-sealed foil lid and an outer cardboard sleeve or printed pot. It is light, moisture-proof, hygienic, portion-sized and tamper-evident. The seal prevents re-entry of micro-organisms, while the pot protects the yoghurt and provides a printable surface. Recyclable or reduced-plastic materials lessen environmental impact.

The label should state the product and brand name; ingredients in descending order; allergens, especially milk, emphasised; net quantity; nutrition per 100 g and serving; use-by date; storage instruction such as 'keep refrigerated'; manufacturer and batch code; country/origin information where required; and directions such as 'stir before use'. Claims such as 'low fat', 'contains live cultures' or calcium content must be accurate and not misleading.
PART (c) [8 marks]
  Q: Give a brief account of the role of the Food Safety Authority of Ireland (FSAI).
  A: The FSAI coordinates national enforcement of food-safety regulation with consumer interests first; advises ministers, regulators, industry and consumers; works with industry to secure safe production; acts against premises breaching regulations; and processes applications for novel foods/new processing methods.

QUESTION homeeconomics-pp-2009-HI-sectionb-foodsafety-B3 (LC Home Economics Higher 2009 — B3; filed under hom-food-safety)
PART (a) [12 marks]
  Q: Outline the main causes of food spoilage.
  A: Causes include micro-organisms (bacteria, mould and yeast fermentation); enzyme action causing over-ripening, browning, oxidation, rancidity or decomposition; putrefaction of protein; rot; physical bruising; chemical contamination by insecticides, antibiotics or metals; and moisture loss causing shrinkage/wrinkling.
PART (b) [20 marks]
  Q: Explain how the action of enzymes can be controlled in order to prevent food spoilage.
  A: Add acid, e.g. lemon juice on apples, to inactivate enzymes; use preservatives such as sulphur dioxide in fruit/vegetable juices and dried fruit; apply heat/cooking; use cold temperatures to slow enzyme action; blanch vegetables before freezing; or irradiate food.
PART (c) [18 marks]
  Q: In relation to food poisoning explain each of the following: incubation period; toxic food poisoning; infectious food poisoning.
  A: Incubation period: time between eating contaminated food and symptoms developing. Toxic food poisoning: food contains bacteria that produce and release exotoxins, e.g. staphylococci or Clostridium botulinum. Infectious food poisoning: food contains large numbers of pathogenic bacteria that produce endotoxins within their cells, e.g. salmonella or listeria.
