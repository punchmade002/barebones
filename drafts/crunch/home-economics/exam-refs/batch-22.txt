QUESTION homeeconomics-pp-2008-HI-sectionb-lipids-B1 (LC Home Economics Higher 2008 — B1; filed under hom-lipids)
PART (a) [24 marks]
  Q: Using the information provided in the table, evaluate each of the three products described (Extra Light Spread, Original Spread, Butter), having regard to current dietary advice on fat intake.
  A: Extra Light Spread has the lowest energy, total fat and saturated fat, so is most suitable for weight reduction and reducing CHD risk; it has three times the PUFA of butter but less than Original. Original Spread is intermediate in energy, total fat and saturates; it has about four times Extra Light's monounsaturates and the highest PUFA level, which may lower total and LDL cholesterol. Butter has the highest energy, total fat and saturated fat, so is least suitable for weight reduction and may raise total and LDL cholesterol; it has the most monounsaturates but the least PUFA. Omega-3 lowers cholesterol and CHD risk; trans fats may raise LDL and lower HDL; excessive sodium may cause hypertension and increase CHD/stroke risk.
PART (b) [24 marks]
  Q: Describe the structure and give one example of each of the following: saturated fatty acids; monounsaturated fatty acids; polyunsaturated fatty acids.
  A: Saturated fatty acids have no double bonds because each carbon has its full complement of hydrogen; they are solid at room temperature, e.g. stearic or butyric acid. Monounsaturated fatty acids have one double bond and are liquid at room temperature, e.g. oleic acid. Polyunsaturated fatty acids have more than one double bond between carbon atoms and are soft or liquid at room temperature, e.g. linoleic, linolenic or arachidonic acid.
PART (c) [12 marks]
  Q: Write an explanatory note on each of the following properties of lipids: rancidity; emulsification.
  A: Rancidity is spoilage of lipids. Oxidative rancidity occurs when oxygen reacts at double bonds in unsaturated carbon chains; hydrolytic rancidity is caused by enzymes or micro-organisms in the lipid and can occur in a freezer. Emulsification joins two immiscible liquids to form a colloidal solution/emulsion, either water-in-oil or oil-in-water; it may be temporary, e.g. French dressing, or permanent, e.g. mayonnaise.
PART (d) [20 marks]
  Q: Explain how (i) advertising and (ii) a person's health status might influence decision making when purchasing dairy products.
  A: Advertising may use famous people, constant exposure and health claims, or introduce new products such as omega-3 milk. Health status affects choice: a person controlling weight may choose low-fat/low-sugar products; someone concerned about CHD may choose low-saturate, high-PUFA/omega-3 or plant-stanol products; hypertension may prompt low-salt products; osteoporosis may prompt calcium-enriched milk; allergies or lactose intolerance may require alternatives such as goat's milk.

QUESTION homeeconomics-pp-2008-HI-sectionb-foodpreservation-B2 (LC Home Economics Higher 2008 — B2; filed under hom-food-preservation)
PART (a) [20 marks]
  Q: Discuss (i) the nutritional significance and (ii) the contribution to the diet of fruit and vegetables.
  A: Fruit and vegetables supply antioxidant vitamins A, C and E; pulses supply protein; green vegetables supply calcium and iron; they generally contain little fat. Frozen produce loses few nutrients, while tinned fruit can be high in sugar. They are useful in low-calorie and low-cholesterol diets, economical, versatile and available in a wide variety, with processed vegetables available all year.
PART (b) [15 marks]
  Q: Suggest one method of food preservation which could be used to preserve a surplus of home-grown fruit or vegetables. Explain the underlying principle of the method of preservation you have selected.
  A: Jam-making: use the correct proportion of sugar and high temperature to destroy enzymes and micro-organisms; the concentrated sugar solution preserves by osmosis, pectin and acid form the set, and sealing prevents re-entry of micro-organisms. Other suitable methods include freezing, chutney-making and drying.
PART (c) [15 marks]
  Q: Write an informative note on food irradiation.
  A: Food irradiation is a physical treatment in which food is exposed to a defined dose of ionising radiation. It destroys food-poisoning bacteria, controls mould on soft fruit, delays ripening and prevents sprouting of potatoes and other vegetables. It has little effect on colour, flavour and texture and reduces the need for chemical preservatives, though vitamins may be destroyed. Irradiated foods in the EU must be labelled; the Radura symbol is the international icon.

QUESTION homeeconomics-pp-2008-HI-sectionb-foodsafety-B3 (LC Home Economics Higher 2008 — B3; filed under hom-food-safety)
PART (a) [16 marks]
  Q: Discuss the importance of temperature control during the storage and cooking/reheating of food in order to minimise the risk of food poisoning.
  A: Store refrigerated food at 0–5°C and frozen food at −18°C (fast-freeze at −25°C); cool food before chilling, never refreeze thawed food and defrost completely in the fridge. Cook meat and poultry thoroughly using correct times and temperatures; heat high-risk foods such as stews and gravies to 100°C, serve immediately, keep hot food above 65°C, avoid the 6–63°C danger zone, consume within 1½ hours and reheat rapidly above 100°C.
PART (b) [24 marks]
  Q: Describe the stages in a basic Hazard Analysis and Critical Control Point (HACCP) system for making a hot meat dish. Refer to (i) possible hazards and (ii) the corresponding control measures to be implemented.
  A: Purchase—hazards: damaged goods, bacterial/chemical contamination and pests; controls: use a reputable shop, separate raw and cooked foods, inspect packaging/dates/temperatures and pest damage, and transport perishables in refrigerator bags. Storage—hazards: bacterial multiplication and cross-contamination; controls: chill below 5°C, freeze below −18°C and check vegetables for mould. Preparation—hazards: multiplication and cross-contamination; controls: wash hands with antibacterial soap, use colour-coded boards and wash equipment in hot water and detergent. Cooking—hazard: surviving bacteria; control: core temperature 74°C for two minutes. Serving/hot holding—hazards: cross-contamination and growth of survivors; controls: clean utensils, hold above 63°C for no more than 1½ hours, reheat once only and cover/store correctly before reheating.
PART (c) [10 marks]
  Q: Outline the role of the environmental health officer in relation to food safety.
  A: The environmental health officer registers new food premises, inspects premises such as hospital kitchens, restaurants, shops and manufacturers, and handles complaints about hygiene standards.

PART 2(a)(iii) [6 marks]
  Q: Name and describe a fabric performance test of your choice.
  A: Crease-recovery test. Cut equal-sized samples of each fabric with the grain running in the same direction and condition them in the same room. Fold each sample exactly in half, place it under the same measured weight for the same period, for example five minutes, and then remove the load. Open each specimen without stretching it and allow the same recovery time. Measure the angle between the two sides with a protractor, or measure the height to which the upper layer recovers. Repeat the test and calculate an average. The fabric that opens to the largest angle, closest to 180°, has the best crease recovery and will keep a neater appearance in an office uniform. Keep sample size, weight, folding direction, loading time, recovery time, temperature and humidity constant so the comparison is fair.
PART 2(b)(i) [9 marks]
  Q: Differentiate between regenerated fibres and synthetic fibres and give one example of each.
  A: Regenerated fibres are made from cellulose that is pulped, chemically treated and forced through nozzles, e.g. rayon, viscose or acetate. Synthetic fibres are made from chemicals such as coal or petroleum, e.g. nylon, polyester, acrylic or PVC.
PART 2(b)(ii) [6 marks]
  Q: Write a note on each of the following: blended fabric; filament modification.
  A: A blended fabric combines fibres to produce different qualities, e.g. cotton/polyester creases less than pure cotton and linen/cotton is cheaper than pure linen. Filament modification changes/improves fabric properties through techniques such as blending, crimping or twisting; polyester can be bulked into Crimplene, cotton mercerised for lustre/strength/dye affinity, or brushed for softness, and finishes can add flame, stain or water resistance.

QUESTION homeeconomics-pp-2008-HI-sectionc-mealplanning-C3 (LC Home Economics Higher 2008 — C3; filed under hom-education)
PART 3(a)(i) [24 marks]
  Q: Discuss the purpose of education in relation to the physical, emotional, moral and intellectual development of the child.
  A: Physical: education develops manual dexterity, hand-eye coordination and the body through practical subjects, PE, games and dance. Emotional: interaction builds empathy and teaches appropriate emotional response and support for others. Moral: school life, ethos, SPHE and religious education develop values and morality.   Q: Comment on how socio-economic status impacts on equality of opportunity in education.

Lower socio-economic groups may have fewer opportunities, higher early-school-leaving and illiteracy rates, lower-stream placement and grades, less parental help and lower teacher/parent expectations.
PART 3(a)(iii) [11 marks]
  Q: Name and give an account of one national initiative that improves access to education.
  A: Early Start is a Department of Education-funded, managed and evaluated community pre-school initiative for disadvantaged three- to four-year-olds. It aims to give a positive educational start, prevent later school failure and improve overall development; primary teachers, childcare assistants and parents deliver language, cognitive, social and personal development. Other accepted initiatives include JCSP, LCA, NEWB, free fees/maintenance grants, Home School Community Liaison, VTOS, Youthreach, learning/resource support, SNAs and subsidised school transport.
PART 3(b)(i) [20 marks]
  Q: In relation to the statement discuss the extent and distribution of poverty in Ireland today. In your answer include reference to the cycle of deprivation in families.
  A: Poverty fell from the 1960s/70s, rose in the 1980s recession and fell again with the 1990s upturn, but high living and housing costs have increased the number below the poverty line. About 20% of Irish households and one in four children live below it; Ireland had the fourth-highest poverty rate in Europe and highest child-poverty rate. At-risk groups include the elderly, disabled, unemployed, low-paid workers, lone parents, refugees, ethnic minorities, homeless people, Travellers and small rural farmers.   Q: Name and give details of two government schemes that reduce expenditure for low-income families.
  A: Examples include rent allowance; back-to-school clothing and footwear allowance; free electricity, telephone rental or TV licence; fuel allowance; school-books grants; school meals; medical cards; and after-school services.
PART 3(c)(i) [12 marks]
  Q: Summarise the factors that can affect a family's requirements for childcare.
  A: Whether both parents or a lone parent work full- or part-time; availability of grandparents/family; cost; need for after-school and school-holiday care; opening hours; proximity to home; and the child's special needs.
PART 3(c)(ii) [18 marks]
  Q: Evaluate two types of child care options.
  A: A crèche and a childminder are two suitable options.

A crèche provides purpose-built indoor and outdoor space, age-appropriate toys and a planned programme of play, rest and learning. Children mix with a group, which promotes language, sharing, independence and social development. Trained staff and agreed adult-to-child ratios provide supervision, and registered services should operate clear hygiene and safety procedures. It may also offer reliable full-day care and cover when one staff member is absent. However, fixed opening hours may not suit shift work, a child receives less individual attention than at home, infection can spread readily through a group, and fees may be high. A very young or shy child may find the busy setting tiring.

A childminder usually cares for fewer children in the minder's or child's home. This gives a homely atmosphere, close individual attention and greater flexibility about early starts, school collection, meals and family routines. Siblings can remain together, and fewer contacts may reduce exposure to infection. The disadvantages are that the quality and qualifications of minders vary, facilities and activities may be more limited, and the child has fewer opportunities for group socialisation. Care may also collapse if the minder is ill or takes leave, unless backup is arranged.

The best choice depends on the child's age, temperament and special needs, cost, location, hours, inspection/registration, staff qualifications, hygiene, safety and emergency arrangements. Parents should visit, check references and observe how warmly adults interact with children before deciding.
