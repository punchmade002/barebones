TARGET chem1-s2: 2. Science as a global enterprise and researching an issue
[chem1-s2.0.b] Science is not done by lone individuals. It is a worldwide effort in which results are shared, so that every scientist can build on what others have found.
[chem1-s2.0.0] Scientists work in universities, industry and government laboratories around the world, sharing results and building on each other's work, so new research starts from what is already known.
[chem1-s2.0.1] Large problems such as climate change, new medicines and clean energy are tackled by international collaborations, because countries working together can share data, equipment and funding.
[chem1-s2.0.2] Scientists publish in journals (magazines that print research reports) and present at conferences (meetings where scientists talk about their work) so that results are available to everyone and can be checked.
[chem1-s2.1.b] A scientific claim is only worth believing if others can look at the evidence behind it. This is why science depends on reports that are complete and clear.
[chem1-s2.1.0] Scientific claims must be supported by evidence that others can examine, so data, methods and conclusions are published together and a reader can judge whether the conclusion follows.
[chem1-s2.1.1] A report must describe the method in enough detail for another scientist to repeat it exactly, because a result can only be checked if the experiment can be redone.
[chem1-s2.1.2] Clear communication uses precise scientific terms, SI units (the international system of units), correct significant figures (the number of meaningful digits in a measurement), labelled tables and graphs, and cited sources (credited references), so the reader cannot misread the results.
[chem1-s2.2.b] Conventions are agreed rules that all scientists use, so that results mean the same everywhere. Without them, a chemist in one country could not be sure what a chemist in another country meant.
[chem1-s2.2.0] IUPAC (the International Union of Pure and Applied Chemistry) sets the rules for naming compounds, so that each compound has one systematic name (a name built by the agreed rules), e.g. but-1-ene, and it agrees chemical symbols and atomic masses.
[chem1-s2.2.1] The SI (Système International) system of units has base units (the basic units from which others are built), including the metre (m), kilogram (kg), second (s), kelvin (K) and mole (mol).
[chem1-s2.2.2] The SI base units also include the ampere (A) and the candela (cd).
[chem1-s2.2.3] Other shared conventions are chemical symbols and formulae, standard conditions for measurements, and international hazard symbols (pictograms, small warning pictures) on chemicals, so that a danger is recognised in any country.
[chem1-s2.3.b] Before research is published, other experts check it. This step, called peer review, is one of the main ways science keeps its standards high.
[chem1-s2.3.0] Peer review is the evaluation of a scientist's work by other independent experts in the same field (peers) before it is published.
[chem1-s2.3.1] 1. The scientist submits a paper (a written report of the research) to a journal.
[chem1-s2.3.2] 2. The editor, who runs the journal, sends the paper to reviewers.
[chem1-s2.3.3] 3. The reviewers check the method, data, analysis and conclusions for errors and originality (whether the work is new), and whether the conclusions are justified by the data.
[chem1-s2.3.4] 4. The paper is accepted, returned for changes, or rejected.
[chem1-s2.3.5] Peer review filters out poor or flawed work, reduces bias (unfair leaning towards one result) and gives readers confidence in published results.
[chem1-s2.3.6] Peer review does not guarantee that a result is correct; results are checked by others repeating the work.
[chem1-s2.4.b] Two similar words describe two different tests of whether a result can be trusted. The difference is who repeats the experiment and how.
[chem1-s2.4.0] Repeatable: the same experimenter, using the same method and equipment, gets the same or very similar results when the experiment is repeated.
[chem1-s2.4.1] Reproducible: a different experimenter, using different equipment or a different method, gets the same results.
[chem1-s2.4.2] A claim is accepted only when its results are reproducible, because results that no one else can reproduce are not accepted.
[chem1-s2.5.b] Researching an issue works best when you plan it. A clear question and a record of where your information came from make the final work easy to check.
[chem1-s2.5.0] Start with a focused research question about the issue, e.g. “What are the environmental effects of lithium mining?”, so you know exactly what you are looking for.
[chem1-s2.5.1] Identify keywords (the important search words) and search systematically rather than reading whatever appears first.
[chem1-s2.5.2] Record every source used: author, title, date, publisher or website, and the date accessed, so others can find and check it.
[chem1-s2.5.3] Take notes in your own words and keep a research log (a running record of what you searched and found).
[chem1-s2.6.b] Data used in a research project come from two places: your own measurements, or someone else's. Knowing which is which matters when you judge how far to trust them.
[chem1-s2.6.0] Primary data are collected first-hand by the investigator, e.g. by experiment or measurement.
[chem1-s2.6.1] Secondary data are collected by someone else, e.g. published results, government statistics, databases or the results of other class groups.
[chem1-s2.6.2] Secondary data are used to compare with primary data, to check or support conclusions, or where an experiment cannot be done in school.
[chem1-s2.7.b] Sources differ in how much you can trust them. Knowing the kind of source gives you a first idea of how reliable it is likely to be.
[chem1-s2.7.0] Peer-reviewed journal articles (reports checked by other experts before publication) are the most reliable sources but are often technical.
[chem1-s2.7.1] Books and textbooks, and reports from government agencies and scientific bodies, are further kinds of source.
[chem1-s2.7.2] The Environmental Protection Agency (EPA) is Ireland's environmental regulator (the body that monitors and enforces environmental rules).
[chem1-s2.7.3] The EPA publishes reports and data on air and water quality.
[chem1-s2.7.4] News media, websites, blogs and social media are accessible but of variable reliability; they may simplify, sensationalise (make more dramatic than the facts) or omit detail.
[chem1-s2.7.5] Company or campaign-group material may be written to sell a product or promote a view, so it may present only what suits that aim.
[chem1-s2.8.b] Once you have found a source, you must judge how far to rely on it. Asking the same set of questions of every source lets you compare them fairly.
[chem1-s2.8.0] Authority: who wrote the source, and are they qualified and named?
[chem1-s2.8.1] Accuracy and evidence: are the claims supported by data and references, and can they be checked against other sources?
[chem1-s2.8.2] Currency: is the source up to date?
[chem1-s2.8.3] Purpose and bias: is it trying to inform, persuade or sell; who funded it; does it present only one side?
[chem1-s2.8.4] Detail: does it explain methods, sample sizes (how many were measured) and uncertainties (how far the values might be off), or does it give only conclusions? A source may lack the detail needed to judge it.
[chem1-s2.8.5] Corroboration (independent confirmation): a claim supported by several independent, reliable sources is more trustworthy than a claim from one source.
