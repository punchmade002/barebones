TARGET chem17-s1: 1. Rate of reaction and collision theory
[chem17-s1.0.b] Some reactions finish in a flash and others take years. Rate of reaction is the measure chemists use to say exactly how fast a reaction goes, and it is always a change per unit of time.
[chem17-s1.0.0] Rate of reaction is the change in concentration (the amount of substance per litre of solution) of a reactant (a substance used up) or a product (a substance formed) per unit time.
[chem17-s1.0.1] When concentration is measured, rate is given in mol L⁻¹ s⁻¹ or mol L⁻¹ min⁻¹, that is, moles per litre changing each second or each minute.
[chem17-s1.0.2] When another quantity is followed, rate is that quantity per unit time, e.g. cm³ of gas per minute (cm³ min⁻¹) or grams of mass lost per second (g s⁻¹).
[chem17-s1.0.3] Rate can be followed by the disappearance of a reactant or the appearance of a product; the two are linked by the mole ratio (the ratio of the numbers in front of the formulas) in the balanced equation.
[chem17-s1.0.4] For example, in 2H₂O₂(aq) → 2H₂O(l) + O₂(g), H₂O₂ is used up twice as fast, in moles, as O₂ is formed, because the equation shows 2 mol H₂O₂ for every 1 mol O₂.
[chem17-s1.0.5] A fast reaction has a large change in a short time; a slow reaction has a small change over a long time.
[chem17-s1.0.6] For the same amount of change, rate is proportional to 1/time (rate ∝ 1/time), so halving the time taken doubles the rate.
[chem17-s1.0.7] The factors that change the rate are concentration, surface area (particle size), temperature, a catalyst (a substance that changes the rate without being used up) and, for reactions of gases, pressure; the nature of the reactants also matters. There is more on this in “Collision theory”.
[chem17-s1.1.b] To find a rate you need a property that changes as the reaction goes and that you can measure repeatedly. When a reaction gives off a gas, either the gas volume or the mass lost can be tracked.
[chem17-s1.1.0] Measure the volume of gas produced at regular time intervals, collected in a gas syringe (a calibrated syringe that the gas pushes out) or over water in an inverted graduated cylinder, e.g. oxygen from hydrogen peroxide.
[chem17-s1.1.1] When volume is measured, collect the gas in a graduated cylinder or gas syringe, not a gas jar, because a gas jar has no scale to read the volume from.
[chem17-s1.1.2] Diagram: a flask joined by a delivery tube to a gas syringe, or to an inverted graduated cylinder full of water standing in a trough; label the flask, reactants, delivery tube and the gas collector.
[chem17-s1.1.3] Measure the loss in mass as a gas escapes, with the flask on an electronic balance, because the escaping gas carries mass away from the flask.
[chem17-s1.1.4] A cotton-wool plug in the flask neck lets the gas out but stops acid spray being lost, which would give a false mass loss.
[chem17-s1.1.5] Equation for the mass-loss method: CaCO₃(s) + 2HCl(aq) → CaCl₂(aq) + H₂O(l) + CO₂(g); the carbon dioxide escapes.
[chem17-s1.1.6] Diagram: a conical flask of calcium carbonate and hydrochloric acid, with a cotton-wool plug in its neck, standing on an electronic balance; label the flask, plug, balance and the CO₂ escaping.
[chem17-s1.2.b] Not every reaction gives off a gas. Another way to follow a reaction is to time how long a fixed amount of change takes, or to watch a colour change.
[chem17-s1.2.0] Measure the time for a fixed amount of reaction to happen, e.g. the time for a sulfur precipitate (a solid formed in a solution) to hide a cross under the flask.
[chem17-s1.2.1] Equation for the cross method: Na₂S₂O₃(aq) + 2HCl(aq) → 2NaCl(aq) + SO₂(g) + S(s) + H₂O(l).
[chem17-s1.2.2] The sulfur precipitate is pale yellow; in the cross method, 1/time is used as the rate, because the same amount of sulfur forms to hide the cross in every run, so a shorter time means a faster rate.
[chem17-s1.2.3] Diagram: a conical flask of sodium thiosulfate solution standing on a paper marked with a cross, viewed from above; label the flask, the solution and the cross.
[chem17-s1.2.4] Follow a colour change, by eye or with a light sensor, when a reactant or product is coloured.
[chem17-s1.2.5] The method chosen must measure a property that changes as the reaction proceeds and can be measured quickly without disturbing the reaction.
[chem17-s1.3.b] A reaction does not run at one steady speed. Its rate falls as it goes, and the shape of a graph shows this.
[chem17-s1.3.0] The rate is greatest at the start, when the concentration of reactants is highest, so there are the most particles available to react.
[chem17-s1.3.1] As the reaction proceeds, reactants are used up, their concentration falls, and the rate decreases.
[chem17-s1.3.2] The reaction stops when the limiting reactant (the reactant that runs out first) is used up; the rate is then zero.
[chem17-s1.3.3] On a graph of product volume against time, the curve is steepest at the start, becomes less steep, and levels off to a horizontal line when the reaction is complete.
[chem17-s1.3.4] The gradient (steepness) of the curve at any point is the rate at that time, so a steep curve means fast and a flat line means no reaction.
[chem17-s1.3.5] On a graph of reactant concentration against time, the curve falls steeply at first and levels off; its gradient is negative, but the rate is quoted as a positive number.
[chem17-s1.3.6] The final amount of product depends only on the amount of limiting reactant, not on how fast the reaction went.
[chem17-s1.3.7] Diagram: graph of volume of gas (y-axis) against time (x-axis), a curve rising steeply from the origin, flattening, and becoming horizontal at the final volume; label “reaction fastest”, “slowing down” and “reaction complete”.
[chem17-s1.4.b] Controlling how fast a reaction goes is useful in industry, in the kitchen and in the body.
[chem17-s1.4.0] Industry wants fast reactions, so that products are made quickly and economically.
[chem17-s1.4.1] Slow reactions are also useful: food is kept in a fridge because the reactions that spoil it are slower at low temperature.
[chem17-s1.4.2] The rates of reactions in the body are controlled by enzymes (biological catalysts). There is more on this in “Catalysis and energy profiles”.
[chem17-s1.5.b] Collision theory explains what has to happen at the level of particles for a reaction to take place. It is the idea behind every factor that changes rate.
[chem17-s1.5.0] For a reaction to happen, the reacting particles (atoms, molecules or ions) must collide.
[chem17-s1.5.1] Most collisions do not lead to reaction: the particles just bounce apart.
[chem17-s1.5.2] A collision leads to reaction only if the particles collide with at least a minimum amount of energy, the activation energy, so that bonds can begin to break.
[chem17-s1.5.3] Activation energy (Ea) is the minimum energy that colliding particles must have for a reaction to occur.
[chem17-s1.5.4] An effective collision is one that results in the formation of products.
[chem17-s1.5.5] The rate of a reaction depends on the number of effective collisions per second: anything that increases the number of collisions per second, or the fraction of collisions with energy at or above Ea, increases the rate.
[chem17-s1.6.b] Reactions range from instantaneous to almost undetectable. The syllabus asks for examples of both.
[chem17-s1.6.0] Fast: mixing silver nitrate and sodium chloride solutions gives a white precipitate of silver chloride instantly.
[chem17-s1.6.1] Ionic equation (showing only the ions that react) for that reaction: Ag⁺(aq) + Cl⁻(aq) → AgCl(s).
[chem17-s1.6.2] Other fast reactions: neutralisation of an acid by an alkali, the explosion of a hydrogen–oxygen mixture when ignited, and burning magnesium.
[chem17-s1.6.3] Slow: rusting of iron takes days to years.
[chem17-s1.6.4] Fermentation of glucose by yeast (the breakdown of glucose by yeast to make ethanol) takes days.
[chem17-s1.6.5] A mixture of hydrogen and oxygen at room temperature, without a spark or catalyst, shows no detectable reaction, even though it explodes when ignited.
[chem17-s1.7.b] Whether a reaction is fast or slow depends on its activation energy, which in turn depends on whether bonds must be broken first.
[chem17-s1.7.0] Reactions between oppositely charged ions in solution are usually very fast: the ions are already free and attract each other, so no bonds need to be broken before they combine.
[chem17-s1.7.1] Because no bonds need to be broken, the activation energy for reactions between oppositely charged ions is very low.
[chem17-s1.7.2] Reactions between covalent molecules are usually slower, because covalent bonds (bonds formed by sharing electrons) must be broken before new bonds can form, so the activation energy is higher.
[chem17-s1.7.3] A reaction with a high activation energy is slow at room temperature because few collisions have enough energy; a reaction with a low activation energy is fast.
[chem17-s1.8.b] Concentration is the first factor that collision theory explains. More particles in the same space means more chances to collide.
[chem17-s1.8.0] Increasing the concentration of a reactant in solution increases the number of particles per unit volume.
[chem17-s1.8.1] The particles collide more often, so there are more collisions, and more effective collisions, per second, and the rate increases.
[chem17-s1.8.2] In many reactions the rate is directly proportional to the concentration of a reactant: a graph of rate against concentration is a straight line through the origin, as shown by the thiosulfate investigation in “Investigation: factors affecting rate”.
[chem17-s1.9.b] For a solid, only the outside can react. Cutting a solid into smaller pieces exposes more of it without adding any more substance.
[chem17-s1.9.0] Only particles at the surface of a solid can collide with the other reactant, so particles buried inside a lump cannot react until the surface is used up.
[chem17-s1.9.1] Breaking a solid into smaller pieces or a powder increases the surface area exposed for the same mass, so there are more collisions per second and the rate increases.
[chem17-s1.9.2] For example, powdered calcium carbonate reacts faster with hydrochloric acid than the same mass of marble chips.
[chem17-s1.9.3] Dust explosions: a very fine combustible powder spread in air has an enormous surface area and can burn so fast that it explodes.
[chem17-s1.9.4] Dust explosions are a risk in flour mills, grain silos and coal mines.
[chem17-s1.9.5] A dust explosion needs a combustible dust, finely divided and dry, dispersed in air (oxygen), in an enclosed space, with a source of ignition such as a spark or flame.
[chem17-s1.10.b] Raising the temperature speeds up most reactions. Collision theory gives two reasons, and one matters far more than the other.
[chem17-s1.10.0] Raising the temperature increases the rate of almost all reactions.
[chem17-s1.10.1] For many reactions near room temperature the rate roughly doubles for each 10 °C rise; this is an approximate rule, not a law.
[chem17-s1.10.2] Reason 1: at a higher temperature particles have more kinetic energy (energy of movement) and move faster, so they collide more often.
[chem17-s1.10.3] Reason 2: a much greater fraction of the collisions have energy equal to or greater than the activation energy, so a greater fraction of collisions are effective.
[chem17-s1.10.4] Reason 2 is the more important: a 10 °C rise increases the number of collisions per second only slightly, but greatly increases the number of particles with energy at or above Ea.
[chem17-s1.10.5] Raising the temperature does not change the activation energy; it changes how many particles have enough energy to overcome it.
[chem17-s1.11.b] Particles in a sample do not all have the same energy. A graph of how the energies are spread shows why a small temperature rise has such a large effect.
[chem17-s1.11.0] On a graph of number of particles against kinetic energy (a Maxwell–Boltzmann distribution), the curve is a skewed hump starting at the origin with a long tail to the right.
[chem17-s1.11.1] The area under the curve to the right of Ea is the number of particles with enough energy to react.
[chem17-s1.11.2] At a higher temperature the curve is flatter, its peak moves to the right, and the area to the right of Ea is much larger, so many more collisions are effective.
[chem17-s1.11.3] Diagram: two energy-distribution curves on the same axes at T₁ and a higher T₂, with Ea marked on the energy axis and the area beyond Ea shaded for each.
[chem17-s1.11.4] Everyday example: food keeps longer in a fridge or freezer, where the reactions that spoil it are slower because fewer particles have energy at or above Ea. There is more on this in “Rate of reaction”.
[chem17-s1.12.b] A catalyst speeds up a reaction without raising the temperature. It does this by changing the route the reaction takes.
[chem17-s1.12.0] A catalyst is a substance that alters the rate of a chemical reaction but is itself chemically unchanged at the end of the reaction.
[chem17-s1.12.1] A catalyst provides an alternative pathway for the reaction with a lower activation energy, so a greater fraction of collisions have energy at or above Ea and are effective, and the rate increases.
[chem17-s1.12.2] The catalyst is not used up, so a small amount can speed up the reaction again and again. There is more on types of catalysis, enzymes and energy profiles in “Catalysis and energy profiles”.
[chem17-s1.13.b] Pressure is the last factor, and it only matters when gases are reacting.
[chem17-s1.13.0] Increasing the pressure of a gas squeezes the same number of molecules into a smaller volume, which is the same as increasing its concentration.
[chem17-s1.13.1] The molecules collide more often, so the rate increases.
[chem17-s1.13.2] Pressure affects only reactions in which one or more reactants are gases; it has almost no effect on reactions in solution or between solids.
[chem17-s1.14.b] The simple collision theory leaves out some things. Orientation, surfaces that become full, and poisoned catalysts all explain why real reactions can behave differently.
[chem17-s1.14.0] Collisions must be correctly oriented: even with enough energy, a collision is effective only if the reacting parts of the molecules face each other.
[chem17-s1.14.1] Orientation is why only a small fraction of energetic collisions lead to reaction, especially for large molecules.
[chem17-s1.14.2] Concentration may not always affect the rate: when a reaction takes place on a surface (a solid reactant or a solid catalyst) and every site on the surface is occupied, adding more of the other reactant cannot increase the number of reacting collisions, so the rate stays the same.
[chem17-s1.14.3] For example, an enzyme-catalysed reaction speeds up as substrate (the substance the enzyme acts on) concentration rises, but levels off when all the enzyme's active sites (the places where the substrate binds) are occupied; further substrate has no effect.
[chem17-s1.14.4] A catalyst can become saturated: it has a limited number of active sites, and when they are all in use, increasing the reactant concentration no longer increases the rate.
[chem17-s1.14.5] A catalyst can be poisoned: a catalyst poison binds strongly to the active sites and blocks them, so the catalyst stops working.
[chem17-s1.14.6] The catalysts in a car's catalytic converter (platinum, palladium and rhodium) are poisoned by lead compounds, one reason leaded petrol was phased out where converters are used, and by sulfur and phosphorus compounds.

CANDIDATES chem9-s1: 1. The kinetic theory and its evidence
[chem9-s1.0.b] Everything around you, from air to iron, is matter. Chemistry starts from one idea: matter is built from particles too small to see, and how those particles are arranged decides whether something is a solid, a liquid or a gas.
[chem9-s1.0.0] All matter is made of tiny particles (atoms, molecules or ions) that are constantly moving, with spaces between them.
[chem9-s1.0.1] Solid: the particles are packed closely in a fixed, regular arrangement and vibrate about fixed positions, so a solid keeps its shape.
[chem9-s1.0.2] Liquid: the particles are close together but can move past each other, so a liquid flows and takes the shape of its container.
[chem9-s1.0.3] Gas: the particles are far apart and move rapidly and randomly in all directions, so a gas spreads out to fill any space.
[chem9-s1.0.4] Diagram: three boxes showing the particle arrangement in a solid, a liquid and a gas.
[chem9-s1.1.b] A pure substance is made of only one kind of particle, so it has fixed properties. There are two kinds of pure substance: elements and compounds.
[chem9-s1.1.0] A pure substance contains only one kind of particle (one element or one compound) and has fixed properties, such as a sharp melting point and boiling point (a single exact temperature, not a range).
[chem9-s1.1.1] An element is a substance made of only one kind of atom; it cannot be split into simpler substances by chemical means (that is, by a chemical reaction), e.g. iron (Fe) and oxygen (O₂).
[chem9-s1.1.2] A compound is a substance made of two or more different elements chemically combined (joined by bonds) in a fixed ratio, e.g. water (H₂O) and sodium chloride (NaCl).
[chem9-s1.1.3] A compound has properties that differ from those of its elements, because the elements are bonded together, so it can be separated into them only by a chemical reaction.
[chem9-s1.1.4] A molecule (a group of atoms joined together) can be of an element (O₂, N₂, Cl₂), where all the atoms are the same, or of a compound (H₂O, CO₂), where the atoms are different.
[chem9-s1.2.b] A mixture is two or more substances that are not chemically combined, so each keeps its own properties. Mixtures are grouped by whether they look the same all the way through.
[chem9-s1.2.0] A mixture can have a variable composition (the amounts of each substance can change), and its components keep their own properties.
[chem9-s1.2.1] A mixture can be separated by physical methods (ones that need no chemical reaction), and it has no sharp melting or boiling point: it melts or boils over a range of temperatures. There is more on this in Using the kinetic theory: separation techniques.
[chem9-s1.2.2] A homogeneous mixture has a uniform composition throughout and a single phase (one visible part), e.g. salt solution, air and an alloy (a mixture of metals).
[chem9-s1.2.3] A heterogeneous mixture is non-uniform, with visibly different parts or phases, e.g. sand and water, oil and water, and granite.
[chem9-s1.3.b] Because a pure substance has a fixed melting point and a mixture does not, melting point can be used to check whether a sample is pure.
[chem9-s1.3.0] A pure substance has a sharp melting point that matches the data-book value (the accepted figure listed in reference tables).
[chem9-s1.3.1] Impurities lower the melting point and make the substance melt over a range of temperatures, so a low or spread-out melting point shows the sample is not pure.
[chem9-s1.4.b] Compounds and mixtures both contain more than one element, so they are easy to confuse. The difference is whether the substances are chemically combined.
[chem9-s1.4.0] A compound has a fixed composition; a mixture has a variable composition.
[chem9-s1.4.1] The elements in a compound are chemically combined; the substances in a mixture are not.
[chem9-s1.4.2] A compound has new properties because its elements are chemically combined; a mixture's components keep their own properties because they are not.
[chem9-s1.4.3] A compound can be separated only chemically, because its elements are bonded; a mixture can be separated physically, because nothing is bonded.
[chem9-s1.4.4] Energy is usually taken in or given out when a compound forms, whereas there is little energy change when a mixture is made.
[chem9-s1.4.5] Diagram: particle diagrams of an element (single atoms and diatomic molecules, which are molecules of two atoms), a compound (identical molecules, each made of two kinds of atom) and a mixture (two kinds of particle mixed together).
[chem9-s1.5.b] The kinetic theory states that matter is particles in motion. We cannot see the particles, so the theory rests on effects we can see.
[chem9-s1.5.0] The kinetic theory of matter states that all matter is made of particles that are in constant, random motion.
[chem9-s1.5.1] The particles have kinetic energy (energy of movement), so the higher the temperature, the faster they move.
[chem9-s1.5.2] The average kinetic energy of the particles is proportional to the temperature in kelvin (the temperature scale that starts at absolute zero), so doubling the kelvin temperature doubles the average kinetic energy.
[chem9-s1.5.3] The evidence for the theory comes from effects we can see that are caused by particles we cannot see.
[chem9-s1.6.b] Brownian motion is the continuous, random, zig-zag movement of small visible particles suspended in a gas or liquid. It shows that the invisible particles around them are moving.
[chem9-s1.6.0] Robert Brown, a botanist (a scientist who studies plants), first observed Brownian motion in 1827, using pollen grains in water.
[chem9-s1.6.1] Aim: to observe the motion of smoke particles in air as evidence for the kinetic theory.
[chem9-s1.6.2] Step 1: Fill a small glass cell with smoke and cover it.
[chem9-s1.6.3] Step 2: Light the cell from the side, so the smoke particles scatter light and show up.
[chem9-s1.6.4] Step 3: View the cell through a microscope.
[chem9-s1.6.5] Observation: the smoke particles appear as bright specks that jiggle about randomly and never stop.
[chem9-s1.7.b] The jiggling of the smoke is the evidence. It can be explained only if the air around the smoke is made of moving particles.
[chem9-s1.7.0] Explanation: the smoke particles are bombarded unevenly from all sides by fast-moving, invisible air molecules; at any instant more molecules hit one side than the other, so the particle moves.
[chem9-s1.7.1] The smoke particles are visible solid particles, not air molecules; they are moved by the invisible air molecules.
[chem9-s1.7.2] Conclusion: the air is made of particles in constant, random motion, and the motion becomes more vigorous when the temperature is raised.
[chem9-s1.7.3] Diagram: a smoke cell lit from the side under a microscope, and the zig-zag path of one smoke particle.
[chem9-s1.8.b] Diffusion is the spreading out of particles from a region of high concentration to a region of low concentration, until they are evenly mixed, as a result of their random motion. Gases show it clearly.
[chem9-s1.8.0] Diffusion needs no stirring or currents, because it results from the random motion of the particles alone.
[chem9-s1.8.1] Bromine experiment: a few drops of liquid bromine are placed in a gas jar, giving brown vapour that spreads upwards through a second jar of air placed on top.
[chem9-s1.8.2] The bromine jars are evenly coloured when the vapour has diffused through both jars, which shows the particles have spread out until evenly mixed.
[chem9-s1.8.3] Safety for bromine: bromine is toxic and corrosive, so use a fume cupboard (a ventilated cabinet that removes fumes) and gloves.
[chem9-s1.9.b] This experiment shows that gases diffuse and that lighter gas particles diffuse faster than heavier ones. Two gases start at opposite ends of a tube and meet where they react.
[chem9-s1.9.0] Method: cotton-wool plugs soaked in concentrated ammonia and concentrated hydrochloric acid are placed at opposite ends of a long glass tube, which is sealed with stoppers.
[chem9-s1.9.1] Observation: after a few minutes a white ring of ammonium chloride forms inside the tube where the two gases meet: NH₃(g) + HCl(g) → NH₄Cl(s).
[chem9-s1.9.2] The ring forms nearer the hydrochloric acid end of the tube, because the lighter ammonia travels further.
[chem9-s1.9.3] Explanation: ammonia molecules (Mr 17, relative molecular mass) are lighter than hydrogen chloride molecules (Mr 36.5), so at the same temperature they diffuse faster and travel further before they meet.
[chem9-s1.9.4] Safety: concentrated ammonia and hydrochloric acid give off irritant, corrosive fumes, so use a fume cupboard, goggles, gloves and tongs to handle the plugs.
[chem9-s1.9.5] Diagram: the long tube with the ammonia plug at one end, the hydrochloric acid plug at the other, and the white ring closer to the hydrochloric acid end.
[chem9-s1.10.b] Diffusion is not limited to gases. It also happens in liquids, but far more slowly.
[chem9-s1.10.0] Diffusion also happens in liquids, e.g. a crystal of potassium manganate(VII) slowly colours still water purple.
[chem9-s1.10.1] Diffusion is much slower in liquids than in gases, because the particles are closer together and collide more often.

CANDIDATES chem8-s2: 2. Solutions and concentration
[chem8-s2.0.b] Much of chemistry happens in solution, so you need exact words for the parts of one. Every later idea in this subsection, from concentration to dilution, is built on them.
[chem8-s2.0.0] A solution is a uniform (homogeneous) mixture of a solute dissolved in a solvent. Uniform means it looks and behaves the same throughout, with no layers or visible particles.
[chem8-s2.0.1] The solute is the substance that dissolves, and the solvent is the substance it dissolves in. When the solvent is water the solution is called an aqueous solution.
[chem8-s2.1.b] A solvent can only hold so much solute at a given temperature. The names unsaturated, saturated and supersaturated describe how close a solution is to that limit, or beyond it.
[chem8-s2.1.0] An unsaturated solution can still dissolve more solute at that temperature, so extra solute added to it simply dissolves.
[chem8-s2.1.1] A saturated solution contains the maximum amount of solute that can dissolve at that temperature, with undissolved solid present. Any more solute added just sits at the bottom.
[chem8-s2.1.2] A supersaturated solution contains more dissolved solute than a saturated solution holds at that temperature. It is unstable, which means it is easily upset and will not stay as it is.
[chem8-s2.1.3] A supersaturated solution is made by cooling a hot saturated solution carefully. A solid is usually more soluble when hot, so the cooled solution is left holding more than it normally could.
[chem8-s2.1.4] Adding a small seed crystal (a tiny crystal of the solute) to a supersaturated solution makes the excess solute crystallise suddenly, because the crystal gives the dissolved solute a surface to grow on.
[chem8-s2.1.5] Examples of solutes that form supersaturated solutions: sodium thiosulfate and sodium ethanoate.
[chem8-s2.2.b] Saying a solution is strong or weak is not precise enough for chemistry. Concentration gives the amount of solute in a fixed volume, so it can be calculated with and compared.
[chem8-s2.2.0] Concentration is the amount of solute dissolved in a given volume of solution, usually in mol L⁻¹ (molarity, M, meaning moles of solute per litre) or g L⁻¹ (grams of solute per litre).
[chem8-s2.2.1] Concentration (mol L⁻¹) = moles of solute ÷ volume of solution in litres, so c = n ÷ V and n = c × V. Remember that the volume is that of the whole solution, not just the solvent.
[chem8-s2.2.2] Model: the same number of solute particles in a smaller volume gives a higher concentration, because the particles are packed more closely. Diluting spreads the same particles through a larger volume.
[chem8-s2.2.3] A standard solution is a solution whose concentration is accurately known. It is the starting point for finding the concentrations of other solutions.
[chem8-s2.3.b] Here are two calculations. One finds the concentration from a mass of solute, and the other works backwards from a concentration to the mass to weigh out. Convert cm³ to litres first by dividing by 1000.
[chem8-s2.3.0] Finding a concentration: 5.30 g of anhydrous sodium carbonate (Na₂CO₃, M = 106 g mol⁻¹) is dissolved. Anhydrous means it has no water of crystallisation. Moles: n = 5.30 ÷ 106 = 0.0500 mol.
[chem8-s2.3.1] The 0.0500 mol is made up to 500 cm³ (0.500 L). Then c = 0.0500 ÷ 0.500 = 0.100 mol L⁻¹.
[chem8-s2.3.2] Finding a mass needed: for 250 cm³ of 0.100 M sodium hydroxide (M = 40 g mol⁻¹), first find the moles from n = c × V = 0.100 × 0.250 = 0.0250 mol.
[chem8-s2.3.3] Then change moles to mass by multiplying by the molar mass (the mass of one mole): m = 0.0250 × 40 = 1.00 g.
[chem8-s2.4.b] To make a standard solution by weighing, you must trust the mass on the balance to be the true amount of the substance. Only certain substances are reliable enough, and they are called primary standards.
[chem8-s2.4.0] A primary standard is a substance that can be weighed out accurately to make a standard solution directly.
[chem8-s2.4.1] A primary standard is available in a very pure state, so the mass weighed is all the substance and not impurities.
[chem8-s2.4.2] A primary standard is stable in air: it does not absorb water or carbon dioxide, or react with oxygen. If it did, its mass would change while being weighed.
[chem8-s2.4.3] A primary standard has a known formula, so its molar mass and the moles weighed out can be calculated, and it dissolves readily in water.
[chem8-s2.4.4] A primary standard preferably has a high molar mass, which reduces weighing errors. A larger mass is needed for each mole, so a small error on the balance is a smaller fraction of the whole.
[chem8-s2.5.b] Which substance you choose depends on the type of titration (a method that reacts measured volumes of two solutions to find an unknown concentration). Some familiar laboratory chemicals fail the tests for a primary standard.
[chem8-s2.5.0] Anhydrous sodium carbonate is a primary standard used in acid–base titrations (reactions between an acid and a base).
[chem8-s2.5.1] Ammonium iron(II) sulfate, (NH₄)₂Fe(SO₄)₂·6H₂O, dissolved in dilute sulfuric acid, is a primary standard for redox titrations (reactions involving the transfer of electrons).
[chem8-s2.5.2] Sodium hydroxide is not a primary standard because it absorbs water and carbon dioxide from the air, so its mass is not the true mass of sodium hydroxide.
[chem8-s2.5.3] Hydrochloric acid is not a primary standard because its concentration changes as HCl evaporates.
[chem8-s2.5.4] Solutions of sodium hydroxide and hydrochloric acid are standardised, meaning their exact concentration is found, by titration against a primary standard.
[chem8-s2.6.b] Making a standard solution from a weighed primary standard is an investigation with a fixed method. The aim is a solution of accurately known concentration, here 250 cm³ in a volumetric flask (a flask with a single mark showing an exact volume).
[chem8-s2.6.0] 1. Weigh accurately (to 0.01 g or better) the calculated mass of the primary standard on a clock glass or weighing boat.
[chem8-s2.6.1] 2. Transfer the solid to a beaker, and rinse the clock glass into the beaker with deionised water from a wash bottle, so that none of the weighed solid is left behind.
[chem8-s2.6.2] 3. Dissolve the solid in a small volume of deionised water, stirring with a glass rod.
[chem8-s2.6.3] 4. Pour the solution through a funnel into a volumetric flask of the required volume, e.g. 250 cm³.
[chem8-s2.6.4] 5. Rinse the beaker, glass rod and funnel into the flask with deionised water so that no solute is lost.
[chem8-s2.6.5] 6. Add deionised water until the flask is near the mark, then add the last drops with a dropper until the bottom of the meniscus (the curved surface of the liquid) sits on the graduation mark, viewed at eye level.
[chem8-s2.6.6] 7. Stopper the flask and invert it about 20 times so the solution is uniform.
[chem8-s2.7.b] The choice of glassware decides how accurate the final concentration is. The diagrams below show the apparatus used in the method.
[chem8-s2.7.0] Precision and accuracy: the volumetric flask holds an accurately known volume, whereas a beaker or measuring cylinder is not accurate enough.
[chem8-s2.7.1] Diagram: a volumetric flask with its graduation mark, and the meniscus shown at eye level.
[chem8-s2.7.2] Diagram: a funnel in the neck of the volumetric flask during the transfer.
[chem8-s2.7.3] Diagram: a beaker with a glass rod stirring the dissolving solid.
[chem8-s2.8.b] A concentrated solution can be turned into a weaker one by adding solvent. The solute is not changed, only spread through more volume, which gives a simple equation to use.
[chem8-s2.8.0] Diluting adds solvent, so the number of moles of solute stays the same. This gives c₁V₁ = c₂V₂, where c₁ and V₁ are the original solution and c₂ and V₂ the diluted one.
[chem8-s2.8.1] Example: to make 250 cm³ of 0.10 M HCl from 1.0 M HCl, V₁ = (0.10 × 250) ÷ 1.0 = 25 cm³.
[chem8-s2.8.2] Pipette 25 cm³ of the 1.0 M acid into a 250 cm³ volumetric flask and make up to the mark with water.
[chem8-s2.8.3] Use a pipette (a glass tube that measures one exact volume), with a pipette filler to draw up the liquid, for the volume of the original solution, and a volumetric flask for the final volume.
[chem8-s2.8.4] Diagram: a pipette with a pipette filler, filled to the graduation mark.
[chem8-s2.9.b] Sometimes a whole range of concentrations is needed, such as for a set of test solutions. Rather than diluting from the stock solution every time, you dilute step by step.
[chem8-s2.9.0] Serial dilution is a series of dilutions, each using the previous solution, usually by the same factor.
[chem8-s2.9.1] Example: starting from 1.0 M, take 10 cm³ and make it up to 100 cm³ to give 0.10 M. Repeating from that solution gives 0.010 M and then 0.0010 M.
[chem8-s2.9.2] Serial dilution is used to make a range of concentrations accurately.
[chem8-s2.10.b] Concentration can be expressed in several ways depending on the substance and the use. Each unit says how much solute is present in a fixed amount of solution or mixture.
[chem8-s2.10.0] mol L⁻¹ (molarity, M): moles of solute per litre of solution.
[chem8-s2.10.1] g L⁻¹: grams of solute per litre of solution.
[chem8-s2.10.2] %w/v (weight per volume): grams of solute per 100 cm³ of solution. For example, 5% w/v glucose contains 5 g per 100 cm³.
[chem8-s2.10.3] %v/v (volume per volume): cm³ of solute per 100 cm³ of solution, used for a liquid dissolved in a liquid. For example, wine labelled 12% v/v contains 12 cm³ of ethanol in every 100 cm³.
[chem8-s2.10.4] %w/w (weight per weight): grams of solute per 100 g of the whole mixture, used for solids and creams. For example, 1% w/w hydrocortisone cream has 1 g in every 100 g.
[chem8-s2.11.b] The molar mass links moles to grams, and the fact that 100 cm³ is one-tenth of a litre links g L⁻¹ to %w/v. Between them you can move from any one of these units to any other.
[chem8-s2.11.0] mol L⁻¹ to g L⁻¹: multiply by the molar mass. Example: 0.10 M NaOH × 40 g mol⁻¹ = 4.0 g L⁻¹.
[chem8-s2.11.1] g L⁻¹ to mol L⁻¹: divide by the molar mass. Example: 9.8 g L⁻¹ of H₂SO₄ ÷ 98 g mol⁻¹ = 0.10 mol L⁻¹.
[chem8-s2.11.2] g L⁻¹ to %w/v: divide by 10, since 100 cm³ is one-tenth of a litre. Example: 4.0 g L⁻¹ = 0.40% w/v.
[chem8-s2.11.3] To reverse the step, multiply %w/v by 10 to get g L⁻¹.
[chem8-s2.11.4] %w/v to mol L⁻¹: multiply by 10 to get g L⁻¹, then divide by the molar mass. Example: 0.9% w/v physiological saline (NaCl, M = 58.5 g mol⁻¹): 0.9 × 10 = 9.0 g L⁻¹.
[chem8-s2.11.5] Then 9.0 g L⁻¹ ÷ 58.5 g mol⁻¹ = 0.15 mol L⁻¹.
[chem8-s2.12.b] Percentages for liquids and creams tell you how much of the active substance is in a given amount of the product. These calculations turn the percentage back into an actual volume or mass.
[chem8-s2.12.0] %v/v to volume: volume of solute = volume of solution × %v/v ÷ 100.
[chem8-s2.12.1] Example: ethanol in 750 cm³ of 12% v/v wine = 750 × 12 ÷ 100 = 90 cm³.
[chem8-s2.12.2] %v/v to mass, using density (the mass of each cm³ of the substance): mass = volume × density.
[chem8-s2.12.3] Example: 90 cm³ of ethanol × 0.789 g cm⁻³ = 71 g.
[chem8-s2.12.4] %w/w to mass: mass of solute = mass of mixture × %w/w ÷ 100.
[chem8-s2.12.5] Example: 30 g of a 1% w/w cream contains 30 × 1 ÷ 100 = 0.30 g of the active ingredient.
[chem8-s2.13.b] Some solutions are so dilute that mol L⁻¹ gives very tiny numbers. Parts per million is a convenient unit for these, and it is used for pollutants and similar substances.
[chem8-s2.13.0] p.p.m. (parts per million) is the number of parts of solute in one million parts of solution or mixture. It is used for very dilute solutions, such as pollutants and dissolved substances in water.
[chem8-s2.13.1] For dilute aqueous solutions, 1 p.p.m. = 1 mg of solute per litre of solution (1 mg L⁻¹). This holds because 1 L of water has a mass of about 1 kg, which is 10⁶ mg.
[chem8-s2.13.2] For solids, 1 p.p.m. = 1 mg per kg.
[chem8-s2.13.3] Example: 0.0050 g of a substance in 2.5 L of water. First 0.0050 g = 5.0 mg. Then 5.0 mg ÷ 2.5 L = 2.0 p.p.m.
[chem8-s2.13.4] 1 p.p.m. = 0.001 g L⁻¹ = 0.0001% w/v.
[chem8-s2.13.5] Example: 1.0 mg L⁻¹ of fluoride is 1.0 p.p.m. Fluoridated water in Ireland is about 0.6–0.8 p.p.m. (mg L⁻¹).
[chem8-s2.13.6] p.p.m. to mol L⁻¹: convert to g L⁻¹ first, e.g. 2.0 p.p.m. = 2.0 mg L⁻¹ = 0.0020 g L⁻¹, then divide by the molar mass.

CANDIDATES chem5-s1: 1. Bonding, formulae and shapes
[chem5-s1.0.b] Most atoms are not stable on their own. They join to other atoms because the joined arrangement has less energy, and the force that holds them together is called a chemical bond.
[chem5-s1.0.0] A chemical bond is a force of attraction that holds atoms or ions (atoms that have gained or lost electrons and so carry a charge) together in an element or compound.
[chem5-s1.0.1] Atoms bond to reach a more stable arrangement of electrons, usually a full outer energy level (the outermost shell of electrons) like the nearest noble gas (the unreactive group 18 elements); this is the octet rule, and bonding lowers their energy.
[chem5-s1.0.2] Only the outer (valence) electrons take part in bonding, because the inner electrons are held close to the nucleus (the atom's positively charged centre) and are not available to be lost, gained or shared.
[chem5-s1.1.b] In ionic bonding one atom hands electrons over to another, leaving two oppositely charged particles that attract each other.
[chem5-s1.1.0] An ionic bond is the electrostatic attraction (the attraction between opposite charges) between oppositely charged ions.
[chem5-s1.1.1] It forms when electrons are transferred from one atom, usually a metal that forms a positive ion, to another atom, usually a non-metal that forms a negative ion.
[chem5-s1.1.2] Example: sodium (2,8,1) gives one electron to chlorine (2,8,7): Na → Na⁺ + e⁻ and Cl + e⁻ → Cl⁻. The numbers show the electrons in each energy level.
[chem5-s1.1.3] Both ions have the electron arrangement of a noble gas, which is why the transfer happens: each atom ends up with a full outer energy level.
[chem5-s1.1.4] Ionic compounds form a giant lattice (a huge regular repeating array) of ions, not molecules (separate small groups of atoms).
[chem5-s1.1.5] In sodium chloride each Na⁺ ion is surrounded by six Cl⁻ ions, and each Cl⁻ ion by six Na⁺ ions.
[chem5-s1.1.6] The formula NaCl gives only the ratio of the ions, one Na⁺ to one Cl⁻, because there is no single unit to count.
[chem5-s1.1.7] Diagram: a section of the sodium chloride lattice, drawn as a cube, showing alternating Na⁺ and Cl⁻ ions.
[chem5-s1.2.b] Non-metal atoms usually cannot give electrons away easily, so they share them instead. The shared electrons hold the atoms together.
[chem5-s1.2.0] A covalent bond is a shared pair of electrons between two atoms, usually two non-metals; both nuclei are attracted to the shared pair, which is what holds the atoms together.
[chem5-s1.2.1] A single bond is one shared pair, such as in H–H and Cl–Cl.
[chem5-s1.2.2] A double bond is two shared pairs, as in O=O and C=O in CO₂.
[chem5-s1.2.3] A triple bond is three shared pairs, as in N≡N.
[chem5-s1.2.4] Covalently bonded atoms form molecules (separate small groups of atoms), e.g. H₂O, CH₄ and NH₃.
[chem5-s1.2.5] A few substances have giant covalent structures, in which atoms are linked by covalent bonds throughout instead of forming separate molecules, e.g. diamond.
[chem5-s1.3.b] Not every shared pair is shared fairly. Whether the sharing is equal or unequal depends on how strongly each atom attracts the electrons.
[chem5-s1.3.0] A pure (non-polar) covalent bond shares electrons equally, because both atoms attract them equally, as in H–H and Cl–Cl.
[chem5-s1.3.1] The C–H bond is nearly non-polar.
[chem5-s1.3.2] A polar covalent bond shares electrons unequally, because one atom attracts them more strongly, as in H–Cl and O–H.
[chem5-s1.3.3] The atom that attracts the shared electrons more strongly gets a partial negative charge (δ−), and the other atom gets a partial positive charge (δ+), e.g. Hδ+–Clδ−. The charges are only partial because the electrons are shifted, not fully transferred.
[chem5-s1.4.b] Ionic and covalent bonding are the two ends of a scale, not two separate boxes. Real bonds sit at different points along it, depending on how the electrons are shared.
[chem5-s1.4.0] Bonds are not simply ionic or covalent; they lie on a continuum (a smooth scale) from pure covalent, through polar covalent, to ionic.
[chem5-s1.4.1] At one end, pure covalent bonds share electrons equally, because both atoms attract them equally.
[chem5-s1.4.2] In the middle, polar covalent bonds share electrons unequally, because one atom pulls harder.
[chem5-s1.4.3] At the other end, ionic bonds involve complete transfer of electrons, so ions form.
[chem5-s1.4.4] Examples along the continuum: Cl–Cl (pure covalent), H–Cl (polar covalent), Na⁺Cl⁻ (ionic).
[chem5-s1.4.5] Most bonds have some ionic and some covalent character, so they sit between the ends, not exactly at them.
[chem5-s1.4.6] The difference in electronegativity (how strongly an atom attracts the shared electrons in a bond) between the atoms decides where a bond lies. There is more on this in Bond type and intermolecular forces.
[chem5-s1.4.7] Diagram: a horizontal line labelled pure covalent – polar covalent – ionic, with the electron pair drawn shared equally at the left end, shifted towards one atom in the middle, and fully transferred at the right end.
[chem5-s1.5.b] Setting ionic and covalent bonding side by side shows what each does to the electrons and what kind of structure results. Metals have a third type of bonding.
[chem5-s1.5.0] Ionic bonding transfers electrons and forms ions; covalent bonding shares electrons and forms molecules (mostly).
[chem5-s1.5.1] Ionic bonding occurs between a metal and a non-metal; covalent bonding occurs mostly between two non-metals.
[chem5-s1.5.2] Ionic compounds are giant lattices; covalent bonds are polar or non-polar depending on the atoms involved.
[chem5-s1.5.3] Metallic bonding (assumed): positive metal ions are held together by a sea of delocalised outer electrons (electrons free to move through the whole metal), which explains why metals conduct electricity.
[chem5-s1.6.b] To predict a formula you need to know how many electrons an atom is likely to lose, gain or share. This number is its valency, and the octet rule explains it.
[chem5-s1.6.0] Valency is the number of electrons an atom loses, gains or shares when it bonds; it is the combining power of an element.
[chem5-s1.6.1] The octet rule: atoms tend to lose, gain or share electrons to reach eight electrons in their outer energy level.
[chem5-s1.6.2] Hydrogen follows a different rule: it needs two electrons in its outer level, matching helium (the noble gas with two electrons).
[chem5-s1.6.3] Elements in group 1 and group 17 have a valency of 1, because they lose one electron (group 1) or gain one (group 17) to reach an octet.
[chem5-s1.6.4] Elements in group 2 and group 16 have a valency of 2, because they lose two electrons (group 2) or gain two (group 16).
[chem5-s1.6.5] Elements in group 13 and group 15 have a valency of 3, because they lose three electrons (group 13) or gain three (group 15).
[chem5-s1.6.6] Elements in group 14 have a valency of 4, because they share four electrons to reach an octet.
[chem5-s1.7.b] An ionic compound has no overall charge, so the positive and negative charges must cancel. This gives a simple method for writing any ionic formula.
[chem5-s1.7.0] Step 1: Write the charge on each ion, e.g. Ca²⁺ and Cl⁻.
[chem5-s1.7.1] Step 2: Combine the ions so that the total positive charge equals the total negative charge.
[chem5-s1.7.2] Calcium ions (Ca²⁺) and chloride ions (Cl⁻) give CaCl₂, as one Ca²⁺ balances two Cl⁻.
[chem5-s1.7.3] Aluminium ions (Al³⁺) and oxide ions (O²⁻) give Al₂O₃, as two Al³⁺ balance three O²⁻.
[chem5-s1.7.4] Magnesium ions (Mg²⁺) and nitride ions (N³⁻) give Mg₃N₂, as three Mg²⁺ balance two N³⁻.
[chem5-s1.8.b] Some ions are made of several atoms bonded together that carry one overall charge. They behave as a single unit when you write formulae, so you need to know them.
[chem5-s1.8.0] Negative compound ions: hydroxide OH⁻, nitrate NO₃⁻, hydrogencarbonate HCO₃⁻, carbonate CO₃²⁻, sulfate SO₄²⁻, sulfite SO₃²⁻ and phosphate PO₄³⁻.
[chem5-s1.8.1] Positive compound ion: ammonium NH₄⁺. It is given only as an ion to use in formulae.
[chem5-s1.8.2] Brackets are used round a compound ion when more than one is needed, so that the subscript applies to the whole ion, e.g. Ca(OH)₂ and (NH₄)₂SO₄.
[chem5-s1.9.b] Covalent formulae follow from valency too. Atoms share electron pairs until each has a complete outer level.
[chem5-s1.9.0] Covalent formulae: atoms share enough electron pairs to complete each octet.
[chem5-s1.9.1] Hydrogen (valency 1) and oxygen (valency 2) form H₂O, because two hydrogens are needed to supply the two bonds oxygen can make.
[chem5-s1.9.2] Nitrogen (valency 3) and hydrogen form NH₃, with three hydrogens for nitrogen's three bonds.
[chem5-s1.9.3] Carbon (valency 4) and hydrogen form CH₄, with four hydrogens for carbon's four bonds.
[chem5-s1.9.4] Carbon and oxygen form CO₂, with two double bonds.
[chem5-s1.10.b] A Lewis diagram is a way of drawing bonding that tracks every outer electron. It shows which electrons are transferred, shared or left unused.
[chem5-s1.10.0] A Lewis diagram shows the outer electrons of each atom as dots or crosses; the different symbols let you see which atom each electron came from.
[chem5-s1.10.1] Shared pairs of electrons are drawn between the atoms they join, so you can count the bonds.
[chem5-s1.10.2] Lone pairs are pairs of outer electrons that are not used in bonding.
[chem5-s1.10.3] Ionic compounds: show each ion in square brackets with its charge, e.g. [Na]⁺ [Cl]⁻, with eight electrons around the chloride ion.
[chem5-s1.10.4] Magnesium oxide, an ionic example, is drawn as [Mg]²⁺ [O]²⁻.
[chem5-s1.10.5] Covalent examples to draw: H₂, Cl₂, HCl and H₂O, where water has two lone pairs on oxygen.
[chem5-s1.10.6] Ammonia, NH₃, has one lone pair on nitrogen, because nitrogen has five outer electrons and uses only three in bonds.
[chem5-s1.10.7] O₂ has a double bond, N₂ has a triple bond, and CO₂ has two double bonds.
[chem5-s1.10.8] Diagram: dot-and-cross diagrams of sodium chloride, water and nitrogen, with lone pairs labelled.
[chem5-s1.11.b] Orbitals are the regions of space around a nucleus where an electron is most likely to be found. Covalent bonding can be described as these regions merging.
[chem5-s1.11.0] A covalent bond forms when an orbital of one atom, holding one electron, overlaps an orbital of another atom holding one electron.
[chem5-s1.11.1] The shared pair occupies the overlap region between the two nuclei, which is where both nuclei can attract it.
[chem5-s1.12.b] The way two orbitals overlap decides the type of bond. Overlap can be head-on or sideways, which gives sigma and pi bonds.
[chem5-s1.12.0] A sigma (σ) bond is formed by head-on overlap of orbitals along the line joining the two nuclei.
[chem5-s1.12.1] Sigma bonds are formed by s–s overlap in H₂, s–p overlap in HCl, and end-on p–p overlap in Cl₂.
[chem5-s1.12.2] A pi (π) bond is formed by sideways overlap of two parallel p orbitals, above and below the line joining the nuclei.
[chem5-s1.12.3] Every single bond is a σ bond, so H–H is one σ bond.
[chem5-s1.12.4] A double bond is one σ bond and one π bond; a triple bond is one σ bond and two π bonds, as in N₂.
[chem5-s1.12.5] π bonds are weaker than σ bonds because sideways overlap is less effective.
[chem5-s1.12.6] π bonds prevent rotation about a double bond.
[chem5-s1.12.7] Diagram: head-on overlap of two s orbitals and of two p orbitals (σ); sideways overlap of two p orbitals, showing electron density above and below the axis (π).
[chem5-s1.13.b] Sometimes a bonding pair cannot be pinned between just two atoms. Benzene is the key example, where electrons spread round a whole ring.
[chem5-s1.13.0] In delocalised bonding, electrons are spread over several atoms rather than confined between two.
[chem5-s1.13.1] In benzene (C₆H₆), each carbon uses one p orbital, and the six p orbitals overlap sideways all round the ring.
[chem5-s1.13.2] This forms a ring of delocalised π electrons above and below the plane of the ring.
[chem5-s1.13.3] All six carbon–carbon bonds in benzene are identical, with lengths between those of single and double bonds, because the electrons are shared evenly round the ring. There is more on this in Allotropes of Carbon. Hydrocarbons.
[chem5-s1.14.b] Higher Level students apply the same valency rules past calcium, to the main-group elements up to krypton (element 36).
[chem5-s1.14.0] The same rules apply to elements 21–36 in the main groups.
[chem5-s1.14.1] Potassium bromide is K⁺Br⁻, because group 1 and group 17 both have a valency of 1.
[chem5-s1.14.2] Calcium bromide is CaBr₂, because Ca²⁺ needs two Br⁻ to balance.
[chem5-s1.14.3] Gallium (group 13) forms Ga³⁺ ions, since group 13 has a valency of 3.
[chem5-s1.14.4] Arsenic (group 15) and hydrogen form AsH₃, since group 15 has a valency of 3.
[chem5-s1.14.5] Selenium (group 16) and hydrogen form H₂Se, since group 16 has a valency of 2.
[chem5-s1.15.b] The octet rule and Lewis diagrams are useful but not perfect. Some real molecules break the rule, and the models leave out several properties.
[chem5-s1.15.0] Some molecules are stable with fewer than eight outer electrons on the central atom, e.g. BF₃, with six around boron.
[chem5-s1.15.1] Elements in period 3 and beyond (the third row of the periodic table and below) can have more than eight outer electrons, e.g. PCl₅, with ten around phosphorus.
[chem5-s1.15.2] SF₆ has twelve electrons around sulfur.
[chem5-s1.15.3] The octet rule and Lewis diagrams do not predict the shape of a molecule, how polar a bond is, or bond strength.
[chem5-s1.15.4] They cannot show delocalised bonding, as in benzene.
[chem5-s1.15.5] Predictions from valency assume purely ionic or purely covalent bonding, but real bonds lie on a continuum.
[chem5-s1.16.b] The shape of a molecule follows from a simple idea: electron pairs carry negative charge, so they push each other away. VSEPR turns that idea into a rule for predicting shape.
[chem5-s1.16.0] VSEPR stands for valence shell electron pair repulsion.
[chem5-s1.16.1] The theory applies to molecules of the form ABn (a central atom A bonded to n atoms B), with up to four pairs of electrons around a central atom and single bonds only.
[chem5-s1.16.2] Electron pairs in the outer (valence) shell of the central atom repel each other and arrange themselves as far apart as possible, which fixes the shape of the molecule.
[chem5-s1.16.3] Both bonding pairs and lone pairs count, because both are regions of negative charge that repel.
[chem5-s1.16.4] Lone pairs repel more strongly than bonding pairs: lone pair–lone pair repulsion is greater than lone pair–bond pair repulsion, which is greater than bond pair–bond pair repulsion.
[chem5-s1.16.5] Lone pairs therefore squeeze the bond angles smaller.
[chem5-s1.16.6] The shape is described by the positions of the atoms only, not the lone pairs, so NH₃ (three bonded atoms plus one lone pair) is called pyramidal.
[chem5-s1.17.b] Predicting a shape is a short routine that starts from the Lewis diagram. Follow the same three steps for every molecule.
[chem5-s1.17.0] Step 1: Draw the Lewis (dot-and-cross) diagram of the molecule.
[chem5-s1.17.1] Step 2: Count the electron pairs around the central atom, and how many of them are bonding pairs and how many are lone pairs.
[chem5-s1.17.2] Step 3: Arrange the pairs as far apart as possible, and name the shape from the positions of the atoms, adjusting the angle for lone pairs.
[chem5-s1.18.b] With up to four pairs around the central atom, these five shapes are the ones you need to know. Each depends on how many pairs are bonding and how many are lone.
[chem5-s1.18.0] Two bonding pairs and no lone pairs: linear (a straight line), 180°, e.g. BeCl₂ (gas).
[chem5-s1.18.1] Three bonding pairs and no lone pairs: trigonal planar (three atoms in one flat plane round the centre), 120°, e.g. BF₃.
[chem5-s1.18.2] Four bonding pairs and no lone pairs: tetrahedral (four atoms pointing to the corners of a pyramid-like solid), 109.5°, e.g. CH₄, CCl₄ and SiH₄.
[chem5-s1.18.3] Three bonding pairs and one lone pair: pyramidal (trigonal pyramidal), about 107°, e.g. NH₃. PH₃ is also pyramidal, but its measured angle is much smaller, about 93.5°.
[chem5-s1.18.4] Two bonding pairs and two lone pairs: V-shaped (bent), about 104.5°, e.g. H₂O.
[chem5-s1.18.5] H₂S is also V-shaped, but its measured bond angle is much smaller than water's, about 92°.
[chem5-s1.19.b] Ammonia and water have the same number of electron pairs as methane, yet their bond angles are smaller. Lone pairs are the reason.
[chem5-s1.19.0] NH₃ and H₂O have four electron pairs arranged roughly tetrahedrally, as in CH₄.
[chem5-s1.19.1] The lone pairs push the bonds closer together, because they repel more strongly than bonding pairs, so the bond angle falls as the number of lone pairs rises from 0 to 2: from CH₄ to NH₃ to H₂O.
[chem5-s1.20.b] Molecules are three-dimensional, so a flat drawing needs a convention to show depth. Models let you see the shape directly.
[chem5-s1.20.0] Diagram: 3D shapes drawn with wedges for bonds towards the viewer and dashed lines for bonds away from the viewer, with any lone pairs shown on the central atom.

[chem5-s1.20.2] Digital models, from molecular-modelling software or apps, can be rotated on screen.

CANDIDATES chem9-s3: 3. Gas laws and the ideal gas equation
[chem9-s3.0.b] Gas pressure is the force of the particles hitting the container walls, per unit area. For a fixed mass of gas (a set amount, with none added or lost) at constant temperature, pressure and volume are linked by Boyle's law.
[chem9-s3.0.0] Boyle's law: at constant temperature, the volume of a fixed mass of gas is inversely proportional to its pressure (when one doubles, the other halves): P₁V₁ = P₂V₂.
[chem9-s3.0.1] The law was stated by Robert Boyle in 1662, and Charles's law is named after Jacques Charles.
[chem9-s3.0.2] Particle explanation: in a smaller volume the particles hit the walls more often, so the pressure rises.
[chem9-s3.0.3] Example: 200 cm³ of gas at 100 kPa (kilopascals, a unit of pressure) is compressed to 50 cm³ at the same temperature.
[chem9-s3.0.4] Step 1: Rearrange P₁V₁ = P₂V₂ to P₂ = P₁V₁ ÷ V₂, because P₂ is the unknown.
[chem9-s3.0.5] Step 2: P₂ = 100 kPa × 200 cm³ ÷ 50 cm³ = 400 kPa. The volume fell to a quarter, so the pressure rose four times.
[chem9-s3.1.b] Heating a gas makes it expand if its pressure is held constant. For a fixed mass of gas at constant pressure, volume and temperature are linked by Charles's law.
[chem9-s3.1.0] Charles's law: at constant pressure, the volume of a fixed mass of gas is directly proportional to its temperature in kelvin (K, the absolute temperature scale): V₁/T₁ = V₂/T₂.
[chem9-s3.1.1] Particle explanation: hotter particles move faster and hit the walls harder, so the gas expands to keep the pressure the same.
[chem9-s3.1.2] Example: 300 cm³ of gas at 27 °C (300 K) is heated to 127 °C (400 K) at constant pressure.
[chem9-s3.1.3] Step 1: Rearrange V₁/T₁ = V₂/T₂ to V₂ = V₁ × T₂ ÷ T₁, because V₂ is the unknown.
[chem9-s3.1.4] Step 2: V₂ = 300 cm³ × 400 K ÷ 300 K = 400 cm³.
[chem9-s3.2.b] A third pair of properties completes the set: pressure and temperature at constant volume. The combined gas law then joins all three relationships into one.
[chem9-s3.2.0] Pressure law (Gay-Lussac's law): at constant volume, the pressure of a fixed mass of gas is directly proportional to its temperature in kelvin: P₁/T₁ = P₂/T₂.
[chem9-s3.2.1] Particle explanation: faster particles hit the walls more often and harder, so the pressure rises.
[chem9-s3.2.2] Combined gas law: P₁V₁/T₁ = P₂V₂/T₂, for a fixed mass of gas. It is useful when pressure, volume and temperature all change together.
[chem9-s3.3.b] The gas laws only work on a temperature scale that starts at the lowest possible temperature. That scale is the kelvin scale.
[chem9-s3.3.0] Temperatures in the gas laws must be in kelvin (K), not in °C; using °C gives wrong answers, because a gas at 0 °C does not have zero volume or pressure.
[chem9-s3.3.1] Absolute zero (0 K = −273 °C) is the temperature at which, in theory, an ideal gas would have zero volume.
[chem9-s3.3.2] At absolute zero the particles would stop moving.
[chem9-s3.4.b] Plotting the relationships shows them as straight lines, which is the easiest way to see what proportional means.
[chem9-s3.4.0] Diagram: a graph of V against 1/P, which is a straight line through the origin for Boyle's law, and a graph of V against T in K, which is a straight line through the origin for Charles's law. Label the axes with their quantities and units.
[chem9-s3.5.b] The ideal gas is a model that allows analysis and prediction of how gases behave. It is a simplified picture of a gas, built on a set of assumptions.
[chem9-s3.5.0] An ideal gas is a gas that obeys the gas laws, and PV = nRT, exactly under all conditions.
[chem9-s3.5.1] The ideal gas model assumes that the gas particles are in constant random motion, so they keep colliding with the walls and each other.
[chem9-s3.5.2] The ideal gas model assumes that the volume of the particles themselves is negligible (so small it can be ignored) compared with the volume of the container.
[chem9-s3.5.3] The ideal gas model assumes that there are no attractive or repulsive forces between the particles.
[chem9-s3.5.4] The ideal gas model assumes that collisions are perfectly elastic, which means no kinetic energy (energy of movement) is lost in a collision.
[chem9-s3.5.5] The ideal gas model assumes that the average kinetic energy is proportional to the kelvin temperature.
[chem9-s3.5.6] No real gas is ideal, but most gases behave nearly ideally at room temperature and pressure, so the model allows useful predictions.
[chem9-s3.6.b] Real gases break the ideal assumptions about particle volume and attractive forces. They deviate most in conditions where those assumptions fail.
[chem9-s3.6.0] Real gases deviate most from ideal behaviour at high pressure and low temperature, and behave most ideally at low pressure and high temperature.
[chem9-s3.6.1] At high pressure the particles are squeezed close together, so their own volume is no longer negligible compared with the volume of the gas.
[chem9-s3.6.2] At low temperature the particles move slowly, so the attractive forces between them become significant; this is close to the point where the gas condenses (turns into a liquid).
[chem9-s3.6.3] Small, non-polar particles (with no charged ends) and weak intermolecular forces (forces between molecules), such as helium and hydrogen, behave most ideally.
[chem9-s3.6.4] Polar molecules with strong attractions, especially those that hydrogen bond, such as ammonia and water vapour, deviate most.
[chem9-s3.7.b] The ideal gas equation combines the gas laws into one relationship between pressure, volume, amount and temperature.
[chem9-s3.7.0] PV = nRT, where P is pressure in pascals (Pa), V is volume in cubic metres (m³), n is the number of moles (the amount of gas), and T is temperature in kelvin (K).
[chem9-s3.7.1] R is the gas constant, 8.314 J K⁻¹ mol⁻¹. It is the same for every gas, and its units match pascals, cubic metres, moles and kelvin.
[chem9-s3.7.2] The equation combines Boyle's law, Charles's law, the pressure law and Avogadro's law (equal volumes of gases at the same temperature and pressure hold equal numbers of molecules) into one equation.
[chem9-s3.7.3] The equation applies exactly only to an ideal gas, but it gives good results for real gases at ordinary conditions.
[chem9-s3.8.b] PV = nRT only works when every quantity is in the units that R uses. Mixed units are the commonest error, so convert every quantity before substituting.
[chem9-s3.8.0] Pressure: kPa × 1000 = Pa, so 101 kPa = 101 000 Pa.
[chem9-s3.8.1] Volume: cm³ × 10⁻⁶ = m³, so 250 cm³ = 2.50 × 10⁻⁴ m³.
[chem9-s3.8.2] Volume: L × 10⁻³ = m³.
[chem9-s3.8.3] Temperature: °C + 273 = K.
[chem9-s3.9.b] When the moles, temperature and pressure are known, rearrange the equation to find the volume. Convert units first.
[chem9-s3.9.0] Given: 0.50 mol of nitrogen at 27 °C and 100 kPa.
[chem9-s3.9.1] Step 1: Convert the temperature: 27 °C + 273 = 300 K.
[chem9-s3.9.2] Step 2: Convert the pressure: 100 kPa = 100 000 Pa.
[chem9-s3.9.3] Step 3: Rearrange the equation to V = nRT ÷ P, because V is the unknown.
[chem9-s3.9.4] Step 4: V = 0.50 × 8.314 × 300 ÷ 100 000 = 0.0125 m³ (to 3 significant figures).
[chem9-s3.9.5] Step 5: Convert to litres: 0.0125 m³ × 1000 = 12.5 L.
[chem9-s3.10.b] If the volume, pressure and temperature are measured, the equation gives the amount of gas in moles.
[chem9-s3.10.0] Given: 600 cm³ of oxygen at 20 °C and 101 kPa.
[chem9-s3.10.1] Step 1: Convert the volume: 600 cm³ = 6.00 × 10⁻⁴ m³; the temperature is 20 °C + 273 = 293 K.
[chem9-s3.10.2] Step 2: Rearrange the equation to n = PV ÷ RT, because n is the unknown.
[chem9-s3.10.3] Step 3: n = (101 000 × 6.00 × 10⁻⁴) ÷ (8.314 × 293) = 60.6 ÷ 2436 = 0.0249 mol.
[chem9-s3.11.b] Measuring the mass of a gas as well as its volume, pressure and temperature lets you work out its molar mass, which identifies the gas.
[chem9-s3.11.0] Since n = m ÷ M (moles = mass ÷ molar mass), the molar mass is M = mRT ÷ PV.
[chem9-s3.11.1] Given: 1.60 g of a gas occupies 1.20 L at 27 °C and 101 kPa (sample data).
[chem9-s3.11.2] Step 1: Convert the volume to m³: 1.20 L = 1.20 × 10⁻³ m³; the temperature is 27 °C + 273 = 300 K.
[chem9-s3.11.3] Step 2: n = PV ÷ RT = (101 000 × 1.20 × 10⁻³) ÷ (8.314 × 300) = 121.2 ÷ 2494 = 0.0486 mol.
[chem9-s3.11.4] Step 3: M = m ÷ n = 1.60 g ÷ 0.0486 mol = 32.9 g mol⁻¹.
[chem9-s3.11.5] The answer is close to 32, the Mr of oxygen, so the gas could be oxygen (sample data).
[chem9-s3.12.b] A fixed amount of gas in a container of known volume exerts a pressure that depends on its temperature. The equation gives that pressure.
[chem9-s3.12.0] Given: 0.10 mol of gas in a 2.0 L container at 25 °C.
[chem9-s3.12.1] Step 1: Convert the volume to m³: 2.0 L = 2.0 × 10⁻³ m³; the temperature is 25 °C + 273 = 298 K.
[chem9-s3.12.2] Step 2: Rearrange the equation to P = nRT ÷ V, because P is the unknown.
[chem9-s3.12.3] Step 3: P = 0.10 × 8.314 × 298 ÷ 2.0 × 10⁻³ = 1.24 × 10⁵ Pa = 124 kPa.
[chem9-s3.13.b] A quick check catches most arithmetic and unit slips before you hand in the answer.
[chem9-s3.13.0] Check the units of the answer, and check whether the answer is sensible.
[chem9-s3.13.1] A mole of gas at room conditions occupies about 24 L, so a volume far from this for one mole signals an error.
[chem9-s3.14.b] The equation can be verified by comparing a measured gas volume with the volume it predicts. Magnesium reacting with acid gives a known amount of hydrogen gas to test it with.
[chem9-s3.14.0] Aim: to verify the ideal gas equation by comparing a measured gas volume with the volume it predicts.
[chem9-s3.14.1] Step 1: React a weighed piece of magnesium ribbon, e.g. 0.048 g, with excess dilute hydrochloric acid (so all the magnesium reacts) in a flask connected to a gas syringe (a syringe that collects and measures gas volume): Mg(s) + 2HCl(aq) → MgCl₂(aq) + H₂(g).
[chem9-s3.14.2] Step 2: When the reaction stops and the apparatus has cooled to room temperature, record the volume of hydrogen, the room temperature and the atmospheric pressure.
[chem9-s3.14.3] Step 3: Moles of hydrogen = moles of magnesium, because the equation shows a 1 : 1 ratio: 0.048 g ÷ 24 g mol⁻¹ = 0.0020 mol, using Ar(Mg) = 24.
[chem9-s3.14.4] Step 4: Predicted volume V = nRT ÷ P = 0.0020 × 8.314 × 293 ÷ 101 000 = 4.82 × 10⁻⁵ m³ = 48.2 cm³ (sample data).
[chem9-s3.14.5] Step 5: Compare the measured volume with the predicted volume; close agreement verifies the equation for a gas behaving nearly ideally.
[chem9-s3.14.6] Diagram: a conical flask containing acid, with the magnesium added, connected by a delivery tube to a gas syringe. Label the flask, acid, magnesium, delivery tube and gas syringe.
[chem9-s3.15.b] The equation can also be checked by testing one relationship at a time, holding the other quantities constant.
[chem9-s3.15.0] A gas syringe connected to a pressure sensor shows that PV is constant at constant temperature.
[chem9-s3.15.1] A sealed flask in a water bath shows that P/T is constant, because the volume stays fixed while the temperature is changed.
