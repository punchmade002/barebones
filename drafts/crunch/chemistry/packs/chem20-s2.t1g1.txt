TARGET chem20-s2: 2. Water contamination
[chem20-s2.0.b] Water covers most of the planet, yet very little of it can be drunk or used. Contamination (pollution of water by unwanted substances) removes even more of that small share.
[chem20-s2.0.0] About 97.5% of the Earth's water is salt water in the oceans, which is unsuitable for drinking or farming; only about 2.5% is fresh water.
[chem20-s2.0.1] Most fresh water is frozen in ice caps and glaciers or lies deep underground, so well under 1% is readily available in rivers, lakes and accessible groundwater (water held in rock and soil that can be reached by wells).
[chem20-s2.0.2] Fresh water is constantly recycled by the water cycle (evaporation, rainfall and run-off), but the amount available in a place at a time is limited, and demand rises with population, farming and industry.
[chem20-s2.0.3] Contamination makes some of the available fresh water unusable, so there is even less to share between all these demands.
[chem20-s2.1.b] Making water fit to drink, and cleaning it again after use, takes machinery, chemicals and electricity. Each of these has a cost to the energy supply and to the environment.
[chem20-s2.1.0] Processing water has an energy cost: pumping water from sources to treatment plants and on to homes, running treatment plants (mixing, filtration, UV lamps), and treating waste water before it returns to rivers.
[chem20-s2.1.1] Processing water also has an environmental impact: chemicals such as aluminium sulfate and chlorine are manufactured and transported, and sludge (the solid waste left after treatment) must be disposed of.
[chem20-s2.1.2] Abstraction (taking water out of a river or lake) lowers the level of rivers and lakes, which affects the living things that depend on them.
[chem20-s2.1.3] Leaking pipes waste treated water and the energy used to treat it, because water that is lost has already been pumped and cleaned.
[chem20-s2.1.4] Making fresh water from sea water by desalination (removing the dissolved salts), e.g. by reverse osmosis, is possible but uses far more energy than treating fresh water.
[chem20-s2.1.5] So conserving water, by fixing leaks and using less, also saves energy and reduces emissions.
[chem20-s2.2.b] Fish breathe oxygen that is dissolved in the water, and only a little dissolves. Pollution that feeds bacteria uses that oxygen up, and biochemical oxygen demand (BOD) is the measure of how much.
[chem20-s2.2.0] Fish and other aquatic life need oxygen dissolved in the water; clean water at 20 °C holds only about 9 mg L⁻¹ (9 p.p.m., parts per million) of dissolved oxygen, and warmer water holds less.
[chem20-s2.2.1] When organic matter (material from living things, such as sewage, slurry, food waste or dead plants) enters water, aerobic bacteria (bacteria that use oxygen) break it down, using up dissolved oxygen.
[chem20-s2.2.2] Biochemical oxygen demand (BOD) is the amount of dissolved oxygen used up by biological (biochemical) action when a water sample is kept in the dark at 20 °C for five days. It is measured in mg L⁻¹ (p.p.m.) of oxygen.
[chem20-s2.2.3] A high BOD means a lot of organic pollution: the oxygen is used up and fish die (a fish kill). When the oxygen is gone, anaerobic bacteria (which live without oxygen) take over, producing foul-smelling gases such as hydrogen sulfide (H₂S) and methane.
[chem20-s2.2.4] Unpolluted river water has a BOD of only a few mg L⁻¹; raw domestic sewage (sewage that has had no treatment) has a BOD of a few hundred mg L⁻¹ (about 300).
[chem20-s2.2.5] Farm wastes such as silage effluent (the liquid that drains from stored fermented grass) and slurry (liquid animal manure) have a BOD in the thousands to tens of thousands of mg L⁻¹, so a small spill can kill a river.
[chem20-s2.3.b] The BOD test compares the dissolved oxygen in a sample before and after five days of bacterial activity. The conditions are fixed so that the only thing using up oxygen is the bacteria and the results can be compared.
[chem20-s2.3.0] The sample is kept in the dark because this prevents photosynthesis by algae and plants from adding oxygen.
[chem20-s2.3.1] The temperature is fixed at 20 °C because both the solubility of oxygen (how much dissolves in the water) and the rate of bacterial activity depend on temperature.
[chem20-s2.3.2] The time is fixed at five days so that results are comparable.
[chem20-s2.3.3] Step 1: Measure the dissolved oxygen in one bottle of the sample at the start.
[chem20-s2.3.4] Step 2: Fill a second bottle completely and seal it, so no air can add oxygen, then keep it in the dark at 20 °C for five days.
[chem20-s2.3.5] Step 3: Measure the dissolved oxygen in the second bottle after five days.
[chem20-s2.3.6] Step 4: The BOD is the difference between the two dissolved oxygen readings, because that difference is the oxygen the bacteria used.
[chem20-s2.3.7] Diagram: two labelled BOD bottles, one for the initial dissolved oxygen measurement and one completely filled and sealed for incubation (keeping the sample under set conditions) in the dark at 20 °C for five days.
[chem20-s2.4.b] Sewage is waste water from toilets, kitchens and washing. If it is untreated or poorly treated, it harms a river in three different ways.
[chem20-s2.4.0] Untreated or poorly treated sewage contains organic matter, which has a high BOD and causes oxygen depletion (the water losing its dissolved oxygen).
[chem20-s2.4.1] Sewage also contains nitrates and phosphates, which cause eutrophication (an overgrowth of algae caused by too many nutrients).
[chem20-s2.4.2] Sewage contains disease-causing microorganisms, including bacteria such as E. coli and those causing cholera and typhoid, as well as viruses and parasites.
[chem20-s2.4.3] Faecal contamination (contamination with human or animal faeces) of drinking water or bathing water causes gastrointestinal illness (illness of the stomach and gut); this is why sewage must be treated before discharge and drinking water disinfected.
[chem20-s2.5.b] Effluent means liquid waste that flows out into the environment. Farms produce several kinds, and each can reach rivers through spills or run-off (water washing over land into streams).
[chem20-s2.5.0] Agricultural effluent includes animal slurry, silage effluent, milking-parlour washings and run-off of artificial fertilisers.
[chem20-s2.5.1] Slurry and silage effluent have a very high BOD, so they use up the dissolved oxygen and cause fish kills if they reach a river.
[chem20-s2.5.2] Fertiliser and slurry run-off carry nitrates (NO₃⁻) and phosphates (PO₄³⁻) into water, causing eutrophication.
[chem20-s2.5.3] Nitrate in drinking water is a health concern, especially for babies, because it can reduce the blood's ability to carry oxygen.
[chem20-s2.5.4] The EU drinking-water limit for nitrate is 50 mg L⁻¹.
[chem20-s2.5.5] Pesticides (chemicals that kill pests) washed off farmland can contaminate water and are toxic to aquatic life.
[chem20-s2.6.b] Eutrophication is the enrichment of water with nutrients, especially nitrates and phosphates, leading to excessive growth of algae and plants. It is a chain of events that starts with added nutrients and can end with the death of fish.
[chem20-s2.6.0] Step 1: Nitrates and phosphates from fertiliser run-off, slurry, sewage and detergents enter a river or lake.
[chem20-s2.6.1] Step 2: Algae and aquatic plants grow rapidly, forming an algal bloom (a thick layer of algae) that covers the surface and blocks light from plants below.
[chem20-s2.6.2] Step 3: The algae die and are decomposed by aerobic bacteria, which use up the dissolved oxygen, so the BOD rises sharply. Living algae growing in light do not use up the oxygen; the bacteria decomposing them after they die do.
[chem20-s2.6.3] Step 4: Dissolved oxygen falls so low that fish and other animals die, and anaerobic bacteria then produce foul-smelling H₂S.
[chem20-s2.6.4] Some algal blooms, e.g. of cyanobacteria ("blue-green algae"), also produce toxins harmful to animals and people.
[chem20-s2.6.5] Prevention: controlled use of fertilisers, meaning the right amount at the right time and not before heavy rain (which would wash it into rivers), and buffer strips (strips of vegetation) along rivers.
[chem20-s2.6.6] Prevention: proper storage of slurry and silage, removing phosphates and nitrates in sewage treatment, and phosphate-free detergents.
[chem20-s2.7.b] Factories and power stations discharge waste water that can harm water in several ways: poisoning it, changing its pH, using up its oxygen or heating it.
[chem20-s2.7.0] Industrial effluent can contain toxic chemicals such as heavy metals, solvents and oils.
[chem20-s2.7.1] Industrial effluent can contain acids or alkalis that change the pH of the water, which harms the organisms living in it.
[chem20-s2.7.2] Organic waste from industries such as breweries and food processing has a high BOD, because bacteria use oxygen to break it down.
[chem20-s2.7.3] Thermal pollution (pollution by heat): warm water from power stations and factories holds less dissolved oxygen.
[chem20-s2.7.4] Warm water also speeds up the metabolism (the chemical reactions of life), and so the oxygen demand, of organisms, so even less oxygen is available.
[chem20-s2.7.5] Oil forms a film on the surface that stops oxygen dissolving and coats birds and animals.
[chem20-s2.8.b] Heavy metals are dense metals whose ions are poisonous to living things. Their danger lies in how they act inside cells and in how they build up over time.
[chem20-s2.8.0] Heavy metal ions such as lead (Pb²⁺), mercury (Hg²⁺) and cadmium (Cd²⁺) are toxic even at very low concentrations, in micrograms per litre.
[chem20-s2.8.1] Heavy metal ions bind to proteins, particularly to sulfur-containing (–SH) groups in enzymes (the proteins that speed up reactions in cells), changing their shape and stopping them working.
[chem20-s2.8.2] Heavy metals damage the nervous system, kidneys and liver.
[chem20-s2.8.3] They are cumulative poisons: the body can't excrete them easily, so they build up in tissues (bioaccumulation).
[chem20-s2.8.4] Their concentration increases up a food chain (biomagnification), so top predators and people who eat fish are most at risk.
[chem20-s2.8.5] Heavy metal ions can be removed from water by precipitation (turning them into a solid that forms in the water) as insoluble compounds, e.g. hydroxides or sulfides, followed by filtration, or by ion exchange (swapping the metal ions for harmless ions).
[chem20-s2.9.b] Each of the three metals reaches water from a different source and harms the body in a particular way. Two Japanese disease outbreaks show what the harm can look like.
[chem20-s2.9.0] Lead comes from old lead pipes in houses built before about 1970, especially where the water is soft and acidic, because such water dissolves lead more readily.
[chem20-s2.9.1] Lead damages the developing brain and nervous system of children, partly because Pb²⁺ takes the place of Ca²⁺.
[chem20-s2.9.2] The EU drinking-water limit for lead is 10 µg L⁻¹ (0.010 mg L⁻¹).
[chem20-s2.9.3] At Minamata, Japan, the Chisso Corporation's chemical factory discharged mercury compounds into the bay.
[chem20-s2.9.4] Methylmercury (an organic mercury compound) built up in fish and shellfish, and people who ate them suffered severe nervous-system damage, birth defects and deaths.
[chem20-s2.9.5] The disease was called Minamata disease and was officially recognised in 1956.
[chem20-s2.9.6] Cadmium comes from mining and some industries and damages the kidneys and bones.
[chem20-s2.9.7] Itai-itai disease in Japan was caused by cadmium from mining, which reached river water used for rice fields.
[chem20-s2.10.b] Plastic that breaks into tiny pieces does not disappear; it spreads through water and into living things. These small pieces are called microplastics.
[chem20-s2.10.0] Microplastics are pieces of plastic smaller than 5 mm.
[chem20-s2.10.1] Primary microplastics are made small, e.g. microbeads once used in cosmetics and plastic pellets.
[chem20-s2.10.2] Secondary microplastics form when larger plastic items break down, e.g. fibres from synthetic clothing in washing machines, tyre and road wear, packaging and bags.
[chem20-s2.10.3] Plastics are very unreactive and not biodegradable (bacteria cannot break them down), so they persist for hundreds of years. There is more on this in Addition polymers.
[chem20-s2.10.4] Microplastics are swallowed by plankton (tiny drifting organisms), shellfish and fish, blocking feeding and digestion, and they pass up the food chain.
[chem20-s2.10.5] Microplastics can carry adsorbed toxic chemicals (chemicals stuck to their surface).
[chem20-s2.10.6] Microplastics are found in drinking water and food; their long-term effects on human health are still being researched.
[chem20-s2.11.b] Acidification is the lowering of the pH of water. Gases from burning fuels dissolve in rain to make acids, and acidic waste water adds to the effect.
[chem20-s2.11.0] Sulfur dioxide dissolves in rain: SO₂ + H₂O → H₂SO₃.
[chem20-s2.11.1] Sulfur dioxide and oxygen form sulfuric acid in rain: 2SO₂ + O₂ + 2H₂O → 2H₂SO₄.
[chem20-s2.11.2] Nitrogen dioxide forms nitric acid in rain: 4NO₂ + O₂ + 2H₂O → 4HNO₃.
[chem20-s2.11.3] Acidic industrial and mine waste water also lowers the pH of rivers and lakes.
[chem20-s2.11.4] Normal rain is slightly acidic, with a pH of about 5.6, because of dissolved CO₂; acid rain has a lower pH.
[chem20-s2.12.b] Low pH harms water life directly, through enzymes, and indirectly, by dissolving substances from rocks, shells and pipes.
[chem20-s2.12.0] Enzymes work only in a narrow pH range, so low pH disrupts enzyme activity and the balance of ions in organisms' cells.
[chem20-s2.12.1] Fish eggs and young fish are most sensitive, and many fish can't survive below about pH 5.
[chem20-s2.12.2] Acidic water dissolves aluminium compounds from soils and rocks; Al³⁺ ions damage fish gills so that fish can't take in oxygen.
[chem20-s2.12.3] Acidic water dissolves the calcium carbonate shells of snails and shellfish: CaCO₃ + 2H⁺ → Ca²⁺ + H₂O + CO₂.
[chem20-s2.12.4] In drinking water, acidic water dissolves lead and copper from pipes.

CANDIDATES chem20-s1: 1. The greenhouse effect
[chem20-s1.0.b] Carbon moves continuously between stores (places where it is held) through natural processes. The balance between sources and sinks decides how much CO₂ is in the air.
[chem20-s1.0.0] Carbon is stored in the atmosphere (mainly as CO₂), in the oceans (dissolved CO₂, hydrogencarbonate and carbonate ions), in living things (carbohydrates, proteins and fats), in soils and peat, and in rocks (limestone and fossil fuels).
[chem20-s1.0.1] Photosynthesis (the process in which plants build sugar from CO₂ and water) removes CO₂ from the air: 6CO₂(g) + 6H₂O(l) → C₆H₁₂O₆(aq) + 6O₂(g). It is powered by sunlight in green plants, algae and phytoplankton (microscopic floating ocean algae).
[chem20-s1.0.2] Respiration, the process in which living things use glucose and oxygen to release energy, returns CO₂ to the air. It happens in plants, animals and microorganisms: C₆H₁₂O₆(aq) + 6O₂(g) → 6CO₂(g) + 6H₂O(l).
[chem20-s1.0.3] Decay is the breakdown of dead matter by microorganisms. It also releases CO₂, and it releases CH₄ (methane) instead where oxygen is absent.
[chem20-s1.0.4] CO₂ dissolves in the oceans and waterways, so the water is also a store: CO₂(g) ⇌ CO₂(aq), and then CO₂(aq) + H₂O(l) ⇌ H₂CO₃(aq), where H₂CO₃ is carbonic acid.
[chem20-s1.0.5] Marine organisms use carbonate to build calcium carbonate shells and skeletons. Over long periods these deposits form limestone, which locks carbon away in rock.
[chem20-s1.1.b] Carbon also returns to the air by burning, and some carbon is held back for very long periods as fuels. This explains how the carbon in fossil fuels reaches the atmosphere.
[chem20-s1.1.0] Combustion (burning) of fuels releases CO₂, for example CH₄(g) + 2O₂(g) → CO₂(g) + 2H₂O(l). Natural sources also include forest fires and volcanic eruptions.
[chem20-s1.1.1] Peat forms from dead organisms buried over thousands of years, while coal, oil and natural gas form over millions of years. In both cases the material is buried without enough oxygen to decay fully, so they are long-term carbon stores.
[chem20-s1.1.2] Burning coal, oil, natural gas or peat therefore returns carbon, stored for a very long time, to the atmosphere as CO₂.
[chem20-s1.1.3] Diagram: the carbon cycle with atmospheric CO₂ at the centre and labelled arrows for photosynthesis, respiration, decay, combustion, dissolving in the oceans, formation of shells and limestone, and formation of fossil fuels.
[chem20-s1.2.b] Each part of the carbon cycle either adds CO₂ to the air or takes it out. When additions and removals match, the CO₂ level stays steady, and human activity has upset that match.
[chem20-s1.2.0] A carbon source adds more CO₂ to the atmosphere than it removes, for example burning fossil fuels, respiration, decay, volcanoes, and heating limestone in cement-making: CaCO₃(s) → CaO(s) + CO₂(g).
[chem20-s1.2.1] A carbon sink removes more carbon from the atmosphere than it releases, and stores it. Examples are growing forests, the oceans, and undisturbed peatlands and wetlands (peat-forming bogs and marshy land).
[chem20-s1.2.2] Waterlogged conditions in undisturbed peatlands and wetlands stop dead plant material from decaying, so the carbon stays stored.
[chem20-s1.2.3] Before industrialisation (the growth of factories and large-scale fuel burning), natural sources and sinks were roughly in balance, so atmospheric CO₂ stayed fairly steady at about 280 ppm (parts per million by volume).
[chem20-s1.2.4] Burning fossil fuels moves carbon from long-term stores into the air far faster than sinks can absorb it. Deforestation (clearing forests) and draining peatlands both remove sinks and release stored carbon.
[chem20-s1.2.5] As a result, atmospheric CO₂ has risen to about 420 ppm in the mid-2020s.
[chem20-s1.2.6] CO₂ is a greenhouse gas, so a carbon cycle out of balance changes the climate. Natural changes in the cycle over thousands of years have caused past climate changes; the present change is human-influenced and much faster.
[chem20-s1.2.7] Sustainability means using resources so that they are not used up and the environment is not damaged for future generations. For carbon this means keeping emissions in balance with what sinks can absorb, for example by using renewable energy and protecting forests and peatlands.
[chem20-s1.3.b] Greenhouse gases in the atmosphere trap some of the heat the Earth radiates, keeping the lower atmosphere warm.
[chem20-s1.3.0] Energy from the Sun reaches the Earth mainly as visible light (shorter wavelength), with some ultraviolet and near-infrared. The atmosphere is largely transparent to visible light, so it passes through.
[chem20-s1.3.1] About 30% of incoming solar radiation is reflected back to space by clouds, ice and the surface. The rest is absorbed and warms the land and oceans.
[chem20-s1.3.2] The warmed Earth radiates energy back out as longer-wavelength infrared (IR) radiation, which is heat. Remember that incoming radiation is mainly visible light and outgoing radiation is infrared.
[chem20-s1.3.3] Greenhouse gases absorb some of this IR and re-emit it in all directions, including back down towards the surface, so heat is kept in the lower atmosphere for longer.
[chem20-s1.3.4] This warming of the Earth's surface and lower atmosphere by naturally occurring greenhouse gases is the natural greenhouse effect.
[chem20-s1.3.5] The main natural greenhouse gases are water vapour, the largest contributor, and carbon dioxide, with smaller amounts of methane and dinitrogen oxide (N₂O); ozone also contributes.
[chem20-s1.3.6] The name is an analogy with a glass greenhouse, which lets sunlight in and keeps heat in. The analogy is not exact, because a real greenhouse works mainly by stopping warm air escaping.
[chem20-s1.3.7] Diagram: the Sun, incoming short-wavelength radiation passing through the atmosphere, some reflected, IR emitted from the surface, some absorbed by a layer of greenhouse gases and re-emitted back down, and some escaping to space.
[chem20-s1.4.b] Not every gas in the air is a greenhouse gas. Whether a gas traps heat depends on the structure of its molecules.
[chem20-s1.4.0] Nitrogen (N₂, about 78% of dry air), oxygen (O₂, about 21%) and argon (about 0.9%) do not absorb IR radiation, so they are not greenhouse gases even though they make up about 99% of dry air.
[chem20-s1.4.1] A molecule absorbs IR when the energy makes its bonds vibrate (stretch or bend) in a way that changes how charge is spread across the molecule.
[chem20-s1.4.2] Molecules of two identical atoms (N₂, O₂) cannot do this, while H₂O, CO₂, CH₄ and N₂O, with three or more atoms and polar bonds (bonds where the electrons are shared unequally), can.
[chem20-s1.4.3] Water vapour contributes most to the natural greenhouse effect because it is by far the most abundant greenhouse gas.
[chem20-s1.4.4] The concentration of water vapour is set by temperature, through evaporation (liquid water turning to vapour) and condensation (vapour turning back to liquid), not directly by human emissions.
[chem20-s1.5.b] The natural greenhouse effect is what makes the Earth habitable, and experiments by John Tyndall in the nineteenth century showed which gases absorb heat radiation.
[chem20-s1.5.0] Without the natural greenhouse effect, the Earth's average surface temperature would be about −18 °C instead of about +15 °C, a difference of about 33 °C.
[chem20-s1.5.1] At −18 °C the oceans would freeze and life as we know it could not exist.
[chem20-s1.5.2] The natural greenhouse effect keeps water liquid over most of the planet and evens out the difference between day and night temperatures.
[chem20-s1.5.3] The natural effect is essential and beneficial. The problem is the extra warming caused by increased greenhouse gas concentrations.
[chem20-s1.5.4] John Tyndall (1820–1893), born in Leighlinbridge, Co. Carlow, used experiments from 1859 at the Royal Institution in London to show which gases absorb heat radiation: water vapour and carbon dioxide do, while nitrogen and oxygen do not.
[chem20-s1.5.5] Tyndall's experiments laid the experimental foundation for understanding the greenhouse effect. The Tyndall National Institute in Cork is named after him.
[chem20-s1.6.b] The natural greenhouse effect is beneficial, but human activity has added greenhouse gases to the air. The extra warming this causes is the enhanced greenhouse effect.
[chem20-s1.6.0] The enhanced greenhouse effect is the extra warming of the Earth's surface and lower atmosphere caused by increased concentrations of greenhouse gases from human (anthropogenic) activities.
[chem20-s1.6.1] The main human activities are burning fossil fuels (for power generation, transport, heating and industry), deforestation and land-use change, agriculture (livestock and fertilisers), cement-making and landfill (waste buried in the ground).
[chem20-s1.6.2] The resulting long-term rise in the Earth's average surface temperature is global warming. Together with the other changes it causes, it is called climate change.
[chem20-s1.7.b] Gases differ in how much they warm the Earth for the same mass emitted. To compare them, scientists use a number called the global warming potential.
[chem20-s1.7.0] Gases differ in their greenhouse effect because they differ in how strongly each molecule absorbs infrared radiation, how abundant they are in the atmosphere, and how long they stay there (atmospheric lifetime).
[chem20-s1.7.1] The greenhouse factor of a gas is its global warming potential (GWP): the warming caused by emitting 1 kg (or 1 tonne) of the gas over a set period, usually 100 years, compared with the warming from the same mass of CO₂. The GWP of CO₂ is 1 by definition.
[chem20-s1.7.2] 100-year GWPs from the IPCC (the Intergovernmental Panel on Climate Change) Fifth Assessment Report (AR5, 2013) are: CO₂ 1; methane (CH₄) 28; dinitrogen oxide (N₂O) 265.
[chem20-s1.7.3] The IPCC Sixth Assessment Report (AR6, 2021) gives slightly different values because the science is refined over time: CH₄ about 27–30, depending on whether the methane is of fossil origin. The AR6 value for N₂O is 273.
[chem20-s1.7.4] Always state which report a GWP comes from, because the values differ between reports.
[chem20-s1.7.5] A high GWP does not make a gas the most important. CO₂ has the lowest GWP of these gases, yet it causes most of the human-caused warming because far more of it is emitted and it stays in the atmosphere for centuries.
[chem20-s1.7.6] Emissions of different gases are compared as CO₂ equivalent, found by multiplying the mass of the gas by its GWP.
[chem20-s1.7.7] Example: 5 tonnes of methane = 5 × 28 = 140 tonnes CO₂ equivalent (AR5 values).
[chem20-s1.8.b] Each greenhouse gas has its own sources and abundance. Abundance is given in ppm, parts per million of the air by volume, and compared with the pre-industrial level.
[chem20-s1.8.0] Carbon dioxide comes from burning fossil fuels, deforestation and cement-making (CaCO₃ → CaO + CO₂).
[chem20-s1.8.1] CO₂ is about 420 ppm in the mid-2020s, up from about 280 ppm before industrialisation, an increase of about 50%.
[chem20-s1.8.2] Much of the extra CO₂ stays in the atmosphere for centuries, and some for thousands of years, which is why its effect builds up.
[chem20-s1.8.3] Methane comes from ruminant livestock (cattle and sheep, which digest food in the stomach), manure, rice paddies, landfill sites, leaks from natural-gas wells and pipelines, and natural wetlands.
[chem20-s1.8.4] Methane is about 1.9 ppm, more than two and a half times the pre-industrial level of about 0.7 ppm, and its atmospheric lifetime is about 12 years.
[chem20-s1.9.b] Dinitrogen oxide is a long-lived greenhouse gas tied to farming. Ireland's emissions show how these gases link to everyday activities.
[chem20-s1.9.0] Dinitrogen oxide (nitrous oxide, N₂O) comes mainly from nitrogen fertilisers and manure on soils. It is about 0.34 ppm, up from about 0.27 ppm.
[chem20-s1.9.1] N₂O stays in the atmosphere for over 100 years (AR5: about 121 years), which is why it is such a potent, long-lived greenhouse gas.
[chem20-s1.9.2] In Ireland agriculture is the largest single source of greenhouse gas emissions, over a third of the total, mainly methane from cattle and N₂O from fertilisers and manure.
[chem20-s1.9.3] In Ireland energy, transport and heating produce most of the CO₂.
[chem20-s1.10.b] Oxides of nitrogen and sulfur form when fuels burn. They matter in a different way from the main greenhouse gases: one group acts indirectly and the other actually cools the climate.
[chem20-s1.10.0] NOx (nitrogen monoxide, NO, and nitrogen dioxide, NO₂) form when nitrogen and oxygen from air combine at the high temperatures in car engines and power stations: N₂(g) + O₂(g) → 2NO(g).
[chem20-s1.10.1] NO then reacts further with oxygen: 2NO(g) + O₂(g) → 2NO₂(g). Lightning also forms NOx.
[chem20-s1.10.2] NOx are short-lived and are not important direct greenhouse gases. They act indirectly, mainly by helping to form ozone, a greenhouse gas, in the lower atmosphere.
[chem20-s1.10.3] NO₂ also causes acid rain (rain made acidic by dissolved pollutant gases) and harms health.
[chem20-s1.10.4] NOx must not be confused with N₂O, the long-lived greenhouse gas from fertilisers.
[chem20-s1.10.5] Sulfur oxides (SO₂ and SO₃) come from burning coal and oil that contain sulfur, S(s) + O₂(g) → SO₂(g), and from volcanoes.
[chem20-s1.10.6] SO₂ forms tiny sulfate particles (aerosols) and droplets in the air that reflect sunlight, so their overall effect on climate is cooling, not warming.
[chem20-s1.10.7] Sulfur oxides also cause acid rain.
[chem20-s1.11.b] Water vapour is the most abundant greenhouse gas, but it behaves differently from the others because temperature, not human emissions, controls how much of it is in the air.
[chem20-s1.11.0] Water vapour is the most abundant greenhouse gas. Its concentration is controlled by temperature, and it stays in the air for only days.
[chem20-s1.11.1] Because of its short stay in the air, human emissions don't change its concentration directly, and water vapour has no GWP.
[chem20-s1.11.2] Water vapour acts as a feedback (a change that reinforces the original change): warming caused by CO₂ and other gases increases evaporation, and the extra water vapour traps more heat, amplifying the warming.
[chem20-s1.11.3] Warmer air holds about 7% more water vapour for each °C of warming (Clausius–Clapeyron relation).
[chem20-s1.12.b] Several independent kinds of measurement show that greenhouse gases have risen and that the planet has warmed. Together they support the conclusion that human activity is the cause.
[chem20-s1.12.0] Continuous measurements at Mauna Loa, Hawaii, begun by Charles David Keeling in 1958 (the “Keeling curve”), show CO₂ rising every year, from about 315 ppm in 1958 to about 420 ppm.
[chem20-s1.12.1] Ice cores (long cylinders of ice drilled from ice sheets): air bubbles trapped in Antarctic ice show that CO₂ stayed between about 180 and 300 ppm for the 800,000 years before industrialisation.
[chem20-s1.12.2] Today's CO₂ level is far above that range, and the rise matches the growth in fossil-fuel use.
[chem20-s1.12.3] The global average surface temperature in 2011–2020 was about 1.1 °C higher than in 1850–1900 (IPCC AR6), with most of the warming since the 1970s. Recent years have been the warmest on record.
[chem20-s1.12.4] Other observed changes include shrinking glaciers and Arctic sea ice, melting ice sheets and warming oceans.
[chem20-s1.12.5] Sea level rose about 20 cm between 1901 and 2018 (IPCC AR6), from melting ice and the expansion of warmer water.
[chem20-s1.12.6] Climate models (computer simulations of the climate) that include only natural factors (changes in the Sun and volcanoes) do not reproduce the warming since the mid-twentieth century. Models that also include human greenhouse gas emissions do.
[chem20-s1.12.7] The IPCC (AR6) concludes that it is unequivocal that human influence has warmed the atmosphere, ocean and land.
[chem20-s1.13.b] Extra warming changes temperature, rainfall, the oceans and living things. The effects are linked: warmer air holds more water vapour, and the oceans absorb extra CO₂.
[chem20-s1.13.0] Global warming brings higher average temperatures, more frequent heatwaves, melting ice and rising sea levels that threaten low-lying coasts and countries, for example Bangladesh, the Netherlands and small island states.
[chem20-s1.13.1] A warmer atmosphere holds more water vapour, so the water cycle intensifies: heavier rainfall and more flooding in some regions, and longer droughts in others.
[chem20-s1.13.2] More winter rain is expected in Ireland.
[chem20-s1.13.3] Other effects include changes to ecosystems (species moving or becoming extinct, coral bleaching), threats to food and water supplies, and more extreme weather events.
[chem20-s1.14.b] The oceans take up part of the CO₂ humans emit. This slows warming in the air, but the dissolved CO₂ makes the sea water less alkaline.
[chem20-s1.14.0] The oceans absorb about a quarter of the CO₂ emitted by humans.
[chem20-s1.14.1] CO₂(aq) + H₂O(l) ⇌ H₂CO₃(aq) ⇌ H⁺(aq) + HCO₃⁻(aq). The H⁺ concentration rises as CO₂ dissolves, so the pH falls.
[chem20-s1.14.2] The average surface ocean pH has fallen by about 0.1, from about 8.2 to about 8.1, since pre-industrial times.
[chem20-s1.14.3] Ocean acidification lowers the pH but does not make the sea acidic, because the pH stays above 7.
[chem20-s1.14.4] The extra H⁺ reacts with carbonate ions, H⁺ + CO₃²⁻ → HCO₃⁻, leaving less carbonate for shell-building organisms such as oysters, mussels, corals and some plankton.
[chem20-s1.14.5] Acidified water can also dissolve calcium carbonate: CaCO₃(s) + CO₂(aq) + H₂O(l) → Ca²⁺(aq) + 2HCO₃⁻(aq).
[chem20-s1.15.b] Reducing the enhanced greenhouse effect means cutting emissions of greenhouse gases and protecting carbon sinks. Each major source has its own response.
[chem20-s1.15.0] Replace fossil fuels with renewable energy (wind, solar, hydroelectric and tidal), and improve energy efficiency by insulating buildings, using heat pumps and buying efficient appliances.
[chem20-s1.15.1] Transport: use public transport, cycling and walking; use electric vehicles charged from renewable electricity; and use hydrogen fuel cells.
[chem20-s1.15.2] Agriculture: use the right amount of fertiliser at the right time, and use clover and other nitrogen-fixing crops.
[chem20-s1.15.3] Agriculture: breed and feed livestock to produce less methane, and capture methane from manure in anaerobic digesters (tanks where microorganisms break down waste without oxygen, giving biogas) for use as a fuel.
[chem20-s1.15.4] Land use: plant forests, stop deforestation, and protect and rewet peatlands so that they act as carbon sinks again.
[chem20-s1.16.b] Some emissions can be captured or avoided in waste and industry, and governments set targets so that action is shared.
[chem20-s1.16.0] Waste: reduce food waste, recycle, and capture landfill methane and burn it to generate electricity. The CO₂ produced has a much smaller warming effect than the methane would have.
[chem20-s1.16.1] Carbon capture and storage (CCS): CO₂ is captured from power-station or factory flue gases (the waste gases from the chimney), compressed to a liquid, piped away and injected into deep rock formations such as depleted oil and gas fields.
[chem20-s1.16.2] Acid rain can be cut by reducing sulfur and NOx emissions: low-sulfur fuels, removing SO₂ from flue gases, and catalytic converters on cars.
[chem20-s1.16.3] International and national action: the Paris Agreement (2015) aims to keep the rise in global temperature well below 2 °C above pre-industrial levels and to pursue efforts to limit it to 1.5 °C.
[chem20-s1.16.4] Ireland's Climate Action and Low Carbon Development (Amendment) Act 2021 sets a 51% cut in emissions by 2030 (compared with 2018) and climate neutrality by 2050.
[chem20-s1.16.5] A carbon footprint is the total greenhouse gas emissions, as CO₂ equivalent, caused by a person, organisation, product or activity. Individuals can reduce theirs through choices in travel, home heating, diet and consumption.

CANDIDATES chem11-s2: 2. Acid–base theories and strength
[chem11-s2.0.b] The first successful definition of acids and bases came from Svante Arrhenius in 1887, in his theory of electrolytic dissociation. He defined them by the ions they produce when dissolved in water.
[chem11-s2.0.0] An Arrhenius acid is a substance that dissociates (splits into ions) in water to produce hydrogen ions (H⁺): HCl(aq) → H⁺(aq) + Cl⁻(aq).
[chem11-s2.0.1] An Arrhenius base is a substance that dissociates in water to produce hydroxide ions (OH⁻): NaOH(aq) → Na⁺(aq) + OH⁻(aq).
[chem11-s2.0.2] Neutralisation (an acid and a base cancelling each other's properties) is the combination of hydrogen and hydroxide ions to form water: H⁺ + OH⁻ → H₂O.
[chem11-s2.1.b] The Arrhenius theory works well for simple cases like HCl and NaOH, but it breaks down as soon as a substance or reaction does not fit its water-and-ions picture. These gaps are why a wider theory was needed.
[chem11-s2.1.0] It applies only to aqueous solutions (substances dissolved in water), so it says nothing about acids and bases in other solvents.
[chem11-s2.1.1] It cannot explain substances that act as bases but contain no hydroxide, such as ammonia (NH₃) and sodium carbonate, because it only allows a base to produce OH⁻ itself.
[chem11-s2.1.2] It cannot explain acid–base reactions that happen without water, e.g. ammonia gas and hydrogen chloride gas reacting to form ammonium chloride: NH₃(g) + HCl(g) → NH₄Cl(s).
[chem11-s2.1.3] It ignores the role of water: a bare hydrogen ion (a proton) does not exist in water; it bonds to a water molecule to form the hydronium ion, H₃O⁺.
[chem11-s2.2.b] Johannes Brønsted and Thomas Lowry proposed this theory independently in 1923. It defines acids and bases by the transfer of a proton, so it no longer depends on water or on OH⁻.
[chem11-s2.2.0] A proton is the same particle as a hydrogen ion, H⁺ (a hydrogen atom that has lost its electron, leaving just a proton).
[chem11-s2.2.1] A Brønsted–Lowry acid is a proton donor; a Brønsted–Lowry base is a proton acceptor.
[chem11-s2.2.2] An acid–base reaction is the transfer of a proton from an acid to a base.
[chem11-s2.2.3] HCl(g) + H₂O(l) → H₃O⁺(aq) + Cl⁻(aq): HCl donates a proton, so it is the acid, and water accepts it, so it is the base.
[chem11-s2.2.4] NH₃(aq) + H₂O(l) ⇌ NH₄⁺(aq) + OH⁻(aq): ammonia accepts a proton, so it is the base, and water donates one, so it is the acid. The ⇌ arrow means the reaction is reversible.
[chem11-s2.2.5] Water can act as an acid or a base depending on what it reacts with, so it is described as amphoteric.
[chem11-s2.3.b] A theory is more extensive if it explains a wider range of substances and reactions with fewer restrictions. Brønsted–Lowry does this by fixing each of the Arrhenius limitations.
[chem11-s2.3.0] It includes every Arrhenius acid and base, and more: the hydroxide ion is itself a proton acceptor, so it counts as a base.
[chem11-s2.3.1] It explains bases with no OH⁻, such as ammonia, and the carbonate ion, which accepts a proton: CO₃²⁻ + H⁺ → HCO₃⁻.
[chem11-s2.3.2] It applies to reactions without water, such as ammonia reacting with hydrogen chloride in the gas phase.
[chem11-s2.3.3] It explains the role of water as a proton acceptor when it forms H₃O⁺, which the Arrhenius theory cannot describe.
[chem11-s2.3.4] Acid: Arrhenius gives a substance that produces H⁺ in water; Brønsted–Lowry gives a proton donor, so the definition does not depend on water.
[chem11-s2.3.5] Base: Arrhenius gives a substance that produces OH⁻ in water; Brønsted–Lowry gives a proton acceptor, so it covers bases such as ammonia.
[chem11-s2.3.6] Scope: Arrhenius applies only to aqueous solutions; Brønsted–Lowry applies in any solvent (the liquid doing the dissolving) and in the absence of a solvent.
[chem11-s2.4.b] Because a Brønsted–Lowry reaction passes a proton along, the acid and base each turn into a new species that can pass the proton back. These linked species are called conjugates.
[chem11-s2.4.0] When an acid donates a proton, what is left can accept a proton back: it is the conjugate base of that acid.
[chem11-s2.4.1] When a base accepts a proton, the species formed is the conjugate acid of that base.
[chem11-s2.4.2] A conjugate acid–base pair is two species that differ by one proton (H⁺), e.g. HCl and Cl⁻; H₃O⁺ and H₂O; NH₄⁺ and NH₃.
[chem11-s2.4.3] H₃O⁺ and OH⁻ are not a conjugate pair, because they differ by two protons.
[chem11-s2.4.4] Every Brønsted–Lowry reaction has two conjugate pairs: acid₁ + base₂ ⇌ acid₂ + base₁.
[chem11-s2.5.b] To label the acids, bases and conjugate pairs in any equation, follow the movement of the proton. These four steps work for every equation.
[chem11-s2.5.0] 1. For each reactant, check whether it loses or gains a proton in going to the products.
[chem11-s2.5.1] 2. The reactant that loses a proton is the acid; the reactant that gains a proton is the base.
[chem11-s2.5.2] 3. On the product side, the species formed from the base is its conjugate acid, and the species formed from the acid is its conjugate base.
[chem11-s2.5.3] 4. In a reversible reaction (one that can run in both directions, shown by ⇌), the products act as an acid and a base in the reverse reaction.
[chem11-s2.6.b] Each equation below is labelled using the four steps: find which reactant loses a proton, which gains one, then match each to its product. Notice that the same species can be an acid in one equation and a base in another.
[chem11-s2.6.0] HCl + H₂O → H₃O⁺ + Cl⁻: HCl is the acid and H₂O is the base; the pairs are HCl/Cl⁻ and H₃O⁺/H₂O.
[chem11-s2.6.1] NH₃ + H₂O ⇌ NH₄⁺ + OH⁻: the acids are H₂O and NH₄⁺; the bases are NH₃ and OH⁻; the pairs are NH₄⁺/NH₃ and H₂O/OH⁻.
[chem11-s2.6.2] CH₃COOH + H₂O ⇌ CH₃COO⁻ + H₃O⁺: the acids are CH₃COOH and H₃O⁺; the bases are H₂O and CH₃COO⁻; the pairs are CH₃COOH/CH₃COO⁻ and H₃O⁺/H₂O.
[chem11-s2.6.3] HCO₃⁻ + H₂O ⇌ CO₃²⁻ + H₃O⁺: here HCO₃⁻ acts as an acid because it loses a proton; the pairs are HCO₃⁻/CO₃²⁻ and H₃O⁺/H₂O.
[chem11-s2.6.4] HCO₃⁻ + H₃O⁺ ⇌ H₂CO₃ + H₂O: here HCO₃⁻ acts as a base because it gains a proton; the pairs are H₂CO₃/HCO₃⁻ and H₃O⁺/H₂O.
[chem11-s2.6.5] NH₃ + HCl → NH₄⁺ + Cl⁻ (no water involved): NH₃ is the base and HCl is the acid; the pairs are NH₄⁺/NH₃ and HCl/Cl⁻.
[chem11-s2.7.b] An acid and its conjugate base are linked like a see-saw: the stronger one is, the weaker the other. Some species can sit on either side, depending on what they react with.
[chem11-s2.7.0] The stronger an acid, the weaker its conjugate base: HCl is a strong acid, so Cl⁻ is a very weak base that barely accepts protons.
[chem11-s2.7.1] A weak acid has a relatively strong conjugate base: ethanoic acid is a weak acid, and the ethanoate ion (CH₃COO⁻) readily accepts a proton.
[chem11-s2.7.2] Water and the hydrogencarbonate ion are amphoteric (also called amphiprotic): each can act as either an acid or a base, depending on the reaction, as shown in the worked examples above.
[chem11-s2.8.b] Strength describes how far an acid splits into ions (dissociates, or ionises) when it dissolves in water. This decides how many H⁺ ions it releases.
[chem11-s2.8.0] A strong acid is completely dissociated (ionised) into ions in water, shown with a single arrow: HCl(aq) → H⁺(aq) + Cl⁻(aq).
[chem11-s2.8.1] Strong acids include hydrochloric acid (HCl), nitric acid (HNO₃) and sulfuric acid (H₂SO₄).
[chem11-s2.8.2] A weak acid is only slightly dissociated in water: most molecules stay undissociated and an equilibrium (a balance between the forward and reverse reactions) is set up, e.g. CH₃COOH(aq) ⇌ CH₃COO⁻(aq) + H⁺(aq).
[chem11-s2.8.3] Weak acids include ethanoic acid (vinegar), citric acid, carbonic acid (CO₂ in water) and most organic acids (acids built on carbon chains).
[chem11-s2.9.b] Bases have strength in the same way as acids: it depends on how fully they produce hydroxide ions in water.
[chem11-s2.9.0] A strong base is completely dissociated in water, e.g. sodium hydroxide and potassium hydroxide: NaOH(aq) → Na⁺(aq) + OH⁻(aq).
[chem11-s2.9.1] A weak base reacts only slightly with water to produce hydroxide ions, e.g. ammonia: NH₃(aq) + H₂O(l) ⇌ NH₄⁺(aq) + OH⁻(aq).
[chem11-s2.10.b] Concentration describes how much acid or base is present; strength describes how far it dissociates. The two are separate properties.
[chem11-s2.10.0] Concentration is the amount of acid or base dissolved per litre of solution, measured in mol L⁻¹ (moles per litre; a mole is a fixed counting amount of particles).
[chem11-s2.10.1] A concentrated acid has a large amount of acid per litre, e.g. 10 M HCl; a dilute acid has a small amount per litre, e.g. 0.1 M HCl.
[chem11-s2.10.2] A dilute strong acid, e.g. 0.01 M HCl, is still fully dissociated.
[chem11-s2.10.3] A concentrated weak acid, e.g. glacial ethanoic acid, is still only slightly dissociated.
[chem11-s2.11.b] Compare 0.1 M hydrochloric acid with 0.1 M ethanoic acid, which have the same concentration. Any difference in behaviour must then come from strength, not concentration.
[chem11-s2.11.0] pH (a scale measuring how acidic a solution is; lower means more H⁺): 0.1 M hydrochloric acid has a pH of 1.0, while 0.1 M ethanoic acid has a pH of about 2.9, because it produces far fewer H⁺ ions.
[chem11-s2.11.1] Ethanoic acid has an acid dissociation constant (Ka) of 1.8 × 10⁻⁵, a small value showing that it is only slightly dissociated.
[chem11-s2.11.2] Rate of reaction: magnesium ribbon fizzes much faster in 0.1 M hydrochloric acid than in 0.1 M ethanoic acid, because hydrogen ions are present at a higher concentration.
[chem11-s2.11.3] Conductivity: 0.1 M hydrochloric acid conducts electricity much better than 0.1 M ethanoic acid, because it contains more ions to carry the current.
[chem11-s2.11.4] Equal volumes of the two acids at the same concentration neutralise the same amount of sodium hydroxide and give the same total volume of hydrogen with excess magnesium, because the weak acid's equilibrium shifts to release more H⁺ as H⁺ is used up.
[chem11-s2.11.5] Safety: concentrated acids and alkalis are corrosive, so wear goggles and gloves; dilute an acid by adding it to water.

CANDIDATES chem8-s2: 2. Solutions and concentration
[chem8-s2.0.b] Much of chemistry happens in solution, so you need exact words for the parts of one. Every later idea in this subsection, from concentration to dilution, is built on them.
[chem8-s2.0.0] A solution is a uniform (homogeneous) mixture of a solute dissolved in a solvent. Uniform means it looks and behaves the same throughout, with no layers or visible particles.
[chem8-s2.0.1] The solute is the substance that dissolves, and the solvent is the substance it dissolves in. When the solvent is water the solution is called an aqueous solution.
[chem8-s2.1.b] A solvent can only hold so much solute at a given temperature. The names unsaturated, saturated and supersaturated describe how close a solution is to that limit, or beyond it.
[chem8-s2.1.0] An unsaturated solution can still dissolve more solute at that temperature, so extra solute added to it simply dissolves.
[chem8-s2.1.1] A saturated solution contains the maximum amount of solute that can dissolve at that temperature, with undissolved solid present. Any more solute added just sits at the bottom.
[chem8-s2.1.2] A supersaturated solution contains more dissolved solute than a saturated solution holds at that temperature. It is unstable, which means it is easily upset and will not stay as it is.
[chem8-s2.1.3] A supersaturated solution is made by cooling a hot saturated solution carefully. A solid is usually more soluble when hot, so the cooled solution is left holding more than it normally could.
[chem8-s2.1.4] Adding a small seed crystal (a tiny crystal of the solute) to a supersaturated solution makes the excess solute crystallise suddenly, because the crystal gives the dissolved solute a surface to grow on.
[chem8-s2.1.5] Examples of solutes that form supersaturated solutions: sodium thiosulfate and sodium ethanoate.
[chem8-s2.2.b] Saying a solution is strong or weak is not precise enough for chemistry. Concentration gives the amount of solute in a fixed volume, so it can be calculated with and compared.
[chem8-s2.2.0] Concentration is the amount of solute dissolved in a given volume of solution, usually in mol L⁻¹ (molarity, M, meaning moles of solute per litre) or g L⁻¹ (grams of solute per litre).
[chem8-s2.2.1] Concentration (mol L⁻¹) = moles of solute ÷ volume of solution in litres, so c = n ÷ V and n = c × V. Remember that the volume is that of the whole solution, not just the solvent.
[chem8-s2.2.2] Model: the same number of solute particles in a smaller volume gives a higher concentration, because the particles are packed more closely. Diluting spreads the same particles through a larger volume.
[chem8-s2.2.3] A standard solution is a solution whose concentration is accurately known. It is the starting point for finding the concentrations of other solutions.
[chem8-s2.3.b] Here are two calculations. One finds the concentration from a mass of solute, and the other works backwards from a concentration to the mass to weigh out. Convert cm³ to litres first by dividing by 1000.
[chem8-s2.3.0] Finding a concentration: 5.30 g of anhydrous sodium carbonate (Na₂CO₃, M = 106 g mol⁻¹) is dissolved. Anhydrous means it has no water of crystallisation. Moles: n = 5.30 ÷ 106 = 0.0500 mol.
[chem8-s2.3.1] The 0.0500 mol is made up to 500 cm³ (0.500 L). Then c = 0.0500 ÷ 0.500 = 0.100 mol L⁻¹.
[chem8-s2.3.2] Finding a mass needed: for 250 cm³ of 0.100 M sodium hydroxide (M = 40 g mol⁻¹), first find the moles from n = c × V = 0.100 × 0.250 = 0.0250 mol.
[chem8-s2.3.3] Then change moles to mass by multiplying by the molar mass (the mass of one mole): m = 0.0250 × 40 = 1.00 g.
[chem8-s2.4.b] To make a standard solution by weighing, you must trust the mass on the balance to be the true amount of the substance. Only certain substances are reliable enough, and they are called primary standards.
[chem8-s2.4.0] A primary standard is a substance that can be weighed out accurately to make a standard solution directly.
[chem8-s2.4.1] A primary standard is available in a very pure state, so the mass weighed is all the substance and not impurities.
[chem8-s2.4.2] A primary standard is stable in air: it does not absorb water or carbon dioxide, or react with oxygen. If it did, its mass would change while being weighed.
[chem8-s2.4.3] A primary standard has a known formula, so its molar mass and the moles weighed out can be calculated, and it dissolves readily in water.
[chem8-s2.4.4] A primary standard preferably has a high molar mass, which reduces weighing errors. A larger mass is needed for each mole, so a small error on the balance is a smaller fraction of the whole.
[chem8-s2.5.b] Which substance you choose depends on the type of titration (a method that reacts measured volumes of two solutions to find an unknown concentration). Some familiar laboratory chemicals fail the tests for a primary standard.
[chem8-s2.5.0] Anhydrous sodium carbonate is a primary standard used in acid–base titrations (reactions between an acid and a base).
[chem8-s2.5.1] Ammonium iron(II) sulfate, (NH₄)₂Fe(SO₄)₂·6H₂O, dissolved in dilute sulfuric acid, is a primary standard for redox titrations (reactions involving the transfer of electrons).
[chem8-s2.5.2] Sodium hydroxide is not a primary standard because it absorbs water and carbon dioxide from the air, so its mass is not the true mass of sodium hydroxide.
[chem8-s2.5.3] Hydrochloric acid is not a primary standard because its concentration changes as HCl evaporates.
[chem8-s2.5.4] Solutions of sodium hydroxide and hydrochloric acid are standardised, meaning their exact concentration is found, by titration against a primary standard.
[chem8-s2.6.b] Making a standard solution from a weighed primary standard is an investigation with a fixed method. The aim is a solution of accurately known concentration, here 250 cm³ in a volumetric flask (a flask with a single mark showing an exact volume).
[chem8-s2.6.0] 1. Weigh accurately (to 0.01 g or better) the calculated mass of the primary standard on a clock glass or weighing boat.
[chem8-s2.6.1] 2. Transfer the solid to a beaker, and rinse the clock glass into the beaker with deionised water from a wash bottle, so that none of the weighed solid is left behind.
[chem8-s2.6.2] 3. Dissolve the solid in a small volume of deionised water, stirring with a glass rod.
[chem8-s2.6.3] 4. Pour the solution through a funnel into a volumetric flask of the required volume, e.g. 250 cm³.
[chem8-s2.6.4] 5. Rinse the beaker, glass rod and funnel into the flask with deionised water so that no solute is lost.
[chem8-s2.6.5] 6. Add deionised water until the flask is near the mark, then add the last drops with a dropper until the bottom of the meniscus (the curved surface of the liquid) sits on the graduation mark, viewed at eye level.
[chem8-s2.6.6] 7. Stopper the flask and invert it about 20 times so the solution is uniform.
[chem8-s2.7.b] The choice of glassware decides how accurate the final concentration is. The diagrams below show the apparatus used in the method.
[chem8-s2.7.0] Precision and accuracy: the volumetric flask holds an accurately known volume, whereas a beaker or measuring cylinder is not accurate enough.
[chem8-s2.7.1] Diagram: a volumetric flask with its graduation mark, and the meniscus shown at eye level.
[chem8-s2.7.2] Diagram: a funnel in the neck of the volumetric flask during the transfer.
[chem8-s2.7.3] Diagram: a beaker with a glass rod stirring the dissolving solid.
[chem8-s2.8.b] A concentrated solution can be turned into a weaker one by adding solvent. The solute is not changed, only spread through more volume, which gives a simple equation to use.
[chem8-s2.8.0] Diluting adds solvent, so the number of moles of solute stays the same. This gives c₁V₁ = c₂V₂, where c₁ and V₁ are the original solution and c₂ and V₂ the diluted one.
[chem8-s2.8.1] Example: to make 250 cm³ of 0.10 M HCl from 1.0 M HCl, V₁ = (0.10 × 250) ÷ 1.0 = 25 cm³.
[chem8-s2.8.2] Pipette 25 cm³ of the 1.0 M acid into a 250 cm³ volumetric flask and make up to the mark with water.
[chem8-s2.8.3] Use a pipette (a glass tube that measures one exact volume), with a pipette filler to draw up the liquid, for the volume of the original solution, and a volumetric flask for the final volume.
[chem8-s2.8.4] Diagram: a pipette with a pipette filler, filled to the graduation mark.
[chem8-s2.9.b] Sometimes a whole range of concentrations is needed, such as for a set of test solutions. Rather than diluting from the stock solution every time, you dilute step by step.
[chem8-s2.9.0] Serial dilution is a series of dilutions, each using the previous solution, usually by the same factor.
[chem8-s2.9.1] Example: starting from 1.0 M, take 10 cm³ and make it up to 100 cm³ to give 0.10 M. Repeating from that solution gives 0.010 M and then 0.0010 M.
[chem8-s2.9.2] Serial dilution is used to make a range of concentrations accurately.
[chem8-s2.10.b] Concentration can be expressed in several ways depending on the substance and the use. Each unit says how much solute is present in a fixed amount of solution or mixture.
[chem8-s2.10.0] mol L⁻¹ (molarity, M): moles of solute per litre of solution.
[chem8-s2.10.1] g L⁻¹: grams of solute per litre of solution.
[chem8-s2.10.2] %w/v (weight per volume): grams of solute per 100 cm³ of solution. For example, 5% w/v glucose contains 5 g per 100 cm³.
[chem8-s2.10.3] %v/v (volume per volume): cm³ of solute per 100 cm³ of solution, used for a liquid dissolved in a liquid. For example, wine labelled 12% v/v contains 12 cm³ of ethanol in every 100 cm³.
[chem8-s2.10.4] %w/w (weight per weight): grams of solute per 100 g of the whole mixture, used for solids and creams. For example, 1% w/w hydrocortisone cream has 1 g in every 100 g.
[chem8-s2.11.b] The molar mass links moles to grams, and the fact that 100 cm³ is one-tenth of a litre links g L⁻¹ to %w/v. Between them you can move from any one of these units to any other.
[chem8-s2.11.0] mol L⁻¹ to g L⁻¹: multiply by the molar mass. Example: 0.10 M NaOH × 40 g mol⁻¹ = 4.0 g L⁻¹.
[chem8-s2.11.1] g L⁻¹ to mol L⁻¹: divide by the molar mass. Example: 9.8 g L⁻¹ of H₂SO₄ ÷ 98 g mol⁻¹ = 0.10 mol L⁻¹.
[chem8-s2.11.2] g L⁻¹ to %w/v: divide by 10, since 100 cm³ is one-tenth of a litre. Example: 4.0 g L⁻¹ = 0.40% w/v.
[chem8-s2.11.3] To reverse the step, multiply %w/v by 10 to get g L⁻¹.
[chem8-s2.11.4] %w/v to mol L⁻¹: multiply by 10 to get g L⁻¹, then divide by the molar mass. Example: 0.9% w/v physiological saline (NaCl, M = 58.5 g mol⁻¹): 0.9 × 10 = 9.0 g L⁻¹.
[chem8-s2.11.5] Then 9.0 g L⁻¹ ÷ 58.5 g mol⁻¹ = 0.15 mol L⁻¹.
[chem8-s2.12.b] Percentages for liquids and creams tell you how much of the active substance is in a given amount of the product. These calculations turn the percentage back into an actual volume or mass.
[chem8-s2.12.0] %v/v to volume: volume of solute = volume of solution × %v/v ÷ 100.
[chem8-s2.12.1] Example: ethanol in 750 cm³ of 12% v/v wine = 750 × 12 ÷ 100 = 90 cm³.
[chem8-s2.12.2] %v/v to mass, using density (the mass of each cm³ of the substance): mass = volume × density.
[chem8-s2.12.3] Example: 90 cm³ of ethanol × 0.789 g cm⁻³ = 71 g.
[chem8-s2.12.4] %w/w to mass: mass of solute = mass of mixture × %w/w ÷ 100.
[chem8-s2.12.5] Example: 30 g of a 1% w/w cream contains 30 × 1 ÷ 100 = 0.30 g of the active ingredient.
[chem8-s2.13.b] Some solutions are so dilute that mol L⁻¹ gives very tiny numbers. Parts per million is a convenient unit for these, and it is used for pollutants and similar substances.
[chem8-s2.13.0] p.p.m. (parts per million) is the number of parts of solute in one million parts of solution or mixture. It is used for very dilute solutions, such as pollutants and dissolved substances in water.
[chem8-s2.13.1] For dilute aqueous solutions, 1 p.p.m. = 1 mg of solute per litre of solution (1 mg L⁻¹). This holds because 1 L of water has a mass of about 1 kg, which is 10⁶ mg.
[chem8-s2.13.2] For solids, 1 p.p.m. = 1 mg per kg.
[chem8-s2.13.3] Example: 0.0050 g of a substance in 2.5 L of water. First 0.0050 g = 5.0 mg. Then 5.0 mg ÷ 2.5 L = 2.0 p.p.m.
[chem8-s2.13.4] 1 p.p.m. = 0.001 g L⁻¹ = 0.0001% w/v.
[chem8-s2.13.5] Example: 1.0 mg L⁻¹ of fluoride is 1.0 p.p.m. Fluoridated water in Ireland is about 0.6–0.8 p.p.m. (mg L⁻¹).
[chem8-s2.13.6] p.p.m. to mol L⁻¹: convert to g L⁻¹ first, e.g. 2.0 p.p.m. = 2.0 mg L⁻¹ = 0.0020 g L⁻¹, then divide by the molar mass.

CANDIDATES chem6-s2: 2. Polarity, shape and physical properties
[chem6-s2.0.b] Melting and boiling are about the attractions between molecules, not the bonds inside them. Intermolecular forces (the attractions between separate molecules) decide how much energy a molecular substance needs to change state.
[chem6-s2.0.0] To melt or boil a molecular substance (one made of separate small molecules), energy must be supplied to overcome the intermolecular forces, so the molecules can move apart.
[chem6-s2.0.1] The stronger the intermolecular forces, the higher the melting and boiling points, because more energy is needed to pull the molecules apart.
[chem6-s2.0.2] Substances with only London dispersion forces (weak attractions caused by temporary uneven electron distribution) between small molecules are gases at room temperature, because the forces are too weak to hold them together. Methane is an example: it boils at −162 °C.
[chem6-s2.0.3] Group 14 hydrides (compounds of hydrogen with the group 14 elements) rise steadily in boiling point: CH₄ < SiH₄ < GeH₄ < SnH₄.
[chem6-s2.0.4] Hydrogen bonding (a strong intermolecular attraction involving hydrogen) gives unusually high boiling points. Water boils at 100 °C, but hydrogen sulfide, a heavier molecule with no hydrogen bonding, boils at −60 °C.
[chem6-s2.1.b] Whether one substance dissolves in another depends on the attractions between their particles. The solvent is the liquid that does the dissolving and the solute is the substance that dissolves.
[chem6-s2.1.0] A substance dissolves when the attractions between solvent and solute particles are similar to or stronger than the attractions they replace. This is summed up as “like dissolves like”.
[chem6-s2.1.1] Polar substances (those whose molecules have a slightly positive end and a slightly negative end) and hydrogen-bonding substances such as ethanol, ammonia and sugar dissolve in water, because they form hydrogen bonds with water molecules.
[chem6-s2.1.2] Non-polar substances such as iodine, hexane and oils do not dissolve in water. They dissolve in non-polar solvents such as cyclohexane, where the London forces are similar to those they replace.
[chem6-s2.1.3] Hexane and water do not mix, which means they form two layers.
[chem6-s2.2.b] Water is a very small molecule, yet it behaves unlike other small molecules. The reason is hydrogen bonding between its molecules.
[chem6-s2.2.0] Water has a high boiling point and a high melting point for such a small molecule, because the hydrogen bonds between molecules take a lot of energy to overcome.
[chem6-s2.2.1] Ice is less dense than liquid water: hydrogen bonds hold the molecules in an open lattice (a regular arrangement with gaps between molecules), so ice floats.
[chem6-s2.2.2] Water is a good solvent for ionic and polar substances, because it can form attractions with them.
[chem6-s2.3.b] Secondary data means results taken from tables or graphs rather than measured yourself. The boiling points below (bp) are the sample data you may be asked to interpret.
[chem6-s2.3.0] Noble gas boiling points (the noble gases are the group 18 elements): He −269 °C; Ne −246 °C; Ar −186 °C; Kr −153 °C; Xe −108 °C.
[chem6-s2.3.1] Halogens (the group 17 elements) at room temperature: fluorine and chlorine are gases, bromine is a liquid and iodine is a solid.
[chem6-s2.3.2] Alkane (hydrocarbon) boiling points: methane −162 °C; ethane −89 °C; propane −42 °C; butane −1 °C; pentane 36 °C; hexane 69 °C.
[chem6-s2.3.3] Group 16 hydride boiling points: H₂O 100 °C; H₂S −60 °C; H₂Se −41 °C; H₂Te −2 °C.
[chem6-s2.3.4] Group 15 and 17 hydride boiling points: NH₃ −33 °C vs PH₃ −88 °C; HF 20 °C vs HCl −85 °C.
[chem6-s2.3.5] Compounds of similar mass: ethanol (bp 78 °C, hydrogen bonding) vs propane (bp −42 °C, London forces only).
[chem6-s2.4.b] At Higher Level you must explain why the boiling points in the data follow their patterns. Two ideas do all the work: London forces grow with molecule size, and hydrogen bonding lifts a boiling point far above the trend.
[chem6-s2.4.0] Down group 18, down group 17 and along the alkane series, boiling points rise because the molecules have more electrons and larger electron clouds, so London dispersion forces get stronger.
[chem6-s2.4.1] In the group 16 hydrides, boiling point rises from H₂S to H₂Te as London forces increase with size. Water is far higher than the trend predicts because of hydrogen bonding.
[chem6-s2.4.2] The same anomaly (a value far out of line with the trend) appears for NH₃ in group 15 and HF in group 17, which both form hydrogen bonds.
[chem6-s2.4.3] Molecules of similar mass have higher boiling points if they are polar (attracted by dipole–dipole forces, the attractions between opposite ends of polar molecules), and much higher ones if they hydrogen bond, e.g. ethanol compared with propane.
[chem6-s2.4.4] Diagram: a graph of boiling point against period for the hydrides of groups 14, 15, 16 and 17, showing the high values of H₂O, HF and NH₃ and the steady rise from CH₄ to SnH₄.
[chem6-s2.5.b] Some bonds share electrons unequally, but that does not always make the whole molecule polar. Whether it does depends on the shape. The shapes named here are given; see “VSEPR theory” for how shapes are predicted.
[chem6-s2.5.0] A polar bond has an uneven share of electrons, giving a slightly positive end (δ+) and a slightly negative end (δ−).
[chem6-s2.5.1] A polar molecule has an overall uneven distribution of charge, with a δ+ end and a δ− end.
[chem6-s2.5.2] Whether a molecule is polar depends on both its bonds and its shape: polar bonds arranged symmetrically cancel out, so the molecule has no δ+ or δ− end.
[chem6-s2.6.b] A dipole is the pair of δ+ and δ− ends of a polar bond. In a symmetrical molecule the dipoles pull in opposite directions and cancel, while in an unsymmetrical molecule they add up.
[chem6-s2.6.0] Carbon dioxide: each C=O bond is polar, but the molecule is linear (O=C=O, in a straight line), so the two bond polarities point in opposite directions and cancel; CO₂ is non-polar.
[chem6-s2.6.1] Tetrachloromethane (CCl₄): four polar C–Cl bonds arranged tetrahedrally (pointing to the corners of a tetrahedron) cancel; CCl₄ is non-polar.
[chem6-s2.6.2] Boron trifluoride (BF₃): three polar B–F bonds at 120° in a trigonal plane (flat, evenly spaced) cancel; BF₃ is non-polar.
[chem6-s2.6.3] Water: two polar O–H bonds in a V-shaped (bent) molecule do not cancel, so water is polar, with δ− on oxygen.
[chem6-s2.6.4] Ammonia: three polar N–H bonds in a pyramidal molecule (a shape like a pyramid, with nitrogen at the top) do not cancel, so NH₃ is polar.
[chem6-s2.6.5] Trichloromethane (CHCl₃): the C–H bond differs from the three C–Cl bonds, so the tetrahedral molecule is unsymmetrical and polar.
[chem6-s2.6.6] Diagram: CO₂, CCl₄, H₂O and NH₃, with arrows (or δ+ and δ− labels) on each bond showing whether the polarities cancel; label each molecule polar or non-polar.
[chem6-s2.7.b] Polarity decides which intermolecular forces act between molecules. Those forces then set the boiling point and the solubility.
[chem6-s2.7.0] Polar molecules attract each other by permanent dipole–dipole forces (or hydrogen bonds) as well as London dispersion forces, so they have stronger intermolecular forces than non-polar molecules of similar size.
[chem6-s2.7.1] Non-polar molecules, including symmetrical ones with polar bonds, have only London dispersion forces between them. For example, carbon dioxide is a gas at room temperature, and tetrachloromethane is immiscible with water (does not mix with it).
[chem6-s2.7.2] A symmetrical molecule usually has a lower boiling point and lower solubility in water than an unsymmetrical one of similar size, because its forces between molecules are weaker.
[chem6-s2.7.3] Tetrachloromethane is immiscible with water, while trichloromethane is slightly soluble; both dissolve in non-polar solvents.
[chem6-s2.8.b] A molecule can contain polar bonds and still be non-polar overall. What matters is the shape. These shapes are stated, not derived.
[chem6-s2.8.0] A molecule with polar bonds is polar overall only if its shape is unsymmetrical, so that its charge is unevenly spread.
[chem6-s2.8.1] Water is V-shaped (bent), so it is polar.
[chem6-s2.8.2] Ammonia is pyramidal, so it is polar.
[chem6-s2.8.3] Carbon dioxide is linear and methane is tetrahedral: both are non-polar.
[chem6-s2.9.b] Once you know a molecule's shape and polarity, you can predict its boiling point and what it dissolves in.
[chem6-s2.9.0] Polar molecules have stronger forces between them than non-polar molecules of similar size, so they have higher boiling points.
[chem6-s2.9.1] Polar molecules tend to dissolve in polar solvents such as water; non-polar molecules dissolve in non-polar solvents such as cyclohexane.
[chem6-s2.9.2] Water is a liquid at room temperature and dissolves salts, because it is polar and forms hydrogen bonds; methane is a non-polar gas and is almost insoluble in water.
[chem6-s2.9.3] Ammonia, which is polar, is very soluble in water; carbon dioxide, which is non-polar, is only slightly soluble.
[chem6-s2.10.b] Polar molecules respond to an electric charge and non-polar ones do not. A charged rod held beside a thin stream of liquid shows which kind a liquid is made of.
[chem6-s2.10.0] Aim: to show whether a liquid is made of polar molecules.
[chem6-s2.10.1] Apparatus: a burette (a long graduated tube with a tap) clamped above a beaker, a polythene (or acetate) rod, a cloth, and liquids such as water and cyclohexane.
[chem6-s2.10.2] Diagram: a burette clamped above a beaker, releasing a thin stream of liquid; label the burette, the beaker and the stream.
[chem6-s2.10.3] Diagram: a charged polythene rod held beside the stream, with the stream bending towards it; label the rod, the stream and the direction of deflection (the sideways bend).
[chem6-s2.10.4] Independent variable (the one you change): the liquid tested.
[chem6-s2.10.5] Dependent variable (the one you observe): whether the stream is deflected.
[chem6-s2.10.6] Controlled variables (kept the same): the same rod and cloth, with the rod recharged before each test, so that every liquid meets a charged rod.
[chem6-s2.11.b] The method is the same for each liquid, and comparing the two streams gives the conclusion.
[chem6-s2.11.0] Step 1: Charge the rod by rubbing it with the cloth.
[chem6-s2.11.1] Step 2: Let a thin stream of the liquid run from the burette, and hold the rod close to the stream without touching it.
[chem6-s2.11.2] Step 3: Repeat with the other liquid, recharging the rod each time.
[chem6-s2.11.3] Result: the stream of water is deflected (attracted) towards the rod; the stream of cyclohexane is not deflected.
[chem6-s2.11.4] The water stream is attracted to the rod whether the rod is charged positively or negatively, because the molecules rotate.
[chem6-s2.11.5] Explanation: polar molecules turn so that their oppositely charged end faces the rod and are attracted to it; non-polar molecules are not attracted.
[chem6-s2.11.6] Safety: cyclohexane is flammable and harmful, so use no flames, small volumes and good ventilation, and collect it for disposal.
