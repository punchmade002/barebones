TARGET chem25-s6: 6. Surfactants and soap
[chem25-s6.0.b] Water on its own is poor at cleaning greasy things, because water and oil do not mix. A surfactant is the kind of substance that fixes this, and soap is the best-known example.
[chem25-s6.0.0] A surfactant (surface-active agent) is a substance whose molecules or ions have a water-attracting (hydrophilic) part and a water-repelling (hydrophobic) part, so one particle can interact with both water and oil.
[chem25-s6.0.1] Dissolved in water, a surfactant lowers the surface tension (the pull between surface water molecules that makes water bead up), which helps water wet surfaces and mix with oil and grease.
[chem25-s6.0.2] Soap is a surfactant: the sodium or potassium salt of a long-chain carboxylic acid, called a fatty acid (an acid with a long hydrocarbon chain ending in –COOH).
[chem25-s6.0.3] Sodium stearate, C₁₇H₃₅COO⁻Na⁺, is sodium octadecanoate, a typical soap.
[chem25-s6.0.4] Potassium soaps are softer and are used in liquid soaps.
[chem25-s6.1.b] A soap ion has two ends with opposite attitudes to water. This split personality is what lets soap clean, and it is the basis of any question on how soap works.
[chem25-s6.1.0] The head, the carboxylate group –COO⁻, is ionic and hydrophilic: it is attracted to polar water molecules (molecules with slightly positive and negative ends) by ion–dipole attraction, and so dissolves in water.
[chem25-s6.1.1] The tail is a long hydrocarbon chain of about 12–18 carbon atoms; it is non-polar and hydrophobic, so it does not mix with water but dissolves in grease and oil through London dispersion forces (weak attractions between non-polar molecules).
[chem25-s6.1.2] This dual nature lets one soap ion interact with both water and grease.
[chem25-s6.1.3] Soap can dissolve the non-polar oils on skin, and through its ionic heads and the water it also removes the salts in sweat.
[chem25-s6.1.4] Diagram: a soap ion drawn as a long zig-zag tail, with a circled –COO⁻ head and a separate Na⁺ ion, labelled hydrophobic tail and hydrophilic head.
[chem25-s6.2.b] Grease holds dirt to skin and fabric, and plain water cannot lift it. Soap works in a fixed sequence, ending with the grease trapped in tiny spheres that rinse away.
[chem25-s6.2.0] 1. In water, soap lowers the surface tension, so the water spreads over and wets the greasy surface or fabric instead of beading up.
[chem25-s6.2.1] 2. The hydrophobic tails dissolve in the grease or oil, while the hydrophilic heads stay in the water at the surface of the grease.
[chem25-s6.2.2] 3. Rubbing or agitation breaks the grease into tiny droplets, each surrounded by soap ions.
[chem25-s6.2.3] 4. Each droplet becomes a micelle: a sphere with the grease and the tails inside, and the negatively charged heads facing out into the water.
[chem25-s6.2.4] 5. The negative surfaces of the micelles repel one another, so the droplets do not join up again and stay dispersed in the water as an emulsion (tiny droplets of one liquid spread through another); this is emulsification.
[chem25-s6.2.5] 6. Rinsing carries the micelles away, with the dirt and microorganisms trapped in the grease.
[chem25-s6.2.6] Diagram: a micelle, with an oil droplet in the centre, soap tails pointing inward into it, and heads (–COO⁻) on the outside in water.
[chem25-s6.3.b] Soap does not work equally well in all water. In hard water it is partly wasted, because ions in the water react with it.
[chem25-s6.3.0] Hard water contains Ca²⁺ and Mg²⁺ ions, for example water from limestone regions.
[chem25-s6.3.1] These ions react with soap ions to form an insoluble scum (a solid that does not dissolve): 2C₁₇H₃₅COO⁻(aq) + Ca²⁺(aq) → (C₁₇H₃₅COO)₂Ca(s).
[chem25-s6.3.2] In hard water, soap forms scum and lathers poorly until all the hardness ions have been precipitated (removed as solid), which wastes soap.
[chem25-s6.3.3] In deionised, soft water (water with the ions removed), soap lathers easily.
[chem25-s6.4.b] Chemists solved the scum problem by changing the head of the surfactant. Surfactants can then be sorted into groups by the charge on that head.
[chem25-s6.4.0] Synthetic detergents, also called soapless detergents, have a similar head-and-tail structure but a different head, e.g. a sulfonate group (–SO₃⁻) in alkylbenzene sulfonates.
[chem25-s6.4.1] Their calcium and magnesium salts are soluble, so synthetic detergents work well in hard water and form no scum.
[chem25-s6.4.2] Surfactants are grouped by their head: anionic (negative head), e.g. soaps and sulfonates in washing-up liquid and laundry detergent.
[chem25-s6.4.3] Cationic surfactants have a positive head, e.g. quaternary ammonium compounds in fabric conditioners and some disinfectants.
[chem25-s6.4.4] Non-ionic surfactants have an uncharged head and are used in many cleaning products.
[chem25-s6.5.b] Any job that needs oil and water to mix, or a liquid to spread over a surface, uses a surfactant. The uses fall into four groups.
[chem25-s6.5.0] Cleaning: bar and liquid soaps, hand wash, shower gel, shampoo, washing-up liquid, laundry detergent, and household and industrial surface cleaners.
[chem25-s6.5.1] Personal care: toothpaste, where sodium lauryl sulfate is the foaming agent; shampoo; and cosmetics and creams, where surfactants act as emulsifiers (substances that keep oil and water mixed).
[chem25-s6.5.2] Food: emulsifiers such as lecithin, a natural emulsifier from soya or egg yolk, keep oil and water mixed in mayonnaise, chocolate and margarine.
[chem25-s6.5.3] Other uses: fabric conditioners; wetting agents (substances that help a liquid spread over a surface) in paints, inks and agricultural sprays, so that the spray spreads over leaves; firefighting foams; and dispersants that break up oil spills at sea.
[chem25-s6.6.b] Surfactants have made a major contribution to public health, because they remove microorganisms and the grease that shelters them.
[chem25-s6.6.0] Handwashing with soap removes microorganisms from the skin, trapping them in micelles that are rinsed away.
[chem25-s6.6.1] Handwashing is one of the most effective ways of preventing the spread of infectious diseases such as diarrhoeal diseases, respiratory infections and influenza.
[chem25-s6.6.2] Soap destroys enveloped viruses (viruses with a fatty outer layer), such as influenza viruses and SARS-CoV-2, the virus that causes COVID-19: the hydrophobic tails insert into the virus's fatty lipid outer membrane and break it apart.
[chem25-s6.6.3] Public-health advice during the COVID-19 pandemic was to wash hands with soap and water, scrubbing for at least 20 seconds.
[chem25-s6.6.4] Detergents used in laundry, dishwashing, hospitals and food processing remove the grease that shelters bacteria, improving hygiene in homes, hospitals and the food industry.
[chem25-s6.7.b] Surfactants also caused pollution problems, and the solutions show how detergent design changed. These facts belong here only as impacts of surfactant use.
[chem25-s6.7.0] Early synthetic detergents with branched hydrocarbon chains were not biodegradable (microorganisms could not break them down), so they caused foam on rivers in the 1950s and 1960s; they were replaced by biodegradable linear-chain detergents.
[chem25-s6.7.1] Phosphates added to some detergents as builders (additives that improve cleaning) can cause eutrophication (over-enrichment with nutrients) of lakes and rivers, so their use has been restricted, e.g. in laundry detergents in the EU.
[chem25-s6.7.2] The EU restricted phosphates in household laundry detergents from 2013 and in household dishwasher detergents from 2017.
[chem25-s6.8.b] Soap can be made at small scale from kitchen materials. The reaction splits a fat or oil into soap and glycerol using sodium hydroxide.
[chem25-s6.8.0] Aim: to make soap on a small scale from household reactants, by base hydrolysis (saponification, the splitting of a fat or oil by an alkali) with sodium hydroxide, and to compare the product when sodium hydroxide is limiting with when it is in excess.
[chem25-s6.8.1] Word equation: fat or oil + sodium hydroxide gives soap (the sodium salts of fatty acids) + glycerol.
[chem25-s6.8.2] One mole of fat reacts with 3 moles of NaOH, because each fat molecule has three ester links to break.
[chem25-s6.8.3] Household reactants: a vegetable oil or fat, e.g. olive, sunflower or coconut oil, or lard; sodium hydroxide, sold as caustic soda; water; and table salt for brine (concentrated salt solution).
[chem25-s6.8.4] A reflux method (boiling with a condenser fitted to return the vapour) is not required: the mixture is heated gently in an open container in a water bath.
[chem25-s6.9.b] The method has two halves: making the soap by heating, then separating it from the other products with brine and washing.
[chem25-s6.9.0] 1. Weigh a known mass of oil, e.g. 10 g, into a beaker or evaporating basin.
[chem25-s6.9.1] 2. Add a measured volume of sodium hydroxide solution of known concentration; a little ethanol, if available, can be added as a solvent in which both the oil and the sodium hydroxide dissolve, so that they mix and react faster.
[chem25-s6.9.2] 3. Heat gently in a water bath, stirring continuously, for about 20–30 minutes, adding a little water if the mixture dries out; the mixture becomes thick and creamy as soap forms.
[chem25-s6.9.3] 4. Pour the mixture into a beaker of saturated salt solution (brine) and stir; the soap separates as solid curds, because soap is much less soluble in concentrated salt solution than in water; this is salting out.
[chem25-s6.9.4] 5. Collect the soap by filtration or by skimming it off, and wash it with a little ice-cold water, because warm water would dissolve the soap.
[chem25-s6.9.5] 6. Press the soap dry between filter papers, leave it to dry and weigh it.
[chem25-s6.9.6] 7. Repeat with the same mass of oil but a different amount of sodium hydroxide, so that one batch has NaOH in excess and the other has NaOH limiting (the calculation block works an example).
[chem25-s6.9.7] Diagram: a beaker of oil and sodium hydroxide solution in a water bath, with a stirring rod and a thermometer, and a filter funnel collecting the soap from the brine.
[chem25-s6.10.b] Simple tests show whether the product behaves like soap and how the amount of sodium hydroxide affected it.
[chem25-s6.10.0] Shake a little soap with deionised water: it forms a lather, and the solution is slightly alkaline, about pH 9–10.
[chem25-s6.10.1] Shake a little soap with hard water, e.g. mineral water from a limestone region: a scum forms and there is little lather.
[chem25-s6.10.2] Test a solution of each batch with universal indicator or a pH meter: soap made with excess NaOH has a much higher pH, unless the NaOH has been washed out.
[chem25-s6.10.3] Look and feel the soap, wearing gloves: soap from a batch with limiting NaOH is greasy and soft, with an oily layer of unreacted oil.
[chem25-s6.10.4] The glycerol co-product (the second product of the reaction) and any excess sodium hydroxide dissolve in the brine filtrate (the liquid that passes through the filter), not in the washed soap.
[chem25-s6.11.b] Each choice in the method has a reason, and exam questions ask for it directly.
[chem25-s6.11.0] Heating speeds up the hydrolysis (the splitting of the fat by water and alkali), which is slow at room temperature; a water bath gives gentle, even heat and avoids a flame, because ethanol is flammable.
[chem25-s6.11.1] Stirring mixes the oil with the aqueous sodium hydroxide (sodium hydroxide dissolved in water), which do not mix by themselves, so they keep reacting.
[chem25-s6.11.2] Brine separates the soap from glycerol, water and excess NaOH, which all stay in solution.
[chem25-s6.11.3] Washing with ice-cold water removes NaOH and glycerol from the soap surface without dissolving much soap.
[chem25-s6.12.b] Sodium hydroxide is the main hazard in this activity. The errors listed below also explain why the mass of soap you get is not exact.
[chem25-s6.12.0] Sodium hydroxide is corrosive and especially harmful to the eyes: wear goggles and gloves.
[chem25-s6.12.1] Add the sodium hydroxide carefully, because dissolving NaOH in water is exothermic (it gives out heat).
[chem25-s6.12.2] Do not use the product on skin, because it may contain unreacted sodium hydroxide.
[chem25-s6.12.3] Sources of error include soap lost in the brine and washings, wet soap being weighed, which gives too high a mass, and a reaction not completed in the time allowed.
[chem25-s6.12.4] Household oils are mixtures, so the mass of NaOH needed can only be estimated.
[chem25-s6.13.b] A reagent is limiting when it runs out first and so sets how much product forms. Whether the sodium hydroxide or the fat runs out first changes the quality of the soap.
[chem25-s6.13.0] If NaOH is limiting, not all the fat is hydrolysed: the yield of soap is set by the moles of NaOH, and unreacted fat or oil remains.
[chem25-s6.13.1] The soap made with limiting NaOH is greasy and soft, and the leftover oil is wasted and can go rancid (turn foul-smelling and spoiled).
[chem25-s6.13.2] If NaOH is in excess, all the fat is converted, giving the maximum yield of soap from the fat.
[chem25-s6.13.3] The soap made with excess NaOH contains free sodium hydroxide, which is caustic, has a high pH and irritates or burns skin.
[chem25-s6.13.4] Excess NaOH must be removed, by washing the soap with brine or water, or by neutralisation (reacting it with an acid).
[chem25-s6.13.5] Excess NaOH is also wasted reagent and makes the waste liquid strongly alkaline.
[chem25-s6.14.b] Because both too little and too much sodium hydroxide cause problems, soap makers work out the amount carefully.
[chem25-s6.14.0] Manufacturers calculate the exact amount of NaOH needed for the fat used, then wash the soap with brine to remove any excess.
[chem25-s6.14.1] Small-scale soap makers often use slightly less NaOH than needed, leaving a little unreacted oil, called superfatting, so that no free NaOH remains and the soap is gentler.
[chem25-s6.14.2] The amount of NaOH needed follows from the 1 : 3 ratio of fat to NaOH; for a real oil it can only be estimated, because oils are mixtures of different tri-esters (molecules with three ester links).
[chem25-s6.15.b] This worked example uses sample data and a model fat to find the soap made when NaOH is in excess and when it is limiting. Remember that the fat reacts with NaOH in a 1 : 3 mole ratio.
[chem25-s6.15.0] Model fat: glyceryl tristearate, M = 890 g mol⁻¹; sodium stearate (soap), M = 306 g mol⁻¹; NaOH, M = 40 g mol⁻¹. Sample data.
[chem25-s6.15.1] 8.90 g of fat = 8.90 ÷ 890 = 0.0100 mol, which needs 3 × 0.0100 = 0.0300 mol of NaOH = 1.20 g.
[chem25-s6.15.2] Batch 1, NaOH in excess: 2.00 g of NaOH = 0.0500 mol; 0.0300 mol reacts, leaving 0.0200 mol (0.800 g) over. The fat is limiting, so soap = 3 × 0.0100 = 0.0300 mol × 306 = 9.18 g.
[chem25-s6.15.3] Batch 2, NaOH limiting: 1.00 g of NaOH = 0.0250 mol, less than the 0.0300 mol needed. Soap = 0.0250 mol × 306 = 7.65 g.
[chem25-s6.15.4] In batch 2, fat used = 0.0250 ÷ 3 = 0.00833 mol, so fat left unreacted = 0.0100 − 0.00833 = 0.001667 mol; keep the extra figure until the end, then 0.001667 × 890 = 1.48 g.
[chem25-s6.15.5] Conclusion: batch 1 gives more soap, 9.18 g, but contains 0.800 g of corrosive NaOH that must be washed out; batch 2 gives less soap, 7.65 g, mixed with 1.48 g of unreacted fat.

CANDIDATES chem6-s2: 2. Polarity, shape and physical properties
[chem6-s2.0.b] Melting and boiling are about the attractions between molecules, not the bonds inside them. Intermolecular forces (the attractions between separate molecules) decide how much energy a molecular substance needs to change state.
[chem6-s2.0.0] To melt or boil a molecular substance (one made of separate small molecules), energy must be supplied to overcome the intermolecular forces, so the molecules can move apart.
[chem6-s2.0.1] The stronger the intermolecular forces, the higher the melting and boiling points, because more energy is needed to pull the molecules apart.
[chem6-s2.0.2] Substances with only London dispersion forces (weak attractions caused by temporary uneven electron distribution) between small molecules are gases at room temperature, because the forces are too weak to hold them together. Methane is an example: it boils at −162 °C.
[chem6-s2.0.3] Group 14 hydrides (compounds of hydrogen with the group 14 elements) rise steadily in boiling point: CH₄ < SiH₄ < GeH₄ < SnH₄.
[chem6-s2.0.4] Hydrogen bonding (a strong intermolecular attraction involving hydrogen) gives unusually high boiling points. Water boils at 100 °C, but hydrogen sulfide, a heavier molecule with no hydrogen bonding, boils at −60 °C.
[chem6-s2.1.b] Whether one substance dissolves in another depends on the attractions between their particles. The solvent is the liquid that does the dissolving and the solute is the substance that dissolves.
[chem6-s2.1.0] A substance dissolves when the attractions between solvent and solute particles are similar to or stronger than the attractions they replace. This is summed up as “like dissolves like”.
[chem6-s2.1.1] Polar substances (those whose molecules have a slightly positive end and a slightly negative end) and hydrogen-bonding substances such as ethanol, ammonia and sugar dissolve in water, because they form hydrogen bonds with water molecules.
[chem6-s2.1.2] Non-polar substances such as iodine, hexane and oils do not dissolve in water. They dissolve in non-polar solvents such as cyclohexane, where the London forces are similar to those they replace.
[chem6-s2.1.3] Hexane and water do not mix, which means they form two layers.
[chem6-s2.2.b] Water is a very small molecule, yet it behaves unlike other small molecules. The reason is hydrogen bonding between its molecules.
[chem6-s2.2.0] Water has a high boiling point and a high melting point for such a small molecule, because the hydrogen bonds between molecules take a lot of energy to overcome.
[chem6-s2.2.1] Ice is less dense than liquid water: hydrogen bonds hold the molecules in an open lattice (a regular arrangement with gaps between molecules), so ice floats.
[chem6-s2.2.2] Water is a good solvent for ionic and polar substances, because it can form attractions with them.
[chem6-s2.3.b] Secondary data means results taken from tables or graphs rather than measured yourself. The boiling points below (bp) are the sample data you may be asked to interpret.
[chem6-s2.3.0] Noble gas boiling points (the noble gases are the group 18 elements): He −269 °C; Ne −246 °C; Ar −186 °C; Kr −153 °C; Xe −108 °C.
[chem6-s2.3.1] Halogens (the group 17 elements) at room temperature: fluorine and chlorine are gases, bromine is a liquid and iodine is a solid.
[chem6-s2.3.2] Alkane (hydrocarbon) boiling points: methane −162 °C; ethane −89 °C; propane −42 °C; butane −1 °C; pentane 36 °C; hexane 69 °C.
[chem6-s2.3.3] Group 16 hydride boiling points: H₂O 100 °C; H₂S −60 °C; H₂Se −41 °C; H₂Te −2 °C.
[chem6-s2.3.4] Group 15 and 17 hydride boiling points: NH₃ −33 °C vs PH₃ −88 °C; HF 20 °C vs HCl −85 °C.
[chem6-s2.3.5] Compounds of similar mass: ethanol (bp 78 °C, hydrogen bonding) vs propane (bp −42 °C, London forces only).
[chem6-s2.4.b] At Higher Level you must explain why the boiling points in the data follow their patterns. Two ideas do all the work: London forces grow with molecule size, and hydrogen bonding lifts a boiling point far above the trend.
[chem6-s2.4.0] Down group 18, down group 17 and along the alkane series, boiling points rise because the molecules have more electrons and larger electron clouds, so London dispersion forces get stronger.
[chem6-s2.4.1] In the group 16 hydrides, boiling point rises from H₂S to H₂Te as London forces increase with size. Water is far higher than the trend predicts because of hydrogen bonding.
[chem6-s2.4.2] The same anomaly (a value far out of line with the trend) appears for NH₃ in group 15 and HF in group 17, which both form hydrogen bonds.
[chem6-s2.4.3] Molecules of similar mass have higher boiling points if they are polar (attracted by dipole–dipole forces, the attractions between opposite ends of polar molecules), and much higher ones if they hydrogen bond, e.g. ethanol compared with propane.
[chem6-s2.4.4] Diagram: a graph of boiling point against period for the hydrides of groups 14, 15, 16 and 17, showing the high values of H₂O, HF and NH₃ and the steady rise from CH₄ to SnH₄.
[chem6-s2.5.b] Some bonds share electrons unequally, but that does not always make the whole molecule polar. Whether it does depends on the shape. The shapes named here are given; see “VSEPR theory” for how shapes are predicted.
[chem6-s2.5.0] A polar bond has an uneven share of electrons, giving a slightly positive end (δ+) and a slightly negative end (δ−).
[chem6-s2.5.1] A polar molecule has an overall uneven distribution of charge, with a δ+ end and a δ− end.
[chem6-s2.5.2] Whether a molecule is polar depends on both its bonds and its shape: polar bonds arranged symmetrically cancel out, so the molecule has no δ+ or δ− end.
[chem6-s2.6.b] A dipole is the pair of δ+ and δ− ends of a polar bond. In a symmetrical molecule the dipoles pull in opposite directions and cancel, while in an unsymmetrical molecule they add up.
[chem6-s2.6.0] Carbon dioxide: each C=O bond is polar, but the molecule is linear (O=C=O, in a straight line), so the two bond polarities point in opposite directions and cancel; CO₂ is non-polar.
[chem6-s2.6.1] Tetrachloromethane (CCl₄): four polar C–Cl bonds arranged tetrahedrally (pointing to the corners of a tetrahedron) cancel; CCl₄ is non-polar.
[chem6-s2.6.2] Boron trifluoride (BF₃): three polar B–F bonds at 120° in a trigonal plane (flat, evenly spaced) cancel; BF₃ is non-polar.
[chem6-s2.6.3] Water: two polar O–H bonds in a V-shaped (bent) molecule do not cancel, so water is polar, with δ− on oxygen.
[chem6-s2.6.4] Ammonia: three polar N–H bonds in a pyramidal molecule (a shape like a pyramid, with nitrogen at the top) do not cancel, so NH₃ is polar.
[chem6-s2.6.5] Trichloromethane (CHCl₃): the C–H bond differs from the three C–Cl bonds, so the tetrahedral molecule is unsymmetrical and polar.
[chem6-s2.6.6] Diagram: CO₂, CCl₄, H₂O and NH₃, with arrows (or δ+ and δ− labels) on each bond showing whether the polarities cancel; label each molecule polar or non-polar.
[chem6-s2.7.b] Polarity decides which intermolecular forces act between molecules. Those forces then set the boiling point and the solubility.
[chem6-s2.7.0] Polar molecules attract each other by permanent dipole–dipole forces (or hydrogen bonds) as well as London dispersion forces, so they have stronger intermolecular forces than non-polar molecules of similar size.
[chem6-s2.7.1] Non-polar molecules, including symmetrical ones with polar bonds, have only London dispersion forces between them. For example, carbon dioxide is a gas at room temperature, and tetrachloromethane is immiscible with water (does not mix with it).
[chem6-s2.7.2] A symmetrical molecule usually has a lower boiling point and lower solubility in water than an unsymmetrical one of similar size, because its forces between molecules are weaker.
[chem6-s2.7.3] Tetrachloromethane is immiscible with water, while trichloromethane is slightly soluble; both dissolve in non-polar solvents.
[chem6-s2.8.b] A molecule can contain polar bonds and still be non-polar overall. What matters is the shape. These shapes are stated, not derived.
[chem6-s2.8.0] A molecule with polar bonds is polar overall only if its shape is unsymmetrical, so that its charge is unevenly spread.
[chem6-s2.8.1] Water is V-shaped (bent), so it is polar.
[chem6-s2.8.2] Ammonia is pyramidal, so it is polar.
[chem6-s2.8.3] Carbon dioxide is linear and methane is tetrahedral: both are non-polar.
[chem6-s2.9.b] Once you know a molecule's shape and polarity, you can predict its boiling point and what it dissolves in.
[chem6-s2.9.0] Polar molecules have stronger forces between them than non-polar molecules of similar size, so they have higher boiling points.
[chem6-s2.9.1] Polar molecules tend to dissolve in polar solvents such as water; non-polar molecules dissolve in non-polar solvents such as cyclohexane.
[chem6-s2.9.2] Water is a liquid at room temperature and dissolves salts, because it is polar and forms hydrogen bonds; methane is a non-polar gas and is almost insoluble in water.
[chem6-s2.9.3] Ammonia, which is polar, is very soluble in water; carbon dioxide, which is non-polar, is only slightly soluble.
[chem6-s2.10.b] Polar molecules respond to an electric charge and non-polar ones do not. A charged rod held beside a thin stream of liquid shows which kind a liquid is made of.
[chem6-s2.10.0] Aim: to show whether a liquid is made of polar molecules.
[chem6-s2.10.1] Apparatus: a burette (a long graduated tube with a tap) clamped above a beaker, a polythene (or acetate) rod, a cloth, and liquids such as water and cyclohexane.
[chem6-s2.10.2] Diagram: a burette clamped above a beaker, releasing a thin stream of liquid; label the burette, the beaker and the stream.
[chem6-s2.10.3] Diagram: a charged polythene rod held beside the stream, with the stream bending towards it; label the rod, the stream and the direction of deflection (the sideways bend).
[chem6-s2.10.4] Independent variable (the one you change): the liquid tested.
[chem6-s2.10.5] Dependent variable (the one you observe): whether the stream is deflected.
[chem6-s2.10.6] Controlled variables (kept the same): the same rod and cloth, with the rod recharged before each test, so that every liquid meets a charged rod.
[chem6-s2.11.b] The method is the same for each liquid, and comparing the two streams gives the conclusion.
[chem6-s2.11.0] Step 1: Charge the rod by rubbing it with the cloth.
[chem6-s2.11.1] Step 2: Let a thin stream of the liquid run from the burette, and hold the rod close to the stream without touching it.
[chem6-s2.11.2] Step 3: Repeat with the other liquid, recharging the rod each time.
[chem6-s2.11.3] Result: the stream of water is deflected (attracted) towards the rod; the stream of cyclohexane is not deflected.
[chem6-s2.11.4] The water stream is attracted to the rod whether the rod is charged positively or negatively, because the molecules rotate.
[chem6-s2.11.5] Explanation: polar molecules turn so that their oppositely charged end faces the rod and are attracted to it; non-polar molecules are not attracted.
[chem6-s2.11.6] Safety: cyclohexane is flammable and harmful, so use no flames, small volumes and good ventilation, and collect it for disposal.

CANDIDATES chem26-s3: 3. Limiting reagents
[chem26-s3.0.b] In a real reaction the reactants are rarely mixed in exactly the proportions of the equation. One of them runs out first, and that one controls how much product you get.
[chem26-s3.0.0] Limiting reagent (limiting reactant): the reactant that is completely used up first in a reaction. It decides the maximum amount of product that can form, and the reaction stops when it runs out.
[chem26-s3.0.1] Reagent in excess: a reactant present in more than the amount needed to react with all of the limiting reagent, so some of it is left unreacted at the end.
[chem26-s3.0.2] The amount of product depends only on the amount of the limiting reagent, not on the amount of the reagent in excess, because once the limiting reagent is gone nothing is left for the excess to react with.
[chem26-s3.0.3] Analogy: a sandwich needs 2 slices of bread and 1 slice of cheese. With 10 slices of bread and 50 slices of cheese, only 5 sandwiches can be made, so the bread is limiting and the cheese is in excess.
[chem26-s3.0.4] The limiting reagent is not necessarily the one with the smaller mass, or even the smaller number of moles (the moles of a substance are its amount).
[chem26-s3.0.5] The mole ratio in the equation must be taken into account. This is the ratio of the coefficients, the big numbers in front of each formula.
[chem26-s3.1.b] Every limiting reagent question follows the same five steps. Work through them in order and show each one.
[chem26-s3.1.0] Step 1: Write the balanced equation, because the coefficients give the mole ratio in which the reactants combine.
[chem26-s3.1.1] Step 2: Convert the amount of each reactant to moles (from mass, from volume and concentration, or from gas volume), so that the reactants can be compared on the same footing.
[chem26-s3.1.2] Step 3 (method A): Use the mole ratio to work out how many moles of the second reactant are needed to react with all of the first. If more than that is present, the second is in excess and the first is limiting.
[chem26-s3.1.3] Step 3 (method A, continued): if less of the second reactant is present than the ratio needs, the second is limiting.
[chem26-s3.1.4] Step 3 (method B): Divide the moles of each reactant by its coefficient in the equation. The reactant with the smallest result is the limiting reagent.
[chem26-s3.1.5] Step 4: Calculate the amount of product from the moles of the limiting reagent only, because the reagent in excess does not decide how much product forms.
[chem26-s3.1.6] Step 5 (if asked): Moles of excess reagent left = moles present − moles that reacted with the limiting reagent. Convert to a mass if needed, by multiplying by the molar mass (the mass of one mole, in g mol⁻¹).
[chem26-s3.2.b] This is the simplest case, a 1 : 1 reaction, so you can see the five steps with no ratio complications. The question asks for three things: the limiting reagent, the mass of product and the mass of excess left.
[chem26-s3.2.0] Question: 10.0 g of iron is heated with 10.0 g of sulfur, Fe(s) + S(s) → FeS(s). Find the limiting reagent, the mass of iron(II) sulfide formed and the mass of the excess reagent left.
[chem26-s3.2.1] First convert both masses to moles. Moles of Fe = 10.0 ÷ 56 = 0.1786 mol.
[chem26-s3.2.2] Moles of S = 10.0 ÷ 32 = 0.3125 mol.
[chem26-s3.2.3] The ratio is 1 : 1, so 0.1786 mol of Fe needs only 0.1786 mol of S. Since 0.3125 mol is present, sulfur is in excess and iron is the limiting reagent.
[chem26-s3.2.4] The product comes from the limiting reagent: moles of FeS = 0.1786 mol; mass = 0.1786 × 88 = 15.7 g.
[chem26-s3.2.5] Sulfur left = 0.3125 − 0.1786 = 0.1339 mol, which is the amount present minus the amount that reacted.
[chem26-s3.2.6] Mass of sulfur left = 0.1339 × 32 = 4.29 g.
[chem26-s3.2.7] Check by conservation of mass (matter is neither created nor destroyed in a reaction): 15.71 g + 4.29 g = 20.0 g, the total mass of reactants.
[chem26-s3.3.b] Here the ratio is 1 : 2, and the reactant with more moles turns out to be the one that runs out. The acid's amount comes from its volume and concentration, and the product is a gas.
[chem26-s3.3.0] Question: 0.480 g of magnesium is added to 50.0 cm³ of 0.500 mol L⁻¹ hydrochloric acid, Mg(s) + 2HCl(aq) → MgCl₂(aq) + H₂(g).
[chem26-s3.3.1] Moles of Mg = 0.480 ÷ 24 = 0.0200 mol. Moles of HCl = 0.500 × 0.0500 = 0.0250 mol (concentration in mol L⁻¹ × volume in litres).
[chem26-s3.3.2] 0.0200 mol of Mg needs 2 × 0.0200 = 0.0400 mol of HCl, but only 0.0250 mol is present, so hydrochloric acid is the limiting reagent and magnesium is in excess.
[chem26-s3.3.3] There are more moles of HCl than of Mg, yet the HCl still runs out first because the equation needs two moles of HCl for each mole of Mg.
[chem26-s3.3.4] Moles of H₂ = 0.0250 ÷ 2 = 0.0125 mol, using the limiting reagent and the 2 : 1 ratio of HCl to H₂.
[chem26-s3.3.5] Volume of H₂ = 0.0125 × 22.4 = 0.280 L, which is 280 cm³, at STP (standard temperature and pressure, where one mole of any gas occupies 22.4 L).
[chem26-s3.3.6] Magnesium that reacted = 0.0125 mol; magnesium left = 0.0200 − 0.0125 = 0.00750 mol.
[chem26-s3.3.7] Mass of magnesium left = 0.00750 × 24 = 0.180 g, seen as unreacted ribbon when the fizzing stops.
[chem26-s3.4.b] For gases at the same temperature and pressure you can skip the moles step and work directly with volumes. The one catch is checking which products are gases.
[chem26-s3.4.0] Question: 50 cm³ of hydrogen is exploded with 40 cm³ of oxygen and cooled to room temperature, 2H₂(g) + O₂(g) → 2H₂O(l). All volumes are at the same temperature and pressure.
[chem26-s3.4.1] By Avogadro's law (equal volumes of gases at the same temperature and pressure contain equal numbers of molecules) the volume ratio equals the mole ratio, so 50 cm³ of H₂ needs 25 cm³ of O₂.
[chem26-s3.4.2] 40 cm³ of O₂ is present, so hydrogen is limiting and 40 − 25 = 15 cm³ of oxygen is left over.
[chem26-s3.4.3] The water is a liquid at room temperature, so it takes up negligible volume and the final gas volume is 15 cm³, all of it oxygen.
[chem26-s3.5.b] This example uses method B from the steps above. It is a good test because the two reactants have exactly equal moles, so only the ratio in the equation can decide which is limiting.
[chem26-s3.5.0] Question: 2.70 g of aluminium is heated in 7.10 g of chlorine, 2Al(s) + 3Cl₂(g) → 2AlCl₃(s).
[chem26-s3.5.1] Moles: Al = 2.70 ÷ 27 = 0.100 mol; Cl₂ = 7.10 ÷ 71 = 0.100 mol.
[chem26-s3.5.2] Dividing by coefficients: Al 0.100 ÷ 2 = 0.0500; Cl₂ 0.100 ÷ 3 = 0.0333.
[chem26-s3.5.3] Chlorine has the smaller value, so chlorine is limiting, even though the moles of the two reactants are equal.
[chem26-s3.5.4] Moles of AlCl₃ = 0.100 × (2 ÷ 3) = 0.0667 mol, from the 3 : 2 ratio of Cl₂ to AlCl₃.
[chem26-s3.5.5] Mass of AlCl₃ = 0.06667 × 133.5 = 8.90 g.
[chem26-s3.5.6] Aluminium left = 0.100 − 0.0667 = 0.0333 mol = 0.900 g.
[chem26-s3.5.7] Check: 8.90 + 0.90 = 9.80 g = 2.70 + 7.10 g, so mass is conserved.
[chem26-s3.6.b] Exams combine a limiting reagent calculation with yield. The limiting reagent gives the theoretical yield (the most product the equation allows), and comparing it with the mass actually obtained gives the percentage yield.
[chem26-s3.6.0] Question: 2.00 cm³ of phenylmethanol (density 1.04 g cm⁻³) was oxidised (reacted with an oxidising agent) with 6.32 g of potassium manganate(VII) under basic conditions. 1.05 g of pure benzoic acid was obtained after acidifying and recrystallising (purifying by dissolving and letting crystals reform).
[chem26-s3.6.1] Equation: 3C₆H₅CH₂OH + 4KMnO₄ → 3C₆H₅COOH + 4MnO₂ + H₂O + 4KOH.
[chem26-s3.6.2] Phenylmethanol is a liquid measured by volume, so find its mass first: 2.00 × 1.04 = 2.08 g. Moles = 2.08 ÷ 108 = 0.01926 mol.
[chem26-s3.6.3] Moles of KMnO₄ = 6.32 ÷ 158 = 0.0400 mol.
[chem26-s3.6.4] 0.01926 mol of phenylmethanol needs 0.01926 × (4 ÷ 3) = 0.0257 mol of KMnO₄. Since 0.0400 mol is present, KMnO₄ is in excess and phenylmethanol is the limiting reagent.
[chem26-s3.6.5] The ratio of phenylmethanol to benzoic acid is 3 : 3 = 1 : 1, so theoretical moles of benzoic acid = 0.01926 mol. Theoretical yield = 0.01926 × 122 = 2.35 g.
[chem26-s3.6.6] Percentage yield = (1.05 ÷ 2.35) × 100 = 44.7%, the actual mass obtained as a percentage of the theoretical yield. There is more on the practical in Investigation: synthesising benzoic acid.
[chem26-s3.7.b] Chemists often add one reactant in excess on purpose. It makes sure the reactant that matters most is used up completely, but it brings a cost.
[chem26-s3.7.0] Using one reagent in excess makes sure that all of the other, usually more expensive or more important, reagent reacts, so none of it is wasted and the yield based on it is as high as possible.
[chem26-s3.7.1] The cheaper or more easily removed reagent is chosen to be in excess. For example, excess air (oxygen) in combustion makes the fuel burn completely to carbon dioxide rather than toxic carbon monoxide.
[chem26-s3.7.2] For a reversible reaction (one that can run in both directions and settles at an equilibrium), an excess of one reactant shifts the equilibrium to the right (Le Châtelier's principle) and increases the yield. An example is excess ethanol or ethanoic acid in making ethyl ethanoate.
[chem26-s3.7.3] In analysis (finding how much of a substance is present), the reagent is added in excess so that the substance being measured reacts completely. Examples are excess silver nitrate in gravimetric analysis (weighing a solid product) and the excess acid in a back titration.
[chem26-s3.7.4] A back titration adds a measured excess of one reagent, then titrates what is left over. There is more on this in Worked acid–base volumetric problems.
[chem26-s3.7.5] Drawback: the excess reagent is left in the product mixture and must be removed, for example excess KMnO₄ in the benzoic acid preparation, or excess NaOH, which would leave a soap caustic (harshly alkaline and irritating to skin, as in Investigation: making soap). Unless recycled, it is wasted.

CANDIDATES chem20-s2: 2. Water contamination
[chem20-s2.0.b] Water covers most of the planet, yet very little of it can be drunk or used. Contamination (pollution of water by unwanted substances) removes even more of that small share.
[chem20-s2.0.0] About 97.5% of the Earth's water is salt water in the oceans, which is unsuitable for drinking or farming; only about 2.5% is fresh water.
[chem20-s2.0.1] Most fresh water is frozen in ice caps and glaciers or lies deep underground, so well under 1% is readily available in rivers, lakes and accessible groundwater (water held in rock and soil that can be reached by wells).
[chem20-s2.0.2] Fresh water is constantly recycled by the water cycle (evaporation, rainfall and run-off), but the amount available in a place at a time is limited, and demand rises with population, farming and industry.
[chem20-s2.0.3] Contamination makes some of the available fresh water unusable, so there is even less to share between all these demands.
[chem20-s2.1.b] Making water fit to drink, and cleaning it again after use, takes machinery, chemicals and electricity. Each of these has a cost to the energy supply and to the environment.
[chem20-s2.1.0] Processing water has an energy cost: pumping water from sources to treatment plants and on to homes, running treatment plants (mixing, filtration, UV lamps), and treating waste water before it returns to rivers.
[chem20-s2.1.1] Processing water also has an environmental impact: chemicals such as aluminium sulfate and chlorine are manufactured and transported, and sludge (the solid waste left after treatment) must be disposed of.
[chem20-s2.1.2] Abstraction (taking water out of a river or lake) lowers the level of rivers and lakes, which affects the living things that depend on them.
[chem20-s2.1.3] Leaking pipes waste treated water and the energy used to treat it, because water that is lost has already been pumped and cleaned.
[chem20-s2.1.4] Making fresh water from sea water by desalination (removing the dissolved salts), e.g. by reverse osmosis, is possible but uses far more energy than treating fresh water.
[chem20-s2.1.5] So conserving water, by fixing leaks and using less, also saves energy and reduces emissions.
[chem20-s2.2.b] Fish breathe oxygen that is dissolved in the water, and only a little dissolves. Pollution that feeds bacteria uses that oxygen up, and biochemical oxygen demand (BOD) is the measure of how much.
[chem20-s2.2.0] Fish and other aquatic life need oxygen dissolved in the water; clean water at 20 °C holds only about 9 mg L⁻¹ (9 p.p.m., parts per million) of dissolved oxygen, and warmer water holds less.
[chem20-s2.2.1] When organic matter (material from living things, such as sewage, slurry, food waste or dead plants) enters water, aerobic bacteria (bacteria that use oxygen) break it down, using up dissolved oxygen.
[chem20-s2.2.2] Biochemical oxygen demand (BOD) is the amount of dissolved oxygen used up by biological (biochemical) action when a water sample is kept in the dark at 20 °C for five days. It is measured in mg L⁻¹ (p.p.m.) of oxygen.
[chem20-s2.2.3] A high BOD means a lot of organic pollution: the oxygen is used up and fish die (a fish kill). When the oxygen is gone, anaerobic bacteria (which live without oxygen) take over, producing foul-smelling gases such as hydrogen sulfide (H₂S) and methane.
[chem20-s2.2.4] Unpolluted river water has a BOD of only a few mg L⁻¹; raw domestic sewage (sewage that has had no treatment) has a BOD of a few hundred mg L⁻¹ (about 300).
[chem20-s2.2.5] Farm wastes such as silage effluent (the liquid that drains from stored fermented grass) and slurry (liquid animal manure) have a BOD in the thousands to tens of thousands of mg L⁻¹, so a small spill can kill a river.
[chem20-s2.3.b] The BOD test compares the dissolved oxygen in a sample before and after five days of bacterial activity. The conditions are fixed so that the only thing using up oxygen is the bacteria and the results can be compared.
[chem20-s2.3.0] The sample is kept in the dark because this prevents photosynthesis by algae and plants from adding oxygen.
[chem20-s2.3.1] The temperature is fixed at 20 °C because both the solubility of oxygen (how much dissolves in the water) and the rate of bacterial activity depend on temperature.
[chem20-s2.3.2] The time is fixed at five days so that results are comparable.
[chem20-s2.3.3] Step 1: Measure the dissolved oxygen in one bottle of the sample at the start.
[chem20-s2.3.4] Step 2: Fill a second bottle completely and seal it, so no air can add oxygen, then keep it in the dark at 20 °C for five days.
[chem20-s2.3.5] Step 3: Measure the dissolved oxygen in the second bottle after five days.
[chem20-s2.3.6] Step 4: The BOD is the difference between the two dissolved oxygen readings, because that difference is the oxygen the bacteria used.
[chem20-s2.3.7] Diagram: two labelled BOD bottles, one for the initial dissolved oxygen measurement and one completely filled and sealed for incubation (keeping the sample under set conditions) in the dark at 20 °C for five days.
[chem20-s2.4.b] Sewage is waste water from toilets, kitchens and washing. If it is untreated or poorly treated, it harms a river in three different ways.
[chem20-s2.4.0] Untreated or poorly treated sewage contains organic matter, which has a high BOD and causes oxygen depletion (the water losing its dissolved oxygen).
[chem20-s2.4.1] Sewage also contains nitrates and phosphates, which cause eutrophication (an overgrowth of algae caused by too many nutrients).
[chem20-s2.4.2] Sewage contains disease-causing microorganisms, including bacteria such as E. coli and those causing cholera and typhoid, as well as viruses and parasites.
[chem20-s2.4.3] Faecal contamination (contamination with human or animal faeces) of drinking water or bathing water causes gastrointestinal illness (illness of the stomach and gut); this is why sewage must be treated before discharge and drinking water disinfected.
[chem20-s2.5.b] Effluent means liquid waste that flows out into the environment. Farms produce several kinds, and each can reach rivers through spills or run-off (water washing over land into streams).
[chem20-s2.5.0] Agricultural effluent includes animal slurry, silage effluent, milking-parlour washings and run-off of artificial fertilisers.
[chem20-s2.5.1] Slurry and silage effluent have a very high BOD, so they use up the dissolved oxygen and cause fish kills if they reach a river.
[chem20-s2.5.2] Fertiliser and slurry run-off carry nitrates (NO₃⁻) and phosphates (PO₄³⁻) into water, causing eutrophication.
[chem20-s2.5.3] Nitrate in drinking water is a health concern, especially for babies, because it can reduce the blood's ability to carry oxygen.
[chem20-s2.5.4] The EU drinking-water limit for nitrate is 50 mg L⁻¹.
[chem20-s2.5.5] Pesticides (chemicals that kill pests) washed off farmland can contaminate water and are toxic to aquatic life.
[chem20-s2.6.b] Eutrophication is the enrichment of water with nutrients, especially nitrates and phosphates, leading to excessive growth of algae and plants. It is a chain of events that starts with added nutrients and can end with the death of fish.
[chem20-s2.6.0] Step 1: Nitrates and phosphates from fertiliser run-off, slurry, sewage and detergents enter a river or lake.
[chem20-s2.6.1] Step 2: Algae and aquatic plants grow rapidly, forming an algal bloom (a thick layer of algae) that covers the surface and blocks light from plants below.
[chem20-s2.6.2] Step 3: The algae die and are decomposed by aerobic bacteria, which use up the dissolved oxygen, so the BOD rises sharply. Living algae growing in light do not use up the oxygen; the bacteria decomposing them after they die do.
[chem20-s2.6.3] Step 4: Dissolved oxygen falls so low that fish and other animals die, and anaerobic bacteria then produce foul-smelling H₂S.
[chem20-s2.6.4] Some algal blooms, e.g. of cyanobacteria ("blue-green algae"), also produce toxins harmful to animals and people.
[chem20-s2.6.5] Prevention: controlled use of fertilisers, meaning the right amount at the right time and not before heavy rain (which would wash it into rivers), and buffer strips (strips of vegetation) along rivers.
[chem20-s2.6.6] Prevention: proper storage of slurry and silage, removing phosphates and nitrates in sewage treatment, and phosphate-free detergents.
[chem20-s2.7.b] Factories and power stations discharge waste water that can harm water in several ways: poisoning it, changing its pH, using up its oxygen or heating it.
[chem20-s2.7.0] Industrial effluent can contain toxic chemicals such as heavy metals, solvents and oils.
[chem20-s2.7.1] Industrial effluent can contain acids or alkalis that change the pH of the water, which harms the organisms living in it.
[chem20-s2.7.2] Organic waste from industries such as breweries and food processing has a high BOD, because bacteria use oxygen to break it down.
[chem20-s2.7.3] Thermal pollution (pollution by heat): warm water from power stations and factories holds less dissolved oxygen.
[chem20-s2.7.4] Warm water also speeds up the metabolism (the chemical reactions of life), and so the oxygen demand, of organisms, so even less oxygen is available.
[chem20-s2.7.5] Oil forms a film on the surface that stops oxygen dissolving and coats birds and animals.
[chem20-s2.8.b] Heavy metals are dense metals whose ions are poisonous to living things. Their danger lies in how they act inside cells and in how they build up over time.
[chem20-s2.8.0] Heavy metal ions such as lead (Pb²⁺), mercury (Hg²⁺) and cadmium (Cd²⁺) are toxic even at very low concentrations, in micrograms per litre.
[chem20-s2.8.1] Heavy metal ions bind to proteins, particularly to sulfur-containing (–SH) groups in enzymes (the proteins that speed up reactions in cells), changing their shape and stopping them working.
[chem20-s2.8.2] Heavy metals damage the nervous system, kidneys and liver.
[chem20-s2.8.3] They are cumulative poisons: the body can't excrete them easily, so they build up in tissues (bioaccumulation).
[chem20-s2.8.4] Their concentration increases up a food chain (biomagnification), so top predators and people who eat fish are most at risk.
[chem20-s2.8.5] Heavy metal ions can be removed from water by precipitation (turning them into a solid that forms in the water) as insoluble compounds, e.g. hydroxides or sulfides, followed by filtration, or by ion exchange (swapping the metal ions for harmless ions).
[chem20-s2.9.b] Each of the three metals reaches water from a different source and harms the body in a particular way. Two Japanese disease outbreaks show what the harm can look like.
[chem20-s2.9.0] Lead comes from old lead pipes in houses built before about 1970, especially where the water is soft and acidic, because such water dissolves lead more readily.
[chem20-s2.9.1] Lead damages the developing brain and nervous system of children, partly because Pb²⁺ takes the place of Ca²⁺.
[chem20-s2.9.2] The EU drinking-water limit for lead is 10 µg L⁻¹ (0.010 mg L⁻¹).
[chem20-s2.9.3] At Minamata, Japan, the Chisso Corporation's chemical factory discharged mercury compounds into the bay.
[chem20-s2.9.4] Methylmercury (an organic mercury compound) built up in fish and shellfish, and people who ate them suffered severe nervous-system damage, birth defects and deaths.
[chem20-s2.9.5] The disease was called Minamata disease and was officially recognised in 1956.
[chem20-s2.9.6] Cadmium comes from mining and some industries and damages the kidneys and bones.
[chem20-s2.9.7] Itai-itai disease in Japan was caused by cadmium from mining, which reached river water used for rice fields.
[chem20-s2.10.b] Plastic that breaks into tiny pieces does not disappear; it spreads through water and into living things. These small pieces are called microplastics.
[chem20-s2.10.0] Microplastics are pieces of plastic smaller than 5 mm.
[chem20-s2.10.1] Primary microplastics are made small, e.g. microbeads once used in cosmetics and plastic pellets.
[chem20-s2.10.2] Secondary microplastics form when larger plastic items break down, e.g. fibres from synthetic clothing in washing machines, tyre and road wear, packaging and bags.
[chem20-s2.10.3] Plastics are very unreactive and not biodegradable (bacteria cannot break them down), so they persist for hundreds of years. There is more on this in Addition polymers.
[chem20-s2.10.4] Microplastics are swallowed by plankton (tiny drifting organisms), shellfish and fish, blocking feeding and digestion, and they pass up the food chain.
[chem20-s2.10.5] Microplastics can carry adsorbed toxic chemicals (chemicals stuck to their surface).
[chem20-s2.10.6] Microplastics are found in drinking water and food; their long-term effects on human health are still being researched.
[chem20-s2.11.b] Acidification is the lowering of the pH of water. Gases from burning fuels dissolve in rain to make acids, and acidic waste water adds to the effect.
[chem20-s2.11.0] Sulfur dioxide dissolves in rain: SO₂ + H₂O → H₂SO₃.
[chem20-s2.11.1] Sulfur dioxide and oxygen form sulfuric acid in rain: 2SO₂ + O₂ + 2H₂O → 2H₂SO₄.
[chem20-s2.11.2] Nitrogen dioxide forms nitric acid in rain: 4NO₂ + O₂ + 2H₂O → 4HNO₃.
[chem20-s2.11.3] Acidic industrial and mine waste water also lowers the pH of rivers and lakes.
[chem20-s2.11.4] Normal rain is slightly acidic, with a pH of about 5.6, because of dissolved CO₂; acid rain has a lower pH.
[chem20-s2.12.b] Low pH harms water life directly, through enzymes, and indirectly, by dissolving substances from rocks, shells and pipes.
[chem20-s2.12.0] Enzymes work only in a narrow pH range, so low pH disrupts enzyme activity and the balance of ions in organisms' cells.
[chem20-s2.12.1] Fish eggs and young fish are most sensitive, and many fish can't survive below about pH 5.
[chem20-s2.12.2] Acidic water dissolves aluminium compounds from soils and rocks; Al³⁺ ions damage fish gills so that fish can't take in oxygen.
[chem20-s2.12.3] Acidic water dissolves the calcium carbonate shells of snails and shellfish: CaCO₃ + 2H⁺ → Ca²⁺ + H₂O + CO₂.
[chem20-s2.12.4] In drinking water, acidic water dissolves lead and copper from pipes.
