TARGET chem7-s1: 1. Families and trends in the periodic table
[chem7-s1.0.b] An element's position in the periodic table tells you how its atoms behave. Columns are called groups, and the groups on the left and right edges are the ones you need to know.
[chem7-s1.0.0] Elements in the same group have the same number of outer electrons (the electrons in the highest occupied energy level), so they undergo similar reactions.
[chem7-s1.0.1] Going down a group, atoms have more energy levels (the shells that hold electrons around the nucleus), which explains the trends in reactivity within each group.
[chem7-s1.0.2] Groups 1 and 2 are on the left of the periodic table; they are metals that lose electrons to form positive ions (charged particles).
[chem7-s1.0.3] Groups 17 and 18 are on the right; the halogens (group 17) are non-metals that gain electrons, and the noble gases (group 18) neither gain nor lose electrons.
[chem7-s1.0.4] Hydrogen appears in group 1 in some tables but is not an alkali metal.
[chem7-s1.1.b] Group 1 is the first column of the periodic table. Its metals are named alkali metals because they form alkaline solutions with water, and each has a single outer electron that it loses very easily.
[chem7-s1.1.0] Members: lithium, sodium, potassium, rubidium, caesium and francium; each atom has one outer electron.
[chem7-s1.1.1] State and conductivity: soft, shiny solids when freshly cut, with low densities and low melting points for metals; good conductors of heat and electricity.
[chem7-s1.1.2] Reactivity: very reactive, so they tarnish quickly in air (the shiny surface goes dull as it reacts) and are stored under oil to keep air and moisture away.
[chem7-s1.1.3] Reactivity increases down the group because the outer electron is further from the nucleus and more shielded (inner electrons block some of the nucleus's pull), so it is lost more easily.
[chem7-s1.1.4] They form 1+ ions (Li⁺, Na⁺, K⁺) because each atom loses its one outer electron, and they make ionic compounds such as sodium chloride.
[chem7-s1.1.5] Safety: use only small pieces, behind a safety screen and wearing goggles, because the reactions are vigorous; never touch them with bare hands.
[chem7-s1.2.b] All the alkali metals react with water to give an alkaline solution and hydrogen gas. What you see changes with the metal, and the observations show the trend in reactivity.
[chem7-s1.2.0] With water they form an alkaline hydroxide and hydrogen: 2Na(s) + 2H₂O(l) → 2NaOH(aq) + H₂(g).
[chem7-s1.2.1] Lithium: 2Li(s) + 2H₂O(l) → 2LiOH(aq) + H₂(g). Potassium: 2K(s) + 2H₂O(l) → 2KOH(aq) + H₂(g).
[chem7-s1.2.2] Lithium floats and fizzes steadily on the water, the gentlest of the three.
[chem7-s1.2.3] Sodium melts into a silvery ball that darts about on the surface.
[chem7-s1.2.4] Potassium reacts violently, and the hydrogen it gives off burns with a lilac flame.
[chem7-s1.2.5] The solution formed turns universal indicator (a mixture of dyes that shows pH by colour) purple, showing that it is alkaline.
[chem7-s1.3.b] Group 2 sits next to group 1. These metals are also reactive, but each atom has two outer electrons to lose, which makes them a little less reactive than their group 1 neighbours.
[chem7-s1.3.0] Members: beryllium, magnesium, calcium, strontium, barium and radium; each atom has two outer electrons.
[chem7-s1.3.1] State and conductivity: solid metals, harder and denser than group 1 metals; good conductors.
[chem7-s1.3.2] Reactivity: reactive, but less so than group 1 metals in the same period (the same row), because two electrons must be lost.
[chem7-s1.3.3] Calcium reacts steadily with cold water: Ca(s) + 2H₂O(l) → Ca(OH)₂(aq) + H₂(g).
[chem7-s1.3.4] Magnesium reacts only very slowly with cold water but burns in air with a bright white flame: 2Mg(s) + O₂(g) → 2MgO(s).
[chem7-s1.3.5] Reactivity increases down the group, for the same reason as group 1: the outer electrons are further from the nucleus and more shielded.
[chem7-s1.3.6] They form 2+ ions (Mg²⁺, Ca²⁺) because each atom loses its two outer electrons.
[chem7-s1.4.b] The halogens are the reactive non-metals in group 17. Each atom is one electron short of a full outer level, so it readily gains one electron.
[chem7-s1.4.0] Members: fluorine, chlorine, bromine and iodine (astatine); each atom has seven outer electrons.
[chem7-s1.4.1] The halogens exist as diatomic molecules (two atoms joined together): F₂, Cl₂, Br₂ and I₂.
[chem7-s1.4.2] Fluorine is a pale yellow gas at room temperature, and chlorine is a yellow-green gas.
[chem7-s1.4.3] Bromine is a red-brown liquid at room temperature. Iodine is a grey-black solid that forms a purple vapour when heated.
[chem7-s1.4.4] Conductivity: halogens are non-metals and do not conduct electricity.
[chem7-s1.4.5] Reactivity: very reactive non-metals that gain one electron to form 1− halide ions (negative ions of a halogen): F⁻, Cl⁻, Br⁻ and I⁻.
[chem7-s1.4.6] They react with metals to form salts, e.g. 2Na(s) + Cl₂(g) → 2NaCl(s).
[chem7-s1.4.7] Reactivity decreases down the group because larger atoms attract an extra electron less strongly, as it is further from the nucleus and more shielded.
[chem7-s1.5.b] Because reactivity falls down group 17, a more reactive halogen can push a less reactive one out of a solution of its salt. This is called displacement, and the colour change shows it has happened.
[chem7-s1.5.0] Displacement: a more reactive halogen displaces a less reactive one from a solution of its salt.
[chem7-s1.5.1] Chlorine and potassium bromide: Cl₂(aq) + 2KBr(aq) → 2KCl(aq) + Br₂(aq); the colourless solution turns orange-brown as bromine forms.
[chem7-s1.5.2] Chlorine and potassium iodide: Cl₂(aq) + 2KI(aq) → 2KCl(aq) + I₂(aq); the solution turns brown as iodine forms.
[chem7-s1.5.3] Bromine displaces iodine from a solution of its salt but does not displace chlorine, because bromine is less reactive than chlorine.
[chem7-s1.5.4] Safety: halogens are toxic and corrosive, so they are used in a fume cupboard (an enclosure that draws fumes away from the user).
[chem7-s1.6.b] The noble gases are the last column of the periodic table. Their atoms already have a full outer energy level, so they have almost no tendency to react, and their uses depend on that.
[chem7-s1.6.0] Members: helium, neon, argon, krypton, xenon and radon.
[chem7-s1.6.1] State: colourless, odourless gases made of single atoms (monatomic).
[chem7-s1.6.2] Conductivity: non-conductors.
[chem7-s1.6.3] Reactivity: very unreactive (inert) because they have full outer energy levels, a stable arrangement: helium has 2 outer electrons and the others have 8.
[chem7-s1.6.4] Helium is used in balloons and airships because it is unreactive and has a low density.
[chem7-s1.6.5] Argon is used in filament light bulbs and as a shield gas in welding, where its lack of reactivity protects the hot metal and the filament.
[chem7-s1.6.6] Neon is used in advertising signs, where it glows red in an electric discharge.
[chem7-s1.7.b] The periodic table lists elements in order of atomic number (the number of protons in an atom). Relative atomic mass (the average mass of an atom of the element, compared with carbon-12) follows a looser pattern.
[chem7-s1.7.0] Atomic number increases by one from each element to the next.
[chem7-s1.7.1] Relative atomic mass generally increases with atomic number.
[chem7-s1.7.2] Relative atomic mass falls in a few places: argon (39.95) comes before potassium (39.10), and tellurium (127.6) comes before iodine (126.9).
[chem7-s1.7.3] These exceptions come from the mix of isotopes (atoms of the same element with different masses) of each element, not from mistakes in the table.
[chem7-s1.8.b] The way outer electrons change across and down the table explains why elements are arranged in periods (rows) and groups (columns).
[chem7-s1.8.0] Across a period, the number of outer electrons increases by one at each step, all in the same main energy level, which is why a period is one row.
[chem7-s1.8.1] Down a group, the number of outer electrons stays the same, so the elements react similarly, but a new main energy level is added each time, so the outer electrons are further out.
[chem7-s1.8.2] The repeating pattern of outer-electron arrangements causes periodicity (the regular repeating of properties down the table): elements with the same outer arrangement fall in the same group.
[chem7-s1.9.b] Electronegativity is the ability of an atom to attract bonding electrons towards itself. Values are on the Pauling scale.
[chem7-s1.9.0] Electronegativity increases across a period, e.g. sodium 0.93 to chlorine 3.16 in period 3.
[chem7-s1.9.1] Electronegativity decreases down a group, e.g. fluorine 3.98 to iodine 2.66 in group 17.
[chem7-s1.9.2] The explanation for these values is in "Bond type and intermolecular forces".
[chem7-s1.10.b] Atoms react because they are more stable with a full outer level. This explains why reactivity rises and falls in a pattern across and down the table.
[chem7-s1.10.0] Atoms react to reach a full outer level of eight electrons (the octet rule), by losing, gaining or sharing electrons.
[chem7-s1.10.1] Metals react by losing electrons: reactivity increases down group 1 and group 2, because the outer electrons are further from the nucleus and more shielded.
[chem7-s1.10.2] Across a period, metal reactivity decreases from group 1 to group 2 to group 13.
[chem7-s1.10.3] Non-metals react by gaining electrons: reactivity decreases down group 17, because larger atoms attract an extra electron less strongly.
[chem7-s1.10.4] Across a period, non-metal reactivity generally increases towards group 17.
[chem7-s1.10.5] Noble gases already have a full outer level, so they have no need to gain or lose electrons and are unreactive.
[chem7-s1.11.b] Period 3 runs from sodium to argon. Across it the elements change from metals to non-metals, and the physical properties change with them.
[chem7-s1.11.0] Sodium, magnesium and aluminium are shiny metals and good conductors.
[chem7-s1.11.1] Silicon is a metalloid (an element with properties between those of a metal and a non-metal) and a semiconductor (it conducts only slightly).
[chem7-s1.11.2] Phosphorus, sulfur, chlorine and argon are non-metals and non-conductors.
[chem7-s1.11.3] Melting points rise across period 3 to a maximum at silicon (1414 °C), which has a giant covalent structure (a huge network of atoms joined by covalent bonds).
[chem7-s1.11.4] Beyond silicon, melting points fall sharply for the molecular non-metals and for argon.
[chem7-s1.11.5] Down group 1, melting points decrease: lithium 181 °C, sodium 98 °C, potassium 63 °C.
[chem7-s1.11.6] Down group 17, melting and boiling points increase, so the halogens change from gases to a liquid to a solid.
[chem7-s1.12.b] These trends are Higher Level only. Ionisation energy is the energy needed to remove an electron from an atom, and atomic radius is the size of the atom.
[chem7-s1.12.0] Atomic (covalent) radius decreases across a period and increases down a group.
[chem7-s1.12.1] First ionisation energy generally increases across a period and decreases down a group.
[chem7-s1.12.2] Successive ionisation energies of an element always increase.
[chem7-s1.12.3] Higher Level trends extend to the first 36 elements, to krypton; Ordinary level uses the first 20, to calcium.
[chem7-s1.13.b] How strongly an atom holds its outer electrons depends on three factors. They explain every trend in atomic radius and ionisation energy that follows.
[chem7-s1.13.0] Effective nuclear charge: the more protons in the nucleus, the stronger the pull on the outer electrons.
[chem7-s1.13.1] Distance: the further the outer electrons are from the nucleus (more energy levels), the weaker the pull on them.
[chem7-s1.13.2] Screening (shielding): inner electrons shield the outer electrons from the full nuclear charge; electrons in the same energy level shield each other very little.
[chem7-s1.14.b] Atomic radius measures the size of an atom. Its trends follow from the nuclear charge, the number of energy levels and the screening.
[chem7-s1.14.0] Atomic (covalent) radius: half the distance between the nuclei of two atoms of the same element joined by a single covalent bond.
[chem7-s1.14.1] Across a period it decreases: nuclear charge increases while electrons enter the same main energy level, with little extra screening, so the outer electrons are pulled closer.
[chem7-s1.14.2] Down a group it increases: each element has an extra energy level, and the extra inner electrons screen the outer electrons, outweighing the increase in nuclear charge.
[chem7-s1.14.3] Noble gases are not given a covalent radius because they don't form bonds.
[chem7-s1.15.b] First ionisation energy measures how tightly an atom holds its outermost electron. The data below show the trends, and a few elements break the pattern because of sublevel stability and electron pairing.
[chem7-s1.15.0] First ionisation energy: the minimum energy needed to completely remove the most loosely bound electron from a neutral gaseous atom in its ground state (lowest energy state): X(g) → X⁺(g) + e⁻. Its unit is kJ mol⁻¹.
[chem7-s1.15.1] Period 2 first ionisation energies (kJ mol⁻¹): Li 520, Be 899, B 801, C 1086, N 1402, O 1314, F 1681, Ne 2081.
[chem7-s1.15.2] Period 3 first ionisation energies (kJ mol⁻¹): Na 496, Mg 738, Al 578, Si 787, P 1012, S 1000, Cl 1251, Ar 1521.
[chem7-s1.15.3] Values are standard data book figures, rounded.
[chem7-s1.15.4] Across a period the value generally increases, because nuclear charge increases and atomic radius decreases, so the outer electron is held more tightly. Noble gases have the highest first ionisation energy in each period.
[chem7-s1.15.5] Down a group the value decreases, because the outer electron is further from the nucleus and more screened, despite the larger nuclear charge.
[chem7-s1.15.6] Group 1 metals have the lowest values in each period, which is why they lose electrons easily and are so reactive; the value falls down the group to potassium at 419 kJ mol⁻¹.
[chem7-s1.16.b] The rise across a period is not perfectly smooth, because a few elements have a lower value than the one before. The cause is the stability of particular sublevels (subdivisions of an energy level, such as 2s and 2p) and electron pairing.
[chem7-s1.16.0] Beryllium is higher than boron (899 against 801 kJ mol⁻¹) because beryllium's 2s sublevel is full (1s² 2s²), a stable arrangement.
[chem7-s1.16.1] Boron's outer electron is alone in a 2p orbital (1s² 2s² 2p¹), which is slightly higher in energy and more screened, so it is removed more easily.
[chem7-s1.16.2] Nitrogen is higher than oxygen (1402 against 1314 kJ mol⁻¹) because nitrogen's 2p sublevel is half full, with one electron in each orbital (2p³), a stable arrangement.
[chem7-s1.16.3] In oxygen (2p⁴) one 2p orbital holds a pair of electrons, and repulsion between the paired electrons makes one of them easier to remove.
[chem7-s1.16.4] The same pattern occurs in period 3: magnesium (738) is higher than aluminium (578), and phosphorus (1012) is higher than sulfur (1000).
[chem7-s1.16.5] Diagram: a graph of first ionisation energy against atomic number for elements 1 to 20, showing peaks at the noble gases, troughs at the alkali metals and small dips after Be, N, Mg and P.
[chem7-s1.17.b] After the first electron is removed, more can be taken away one at a time. The sizes of the successive energies reveal how an atom's electrons are arranged in energy levels.
[chem7-s1.17.0] Second ionisation energy: the energy needed to remove an electron from a 1+ ion, not a second electron from the neutral atom: X⁺(g) → X²⁺(g) + e⁻; the third and later follow the same pattern.
[chem7-s1.17.1] Successive ionisation energies always increase, because each electron is removed from an increasingly positive ion, so the same nuclear charge holds fewer electrons more tightly.
[chem7-s1.17.2] A very large jump occurs when an electron is removed from a new, inner energy level, which is closer to the nucleus and less screened.
[chem7-s1.17.3] Magnesium: 738, 1451 and 7733 kJ mol⁻¹; the big jump between the second and third shows two outer electrons, so magnesium is in group 2.
[chem7-s1.17.4] Sodium: 496 then 4562 kJ mol⁻¹; the big jump after the first shows one outer electron, so sodium is in group 1.
[chem7-s1.17.5] A graph of the log of successive ionisation energies against electron number for potassium shows jumps that group its electrons into 2, 8, 8 and 1, which is evidence for main energy levels.

CANDIDATES chem4-s1: 1. The periodic table: arrangement and use
[chem4-s1.0.b] By the mid-1800s over 60 elements were known, and chemists looked for relationships among their properties. Two early attempts at a pattern, Döbereiner's triads and Newlands' octaves, led towards the periodic table.
[chem4-s1.0.0] An element is a substance that cannot be split into simpler substances by chemical means, so each of the 60-plus known elements was a basic building block that chemists tried to sort by its properties.
[chem4-s1.0.1] Johann Döbereiner (1829) noticed triads: groups of three elements with similar properties in which the atomic weight (the old term for relative atomic mass, the mass of an atom compared with other atoms) of the middle element is about the average of the other two.
[chem4-s1.0.2] Döbereiner's triads included lithium, sodium and potassium; calcium, strontium and barium; and chlorine, bromine and iodine. In each, the three elements behave alike and the middle one sits between the others in atomic weight.
[chem4-s1.0.3] John Newlands (1864–1865) proposed the law of octaves: when the elements were arranged by atomic weight, every eighth element had properties similar to the first, like the eighth note repeating in a musical scale.
[chem4-s1.0.4] Newlands' law worked for the lighter elements but broke down for the heavier ones, and he left no gaps in his table, so there was no room for elements that had not yet been discovered.
[chem4-s1.1.b] Dmitri Mendeleev produced a table that chemists came to accept as correct. His success came from trusting a pattern enough to leave gaps and make predictions that were later confirmed.
[chem4-s1.1.0] Dmitri Mendeleev (1869) arranged the elements in order of increasing atomic weight (relative atomic mass), placing elements with similar properties in the same vertical column, called a group.
[chem4-s1.1.1] Mendeleev's periodic law: the properties of the elements vary periodically with their atomic weights, which means similar properties recur at regular intervals as atomic weight increases.
[chem4-s1.1.2] He left gaps for elements not yet discovered and predicted their properties, rather than forcing the known elements to fit with no spaces as Newlands had done.
[chem4-s1.1.3] His predicted elements eka-aluminium and eka-silicon were matched by gallium (discovered 1875) and germanium (discovered 1886); scandium (discovered 1879) matched eka-boron.
[chem4-s1.1.4] The discovery of elements with the predicted properties convinced chemists that the table was correct, because a prediction that comes true is strong evidence for the pattern behind it.
[chem4-s1.1.5] Where atomic weight order placed an element in the wrong group, he reversed the order of the pair: tellurium (relative atomic mass 127.6) is placed before iodine (relative atomic mass 126.9) so that iodine sits with the other halogens (the reactive non-metals chlorine, bromine and so on).
[chem4-s1.1.6] The table organised all the known elements, showed relationships between them, made predictions that were confirmed, and became the basis of the modern table.
[chem4-s1.2.b] Mendeleev's table was later improved by new discoveries. These explained why his pattern worked and removed the need for his reversed pairs.
[chem4-s1.2.0] The noble gases helium, neon, argon, krypton and xenon were discovered in the 1890s (William Ramsay and others) and were added as a new group, now group 18.
[chem4-s1.2.1] Henry Moseley (1913) showed by experiment that each element has a whole-number atomic number, equal to the number of protons (positively charged particles) in its nucleus (the atom's centre).
[chem4-s1.2.2] The modern periodic table is arranged in order of increasing atomic number, so tellurium (atomic number 52) naturally comes before iodine (atomic number 53) and Mendeleev's reversal is unnecessary.
[chem4-s1.2.3] Modern periodic law: the properties of the elements are a periodic function of their atomic numbers, meaning the properties repeat in a regular pattern as atomic number increases.
[chem4-s1.2.4] The repeating pattern is explained by electron arrangement: elements in the same group have the same outer-electron arrangement (the same number of electrons in the outermost energy level), so they behave alike.
[chem4-s1.3.b] The modern table keeps Mendeleev's idea of groups of similar elements but differs from his in five ways.
[chem4-s1.3.0] Order: Mendeleev ordered the elements by atomic weight; the modern table orders them by atomic number.
[chem4-s1.3.1] Gaps: Mendeleev's table had gaps for undiscovered elements; the modern table has none, because all the elements up to 118 are now known.
[chem4-s1.3.2] Noble gases: absent from Mendeleev's table, which was drawn up before they were discovered; present in the modern table as group 18.
[chem4-s1.3.3] Number of elements: Mendeleev's table contained fewer elements, while the modern table contains 118.
[chem4-s1.3.4] Blocks: Mendeleev's table had no separate blocks of elements (sets of columns grouped by the type of sublevel being filled); the modern table is divided into blocks.
[chem4-s1.4.b] The table is arranged so that elements with the same outer electrons fall in the same column. This is why the elements in a column behave alike.
[chem4-s1.4.0] A group is a vertical column of elements; using IUPAC numbering (the internationally agreed system) there are 18 groups, numbered 1 to 18.
[chem4-s1.4.1] Elements in the same group have the same number of electrons in their outer energy level (the outermost shell of electrons), so they have similar chemical properties.
[chem4-s1.4.2] For groups 1 and 2, the group number equals the number of outer electrons, so group 2 elements have 2 outer electrons.
[chem4-s1.4.3] For groups 13 to 18, the number of outer electrons is the group number minus 10, so group 17 has 7 outer electrons; helium, in group 18, is the exception with 2 outer electrons.
[chem4-s1.4.4] Group 1 elements are the alkali metals and group 2 elements are the alkaline earth metals.
[chem4-s1.4.5] Group 17 elements are the halogens and group 18 elements are the noble gases.
[chem4-s1.5.b] While a group is a column of similar elements, a period is a row, and its number tells you how many energy levels the atoms use.
[chem4-s1.5.0] A period is a horizontal row of elements; there are 7 periods.
[chem4-s1.5.1] The period number equals the number of occupied main energy levels (shells holding electrons); sodium, with electron arrangement 2,8,1, has three occupied levels, so it is in period 3.
[chem4-s1.5.2] Across a period, one proton and one electron are added at each step, and the elements change from metals on the left to non-metals on the right.
[chem4-s1.5.3] Period 1 has 2 elements (hydrogen and helium); periods 2 and 3 have 8 each; period 4 has 18.
[chem4-s1.6.b] The elements fall into three types according to where they sit on the table. A staircase line marks the boundary between metals and non-metals.
[chem4-s1.6.0] About three-quarters of the elements are metals, found on the left and in the centre of the table.
[chem4-s1.6.1] Non-metals are found on the right of the table; hydrogen is also a non-metal, even though it sits at the top left.
[chem4-s1.6.2] A staircase line running from boron to astatine separates metals from non-metals.
[chem4-s1.6.3] Metalloids (semi-metals) lie next to the staircase line, including boron, silicon, germanium, arsenic, antimony and tellurium; they have some properties of metals and some of non-metals.
[chem4-s1.6.4] Metals are shiny, good conductors of heat and electricity, malleable (can be hammered into shape) and ductile (can be drawn into wires), and form positive ions (charged atoms).
[chem4-s1.6.5] Non-metals are dull, poor conductors of electricity (graphite is an exception), brittle as solids, and often form negative ions or share electrons.
[chem4-s1.7.b] A block is the set of elements whose highest-energy electron enters the same type of sublevel (a subdivision of an energy level, labelled s, p, d or f). The blocks explain the overall shape of the table.
[chem4-s1.7.0] The s-block is groups 1 and 2, together with helium; its highest-energy electron is in an s sublevel.
[chem4-s1.7.1] The p-block is groups 13 to 18, except helium; its highest-energy electron is in a p sublevel.
[chem4-s1.7.2] The d-block is groups 3 to 12, in the middle of periods 4 to 7; its electrons fill a d sublevel.
[chem4-s1.7.3] The f-block is the lanthanides and actinides, usually shown below the main table; its electrons fill an f sublevel.
[chem4-s1.7.4] The shape of the table follows from sublevel capacities: 2 columns for s, 6 for p, 10 for d and 14 for f, because an s sublevel holds 2 electrons, p holds 6, d holds 10 and f holds 14. This is why the d-block is ten elements wide.
[chem4-s1.7.5] Diagram: an outline of the periodic table with the s, p, d and f blocks shaded and labelled, and group and period numbers marked.
[chem4-s1.8.b] Each element's box and position give information about its atoms and its behaviour, so the table works as a quick reference.
[chem4-s1.8.0] Each box gives the symbol, atomic number and relative atomic mass of an element.
[chem4-s1.8.1] The atomic number gives the number of protons and, in a neutral atom, the number of electrons, because the positive and negative charges must balance.
[chem4-s1.8.2] The group gives the element's outer-electron arrangement.
[chem4-s1.8.3] The period gives the number of occupied energy levels.
[chem4-s1.8.4] The element's position relative to the staircase line shows whether it is a metal, non-metal or metalloid.
[chem4-s1.9.b] Elements in the same group share an outer-electron arrangement, so they behave in similar ways. This lets you predict reactions and ion charges from the group alone.
[chem4-s1.9.0] Reactions can be predicted from a group: sodium reacts with water to give sodium hydroxide and hydrogen, so potassium, in the same group, will react with water in the same way, giving potassium hydroxide and hydrogen.
[chem4-s1.9.1] Group 1 elements form 1⁺ ions, group 2 elements form 2⁺ ions and group 13 elements form 3⁺ ions, because each loses its outer electrons (1, 2 and 3 respectively).
[chem4-s1.9.2] Group 15 elements form 3⁻ ions, group 16 elements form 2⁻ ions and group 17 elements form 1⁻ ions, because each gains the electrons needed to fill its outer level.
[chem4-s1.9.3] Noble gases (group 18) have full outer energy levels and are very unreactive, so they have no tendency to form ions.
[chem4-s1.10.b] The formula of a compound follows from the charges of its ions, which must balance to give no overall charge. The group of each element gives you its ion's charge.
[chem4-s1.10.0] Magnesium (group 2) forms Mg²⁺ and chlorine (group 17) forms Cl⁻, so two chloride ions balance one magnesium ion and the formula is MgCl₂.
[chem4-s1.10.1] Aluminium (group 13) forms Al³⁺ and oxygen (group 16) forms O²⁻, so two aluminium ions balance three oxide ions and the formula is Al₂O₃.
[chem4-s1.11.b] Trends in the table allow properties of unfamiliar elements to be estimated from their neighbours. The trends themselves are taught in full elsewhere.
[chem4-s1.11.0] Properties change gradually and predictably down a group and across a period, so an unknown property can be estimated from neighbouring elements.
[chem4-s1.11.1] Astatine (At, atomic number 85) lies at the bottom of group 17; from the trend in the halogens it is predicted to be a dark solid that is less reactive than iodine.
[chem4-s1.11.2] Mendeleev used the same approach to predict the properties of undiscovered elements.
[chem4-s1.11.3] Every trend is explained by electron arrangement: the number of outer electrons, the number of occupied energy levels and the nuclear charge (the positive charge of the nucleus, equal to the number of protons).

CANDIDATES chem3-s1: 1. The orbital model and electronic structure
[chem3-s1.0.b] The current model of the atom has a tiny nucleus at the centre and electrons arranged around it in energy levels and orbitals. Knowing these four ideas, nucleus, energy level, orbital and capacity, lets you describe any atom.
[chem3-s1.0.0] The nucleus is a tiny, dense, positively charged centre of the atom, containing protons (positive particles) and neutrons (particles with no charge). There is more on this in Atomic structure: the nuclear model.
[chem3-s1.0.1] Electrons (the negatively charged particles) surround the nucleus and are arranged in main energy levels, numbered n = 1, 2, 3, 4…
[chem3-s1.0.2] The main energy levels increase in energy, and in average distance from the nucleus, as n increases, so an electron in n = 3 has more energy and is on average further out than one in n = 1.
[chem3-s1.0.3] Within a main energy level, electrons occupy orbitals, which are the smaller regions that make up the level.
[chem3-s1.0.4] An orbital is a region of space around the nucleus in which there is a high probability of finding an electron.
[chem3-s1.0.5] Each orbital can hold a maximum of two electrons, so an orbital that is full is holding a pair.
[chem3-s1.0.6] The maximum number of electrons in main energy level n is 2n²: 2 in n = 1, 8 in n = 2, 18 in n = 3 and 32 in n = 4.
[chem3-s1.0.7] Each main energy level is divided into sublevels (smaller groups of orbitals within the level), made of orbitals; the sublevels and how they fill are covered later in this subsection.
[chem3-s1.1.b] The Bohr model, an older picture of the atom, drew electrons orbiting like planets. The orbital model keeps part of that idea but changes how we describe where an electron is.
[chem3-s1.1.0] The Bohr model gives electrons fixed circular paths at fixed distances from the nucleus.
[chem3-s1.1.1] The orbital model gives no exact path or position for an electron, only the probability of it being found in each region.
[chem3-s1.1.2] The orbital model keeps the Bohr idea of fixed energy levels, so electrons can only have certain energies.
[chem3-s1.1.3] Electrons behave both as particles and as waves, so their exact position and motion cannot both be known; the orbital model therefore describes probability instead of a path.
[chem3-s1.1.4] Diagram: the nucleus at the centre, with fuzzy regions (electron clouds) around it; label the nucleus (protons, neutrons) and the orbitals (electron density). Beside it, draw a Bohr diagram of circular shells for comparison.
[chem3-s1.2.b] In hydrogen, the single electron cannot have just any energy. This is the idea behind the hydrogen spectrum, and it gives two terms, ground state and excited state, that you must be able to distinguish.
[chem3-s1.2.0] The electron in a hydrogen atom can exist only in certain allowed energy levels, n = 1, 2, 3…, so its energy is quantised: it can take only certain fixed values.
[chem3-s1.2.1] The ground state is the lowest energy level available to an electron, which is n = 1 for hydrogen.
[chem3-s1.2.2] An excited state is any higher energy level, reached when the atom absorbs energy, for example from heat or an electric discharge (a current passed through a gas).
[chem3-s1.2.3] Excited states are unstable, so the electron soon falls back to a lower level.
[chem3-s1.3.b] When an excited electron drops to a lower level, the atom has to get rid of the energy difference. It does so as light, and the equation Em − En = hf links the size of the drop to the light given out.
[chem3-s1.3.0] An electron transition is a jump of an electron from one energy level to another.
[chem3-s1.3.1] When an electron falls from a higher level (energy Em) to a lower level (energy En), the energy difference is emitted as a photon, a packet of light energy.
[chem3-s1.3.2] The photon's energy is related to its frequency (the number of wave cycles per second) by Em − En = hf, where h is Planck's constant, 6.626 × 10⁻³⁴ J s, and f is the frequency in hertz (s⁻¹).
[chem3-s1.3.3] Light is emitted when electrons fall to lower levels; when electrons jump up to higher levels they absorb energy instead.
[chem3-s1.3.4] Because the energy levels are fixed, each transition has one definite energy difference, so it emits light of one definite frequency, seen as one line of one colour.
[chem3-s1.3.5] A larger energy gap gives light of higher frequency and shorter wavelength.
[chem3-s1.4.b] An emission spectrum is the pattern of light an element gives out when its atoms are excited. Hydrogen's pattern is separate lines, and that is direct evidence for fixed energy levels.
[chem3-s1.4.0] An emission spectrum is produced by passing an electric discharge through hydrogen gas at low pressure and viewing the light through a spectroscope (an instrument that splits light into its colours) or diffraction grating.
[chem3-s1.4.1] It is a line spectrum: separate bright coloured lines on a dark background, not the continuous band of colours produced by white light.
[chem3-s1.4.2] Each line corresponds to one transition from a higher energy level to a lower one.
[chem3-s1.4.3] A continuous spectrum would be seen if the electron could have any energy, so the line spectrum is evidence that energy levels are fixed.
[chem3-s1.5.b] The lines of hydrogen fall into groups called series, named by the level the electrons fall to. The visible lines are the Balmer series, and each colour comes from a different starting level.
[chem3-s1.5.0] The visible lines form the Balmer series, made by electrons falling to n = 2.
[chem3-s1.5.1] Red line, 656 nm: the electron falls from n = 3 to n = 2.
[chem3-s1.5.2] Blue-green line, 486 nm: the electron falls from n = 4 to n = 2.
[chem3-s1.5.3] Blue-violet line, 434 nm: the electron falls from n = 5 to n = 2.
[chem3-s1.5.4] Violet line, 410 nm: the electron falls from n = 6 to n = 2.
[chem3-s1.6.b] Falls to levels other than n = 2 give lines outside the visible region. The spacing of the lines also reflects how the levels are spaced.
[chem3-s1.6.0] Transitions down to n = 1 form the Lyman series, which lies in the ultraviolet (light of higher energy than visible light).
[chem3-s1.6.1] Transitions down to n = 3 form the Paschen series, which lies in the infrared (light of lower energy than visible light).
[chem3-s1.6.2] The lines get closer together at higher energy, because the energy levels themselves get closer together further from the nucleus.
[chem3-s1.6.3] Diagram: an energy-level diagram with levels n = 1 to 6, spaced more closely going up; downward arrows to n = 2 labelled with the four visible lines, plus arrows to n = 1 (ultraviolet) and n = 3 (infrared).
[chem3-s1.6.4] Diagram: beside it, sketch the line spectrum with the four visible lines labelled.
[chem3-s1.7.b] Hydrogen's lines come from electrons changing level. The same thing happens in every element, which is why heating a compound in a flame makes a colour you can use to identify it.
[chem3-s1.7.0] Heating an atom in a flame gives energy to its electrons, which move up to higher energy levels.
[chem3-s1.7.1] The electrons fall back to lower levels and give out the energy as light.
[chem3-s1.7.2] Each element has its own set of energy levels, so it gives out its own colours of light, which identifies the element.
[chem3-s1.8.b] A flame test identifies a metal from the colour its salt gives to a flame. A fair test needs the right equipment, and everything except the metal kept the same.
[chem3-s1.8.0] Aim: to identify the metal in a salt from the colour it gives to a flame.
[chem3-s1.8.1] Equipment: Bunsen burner; platinum or nichrome wire loop (or wooden splints soaked overnight in deionised water); watch glasses; concentrated hydrochloric acid; samples of the chlorides of the six metals (lithium, sodium, potassium, copper, barium and strontium); deionised water.
[chem3-s1.8.2] Independent variable (the one you change): the metal in the salt being tested.
[chem3-s1.8.3] Dependent variable (the one you observe): the colour of the flame.
[chem3-s1.8.4] Controlled variables: the same flame, the same amount of salt on the wire, and the same cleaning procedure before every test.
[chem3-s1.8.5] Control: a clean wire with no salt on it, held in the flame, gives no colour, so any colour seen with a salt comes from the salt.
[chem3-s1.9.b] The method is four steps, repeated for each salt. The cleaning step matters most, because leftover salt from a previous test would colour the flame and give a wrong result.
[chem3-s1.9.0] 1. Clean the wire by dipping it in concentrated hydrochloric acid and holding it in the hottest part of a non-luminous (blue) Bunsen flame, with the air hole open, until it gives no colour.
[chem3-s1.9.1] 2. Dip the wire in the acid again, then into the salt so that a little sticks to it; chlorides are used because they are volatile (they vaporise easily in the flame).
[chem3-s1.9.2] 3. Hold the wire at the edge of the non-luminous flame and note the colour.
[chem3-s1.9.3] 4. Clean the wire again before testing the next salt; if splints are used, use a different splint for each salt.
[chem3-s1.9.4] Repeats: repeat each test to confirm the colour observed.
[chem3-s1.9.5] Viewing potassium through cobalt-blue glass filters out the yellow light from any sodium impurity, so the lilac colour can be seen.
[chem3-s1.10.b] The chemicals and the hot wire are hazards, and the result only means something when it is compared with known colours. Each piece of apparatus also needs a labelled diagram.
[chem3-s1.10.0] Safety: wear goggles, so that no splash or spark reaches the eyes.
[chem3-s1.10.1] Safety: concentrated hydrochloric acid is corrosive, so wear gloves and use a small volume.
[chem3-s1.10.2] Safety: barium salts are toxic, so wash hands after handling them and do not ingest them.
[chem3-s1.10.3] Safety: keep the hot wire away from the skin, because it can burn.
[chem3-s1.10.4] Conclusion: a flame colour that matches a reference colour in the list of flame colours below identifies the metal in the salt.
[chem3-s1.10.5] Diagram: a Bunsen burner with a non-luminous (blue) flame, and a wire loop holding the salt at the edge of the flame; label the air hole, the non-luminous flame and the wire loop.
[chem3-s1.10.6] Diagram: a wooden splint loaded with salt, held at the edge of the flame; label the splint and the salt.
[chem3-s1.10.7] Diagram: cobalt-blue glass held between the eye and the flame; label the cobalt-blue glass.
[chem3-s1.11.b] Each of the six metals gives its own flame colour. Learn the colour for each, and note the one pair that look alike.
[chem3-s1.11.0] Lithium: crimson red, which is why it is easily confused with strontium.
[chem3-s1.11.1] Sodium: bright yellow-orange; the colour is persistent (it lasts and is strong).
[chem3-s1.11.2] Potassium: lilac (pale purple).
[chem3-s1.11.3] Copper: blue-green.
[chem3-s1.11.4] Barium: pale (apple) green.
[chem3-s1.11.5] Strontium: red (scarlet).
[chem3-s1.11.6] Lithium and strontium are both red and are hard to tell apart by eye, so their line spectra are used to distinguish them.
[chem3-s1.12.b] A flame colour is a mix of light of several wavelengths. Splitting the light with a spectroscope separates it into lines, which gives a more precise way to identify an element.
[chem3-s1.12.0] A spectroscope splits the light from a flame or electric discharge into its separate colours.
[chem3-s1.12.1] Each element gives a line emission spectrum: a pattern of bright lines at specific wavelengths on a dark background, unique to that element like a fingerprint.
[chem3-s1.12.2] To identify an element, compare the lines from an unknown sample, obtained as primary data (your own measurements) from a spectroscope or as secondary data (from another source) such as a published spectrum, with reference spectra of known elements.
[chem3-s1.12.3] A match of all the lines identifies the element.
[chem3-s1.13.b] The syllabus names three elements whose line spectra you must be able to use. Their lines explain the flame colours seen, and the same idea is applied in stars and fireworks.
[chem3-s1.13.0] Sodium: two very close bright yellow lines at 589.0 nm and 589.6 nm (the sodium D lines), which give its yellow flame.
[chem3-s1.13.1] Strontium: several lines, strongest in the red, so its flame looks red.
[chem3-s1.13.2] Copper: lines mainly in the green, giving its blue-green flame.
[chem3-s1.13.3] Line spectra are used to identify elements in samples and in stars.
[chem3-s1.13.4] Fireworks use metal salts for their colours: strontium for red, barium for green, sodium for yellow and copper for blue-green.
[chem3-s1.14.b] For the first 20 elements, you can write an atom's electrons as a list of numbers, one for each main energy level. The electrons fill from the inside out.
[chem3-s1.14.0] Electrons fill the lowest energy levels available first, because that is the most stable arrangement.
[chem3-s1.14.1] For elements 1 to 20, the first main energy level holds up to 2 electrons, the second up to 8 and the third up to 8; the fourth level starts to fill at potassium.
[chem3-s1.14.2] The outer-shell (valence) electrons, those in the outermost occupied level, determine the chemical properties of an element.
[chem3-s1.14.3] Elements in the same group have the same number of outer electrons, which is why they have similar properties.
[chem3-s1.15.b] Write the number of electrons in each main energy level, from the inside out, separated by commas. These ten examples show the pattern.
[chem3-s1.15.0] Hydrogen (H) 1.
[chem3-s1.15.1] Helium (He) 2.
[chem3-s1.15.2] Carbon (C) 2,4.
[chem3-s1.15.3] Oxygen (O) 2,6.
[chem3-s1.15.4] Sodium (Na) 2,8,1.
[chem3-s1.15.5] Magnesium (Mg) 2,8,2.
[chem3-s1.15.6] Chlorine (Cl) 2,8,7.
[chem3-s1.15.7] Argon (Ar) 2,8,8.
[chem3-s1.16.b] Potassium and calcium show the fourth level beginning to fill, and a shell diagram is a way to draw an arrangement.
[chem3-s1.16.0] Potassium (K) 2,8,8,1.
[chem3-s1.16.1] Calcium (Ca) 2,8,8,2.
[chem3-s1.16.2] Diagram: a Bohr-style shell diagram for sodium; label the nucleus as 11 protons and 12 neutrons, and label the shells holding 2, 8 and 1 electrons.
[chem3-s1.17.b] Atoms with a full outer energy level are stable. Many atoms reach one by losing or gaining electrons, which turns them into ions (charged particles).
[chem3-s1.17.0] A stable electronic configuration has a full outer energy level, as in the noble gases (the very unreactive elements in group 18): helium 2, neon 2,8 and argon 2,8,8.
[chem3-s1.17.1] Atoms usually reach a full outer level of eight electrons (an octet), or two for the first level.
[chem3-s1.17.2] Metals in groups 1, 2 and 13 lose electrons to form positive ions with a noble-gas configuration: Na⁺ 2,8; Mg²⁺ 2,8; Al³⁺ 2,8.
[chem3-s1.17.3] Potassium and calcium ions also have noble-gas configurations: K⁺ 2,8,8; Ca²⁺ 2,8,8.
[chem3-s1.17.4] Non-metals in groups 15, 16 and 17 gain electrons to form negative ions with a noble-gas configuration: N³⁻ 2,8; O²⁻ 2,8; F⁻ 2,8.
[chem3-s1.17.5] Sulfide and chloride ions have noble-gas configurations: S²⁻ 2,8,8; Cl⁻ 2,8,8.
[chem3-s1.17.6] Ions with the same electron arrangement as each other are isoelectronic; for example Na⁺, Mg²⁺, F⁻, O²⁻ and Ne all have the arrangement 2,8.
[chem3-s1.18.b] A main energy level is split into sublevels, and each sublevel is made of orbitals. The type of sublevel decides how many orbitals it has and so how many electrons it holds.
[chem3-s1.18.0] A sublevel is a subdivision of a main energy level, made of orbitals of equal energy; the sublevels are s, p, d and f.
[chem3-s1.18.1] An s sublevel has 1 orbital, holding 2 electrons, because each orbital holds 2.
[chem3-s1.18.2] A p sublevel has 3 orbitals, holding 6 electrons, because each orbital holds 2.
[chem3-s1.18.3] A d sublevel has 5 orbitals, holding 10 electrons, because 5 × 2 = 10.
[chem3-s1.19.b] The higher the main energy level, the more types of sublevel it contains. The sublevels fill in order of increasing energy, and this order has a surprise at 4s and 3d.
[chem3-s1.19.0] Level 1 contains 1s only, so it holds just 2 electrons.
[chem3-s1.19.1] Level 2 contains 2s and 2p, so it holds 2 + 6 = 8 electrons.
[chem3-s1.19.2] Level 3 contains 3s, 3p and 3d.
[chem3-s1.19.3] Level 4 contains 4s, 4p, 4d and 4f.
[chem3-s1.19.4] The order of filling, from lowest to highest energy, is 1s, 2s, 2p, 3s, 3p, 4s, 3d, 4p.
[chem3-s1.19.5] The 4s sublevel is lower in energy than 3d, so it fills first.
[chem3-s1.20.b] Three rules decide how electrons are placed in orbitals. Together they let you draw an orbital (box) diagram and count unpaired electrons.
[chem3-s1.20.0] Aufbau principle: the filling rule above, applied to orbitals; electrons occupy the lowest-energy orbital available first.
[chem3-s1.20.1] Pauli exclusion principle: an orbital holds at most two electrons, and they must have opposite spins (a property of the electron, like turning in opposite directions), shown as ↑↓.
[chem3-s1.20.2] Hund's rule: when filling orbitals of equal energy, such as the three 2p orbitals, electrons occupy them singly with parallel spins before any pairing occurs.
[chem3-s1.20.3] Orbital (box) diagrams show one box per orbital, with arrows for electrons.
[chem3-s1.20.4] Nitrogen: 1s ↑↓; 2s ↑↓; 2p ↑ ↑ ↑, giving three unpaired electrons (electrons alone in an orbital).
[chem3-s1.20.5] Oxygen: 1s ↑↓; 2s ↑↓; 2p ↑↓ ↑ ↑, giving two unpaired electrons, because its fourth 2p electron has to pair up with one already in an orbital.
[chem3-s1.21.b] The s, p, d notation writes each sublevel followed by its number of electrons as a superscript. Up to calcium the sublevels simply fill in the order given above.
[chem3-s1.21.0] Notation writes each sublevel followed by its number of electrons as a superscript: sodium 1s² 2s² 2p⁶ 3s¹.
[chem3-s1.21.1] Chlorine: 1s² 2s² 2p⁶ 3s² 3p⁵.
[chem3-s1.21.2] Calcium: 1s² 2s² 2p⁶ 3s² 3p⁶ 4s², with 4s filling before 3d because 4s is lower in energy.
[chem3-s1.21.3] Beyond calcium the 3d sublevel fills.
[chem3-s1.22.b] Once 3d starts to fill, the configurations get longer. The 4s sublevel fills before 3d, but the configuration is written with the levels in order.
[chem3-s1.22.0] Iron: 1s² 2s² 2p⁶ 3s² 3p⁶ 3d⁶ 4s², written in level order rather than filling order.
[chem3-s1.22.1] Bromine: 1s² 2s² 2p⁶ 3s² 3p⁶ 3d¹⁰ 4s² 4p⁵.
[chem3-s1.22.2] Krypton, element 36, ends 3d¹⁰ 4s² 4p⁶.
[chem3-s1.22.3] Noble-gas shorthand replaces the configuration of the previous noble gas with its symbol in square brackets: potassium [Ar] 4s¹; iron [Ar] 3d⁶ 4s².
[chem3-s1.23.b] Two elements do not follow the filling order exactly. One electron moves from 4s into 3d, because a half-full or full d sublevel is more stable.
[chem3-s1.23.0] Chromium is an exception: 1s² 2s² 2p⁶ 3s² 3p⁶ 3d⁵ 4s¹.
[chem3-s1.23.1] Copper is an exception: 1s² 2s² 2p⁶ 3s² 3p⁶ 3d¹⁰ 4s¹.
[chem3-s1.23.2] The chromium and copper exceptions occur because a half-full or full d sublevel is especially stable.
[chem3-s1.24.b] To write an ion, take the atom's configuration and remove or add electrons at the outermost sublevel. The result is usually a stable configuration, with full sublevels.
[chem3-s1.24.0] Ions, excluding transition metal ions, are written by removing or adding electrons from the outermost sublevel: Na⁺ 1s² 2s² 2p⁶.
[chem3-s1.24.1] Chloride ion: Cl⁻ 1s² 2s² 2p⁶ 3s² 3p⁶.
[chem3-s1.24.2] Bromide ion: Br⁻ 1s² 2s² 2p⁶ 3s² 3p⁶ 3d¹⁰ 4s² 4p⁶.
[chem3-s1.24.3] Calcium ion: Ca²⁺ 1s² 2s² 2p⁶ 3s² 3p⁶.
[chem3-s1.24.4] Stable configurations at this level are full sublevels (s², p⁶) and the noble-gas configuration ns² np⁶.
[chem3-s1.25.b] An orbital's shape shows where the electron is most likely to be found. You need only the s and p shapes.
[chem3-s1.25.0] An s orbital is spherical and centred on the nucleus.
[chem3-s1.25.1] The 2s orbital is larger than the 1s orbital, because it belongs to a higher energy level, further from the nucleus.
[chem3-s1.25.2] A p orbital is dumbbell-shaped, with two lobes on either side of the nucleus.
[chem3-s1.25.3] The three p orbitals in a sublevel, px, py and pz, lie at right angles to each other along the x, y and z axes, and have equal energy.
[chem3-s1.25.4] Diagram: a sphere labelled s, and three dumbbells along the x, y and z axes labelled px, py and pz.

CANDIDATES chem2-s1: 1. Atomic structure: the nuclear model
[chem2-s1.0.b] A model of the atom is a picture scientists use to explain the evidence they have. These two early models are background to the nuclear model: each was accepted in its time and then replaced.
[chem2-s1.0.0] Dalton's atomic theory (John Dalton, 1803–1808): all matter is made of tiny indivisible particles called atoms, which means that atoms cannot be split into anything smaller.
[chem2-s1.0.1] Dalton's atoms of the same element are identical, atoms of different elements differ in mass, and atoms combine in simple whole-number ratios to form compounds.
[chem2-s1.0.2] Dalton's atoms are not created or destroyed in chemical reactions, so a reaction only rearranges the atoms that were already there.
[chem2-s1.0.3] Thomson discovered the electron in 1897 using cathode rays (beams of particles given off in a glass tube from which the air has been removed). The electron is a negatively charged particle much lighter than an atom and present in all atoms, so atoms are divisible.
[chem2-s1.0.4] Thomson's plum pudding model: the atom is a sphere of positive charge with negative electrons embedded in it, like plums in a pudding, so the atom is neutral overall because the charges cancel.
[chem2-s1.1.b] The nuclear model came from a scattering experiment, in which a beam of particles is fired at a target and the paths of the deflected particles are studied. The results could not be explained by the plum pudding model.
[chem2-s1.1.0] Gold-foil experiment (1909): Hans Geiger and Ernest Marsden, directed by Rutherford, fired a beam of alpha particles, which are small, fast, positively charged particles, at a very thin sheet of gold foil.
[chem2-s1.1.1] A fluorescent screen (a screen that gives off a flash of light where a particle hits it) was placed around the foil and showed where the particles went after they hit the foil.
[chem2-s1.1.2] Observation: most particles passed straight through the foil, so most of the atom is empty space.
[chem2-s1.1.3] Observation: some particles were deflected through large angles, so there is a concentrated region of positive charge that repels them, because like charges repel.
[chem2-s1.1.4] Observation: a very few particles bounced straight back, so this positive region is very small and very dense, containing almost all of the atom's mass.
[chem2-s1.1.5] This positive region is the nucleus. Rutherford published the nuclear model in 1911: a tiny, dense, positively charged nucleus at the centre of the atom, with electrons moving around it in the mostly empty space outside.
[chem2-s1.1.6] Later work found that the nucleus contains protons (positively charged particles) and neutrons (particles with no charge): Rutherford identified the proton, and Chadwick discovered the neutron in 1932.
[chem2-s1.1.7] Diagram: the gold-foil set-up, labelled with the alpha particle source, the beam, the gold foil and the fluorescent screen, and the paths of particles past the nuclei: straight through, deflected, and bounced back.
[chem2-s1.2.b] Niels Bohr proposed energy levels in 1913 to explain why atoms emit light only at certain frequencies. His model keeps the nucleus but gives the electrons a definite arrangement around it.
[chem2-s1.2.0] Electrons move around the nucleus in fixed circular orbits called energy levels (shells), each with a fixed energy.
[chem2-s1.2.1] An electron in a given energy level does not lose energy, so it stays in that level rather than spiralling inwards.
[chem2-s1.2.2] An electron can move to a higher energy level by absorbing a definite amount of energy.
[chem2-s1.2.3] When the electron falls back to a lower level, it emits light of a definite energy. This explains the lines in emission spectra (the separate coloured lines an element gives out when heated).
[chem2-s1.2.4] Energy levels are numbered n = 1, 2, 3…, with n = 1 closest to the nucleus and lowest in energy.
[chem2-s1.2.5] The energy levels hold up to 2, 8, 18… electrons.
[chem2-s1.3.b] The orbital model is the current model. It describes where electrons are likely to be found, not the path they follow.
[chem2-s1.3.0] Electrons do not follow fixed paths; they occupy orbitals, which are regions of space around the nucleus where there is a high probability of finding an electron.
[chem2-s1.3.1] Main energy levels are divided into sublevels, labelled s, p, d and f; each sublevel is made of orbitals, and each orbital holds up to two electrons. There is more on this in the later subsection on the orbital model.
[chem2-s1.3.2] The model developed from evidence that electrons behave as waves as well as particles, which a fixed circular path cannot describe.
[chem2-s1.3.3] It also rests on the principle that the exact position and the exact path of an electron cannot be known at the same time, which is why only probabilities can be given.
[chem2-s1.4.b] Each model rested on an assumption, and each had a limitation: evidence it could not explain. The first two models fail because the atom has parts and a structure they did not allow for.
[chem2-s1.4.0] Dalton: assumed that atoms are indivisible and that all atoms of an element are identical.
[chem2-s1.4.1] Dalton: limited because subatomic particles (particles smaller than the atom) exist, and because isotopes show that atoms of the same element can differ in mass.
[chem2-s1.4.2] Plum pudding model: assumed that positive charge is spread through the atom.
[chem2-s1.4.3] Plum pudding model: limited because it cannot explain the large deflections and the particles that bounced back in the gold-foil experiment, which need a small, dense positive centre.
[chem2-s1.5.b] The nuclear model fixed the problem of the positive charge but said little about the electrons.
[chem2-s1.5.0] Nuclear model: assumed that electrons orbit the nucleus like planets.
[chem2-s1.5.1] Nuclear model: limited because it does not say how electrons are arranged in the space around the nucleus.
[chem2-s1.5.2] Nuclear model: limited because a charged electron moving in a circle would be expected to lose energy and spiral into the nucleus, so the atom would not be stable.
[chem2-s1.5.3] Nuclear model: limited because it cannot explain line spectra (the separate lines of light an element emits).
[chem2-s1.6.b] The Bohr model fixed the nuclear model's problems for hydrogen but not for other atoms. The orbital model is the best fit for the evidence, but it has its own limitation.
[chem2-s1.6.0] Bohr model: assumed that electrons move in fixed circular orbits with fixed energies.
[chem2-s1.6.1] Bohr model: explains the spectrum of hydrogen, which has one electron, but not the spectra of atoms with more than one electron.
[chem2-s1.6.2] Bohr model: does not account for sublevels or for the wave nature of electrons.
[chem2-s1.6.3] Bohr model: treats the electron's exact path as known, but the exact position and path of an electron cannot be known at the same time.
[chem2-s1.6.4] Orbital model: the best fit for the evidence available, but its limitation is that it is mathematical and abstract.
[chem2-s1.6.5] Orbital model: gives probabilities of where an electron is found, not exact positions, and is hard to picture.
[chem2-s1.7.b] Scientists judge a model by how well it explains the evidence. Replaced models are not useless, because they still work for the things they explain well.
[chem2-s1.7.0] Each model was accepted because it explained the evidence of its time, and was replaced or refined when new evidence did not fit.
[chem2-s1.7.1] The Bohr model is still enough to explain flame colours and simple line spectra.
[chem2-s1.7.2] The Bohr model's arrangement of electrons in shells, such as 2, 8, 8, is still used to predict bonding.
[chem2-s1.7.3] The nuclear model still explains why atoms are mostly empty space.
[chem2-s1.8.b] An atom is made of protons, neutrons and electrons. Their masses and charges are given relative to the proton (that is, as comparisons with it), because the real values are very small numbers.
[chem2-s1.8.0] Proton: relative mass 1, relative charge +1, found in the nucleus.
[chem2-s1.8.1] Neutron: relative mass 1, relative charge 0 (no charge), found in the nucleus.
[chem2-s1.8.2] Electron: relative mass about 1/1840 of a proton, so negligible though not zero; relative charge −1; found outside the nucleus, in energy levels (orbitals).
[chem2-s1.9.b] The relative values hide the real sizes. The actual masses show how tiny the particles are and how much lighter the electron is.
[chem2-s1.9.0] Actual masses: proton 1.6726 × 10⁻²⁷ kg; neutron 1.6749 × 10⁻²⁷ kg; electron 9.109 × 10⁻³¹ kg.
[chem2-s1.9.1] The proton and neutron have almost the same mass, which is why each is given a relative mass of 1.
[chem2-s1.9.2] The charge on a proton is equal in size and opposite in sign to the charge on an electron, so equal numbers of each cancel out.
[chem2-s1.10.b] The particles are not spread evenly. The nucleus is tiny, yet it holds almost all the mass, and the rest of the atom is mostly empty.
[chem2-s1.10.0] Protons and neutrons, collectively called nucleons, make up the nucleus, which holds almost all of the atom's mass because electrons are so light.
[chem2-s1.10.1] The nucleus is tiny compared with the atom: an atom is about 10⁵ (100 000) times the diameter of its nucleus, so most of the atom is empty space.
[chem2-s1.10.2] A neutral atom has equal numbers of protons and electrons, so the positive and negative charges cancel.
[chem2-s1.10.3] The number of protons identifies the element: every atom with 6 protons, for example, is carbon.
[chem2-s1.11.b] Exams ask you to state the numbers of protons, neutrons and electrons in an atom or ion. An ion is an atom that has lost or gained electrons, so it carries a charge. The number of protons never changes in an ion.
[chem2-s1.11.0] Number of protons = atomic number (the number of protons in the nucleus).
[chem2-s1.11.1] Number of neutrons = mass number − atomic number, where the mass number is the total of protons and neutrons.
[chem2-s1.11.2] In a neutral atom, number of electrons = number of protons.
[chem2-s1.11.3] A positive ion has lost electrons, so number of electrons = number of protons − charge, e.g. Na⁺ has 11 protons and 10 electrons.
[chem2-s1.11.4] A negative ion has gained electrons, so number of electrons = number of protons + charge, e.g. Cl⁻ (chlorine-35) has 17 protons, 18 neutrons and 18 electrons; O²⁻ has 8 protons and 10 electrons.
[chem2-s1.12.b] The three particles were found one at a time. The order of discovery follows how easy each is to detect.
[chem2-s1.12.0] The electron was discovered by J. J. Thomson in 1897.
[chem2-s1.12.1] The proton was identified by Rutherford about 1917–1919.
[chem2-s1.12.2] The neutron was discovered by Chadwick in 1932.
[chem2-s1.12.3] The neutron was found last because it has no charge, which makes it hard to detect.
[chem2-s1.13.b] Two whole numbers describe the nucleus of an atom. Together they give the number of each particle and are written beside the element's symbol.
[chem2-s1.13.0] Atomic number (Z): the number of protons in the nucleus of an atom of an element.
[chem2-s1.13.1] Each element has its own atomic number, and the periodic table is arranged in order of atomic number.
[chem2-s1.13.2] Mass number (A): the total number of protons and neutrons in the nucleus of an atom.
[chem2-s1.13.3] Number of neutrons = mass number − atomic number (A − Z).
[chem2-s1.13.4] Nuclear notation writes the mass number at the top left and the atomic number at the bottom left of the element symbol, e.g. ²³₁₁Na has 11 protons, 12 neutrons and (as an atom) 11 electrons.
[chem2-s1.14.b] Atoms of one element are not all identical. They can have different numbers of neutrons, and such atoms are called isotopes.
[chem2-s1.14.0] Isotopes have the same atomic number, so the same number of protons, but different mass numbers because they have different numbers of neutrons.
[chem2-s1.14.1] Hydrogen-1 (¹H) has no neutrons; hydrogen-2 (²H), called deuterium, has one neutron; hydrogen-3 (³H), called tritium, has two.
[chem2-s1.14.2] Chlorine-35 (³⁵Cl) has 18 neutrons and chlorine-37 (³⁷Cl) has 20 neutrons.
[chem2-s1.14.3] Carbon-12, carbon-13 and carbon-14 are isotopes of carbon.
[chem2-s1.14.4] Isotopes of an element have the same chemical properties because they have the same number of electrons and the same electron arrangement, and electrons control how an atom reacts.
[chem2-s1.14.5] Isotopes have slightly different physical properties that depend on mass, such as density and rate of diffusion (how fast a gas spreads out).
[chem2-s1.14.6] Isotopes are written by name and mass number, e.g. chlorine-35, or in nuclear notation, e.g. ³⁵₁₇Cl.
[chem2-s1.15.b] Because an element's isotopes have different masses, one atom's mass number does not describe the element as a whole. Relative atomic mass is an average over its isotopes.
[chem2-s1.15.0] Relative atomic mass (Ar): the average mass of the atoms of an element, taking account of the abundance of its isotopes, relative to one-twelfth of the mass of an atom of carbon-12.
[chem2-s1.15.1] Carbon-12 is the standard: an atom of carbon-12 is defined as having a mass of exactly 12 units.
[chem2-s1.15.2] Ar has no units, because it compares masses.
[chem2-s1.15.3] The mass number of a single isotope is always a whole number; Ar is a weighted average of the isotopes present, so it is usually not whole.
[chem2-s1.15.4] Chlorine is about 75.8% chlorine-35 and 24.2% chlorine-37, giving Ar(Cl) = 35.45 (quoted as 35.5 in school tables). There is more on calculating Ar from isotopic abundance in the later subsection on the mole.
