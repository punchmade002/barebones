TARGET chem18-s1: 1. Dynamic equilibrium and Kc
[chem18-s1.0.b] Some reactions run to completion; others reach a balance in which the forward and reverse reactions both continue. Telling the two types apart is the starting point for the whole chapter.
[chem18-s1.0.0] Some reactions go to completion: the reactants are converted completely into products and the reaction cannot easily be reversed, e.g. burning magnesium, 2Mg(s) + O₂(g) → 2MgO(s).
[chem18-s1.0.1] A reversible reaction is one in which the products can react to form the reactants again, so both the forward and the reverse reactions take place. It is shown by the sign ⇌, a double arrow.
[chem18-s1.0.2] The forward reaction is the one from left to right as written; the reverse (back) reaction is from right to left. Both are always labelled relative to how the equation is written.
[chem18-s1.0.3] The Haber process, N₂(g) + 3H₂(g) ⇌ 2NH₃(g), makes ammonia from nitrogen and hydrogen. It is named after Fritz Haber (1868–1934); Carl Bosch (1874–1940) developed it industrially.
[chem18-s1.0.4] Hydrogen and iodine react reversibly: H₂(g) + I₂(g) ⇌ 2HI(g), where HI is hydrogen iodide gas.
[chem18-s1.0.5] Esterification (an acid reacting with an alcohol to make an ester and water) is reversible: CH₃COOH + C₂H₅OH ⇌ CH₃COOC₂H₅ + H₂O.
[chem18-s1.0.6] Nitrogen dioxide and dinitrogen tetroxide: 2NO₂(g) ⇌ N₂O₄(g). NO₂ is brown and N₂O₄ is colourless, so the colour of the mixture shows how far the reaction has moved.
[chem18-s1.0.7] Weak acids (acids that only partly split into ions in water) and acid–base indicators (dyes that change colour with acidity) are also reversible, e.g. CH₃COOH(aq) ⇌ CH₃COO⁻(aq) + H⁺(aq) and HIn ⇌ H⁺ + In⁻. There is more on this in How indicators work.
[chem18-s1.1.b] In a reversible reaction in a sealed container, the mixture settles into a balance. At chemical equilibrium both reactions continue, and their rates are equal.
[chem18-s1.1.0] Chemical equilibrium is a state of dynamic balance in a reversible reaction in which the rate of the forward reaction (how fast reactants turn into products) equals the rate of the reverse reaction.
[chem18-s1.1.1] It is called dynamic because both reactions are still happening: reactant particles are still forming products and product particles are still forming reactants, at the same rate.
[chem18-s1.1.2] Because substances are formed and used up at the same rate, their concentrations (amounts per litre) stay constant at equilibrium, so observable properties such as colour, pressure and density stop changing.
[chem18-s1.1.3] The concentrations are constant but not equal: at equilibrium there may be much more product than reactant, or the reverse. It is the rates that are equal, not the amounts.
[chem18-s1.1.4] Static equilibrium is different: nothing moves at all, e.g. a metre stick balanced at its centre.
[chem18-s1.1.5] Analogy: a person walking up a down-escalator at the same speed it moves stays in the same place, although both movements continue. Likewise nothing seems to change at equilibrium, but both reactions carry on.
[chem18-s1.2.b] Equilibrium only forms if nothing can escape. This is why the container matters.
[chem18-s1.2.0] Equilibrium can be reached only in a closed system, where no reactants or products can enter or leave; energy such as heat can still pass to and from the surroundings.
[chem18-s1.2.1] Heating calcium carbonate in a sealed container, CaCO₃(s) ⇌ CaO(s) + CO₂(g): the CO₂ gas builds up and equilibrium is reached.
[chem18-s1.2.2] Heating calcium carbonate in an open container: the CO₂ escapes, so the reverse reaction cannot happen and the calcium carbonate decomposes completely.
[chem18-s1.2.3] A fizzy drink in a closed bottle contains CO₂(g) ⇌ CO₂(aq) at equilibrium (gas above the drink and gas dissolved in it); once the bottle is opened the gas escapes and the drink goes flat.
[chem18-s1.3.b] Equilibrium is not instant. The two rates start far apart and move towards each other until they meet.
[chem18-s1.3.0] At the start, when only reactants are present, the forward rate is high and the reverse rate is zero, because there are no products yet to react back.
[chem18-s1.3.1] As reactants are used up the forward rate falls; as products build up the reverse rate rises.
[chem18-s1.3.2] Equilibrium is reached when the two rates become equal; from then on all concentrations are constant. This is why equilibrium is reached some time after the reactants are mixed.
[chem18-s1.3.3] The same equilibrium is reached from either direction: starting with 1 mol of H₂ and 1 mol of I₂, or with 2 mol of HI only, in the same sealed flask at the same temperature, gives the same equilibrium mixture.
[chem18-s1.3.4] Concentration–time graph for N₂ + 3H₂ ⇌ 2NH₃ starting with N₂ and H₂: the reactant curves fall and the product curve rises from zero, each changing quickly at first because the reactants are most concentrated then.
[chem18-s1.3.5] All three curves level off (become horizontal) at the same moment, when equilibrium is reached. H₂ falls three times as fast as N₂, and NH₃ rises twice as fast as N₂ falls, matching the 1 : 3 : 2 ratio in the equation.
[chem18-s1.3.6] Rate–time graph: the forward-rate curve falls and the reverse-rate curve rises from zero until they meet and continue as one horizontal line at equilibrium.
[chem18-s1.3.7] Diagram: draw (a) concentration against time, with the reactant and product curves levelling off and the moment equilibrium is reached marked; and (b) forward and reverse rates against time, meeting at equilibrium.
[chem18-s1.4.b] At equilibrium the concentrations are not random: for a given reaction at a given temperature they always fit one fixed pattern. That pattern is a number, the equilibrium constant Kc.
[chem18-s1.4.0] The equilibrium law: at equilibrium, at a fixed temperature, the concentrations of products and reactants are related by a constant, the equilibrium constant Kc.
[chem18-s1.4.1] For aA + bB ⇌ cC + dD (where a, b, c and d are the coefficients, the numbers in front of each formula in the balanced equation): Kc = [C]ᶜ[D]ᵈ ÷ [A]ᵃ[B]ᵇ. Square brackets mean the concentration in mol L⁻¹ at equilibrium.
[chem18-s1.4.2] Products go on top and reactants on the bottom; each concentration is raised to the power of its coefficient in the balanced equation, and the concentrations are multiplied, not added.
[chem18-s1.4.3] Example: N₂(g) + 3H₂(g) ⇌ 2NH₃(g) gives Kc = [NH₃]² ÷ [N₂][H₂]³. The 3 in front of H₂ becomes the power 3.
[chem18-s1.4.4] Example: H₂(g) + I₂(g) ⇌ 2HI(g) gives Kc = [HI]² ÷ [H₂][I₂], with HI squared because its coefficient is 2.
[chem18-s1.4.5] Example: PCl₅(g) ⇌ PCl₃(g) + Cl₂(g) gives Kc = [PCl₃][Cl₂] ÷ [PCl₅].
[chem18-s1.4.6] Example: esterification, CH₃COOH + C₂H₅OH ⇌ CH₃COOC₂H₅ + H₂O, gives Kc = [CH₃COOC₂H₅][H₂O] ÷ [CH₃COOH][C₂H₅OH]; water is included because it is a product, not the solvent.
[chem18-s1.4.7] Example: Fe³⁺(aq) + SCN⁻(aq) ⇌ FeSCN²⁺(aq) gives Kc = [FeSCN²⁺] ÷ [Fe³⁺][SCN⁻].
[chem18-s1.5.b] Kc is a ratio of concentrations, so whether it has units depends on how many concentrations are on top and on the bottom.
[chem18-s1.5.0] To find the units, substitute mol L⁻¹ for each concentration in the Kc expression and cancel.
[chem18-s1.5.1] When the total of the powers is the same on top and bottom, as in H₂ + I₂ ⇌ 2HI or esterification, the units cancel and Kc has no units.
[chem18-s1.5.2] For N₂ + 3H₂ ⇌ 2NH₃: (mol L⁻¹)² ÷ (mol L⁻¹)⁴ = (mol L⁻¹)⁻², that is L² mol⁻², because there are fewer powers on top than on the bottom.
[chem18-s1.5.3] For PCl₅ ⇌ PCl₃ + Cl₂: (mol L⁻¹)² ÷ (mol L⁻¹), so the units of Kc are mol L⁻¹.
[chem18-s1.6.b] Many changes alter the composition of an equilibrium mixture, but the constant itself is much harder to move. Only one factor changes its value.
[chem18-s1.6.0] Kc changes only with temperature, so a value of Kc must always be quoted with its temperature.
[chem18-s1.6.1] Changing a concentration does not change Kc: the system shifts until the ratio in the Kc expression has its original value again.
[chem18-s1.6.2] Changing the pressure of a gas mixture does not change Kc: the concentrations change, but the new equilibrium mixture gives the same value of Kc.
[chem18-s1.6.3] A catalyst (a substance that speeds up a reaction without being used up) does not change Kc: it speeds up the forward and reverse reactions equally, so equilibrium is reached sooner with the same composition.
[chem18-s1.6.4] Exothermic forward reaction (one that gives out heat, ΔH negative): raising the temperature decreases Kc, so less product is present at equilibrium; lowering the temperature increases Kc. Example: N₂ + 3H₂ ⇌ 2NH₃, with ΔH = −92 kJ mol⁻¹.
[chem18-s1.6.5] Endothermic forward reaction (one that takes in heat, ΔH positive): raising the temperature increases Kc; lowering it decreases Kc. Example: PCl₅ ⇌ PCl₃ + Cl₂, which is endothermic in the forward direction; PCl₅ decomposes further on heating.
[chem18-s1.6.6] So if Kc rises with temperature the forward reaction is endothermic; if Kc falls with temperature the forward reaction is exothermic.
[chem18-s1.7.b] The size of Kc tells you where the equilibrium lies, and comparing it with the current mixture tells you which way a reaction will go.
[chem18-s1.7.0] A large Kc (much greater than 1) means the equilibrium mixture is mainly products: the equilibrium lies to the right and the reaction goes almost to completion. A small Kc (much less than 1) means mainly reactants: it lies to the left and little product forms.
[chem18-s1.7.1] A Kc near 1 means appreciable amounts of both reactants and products are present at equilibrium. Esterification has Kc ≈ 4 (at room temperature), so starting with 1 mol each of ethanoic acid and ethanol, about two-thirds of a mole of ester forms.
[chem18-s1.7.2] Kc tells how far a reaction goes, not how fast: a reaction with a large Kc may be very slow, e.g. without a catalyst.
[chem18-s1.7.3] The reaction quotient, Q, has the same expression as Kc but uses the concentrations at any moment, not necessarily at equilibrium.
[chem18-s1.7.4] If Q is less than Kc, the mixture has too little product and the reaction proceeds forward (to the right) until Q = Kc; if Q is greater than Kc, it proceeds in reverse (to the left); if Q = Kc, the mixture is at equilibrium.
[chem18-s1.7.5] Worked prediction (sample data): for esterification with Kc = 4.0, a mixture with 0.50 mol each of acid, ethanol, ester and water has Q = (0.50 × 0.50) ÷ (0.50 × 0.50) = 1.0, which is less than Kc, so more ester forms.
[chem18-s1.7.6] This explains Le Châtelier’s principle (a system at equilibrium shifts to oppose a change) mathematically: adding a reactant makes the denominator larger, Q falls below Kc, and the reaction proceeds forward until Q = Kc again.
[chem18-s1.7.7] Writing the equation the other way round inverts Kc: for 2HI ⇌ H₂ + I₂, Kc = [H₂][I₂] ÷ [HI]², which is 1 ÷ Kc for H₂ + I₂ ⇌ 2HI at the same temperature.
[chem18-s1.8.b] Kc calculations follow the same routine every time. Working through a table of amounts keeps the steps in order and avoids the usual mistakes.
[chem18-s1.8.0] 1. Write the balanced equation and the Kc expression.
[chem18-s1.8.1] 2. Convert any masses to moles using n = mass ÷ M, where M is the molar mass in g mol⁻¹.
[chem18-s1.8.2] 3. Set up a table with rows Initial (moles at the start), Change (moles reacting or forming, in the ratio of the coefficients) and Equilibrium (initial + change) for every species.
[chem18-s1.8.3] 4. Divide each equilibrium amount by the volume of the container in litres to get concentrations in mol L⁻¹. When the total number of moles is the same on each side, as in H₂ + I₂ ⇌ 2HI or esterification, the volume cancels, so moles can be used directly.
[chem18-s1.8.4] 5. Substitute the concentrations into the Kc expression and calculate; give the answer to 3 significant figures, with units if asked.
[chem18-s1.8.5] To find equilibrium amounts from Kc: let x be the amount that reacts, write each equilibrium amount in terms of x, substitute into the Kc expression and solve for x.
[chem18-s1.8.6] If both sides of the resulting equation are perfect squares, take the square root of both sides. Otherwise rearrange into a quadratic, ax² + bx + c = 0, and use x = (−b ± √(b² − 4ac)) ÷ 2a.
[chem18-s1.8.7] Choose the root that makes physical sense: no equilibrium amount can be negative, so x cannot exceed the starting amount of a reactant.
[chem18-s1.9.b] The simplest type: every equilibrium amount is given, so convert to concentrations and substitute.
[chem18-s1.9.0] Data: H₂(g) + I₂(g) ⇌ 2HI(g) in a 2.00 L flask at a fixed temperature; at equilibrium there are 0.100 mol H₂, 0.150 mol I₂ and 0.840 mol HI.
[chem18-s1.9.1] [H₂] = 0.100 mol ÷ 2.00 L = 0.0500 mol L⁻¹, because Kc needs concentrations, not moles.
[chem18-s1.9.2] [I₂] = 0.150 mol ÷ 2.00 L = 0.0750 mol L⁻¹.
[chem18-s1.9.3] [HI] = 0.840 mol ÷ 2.00 L = 0.420 mol L⁻¹.
[chem18-s1.9.4] Kc = [HI]² ÷ [H₂][I₂] = (0.420)² ÷ (0.0500 × 0.0750) = 0.1764 ÷ 0.00375 = 47.0 (no units, because the powers on top and bottom match).
[chem18-s1.10.b] Here only one equilibrium amount is given. Use the mole ratio from the equation to work out all the others.
[chem18-s1.10.0] Data: 2.00 mol N₂ and 6.00 mol H₂ are sealed in a 4.00 L vessel; at equilibrium 1.20 mol NH₃ is present, for N₂(g) + 3H₂(g) ⇌ 2NH₃(g).
[chem18-s1.10.1] Change: forming 1.20 mol NH₃ uses 0.600 mol N₂ and 1.80 mol H₂, in the ratio 1 : 3 : 2 from the coefficients.
[chem18-s1.10.2] Equilibrium amounts: N₂ = 2.00 − 0.600 = 1.40 mol; H₂ = 6.00 − 1.80 = 4.20 mol; NH₃ = 1.20 mol.
[chem18-s1.10.3] Concentrations (÷ 4.00 L): [N₂] = 0.350 mol L⁻¹; [H₂] = 1.05 mol L⁻¹; [NH₃] = 0.300 mol L⁻¹.
[chem18-s1.10.4] Kc = [NH₃]² ÷ [N₂][H₂]³ = (0.300)² ÷ (0.350 × 1.05³) = 0.0900 ÷ 0.405 = 0.222 L² mol⁻².
[chem18-s1.11.b] Here the starting amount is a mass, and the extent of reaction is a percentage dissociated (the percentage of the starting compound that has split up into products).
[chem18-s1.11.0] Data: 41.7 g of PCl₅ is heated in a sealed 5.00 L vessel; at equilibrium 40.0% has dissociated, for PCl₅(g) ⇌ PCl₃(g) + Cl₂(g).
[chem18-s1.11.1] Molar mass: M(PCl₅) = 31 + 5 × 35.5 = 208.5 g mol⁻¹.
[chem18-s1.11.2] Moles PCl₅ = 41.7 g ÷ 208.5 g mol⁻¹ = 0.200 mol, using n = mass ÷ M.
[chem18-s1.11.3] Moles dissociated = 40.0% of 0.200 mol = 0.0800 mol, because the percentage applies to the starting amount.
[chem18-s1.11.4] At equilibrium: PCl₅ = 0.120 mol; PCl₃ = 0.0800 mol; Cl₂ = 0.0800 mol, because each PCl₅ that dissociates gives one PCl₃ and one Cl₂.
[chem18-s1.11.5] Concentrations (÷ 5.00 L): [PCl₅] = 0.0240 mol L⁻¹; [PCl₃] = [Cl₂] = 0.0160 mol L⁻¹.
[chem18-s1.11.6] Kc = [PCl₃][Cl₂] ÷ [PCl₅] = (0.0160 × 0.0160) ÷ 0.0240 = 0.0107 mol L⁻¹.
[chem18-s1.12.b] Now Kc is given and you find an amount. When the expression is a perfect square (the same bracket squared on top and bottom), taking a square root avoids a quadratic.
[chem18-s1.12.0] Data: 1.00 mol of ethanoic acid and 1.00 mol of ethanol are mixed and reach equilibrium; Kc = 4.00 for CH₃COOH + C₂H₅OH ⇌ CH₃COOC₂H₅ + H₂O.
[chem18-s1.12.1] Let x mol of ester form: at equilibrium the acid and the ethanol are each (1.00 − x) mol, and the ester and the water are each x mol; the volume cancels.
[chem18-s1.12.2] Kc = x² ÷ (1.00 − x)² = 4.00.
[chem18-s1.12.3] Taking the square root of both sides, which removes the squares, gives x ÷ (1.00 − x) = 2.00.
[chem18-s1.12.4] Rearranging: x = 2.00 − 2.00x, so 3.00x = 2.00 and x = 0.667 mol of ester.
[chem18-s1.12.5] The acid and the ethanol each remain at 1.00 − 0.667 = 0.333 mol, because each mole of ester formed used up one mole of each.
[chem18-s1.12.6] Mass of ester = 0.667 mol × 88 g mol⁻¹ = 58.7 g, using M(CH₃COOC₂H₅) = 88 g mol⁻¹.
[chem18-s1.13.b] When the expression is not a perfect square, it rearranges to a quadratic equation (one containing x²) with two roots, and only one of them is physically possible (the other would need more of a reactant than is present).
[chem18-s1.13.0] Data: 1.00 mol H₂ and 2.00 mol I₂ are placed in a 1.00 L flask; Kc = 49.0 at this temperature, for H₂(g) + I₂(g) ⇌ 2HI(g).
[chem18-s1.13.1] Let x mol of H₂ react: at equilibrium H₂ = (1.00 − x), I₂ = (2.00 − x) and HI = 2x; as the volume is 1.00 L, these amounts are also the concentrations in mol L⁻¹.
[chem18-s1.13.2] 49.0 = (2x)² ÷ [(1.00 − x)(2.00 − x)], so 4x² = 49.0(2.00 − 3.00x + x²).
[chem18-s1.13.3] Rearranging gives 45.0x² − 147x + 98.0 = 0, which is the form ax² + bx + c = 0 needed for the formula.
[chem18-s1.13.4] x = (147 ± √(147² − 4 × 45.0 × 98.0)) ÷ (2 × 45.0) = (147 ± 63.0) ÷ 90.0, so x = 2.33 or x = 0.933.
[chem18-s1.13.5] x = 2.33 is impossible because it exceeds the 1.00 mol of H₂ present, so x = 0.933.
[chem18-s1.13.6] Equilibrium concentrations: [H₂] = 0.0667 mol L⁻¹; [I₂] = 1.07 mol L⁻¹; [HI] = 1.87 mol L⁻¹.
[chem18-s1.13.7] Check: 1.87² ÷ (0.0667 × 1.07) ≈ 49, which matches Kc.

CANDIDATES chem8-s1: 1. The mole and molar volume
[chem8-s1.0.b] Atoms are far too small to count, so chemists count them by weighing. The mole links a mass that can be weighed to a number of particles.
[chem8-s1.0.0] The mole (symbol mol) is the SI unit (the internationally agreed system of units) of amount of substance, so it measures how much of a substance there is by counting its particles.
[chem8-s1.0.1] One mole contains exactly 6.02214076 × 10²³ elementary entities (atoms, molecules, ions or electrons); this number is the Avogadro constant, NA = 6.02 × 10²³ mol⁻¹.
[chem8-s1.0.2] Before 2019 the mole was defined through carbon-12 (the carbon atom with 6 protons and 6 neutrons): the Avogadro constant was the number of atoms in 12 g of carbon-12.
[chem8-s1.0.3] Since the 2019 SI redefinition the value of the Avogadro constant is exact by definition.
[chem8-s1.0.4] Always state the particle, because a mole of different particles contains different numbers of atoms: one mole of oxygen atoms (O) contains 6.02 × 10²³ atoms.
[chem8-s1.0.5] One mole of oxygen molecules (O₂) contains 6.02 × 10²³ molecules and 1.204 × 10²⁴ atoms, because each O₂ molecule is made of two atoms.
[chem8-s1.0.6] This shows the scale of the mole: one mole of water is only 18 g (about 18 cm³, since water has a density of about 1.00 g cm⁻³) but contains 6.02 × 10²³ molecules.
[chem8-s1.1.b] To weigh out a mole you need to know what one mole of the substance weighs. That comes from the relative atomic masses of its atoms, and density links mass to volume for liquids and solids.
[chem8-s1.1.0] Relative molecular mass (Mr) is the sum of the relative atomic masses (Ar, the mass of an atom compared with one-twelfth of a carbon-12 atom) of all the atoms in a molecule (or formula unit, the simplest ratio of ions in an ionic compound); it has no units.
[chem8-s1.1.1] Relative atomic masses used in these calculations: H 1, C 12, N 14, O 16, Na 23, Mg 24, S 32, Cl 35.5, Ca 40.
[chem8-s1.1.2] Example: Mr(H₂O) = 2(1) + 16 = 18, because water has two hydrogen atoms and one oxygen atom.
[chem8-s1.1.3] Example: Mr(H₂SO₄) = 2(1) + 32 + 4(16) = 98, because there are two H, one S and four O.
[chem8-s1.1.4] Example: Mr(CaCO₃) = 40 + 12 + 3(16) = 100, because there is one Ca, one C and three O.
[chem8-s1.1.5] Molar mass (M) is the mass of one mole of a substance, in grams per mole (g mol⁻¹); it is numerically equal to Ar or Mr, so the molar mass of water is 18 g mol⁻¹.
[chem8-s1.1.6] Density is mass per unit volume (how much mass is packed into a given space): ρ = m ÷ V, in g cm⁻³ or kg m⁻³.
[chem8-s1.2.b] Every mole calculation uses the same few relationships. Each one is a single step, and you can chain them together.
[chem8-s1.2.0] Moles from mass: n = m ÷ M, with m in g and M in g mol⁻¹. Example: 8.8 g of CO₂ (M = 44 g mol⁻¹): n = 8.8 ÷ 44 = 0.20 mol.
[chem8-s1.2.1] Mass from moles: m = n × M, which is the same relationship turned round. Example: 0.25 mol of NaCl (M = 58.5 g mol⁻¹): m = 0.25 × 58.5 = 14.6 g.
[chem8-s1.2.2] Number of particles from moles: N = n × NA, because each mole holds 6.02 × 10²³ particles. Example: 0.20 mol of CO₂: N = 0.20 × 6.02 × 10²³ = 1.20 × 10²³ molecules.
[chem8-s1.2.3] Moles from number of particles: n = N ÷ NA, which reverses the previous step.
[chem8-s1.2.4] Number of atoms: multiply the number of molecules by the number of atoms in each molecule, because each molecule contains that many atoms.
[chem8-s1.2.5] Example: 0.20 mol of CO₂ contains 0.20 × 3 = 0.60 mol of atoms, so 0.60 × 6.02 × 10²³ = 3.61 × 10²³ atoms.
[chem8-s1.3.b] Gases are usually measured by volume and liquids by volume and density, so these also have to be converted to moles. Moles are the middle step in every conversion.
[chem8-s1.3.0] Gas volume from moles: V = n × molar volume (the volume of one mole of gas), using 22.4 L mol⁻¹ at STP (standard temperature and pressure, the agreed reference conditions for gases: 0 °C and 101.3 kPa).
[chem8-s1.3.1] Mass from volume and density: m = ρ × V, then convert the mass to moles with n = m ÷ M.
[chem8-s1.3.2] Example: 10.0 cm³ of ethanol (density 0.789 g cm⁻³): m = 10.0 × 0.789 = 7.89 g.
[chem8-s1.3.3] Continuing the ethanol example (M = 46 g mol⁻¹): n = 7.89 ÷ 46 = 0.172 mol.
[chem8-s1.3.4] Conversion route: mass ⇄ moles ⇄ number of particles, and moles ⇄ gas volume; always go through moles.
[chem8-s1.4.b] Not all atoms of an element have the same mass, so the relative atomic mass in the data tables is an average. That average rests on two ideas: isotopes and mass number.
[chem8-s1.4.0] Isotopes are atoms of the same element with different mass numbers, because they have the same number of protons but different numbers of neutrons.
[chem8-s1.4.1] The mass number of an isotope is the total number of protons and neutrons in its nucleus (the central core of the atom).
[chem8-s1.4.2] Relative atomic mass (Ar) is the weighted average of the isotope masses, using their percentage abundances (the percentage of the element's atoms that are that isotope), relative to one-twelfth of the mass of a carbon-12 atom.
[chem8-s1.5.b] Each isotope contributes to the average in proportion to how abundant it is. Common isotopes count for more than rare ones.
[chem8-s1.5.0] Step 1: multiply each isotope's mass number by its percentage abundance.
[chem8-s1.5.1] Step 2: add the products together, to get the total mass contributed by 100 atoms.
[chem8-s1.5.2] Step 3: divide the total by 100, because the abundances are percentages, so Ar = Σ(mass number × % abundance) ÷ 100.
[chem8-s1.5.3] Quick check: the answer must lie between the lightest and heaviest isotope mass numbers, nearer to the more abundant isotope.
[chem8-s1.6.b] Here the three-step method is applied to chlorine, boron and magnesium, the last with three isotopes.
[chem8-s1.6.0] Chlorine example (75% ³⁵Cl, 25% ³⁷Cl): the products are 35 × 75 = 2625 and 37 × 25 = 925.
[chem8-s1.6.1] Adding the products gives 2625 + 925 = 3550, and 3550 ÷ 100 = 35.5, so Ar(Cl) = 35.5, nearer 35 because ³⁵Cl is three times as abundant as ³⁷Cl.
[chem8-s1.6.2] Boron example (19.9% ¹⁰B, 80.1% ¹¹B): the products are 10 × 19.9 = 199 and 11 × 80.1 = 881.1.
[chem8-s1.6.3] Adding the products gives 199 + 881.1 = 1080.1, and 1080.1 ÷ 100 = 10.8, so Ar(B) = 10.8.
[chem8-s1.6.4] Magnesium example, three isotopes (79.0% ²⁴Mg, 10.0% ²⁵Mg, 11.0% ²⁶Mg): the products are 24 × 79.0 = 1896, 25 × 10.0 = 250 and 26 × 11.0 = 286.
[chem8-s1.6.5] Adding the products gives 1896 + 250 + 286 = 2432, and 2432 ÷ 100 = 24.3, so Ar(Mg) = 24.3.
[chem8-s1.7.b] The method also runs backwards: if you are given the relative atomic mass and the isotopes, you can find how much of each there is, using algebra.
[chem8-s1.7.0] For two isotopes, let the abundance of one isotope be x%; the other is then (100 − x)%, because the two abundances add up to 100%.
[chem8-s1.7.1] Set up Ar = [mass₁ × x + mass₂ × (100 − x)] ÷ 100 and solve for x.
[chem8-s1.7.2] Boron example (Ar = 10.8; isotopes ¹⁰B and ¹¹B): 10x + 11(100 − x) = 1080.
[chem8-s1.7.3] Expanding gives 10x + 1100 − 11x = 1080, so 1100 − x = 1080 and x = 20.
[chem8-s1.7.4] The abundances are therefore about 20% boron-10 and 80% boron-11, matching the 19.9% and 80.1% used in the Ar calculation.
[chem8-s1.7.5] Using whole mass numbers is an approximation, because real isotope masses are not exact whole numbers; data-book isotope masses give more accurate abundances.
[chem8-s1.8.b] Gas volumes can be compared directly, with no need to weigh. Avogadro's law says why: the volume of a gas depends only on how many molecules it holds.
[chem8-s1.8.0] Avogadro's law: equal volumes of all gases, at the same temperature and pressure, contain equal numbers of molecules.
[chem8-s1.8.1] So at fixed temperature and pressure, the volume of a gas is directly proportional to the number of moles: V ∝ n (double the moles and the volume doubles).
[chem8-s1.8.2] Gay-Lussac's law of combining volumes: gases react in volumes that are in simple whole-number ratios to one another and to the volumes of any gaseous products, at the same temperature and pressure.
[chem8-s1.8.3] Example of combining volumes: 2 volumes of hydrogen react with 1 volume of oxygen.
[chem8-s1.8.4] Avogadro's law explains Gay-Lussac's law: the volume ratio equals the mole ratio in the balanced equation, because equal volumes hold equal numbers of molecules.
[chem8-s1.8.5] Example: N₂(g) + 3H₂(g) → 2NH₃(g) reads as 1 volume of nitrogen + 3 volumes of hydrogen → 2 volumes of ammonia.
[chem8-s1.9.b] Gas volumes change with temperature and pressure, so a volume is only useful if the conditions are stated. Chemists agree a standard set of conditions, STP, and quote the molar volume under them.
[chem8-s1.9.0] Standard temperature and pressure (STP) is 0 °C (273 K) and 101.3 kPa (1 atmosphere, 1.013 × 10⁵ Pa).
[chem8-s1.9.1] Temperature in gas calculations must be in kelvin (the absolute temperature scale, where 0 °C is 273 K).
[chem8-s1.9.2] Molar volume is the volume occupied by one mole of any gas at a stated temperature and pressure.
[chem8-s1.9.3] At STP the molar volume is 22.4 L mol⁻¹ (22 400 cm³).
[chem8-s1.9.4] At room temperature (about 20 °C) and pressure, the molar volume is about 24.0 L mol⁻¹, larger than at STP because the gas is warmer.
[chem8-s1.9.5] IUPAC (the international chemistry body) now uses a standard pressure of 100 kPa, at which the molar volume at 273.15 K is 22.7 L mol⁻¹.
[chem8-s1.9.6] Leaving Certificate calculations use 22.4 L mol⁻¹ at STP unless told otherwise.
[chem8-s1.9.7] Molar volume applies to gases only, not to liquids or solids.
[chem8-s1.10.b] The value 22.4 L mol⁻¹ can be calculated from the ideal gas equation, and it can be found by experiment.
[chem8-s1.10.0] From the ideal gas equation PV = nRT (P pressure, V volume, n moles, R the gas constant, T temperature in kelvin), the volume of n moles is V = nRT ÷ P. There is more on this in Gas laws and the ideal gas equation.
[chem8-s1.10.1] For 1 mol at 273 K and 101 325 Pa, with R = 8.314 J K⁻¹ mol⁻¹: V = 1 × 8.314 × 273 ÷ 101 325.
[chem8-s1.10.2] This gives V = 0.0224 m³ = 22.4 L, the molar volume at STP.
[chem8-s1.10.3] Experimentally, weigh a gas cylinder (for example, of oxygen) before and after releasing a known volume of gas into a gas syringe (a calibrated syringe that collects and measures gas volume).
[chem8-s1.10.4] Find the number of moles of gas released from the mass lost, using n = m ÷ M.
[chem8-s1.10.5] Scale the volume to one mole and correct it to STP to obtain the molar volume.
[chem8-s1.10.6] Diagram: a gas cylinder on a balance, connected by tubing to a gas syringe; label the cylinder, the balance, the tubing and the scale of the gas syringe.
[chem8-s1.11.b] At STP every mole of gas occupies 22.4 L, so converting between volume and moles takes one step. Mass and volume are linked through moles.
[chem8-s1.11.0] Moles from gas volume at STP: n = V ÷ 22.4, with V in L. Example: 5.6 L of CO₂ at STP: n = 5.6 ÷ 22.4 = 0.25 mol.
[chem8-s1.11.1] Volume from moles at STP: V = n × 22.4 L. Example: 0.50 mol of O₂ occupies 0.50 × 22.4 = 11.2 L at STP.
[chem8-s1.11.2] Mass to volume goes through moles, because 22.4 L is the volume of a mole: first n = m ÷ M.
[chem8-s1.11.3] Example: 8.0 g of methane (M = 16 g mol⁻¹): n = 8.0 ÷ 16 = 0.50 mol.
[chem8-s1.11.4] Then V = 0.50 × 22.4 = 11.2 L at STP.
[chem8-s1.12.b] Because volume ratios equal mole ratios, the balanced equation tells you the gas volumes directly, with no need to convert to moles.
[chem8-s1.12.0] Example of reacting gases: CH₄ + 2O₂ → CO₂ + 2H₂O.
[chem8-s1.12.1] The volumes of methane and oxygen are in the ratio 50 cm³ : 100 cm³, which is 1 : 2 as in the equation, so all the reactants are used up.
[chem8-s1.12.2] So 50 cm³ of CO₂ is formed, measured at the same temperature and pressure, because the equation gives 1 mole of CO₂ for every 1 mole of methane.
[chem8-s1.13.b] Aim: to determine the relative molecular mass of a volatile liquid by turning a measured mass of it into vapour. The vapour fills a flask of known volume, so its moles can be found from the gas equation.
[chem8-s1.13.0] A volatile liquid evaporates easily, i.e. it has a low boiling point, so it can be turned completely into vapour at 100 °C. Propanone (CH₃COCH₃, boiling point 56 °C) is used.
[chem8-s1.13.1] If the mass (m), volume (V), temperature (T) and pressure (P) of the vapour are measured, the number of moles is n = PV ÷ RT, where R is the gas constant.
[chem8-s1.13.2] Relative molecular mass is then Mr = m ÷ n, giving the molar mass in g mol⁻¹.
[chem8-s1.13.3] The vapour is assumed to behave as an ideal gas (a gas that obeys the gas equation exactly).
[chem8-s1.14.b] The flask holds the vapour, the boiling water bath heats it, and the balance and barometer supply the measurements needed for the calculation.
[chem8-s1.14.0] A clean, dry conical flask (about 250 cm³), a small square of aluminium foil held by a rubber band, and a pin.
[chem8-s1.14.1] A large beaker of boiling water (the water bath) on a hotplate, with a thermometer in the water.
[chem8-s1.14.2] A top-pan balance reading to 0.01 g, a barometer (to measure atmospheric pressure), a graduated (measuring) cylinder, and a dropper of the volatile liquid (propanone).
[chem8-s1.14.3] Diagram: the conical flask with its foil cap, rubber band and pinhole, clamped in a beaker of boiling water on a hotplate; label the water bath and the thermometer.
[chem8-s1.14.4] Diagram: a top-pan balance with the flask on the pan, and a barometer; label the pan and the display or scale.
[chem8-s1.14.5] Diagram: a graduated (measuring) cylinder, read at eye level with the bottom of the meniscus (the curved surface of the water) on the scale.
[chem8-s1.15.b] The aim of the first steps is to fill the flask with pure propanone vapour at a known temperature.
[chem8-s1.15.0] 1. Weigh the dry conical flask with the foil cap and rubber band.
[chem8-s1.15.1] 2. Add about 2–3 cm³ of propanone, replace the foil cap and pierce one small pinhole in it.
[chem8-s1.15.2] 3. Clamp the flask in the boiling water bath so that as much of it as possible is immersed.
[chem8-s1.15.3] 4. Leave it until all the liquid has vaporised; the vapour pushes the air out of the flask through the pinhole and excess vapour escapes.
[chem8-s1.15.4] The flask is then left full of propanone vapour at the temperature of the boiling water.
[chem8-s1.15.5] 5. Record the temperature of the water bath and the atmospheric pressure from the barometer.
[chem8-s1.16.b] Once the flask has been filled with vapour, you measure the mass of vapour and the volume it occupied.
[chem8-s1.16.0] 6. Remove the flask and cool it, so the vapour condenses.
[chem8-s1.16.1] Dry the outside of the flask thoroughly and reweigh it; the increase in mass is the mass of vapour that filled the flask.
[chem8-s1.16.2] 7. Find the volume of the flask by filling it completely with water and measuring the water with a graduated cylinder.
[chem8-s1.17.b] The numbers below are sample data for illustration. Every quantity must be in SI units before it goes into the equation.
[chem8-s1.17.0] Sample data: mass of vapour 0.51 g; volume of flask 270 cm³ = 2.70 × 10⁻⁴ m³; temperature 100 °C = 373 K; pressure 101 000 Pa.
[chem8-s1.17.1] Units: P in Pa, V in m³ and T in K; 1 cm³ = 1 × 10⁻⁶ m³.
[chem8-s1.17.2] Moles: n = PV ÷ RT, with R = 8.314 J K⁻¹ mol⁻¹.
[chem8-s1.17.3] Numerator: PV = 101 000 × 2.70 × 10⁻⁴ = 27.27.
[chem8-s1.17.4] Denominator: RT = 8.314 × 373 = 3101.
[chem8-s1.17.5] Moles: n = 27.27 ÷ 3101 = 8.79 × 10⁻³ mol.
[chem8-s1.17.6] Relative molecular mass: Mr = m ÷ n = 0.51 ÷ (8.79 × 10⁻³) = 58.0.
[chem8-s1.17.7] The accepted Mr of propanone (CH₃COCH₃) is 58, so the result agrees with it.
[chem8-s1.18.b] Propanone is flammable and its vapour is harmful to breathe, and the apparatus is hot.
[chem8-s1.18.0] Propanone is highly flammable: use no naked flames, and heat the water bath with a hotplate or electric heater.
[chem8-s1.18.1] Propanone vapour is an irritant: work in a well-ventilated room.
[chem8-s1.18.2] Take care with boiling water and hot glassware, because they can cause burns.
[chem8-s1.19.b] The result depends on measuring the mass of vapour, so anything that changes the measured mass, or makes the vapour differ from the ideal, gives an error.
[chem8-s1.19.0] If not all the liquid vaporises, or the flask is removed too early, the liquid left in it makes the mass too high and so Mr too high.
[chem8-s1.19.1] Wait until no liquid is visible in the flask before removing it from the bath, so that all the liquid has become vapour.
[chem8-s1.19.2] Water left on the outside of the flask when it is reweighed makes the mass too high; dry it carefully.
[chem8-s1.19.3] A pinhole that is too large lets air re-enter as the flask cools, or lets vapour escape before weighing, which affects the measured mass.
[chem8-s1.19.4] Part of the flask is above the water, so the vapour there is cooler than 373 K.
[chem8-s1.19.5] The vapour is not a perfect ideal gas, especially close to its boiling point.
[chem8-s1.19.6] The mass of vapour is small compared with the flask, so a balance reading to 0.01 g (or better) is needed.
[chem8-s1.19.7] Repeat the experiment and average the results, because this reduces the effect of random errors in a single run.
