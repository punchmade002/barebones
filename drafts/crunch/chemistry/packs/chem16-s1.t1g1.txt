TARGET chem16-s1: 1. Redox volumetric problems
[chem16-s1.0.b] A redox titration is one where a measured volume of an oxidising agent (a substance that takes electrons) reacts with a reducing agent (a substance that gives electrons). It is solved with the same mole steps as an acid–base titration; only the source of the mole ratio changes.
[chem16-s1.0.0] Recap: write the balanced equation to get the mole ratio, calculate moles of the known solution with n = c × V (V in litres), convert to moles of the other substance using the mole ratio, divide by its volume, then convert units and allow for dilution.
[chem16-s1.0.1] These are the steps used in acid–base volumetric problems. Allowing for dilution means scaling back up when extra water was added, which lowered the concentration.
[chem16-s1.0.2] The difference in redox problems is that the mole ratio comes from the balanced redox equation (the same number of each atom and the same total charge on both sides), which may need to be balanced first. There is more on this in Balancing redox equations.
[chem16-s1.0.3] The ratio can be checked from electrons: the moles of electrons gained by the oxidising agent equal the moles of electrons lost by the reducing agent, because electrons are only transferred, never created or lost.
[chem16-s1.0.4] Example: MnO₄⁻ gains 5 e⁻ and Fe²⁺ loses 1 e⁻, so 1 MnO₄⁻ reacts with 5 Fe²⁺, because five Fe²⁺ are needed to supply the five electrons one MnO₄⁻ takes.
[chem16-s1.0.5] Titration formula for redox: (V_ox × M_ox) ÷ n_ox = (V_red × M_red) ÷ n_red, where V is volume, M is concentration in mol L⁻¹, and n_ox and n_red are the coefficients of the oxidising and reducing agents in the balanced equation.
[chem16-s1.0.6] Volumes can stay in cm³ in this formula because the units cancel, exactly as in acid–base volumetric problems.
[chem16-s1.0.7] Worked example of the formula: 23.8 cm³ of KMnO₄ reacts with 25.0 cm³ of 0.100 M Fe²⁺, with n_ox = 1 and n_red = 5 (sample data).
[chem16-s1.0.8] M_ox = (25.0 × 0.100) ÷ (5 × 23.8) = 0.0210 mol L⁻¹, the concentration of the KMnO₄.
[chem16-s1.1.b] These ratios turn up again and again in redox titrations. Each comes straight from a balanced equation, so learn the equation and the ratio together.
[chem16-s1.1.0] MnO₄⁻ : Fe²⁺ = 1 : 5, from MnO₄⁻ + 5Fe²⁺ + 8H⁺ → Mn²⁺ + 5Fe³⁺ + 4H₂O. Permanganate (MnO₄⁻) is the oxidising agent and iron(II) the reducing agent.
[chem16-s1.1.1] I₂ : S₂O₃²⁻ = 1 : 2, from I₂ + 2S₂O₃²⁻ → 2I⁻ + S₄O₆²⁻. Here thiosulfate (S₂O₃²⁻) is the reducing agent that uses up iodine.
[chem16-s1.1.2] MnO₄⁻ : I₂ = 2 : 5, from 2MnO₄⁻ + 10I⁻ + 16H⁺ → 2Mn²⁺ + 5I₂ + 8H₂O, in which permanganate oxidises iodide (I⁻) to iodine.
[chem16-s1.1.3] MnO₄⁻ : S₂O₃²⁻ = 1 : 5 when iodine made from permanganate is titrated with thiosulfate. The combined ratio is 2 : 5 : 10 for MnO₄⁻ : I₂ : S₂O₃²⁻, because 2 MnO₄⁻ make 5 I₂, which use up 10 S₂O₃²⁻.
[chem16-s1.1.4] Ammonium iron(II) sulfate supplies one Fe²⁺ per formula unit, so moles of (NH₄)₂SO₄·FeSO₄·6H₂O = moles of Fe²⁺.
[chem16-s1.1.5] Each Na₂S₂O₃·5H₂O supplies one S₂O₃²⁻, so moles of the salt equal moles of thiosulfate ions.
[chem16-s1.1.6] Each I₂ is one mole whether it is written as I₂ or I₃⁻ (the form iodine takes when dissolved in iodide solution).
[chem16-s1.2.b] Some substances can't be titrated directly. Instead they release a product that can be, so the calculation has to run back through two equations.
[chem16-s1.2.0] In an indirect titration, the substance being analysed (the analyte) first reacts with an excess of a reagent (more than enough, so the analyte is completely used up) to release a product in proportion to it.
[chem16-s1.2.1] The product released is then titrated with a solution of known concentration (the titrant). For example, an oxidising agent added to excess iodide releases iodine, which is titrated with thiosulfate.
[chem16-s1.2.2] Work backwards through each equation in turn, starting from the titrant, because the titrant is the only amount you measured directly.
[chem16-s1.2.3] Step 1: divide the moles of thiosulfate by 2 to give the moles of iodine, because I₂ : S₂O₃²⁻ = 1 : 2.
[chem16-s1.2.4] Step 2: use the first equation to convert the moles of iodine to moles of the oxidising agent.
[chem16-s1.2.5] The excess reagent, e.g. KI, doesn't need to be measured accurately, because it is not part of the calculation.
[chem16-s1.2.6] Only the analyte and the titrant must be measured exactly, since the moles you calculate depend only on them.
[chem16-s1.3.b] Titration data rarely give the answer in the form the question wants. You scale up from the small portion titrated, then convert moles into masses and percentages.
[chem16-s1.3.0] Scale from the portion titrated to the whole solution, e.g. × 10 for 25.0 cm³ out of 250 cm³, because only a portion was run against the titrant.
[chem16-s1.3.1] Then divide by the number of tablets if several were dissolved, because the flask holds all of them together.
[chem16-s1.3.2] Mass = moles × molar mass (M, the mass of one mole in g mol⁻¹), and mg = g × 1000.
[chem16-s1.3.3] Percentage by mass of a tablet = mass of the substance ÷ mass of the tablet × 100.
[chem16-s1.3.4] Percentage of the label value = measured mass ÷ stated mass × 100, which shows how close a tablet is to what its label claims.
[chem16-s1.3.5] Concentration in g L⁻¹ = mol L⁻¹ × M.
[chem16-s1.3.6] %w/v (grams of solute per 100 cm³ of solution) = g L⁻¹ ÷ 10. There is more on this in Units of concentration.
[chem16-s1.3.7] A concentration in g L⁻¹ of a hydrated salt (one with water locked into its crystals) uses the molar mass of the hydrate, e.g. Na₂S₂O₃·5H₂O for thiosulfate solution.
[chem16-s1.4.b] Use whole-number relative atomic masses (Ar, the mass of an atom relative to carbon-12), as in the SEC Formulae and Tables. Use these values.
[chem16-s1.4.0] Ar: H 1, C 12, N 14, O 16, Na 23, S 32, Cl 35.5, K 39, Mn 55, Fe 56, I 127. Use Ar(Fe) = 56 for iron.
[chem16-s1.4.1] M(FeSO₄) = 152 for iron(II) sulfate, and M(KMnO₄) = 158.
[chem16-s1.4.2] M(Na₂S₂O₃·5H₂O) = 248, which includes the five waters of crystallisation.
[chem16-s1.4.3] M((NH₄)₂SO₄·FeSO₄·6H₂O) = 392 for ammonium iron(II) sulfate, and M(I₂) = 254.
[chem16-s1.5.b] Careful technique and a sense check separate a correct answer from one that is slightly off. Redox titrations also have particular errors that push the result in a known direction.
[chem16-s1.5.0] Use the mean of concordant titres (titres that agree closely), because averaging results that agree reduces the effect of random error.
[chem16-s1.5.1] Give answers to three significant figures, with units.
[chem16-s1.5.2] Check the answer is sensible: a tablet usually contains tens of milligrams of iron, not grams.
[chem16-s1.5.3] A percentage cannot exceed 100%, so a result above that signals a mistake.
[chem16-s1.5.4] Taking deep purple as the end point with KMnO₄ (the point where the reaction is complete) makes the titre too high, because extra permanganate has been added. This is a systematic error (one that shifts every result the same way).
[chem16-s1.5.5] Oxidation of Fe²⁺ by air before titration makes the iron result too low, because some Fe²⁺ has already been used up and less permanganate is needed. There is more on random and systematic error in Measurement and error.
[chem16-s1.6.b] At Higher Level a redox titration may use a substance you have not met in class. The chemistry looks new, but the question supplies what you need and the same method applies.
[chem16-s1.6.0] An unfamiliar redox problem may involve a reagent or product not studied before, e.g. hydrogen peroxide, hypochlorite in bleach, vitamin C or sodium oxalate (sodium ethanedioate, Na₂C₂O₄).
[chem16-s1.6.1] The question gives the balanced equation or asks for it, and the same method applies.
[chem16-s1.6.2] 1. Identify the oxidising and reducing agents (which substance takes electrons and which gives them).
[chem16-s1.6.3] 2. Write or balance the equation, using oxidation numbers (the charge-like numbers that track electron transfer) if needed.
[chem16-s1.6.4] 3. List the data with units.
[chem16-s1.6.5] 4. Plan the route through moles, including any two-step reaction and dilution, before calculating.
[chem16-s1.7.b] Four reactions show how the ratio changes with the substance. In each case read the ratio from the coefficients of the balanced equation.
[chem16-s1.7.0] MnO₄⁻ : H₂O₂ = 2 : 5, from 2MnO₄⁻ + 5H₂O₂ + 6H⁺ → 2Mn²⁺ + 5O₂ + 8H₂O, where acidified permanganate oxidises hydrogen peroxide to oxygen.
[chem16-s1.7.1] MnO₄⁻ : C₂O₄²⁻ = 2 : 5, from 2MnO₄⁻ + 5C₂O₄²⁻ + 16H⁺ → 2Mn²⁺ + 10CO₂ + 8H₂O.
[chem16-s1.7.2] ClO⁻ : I₂ = 1 : 1, from ClO⁻ + 2I⁻ + 2H⁺ → Cl⁻ + I₂ + H₂O, because hypochlorite oxidises iodide to iodine in acid solution.
[chem16-s1.7.3] Vitamin C : I₂ = 1 : 1, from C₆H₈O₆ + I₂ → C₆H₆O₆ + 2H⁺ + 2I⁻.
[chem16-s1.8.b] Vitamin C and sodium oxalate are two substances that exams may use in redox titrations. You need their formulae and one practical detail for each.
[chem16-s1.8.0] Vitamin C is ascorbic acid, C₆H₈O₆, with M = 176 g mol⁻¹; iodine oxidises it to dehydroascorbic acid, C₆H₆O₆.
[chem16-s1.8.1] Sodium oxalate is an alternative primary standard for KMnO₄, meaning a pure, stable solid that can be weighed accurately to make a solution of known concentration for standardising it.
[chem16-s1.8.2] The oxalate titration is done warm, at about 60 °C, because the reaction is slow at room temperature.
[chem16-s1.9.b] Standardising means finding the exact concentration of a solution by titrating it against one of known concentration. Here ammonium iron(II) sulfate, which can be weighed accurately, is used to standardise KMnO₄. The mean titre is the average volume run in from the burette, and all data in these worked problems are sample data.
[chem16-s1.9.0] Data: 9.80 g of ammonium iron(II) sulfate, (NH₄)₂SO₄·FeSO₄·6H₂O (M = 392), was dissolved in dilute sulfuric acid and made up to 250 cm³; 25.0 cm³ portions needed a mean titre of 24.0 cm³ of KMnO₄.
[chem16-s1.9.1] Equation: MnO₄⁻ + 5Fe²⁺ + 8H⁺ → Mn²⁺ + 5Fe³⁺ + 4H₂O, a 1 : 5 ratio. The dilute sulfuric acid supplies the H⁺ in this equation.
[chem16-s1.9.2] Moles of salt = 9.80 g ÷ 392 g mol⁻¹ = 0.0250 mol, in 250 cm³.
[chem16-s1.9.3] Concentration of Fe²⁺ = 0.0250 mol ÷ 0.250 L = 0.100 mol L⁻¹, because each formula unit gives one Fe²⁺.
[chem16-s1.9.4] Moles Fe²⁺ in 25.0 cm³ = 0.100 mol L⁻¹ × 0.0250 L = 2.50 × 10⁻³ mol.
[chem16-s1.9.5] Moles MnO₄⁻ = 2.50 × 10⁻³ mol ÷ 5 = 5.00 × 10⁻⁴ mol, using the 1 : 5 ratio.
[chem16-s1.9.6] Concentration of KMnO₄ = 5.00 × 10⁻⁴ mol ÷ 0.0240 L = 0.0208 mol L⁻¹.
[chem16-s1.9.7] In g L⁻¹: 0.0208 mol L⁻¹ × 158 g mol⁻¹ = 3.29 g L⁻¹.
[chem16-s1.10.b] Here the titration checks a tablet's label. You work from the titre back to the mass of iron(II) sulfate in one tablet and compare it with the stated value.
[chem16-s1.10.0] Data: five tablets, each labelled “200 mg iron(II) sulfate”, were dissolved in dilute sulfuric acid and made up to 250 cm³; 25.0 cm³ portions needed a mean titre of 12.9 cm³ of 0.0100 M KMnO₄.
[chem16-s1.10.1] Moles MnO₄⁻ = 0.0100 mol L⁻¹ × 0.0129 L = 1.29 × 10⁻⁴ mol.
[chem16-s1.10.2] Moles Fe²⁺ in 25.0 cm³ = 5 × 1.29 × 10⁻⁴ mol = 6.45 × 10⁻⁴ mol, using MnO₄⁻ : Fe²⁺ = 1 : 5.
[chem16-s1.10.3] Moles Fe²⁺ in 250 cm³ = 6.45 × 10⁻⁴ mol × 10 = 6.45 × 10⁻³ mol, because 25.0 cm³ is one tenth of the flask.
[chem16-s1.10.4] Moles Fe²⁺ per tablet = 6.45 × 10⁻³ mol ÷ 5 = 1.29 × 10⁻³ mol, because five tablets were dissolved.
[chem16-s1.10.5] Mass of FeSO₄ per tablet = 1.29 × 10⁻³ mol × 152 g mol⁻¹ = 0.196 g = 196 mg.
[chem16-s1.10.6] As a percentage of the label value: 196 mg ÷ 200 mg × 100 = 98.0%.
[chem16-s1.10.7] Mass of elemental iron per tablet = 1.29 × 10⁻³ mol × 56 g mol⁻¹ = 0.0722 g = 72.2 mg.
[chem16-s1.11.b] Thiosulfate is standardised in two steps. Permanganate first releases iodine from excess iodide, and the iodine is then titrated with the thiosulfate.
[chem16-s1.11.0] Data: 25.0 cm³ of 0.0200 M KMnO₄ was acidified and excess KI added; the iodine liberated (released) needed a mean titre of 25.0 cm³ of sodium thiosulfate solution, using starch (an indicator for iodine) near the end point.
[chem16-s1.11.1] Equation: 2MnO₄⁻ + 10I⁻ + 16H⁺ → 2Mn²⁺ + 5I₂ + 8H₂O.
[chem16-s1.11.2] Equation: I₂ + 2S₂O₃²⁻ → 2I⁻ + S₄O₆²⁻.
[chem16-s1.11.3] Moles MnO₄⁻ = 0.0200 mol L⁻¹ × 0.0250 L = 5.00 × 10⁻⁴ mol.
[chem16-s1.11.4] Moles I₂ = 5.00 × 10⁻⁴ mol × 5 ÷ 2 = 1.25 × 10⁻³ mol, using MnO₄⁻ : I₂ = 2 : 5.
[chem16-s1.11.5] Moles S₂O₃²⁻ = 2 × 1.25 × 10⁻³ mol = 2.50 × 10⁻³ mol, the same as 5 × the moles of MnO₄⁻.
[chem16-s1.11.6] Concentration of S₂O₃²⁻ = 2.50 × 10⁻³ mol ÷ 0.0250 L = 0.100 mol L⁻¹.
[chem16-s1.11.7] In g L⁻¹ of Na₂S₂O₃·5H₂O: 0.100 mol L⁻¹ × 248 g mol⁻¹ = 24.8 g L⁻¹.
[chem16-s1.12.b] Bleach contains hypochlorite (ClO⁻), which oxidises iodide to iodine. The iodine is then titrated with thiosulfate, and because the bleach was diluted first, the answer must be scaled back up.
[chem16-s1.12.0] Data: 25.0 cm³ of bleach was diluted to 250 cm³; 25.0 cm³ of the diluted bleach was added to excess KI and dilute sulfuric acid, and the iodine liberated needed a mean titre of 18.4 cm³ of 0.100 M sodium thiosulfate.
[chem16-s1.12.1] Equations: ClO⁻ + 2I⁻ + 2H⁺ → Cl⁻ + I₂ + H₂O, so ClO⁻ : I₂ = 1 : 1; and I₂ + 2S₂O₃²⁻ → 2I⁻ + S₄O₆²⁻.
[chem16-s1.12.2] Moles S₂O₃²⁻ = 0.100 mol L⁻¹ × 0.0184 L = 1.84 × 10⁻³ mol.
[chem16-s1.12.3] Moles I₂ = 1.84 × 10⁻³ mol ÷ 2 = 9.20 × 10⁻⁴ mol, using I₂ : S₂O₃²⁻ = 1 : 2; this equals the moles of ClO⁻ in 25.0 cm³ of diluted bleach.
[chem16-s1.12.4] Diluted concentration = 9.20 × 10⁻⁴ mol ÷ 0.0250 L = 0.0368 mol L⁻¹.
[chem16-s1.12.5] Original bleach = 0.0368 mol L⁻¹ × 10 = 0.368 mol L⁻¹, because the dilution factor is 250 ÷ 25.0 = 10.
[chem16-s1.12.6] As sodium hypochlorite (NaOCl, M = 74.5): 0.368 mol L⁻¹ × 74.5 g mol⁻¹ = 27.4 g L⁻¹.
[chem16-s1.12.7] As %w/v: 27.4 g L⁻¹ ÷ 10 = 2.74% w/v.
[chem16-s1.13.b] Here hydrogen peroxide acts as the reducing agent, and the dilution factor is 25 rather than 10.
[chem16-s1.13.0] Data: 10.0 cm³ of a hydrogen peroxide solution was diluted to 250 cm³; 25.0 cm³ portions, acidified with dilute sulfuric acid, needed a mean titre of 20.0 cm³ of 0.0200 M KMnO₄.
[chem16-s1.13.1] Equation: 2MnO₄⁻ + 5H₂O₂ + 6H⁺ → 2Mn²⁺ + 5O₂ + 8H₂O, so MnO₄⁻ : H₂O₂ = 2 : 5.
[chem16-s1.13.2] Here H₂O₂ is the reducing agent, with oxygen changing from −1 to 0 as it is oxidised to O₂. The permanganate is still the oxidising agent.
[chem16-s1.13.3] Moles MnO₄⁻ = 0.0200 mol L⁻¹ × 0.0200 L = 4.00 × 10⁻⁴ mol.
[chem16-s1.13.4] Moles H₂O₂ = 4.00 × 10⁻⁴ mol × 5 ÷ 2 = 1.00 × 10⁻³ mol in 25.0 cm³.

CANDIDATES chem15-s1: 1. Redox titrations and iron tablets
[chem15-s1.0.b] A titration (adding one solution from a burette to a measured volume of another until they have exactly reacted) can be used for redox reactions as well as acid–base ones. Here the reaction involves transfer of electrons between an oxidising agent and a reducing agent.
[chem15-s1.0.0] A redox titration finds the concentration of an oxidising or reducing agent by titrating it against a reducing or oxidising agent of known concentration.
[chem15-s1.0.1] The mole ratio used in the calculation comes from the balanced redox equation, so you must be able to write or recall the equation for each reaction.
[chem15-s1.0.2] The apparatus and technique are the same as in acid–base titrations: pipette, burette, conical flask and white tile, with rough and accurate titrations agreeing within 0.1 cm³.
[chem15-s1.0.3] The end point (the moment the reaction is exactly complete) is shown either by the colour of one of the reagents themselves (self-indicating, as with potassium manganate(VII)) or by an indicator (starch in iodine–thiosulfate titrations).
[chem15-s1.0.4] The two redox titrations in the specification are potassium manganate(VII) with iron(II), and iodine with sodium thiosulfate.
[chem15-s1.1.b] Potassium manganate(VII) is the oxidising agent in the first redox titration. Its strong colour is useful, but its instability means it can never be made up by weighing alone.
[chem15-s1.1.0] Potassium manganate(VII), KMnO₄ (potassium permanganate), is a dark purple solid; its solution is an intense purple and it is a strong oxidising agent (it readily takes electrons from other substances).
[chem15-s1.1.1] It is usually used at about 0.01–0.02 mol L⁻¹.
[chem15-s1.1.2] It is not a primary standard (a substance pure and stable enough to make a solution of exactly known concentration by weighing): it can't be obtained pure.
[chem15-s1.1.3] Its solution slowly decomposes in light and in the presence of traces of organic matter, depositing brown MnO₂, so its concentration changes.
[chem15-s1.1.4] It is therefore standardised (its concentration found by titration) against ammonium iron(II) sulfate shortly before use.
[chem15-s1.2.b] In acidic solution, permanganate takes electrons from iron(II). Knowing the half-equations gives the 1 : 5 mole ratio on which every calculation in this subsection rests.
[chem15-s1.2.0] In acidic solution MnO₄⁻ is reduced (gains electrons) to the almost colourless manganese(II) ion: MnO₄⁻(aq) + 8H⁺(aq) + 5e⁻ → Mn²⁺(aq) + 4H₂O(l), with manganese changing from +7 to +2 and the solution going from purple to colourless.
[chem15-s1.2.1] Iron(II) is oxidised (loses an electron): Fe²⁺(aq) → Fe³⁺(aq) + e⁻.
[chem15-s1.2.2] Overall: MnO₄⁻(aq) + 5Fe²⁺(aq) + 8H⁺(aq) → Mn²⁺(aq) + 5Fe³⁺(aq) + 4H₂O(l), so MnO₄⁻ : Fe²⁺ = 1 : 5, because each MnO₄⁻ takes five electrons and each Fe²⁺ gives one.
[chem15-s1.2.3] The Mn²⁺ ions produced catalyse the reaction (autocatalysis: a product of the reaction is its catalyst), so the first few drops are decolourised slowly and later drops almost at once.
[chem15-s1.3.b] Permanganate acts as its own indicator, which makes the titration simple, but its deep colour changes how you read the burette.
[chem15-s1.3.0] Potassium manganate(VII) is self-indicating (its own colour shows the end point), so no indicator is added.
[chem15-s1.3.1] The KMnO₄ goes in the burette; each drop is decolourised while Fe²⁺ remains in the flask.
[chem15-s1.3.2] The end point is the first permanent pale pink colour, produced by the first drop of excess MnO₄⁻ once all the Fe²⁺ has been used up.
[chem15-s1.3.3] The solution is so deeply coloured that the bottom of the meniscus (the curved surface of the liquid) can't be seen, so read the top of the meniscus, consistently for both readings.
[chem15-s1.3.4] Rinse the burette thoroughly after use, because permanganate leaves brown stains of MnO₂ on glass.
[chem15-s1.4.b] The reduction of MnO₄⁻ to Mn²⁺ uses up H⁺ ions, so the flask must contain excess acid. The choice of acid matters, because the wrong one reacts with the permanganate or the iron(II).
[chem15-s1.4.0] Excess dilute sulfuric acid is added to the flask to supply the H⁺ ions needed for the reduction.
[chem15-s1.4.1] With too little acid, MnO₄⁻ is reduced only to manganese(IV) oxide, forming a brown precipitate: MnO₄⁻ + 2H₂O + 3e⁻ → MnO₂(s) + 4OH⁻, with manganese changing from +7 to +4.
[chem15-s1.4.2] With too little acid the ratio is no longer 1 : 5 and the end point can't be seen.
[chem15-s1.4.3] Hydrochloric acid is not used, because MnO₄⁻ oxidises Cl⁻ to chlorine, using up extra permanganate.
[chem15-s1.4.4] Nitric acid is not used, because it is itself an oxidising agent and would oxidise some of the Fe²⁺.
[chem15-s1.5.b] To find the concentration of permanganate you need an iron(II) solution of exactly known concentration. Ammonium iron(II) sulfate is the salt used to make it.
[chem15-s1.5.0] Ammonium iron(II) sulfate hexahydrate, (NH₄)₂SO₄·FeSO₄·6H₂O, also written (NH₄)₂Fe(SO₄)₂·6H₂O, has M = 392 g mol⁻¹ and forms pale green crystals.
[chem15-s1.5.1] It is a double salt: ammonium sulfate and iron(II) sulfate crystallise together in a fixed ratio in one crystal.
[chem15-s1.5.2] Its standard solution (one of accurately known concentration) is made up by dissolving a weighed mass in a volumetric flask, with the crystals dissolved in deionised water and some dilute sulfuric acid added.
[chem15-s1.5.3] The acid stops Fe²⁺ being oxidised to Fe³⁺ by dissolved oxygen, which would make the solution weaker than calculated.
[chem15-s1.6.b] A primary standard must give a known amount of substance from a weighed mass. Ammonium iron(II) sulfate does, whereas plain iron(II) sulfate does not.
[chem15-s1.6.0] It can be obtained very pure, so the weighed mass is all the compound.
[chem15-s1.6.1] It is stable in air: the solid is not oxidised by air, does not absorb water and does not lose its water of crystallisation (water locked into the crystal), so its formula stays fixed.
[chem15-s1.6.2] It is soluble and has a high molar mass, so weighing errors are a small percentage of the mass weighed.
[chem15-s1.6.3] Iron(II) sulfate itself is not used: its crystals are oxidised by air to iron(III) and their water content varies, so a weighed mass does not correspond to a known amount of Fe²⁺.
[chem15-s1.7.b] Standardising means titrating permanganate against the standard iron(II) solution to find its true concentration. Do this shortly before the permanganate is used.
[chem15-s1.7.0] 1. Pipette 25.0 cm³ of the standard ammonium iron(II) sulfate solution into a conical flask.
[chem15-s1.7.1] 2. Add about 20 cm³ of dilute sulfuric acid (an excess) with a graduated cylinder; its volume does not need to be exact, because the acid is in excess and is not being measured.
[chem15-s1.7.2] 3. Titrate with the KMnO₄ solution from the burette, swirling, until one drop gives a permanent pale pink colour.
[chem15-s1.7.3] 4. Repeat until two titres (the volumes of solution added from the burette) agree within 0.1 cm³.
[chem15-s1.7.4] Diagram: pipette with its filler, labelled with the graduation mark at 25.0 cm³.
[chem15-s1.7.5] Diagram: graduated cylinder marked in cm³, labelled with the scale used to measure the sulfuric acid.
[chem15-s1.7.6] Diagram: burette of purple KMnO₄ above a conical flask on a white tile, labelled with the burette tap and the top of the meniscus.
[chem15-s1.8.b] The titre gives the moles of permanganate. Dividing the moles of Fe²⁺ by 5 gives the moles of MnO₄⁻, from the 1 : 5 ratio, and then the concentration.
[chem15-s1.8.0] Data: 25.0 cm³ of 0.0500 mol L⁻¹ ammonium iron(II) sulfate needs a mean titre of 24.4 cm³ of KMnO₄.
[chem15-s1.8.1] Moles Fe²⁺ = 0.0500 mol L⁻¹ × 0.0250 L = 1.25 × 10⁻³ mol.
[chem15-s1.8.2] Moles MnO₄⁻ = 1.25 × 10⁻³ mol ÷ 5 = 2.50 × 10⁻⁴ mol, since MnO₄⁻ : Fe²⁺ = 1 : 5.
[chem15-s1.8.3] Concentration of KMnO₄ = 2.50 × 10⁻⁴ mol ÷ 0.0244 L = 0.0102 mol L⁻¹.
[chem15-s1.9.b] The second redox titration uses sodium thiosulfate as the reducing agent and iodine as the oxidising agent. Neither is a primary standard, so each needs care.
[chem15-s1.9.0] Sodium thiosulfate, Na₂S₂O₃·5H₂O (M = 248 g mol⁻¹), is a colourless crystalline solid and a reducing agent.
[chem15-s1.9.1] The thiosulfate ion, S₂O₃²⁻, is like a sulfate ion with one oxygen replaced by sulfur; “thio” means sulfur.
[chem15-s1.9.2] Sodium thiosulfate is not a primary standard: its crystals are efflorescent, losing water of crystallisation to the air, so their exact formula and the mass of thiosulfate are uncertain.
[chem15-s1.9.3] Its solution is therefore standardised against iodine made from a standard oxidising agent.
[chem15-s1.9.4] Iodine is not a primary standard either: it sublimes slowly at room temperature (turns from solid to vapour), so it cannot be weighed accurately, and it is almost insoluble in water.
[chem15-s1.10.b] Iodine barely dissolves in water, so it is dissolved with potassium iodide. The resulting red-brown solution is then titrated with thiosulfate.
[chem15-s1.10.0] Iodine is brought into solution with potassium iodide: I₂ + I⁻ ⇌ I₃⁻.
[chem15-s1.10.1] The tri-iodide ion is charged, so it dissolves in water; the solution behaves as a solution of iodine and is red-brown.
[chem15-s1.10.2] Titration reaction: I₂(aq) + 2S₂O₃²⁻(aq) → 2I⁻(aq) + S₄O₆²⁻(aq), so I₂ : S₂O₃²⁻ = 1 : 2.
[chem15-s1.10.3] Iodine is the oxidising agent; thiosulfate is the reducing agent and is oxidised to the tetrathionate ion, S₄O₆²⁻.
[chem15-s1.10.4] The iodine solution is pipetted into the conical flask and the thiosulfate is in the burette.
[chem15-s1.11.b] Unlike permanganate, this titration needs an indicator for a sharp end point. Starch is used, but only at the right moment.
[chem15-s1.11.0] As thiosulfate is added, the red-brown iodine fades to yellow and then to pale yellow (straw-coloured).
[chem15-s1.11.1] At pale yellow, add a few drops of freshly made starch solution, which turns blue-black because of the starch–iodine complex.
[chem15-s1.11.2] Add thiosulfate drop by drop until the blue-black colour just disappears, leaving a colourless solution: the end point.
[chem15-s1.11.3] Starch is added only near the end point, because with a lot of iodine present, iodine binds strongly to the starch and is released only slowly, so the colour change would be gradual and the end point inaccurate.
[chem15-s1.11.4] The pale yellow colour also shows that the end point is close.
[chem15-s1.12.b] Water quality affects this titration, and thiosulfate's reducing power has uses outside the laboratory.
[chem15-s1.12.0] Use deionised water throughout, not tap water: tap water contains dissolved chlorine from water treatment, which oxidises iodide to extra iodine (Cl₂ + 2I⁻ → 2Cl⁻ + I₂), so the titre would be too large.
[chem15-s1.12.1] Sodium thiosulfate is used to remove excess chlorine from water, reducing it to harmless chloride ions.
[chem15-s1.12.2] Sodium thiosulfate is the “fixer” (“hypo”) in film photography.
[chem15-s1.13.b] Thiosulfate is standardised against iodine that is made fresh from a standardised permanganate solution. Permanganate oxidises iodide to iodine, and the iodine is then titrated.
[chem15-s1.13.0] 1. Pipette 25.0 cm³ of standardised KMnO₄ solution into a conical flask and add about 20 cm³ of dilute sulfuric acid.
[chem15-s1.13.1] 2. Add an excess of potassium iodide, as solution or solid: the purple colour disappears and red-brown iodine forms: 2MnO₄⁻ + 10I⁻ + 16H⁺ → 2Mn²⁺ + 5I₂ + 8H₂O.
[chem15-s1.13.2] Potassium iodide is in excess for two reasons: so that all the MnO₄⁻ reacts, producing a known amount of iodine; and so that the iodine stays in solution as I₃⁻.
[chem15-s1.13.3] 3. Titrate the liberated (released) iodine with the thiosulfate solution by the starch method: starch added at pale yellow, end point blue-black to colourless.
[chem15-s1.13.4] Combined ratio: 2MnO₄⁻ → 5I₂ → 10S₂O₃²⁻, so MnO₄⁻ : S₂O₃²⁻ = 1 : 5.
[chem15-s1.14.b] Work through the chain of ratios one step at a time: permanganate to iodine, then iodine to thiosulfate.
[chem15-s1.14.0] Data: 25.0 cm³ of 0.0200 mol L⁻¹ KMnO₄ liberates iodine that needs a mean titre of 25.3 cm³ of thiosulfate.
[chem15-s1.14.1] Moles MnO₄⁻ = 0.0200 mol L⁻¹ × 0.0250 L = 5.00 × 10⁻⁴ mol.
[chem15-s1.14.2] Moles I₂ = 5.00 × 10⁻⁴ mol × 5 ÷ 2 = 1.25 × 10⁻³ mol, since 2MnO₄⁻ gives 5I₂.
[chem15-s1.14.3] Moles S₂O₃²⁻ = 2 × 1.25 × 10⁻³ mol = 2.50 × 10⁻³ mol, since I₂ : S₂O₃²⁻ = 1 : 2.
[chem15-s1.14.4] Concentration of thiosulfate = 2.50 × 10⁻³ mol ÷ 0.0253 L = 0.0988 mol L⁻¹.
[chem15-s1.15.b] Three words describe how solids behave with water from the air. They explain why some substances can't be primary standards.
[chem15-s1.15.0] Efflorescent: a hydrated crystal that loses some of its water of crystallisation to the air on standing, e.g. sodium thiosulfate pentahydrate and washing soda (Na₂CO₃·10H₂O).
[chem15-s1.15.1] Hygroscopic: absorbs water vapour from the air without dissolving, e.g. concentrated sulfuric acid and silica gel.
[chem15-s1.15.2] Deliquescent: absorbs so much water from the air that it dissolves in it and forms a solution, e.g. sodium hydroxide pellets.
[chem15-s1.15.3] None of these substances can be a primary standard, because the mass weighed does not correspond to a known amount of substance.
[chem15-s1.16.b] The reagents in these titrations are oxidising, staining or irritant, so protect your eyes, skin and clothes.
[chem15-s1.16.0] Wear goggles: potassium manganate(VII) is an oxidising agent, is harmful, and stains skin and clothes.
[chem15-s1.16.1] Dilute sulfuric acid is an irritant; iodine solution stains and is harmful.
[chem15-s1.16.2] Use a pipette filler, so that no solution is drawn into the mouth.
[chem15-s1.17.b] Iron tablets can be analysed by a redox titration. The iron(II) in a dissolved tablet is titrated with standardised permanganate, which gives the mass of iron per tablet.
[chem15-s1.17.0] Aim: to find the mass of iron in an iron tablet, and the percentage of elemental iron by mass in the tablet, by titration with a standard solution of potassium manganate(VII).
[chem15-s1.17.1] Iron tablets contain iron as iron(II), commonly as iron(II) sulfate (iron(II) fumarate and iron(II) gluconate are also used), and are prescribed to treat iron-deficiency anaemia.
[chem15-s1.17.2] The label usually gives the mass of the iron compound or of iron per tablet, which is the value your result is compared with.
[chem15-s1.17.3] Equation: MnO₄⁻(aq) + 5Fe²⁺(aq) + 8H⁺(aq) → Mn²⁺(aq) + 5Fe³⁺(aq) + 4H₂O(l), so MnO₄⁻ : Fe²⁺ = 1 : 5.
[chem15-s1.17.4] The KMnO₄ solution is first standardised against ammonium iron(II) sulfate, just before use, because its concentration changes on standing.
[chem15-s1.17.5] Hypothesis (example): the tablet contains the mass of iron stated on the label, within a few percent.
[chem15-s1.18.b] The tablets must be turned into a solution of known total volume. Only a measured fraction of it is then titrated.
[chem15-s1.18.0] 1. Weigh five tablets together accurately and record the mass; dividing by five gives the mean mass of one tablet.
[chem15-s1.18.1] 2. Grind the tablets to a fine powder in a mortar and pestle, adding a little dilute sulfuric acid.
[chem15-s1.18.2] 3. Transfer the paste to a beaker with dilute sulfuric acid and deionised water, rinsing the mortar and pestle into the beaker, and stir to dissolve.
[chem15-s1.18.3] 4. Transfer the solution, including any insoluble material, through a funnel into a 250 cm³ volumetric flask, rinsing the beaker, glass rod and funnel into the flask with deionised water.
[chem15-s1.18.4] 5. Make up to the mark with deionised water, with the bottom of the meniscus on the mark at eye level and the last drops added with a dropper; stopper and invert about 20 times to mix.
[chem15-s1.18.5] Diagram: mortar and pestle holding the tablet powder, labelled with the pestle and the mortar bowl.
[chem15-s1.18.6] Diagram: 250 cm³ volumetric flask with a funnel in its neck, labelled with the graduation mark.
[chem15-s1.18.7] Diagram: beaker with a glass rod stirring the solution, labelled with the glass rod.
[chem15-s1.19.b] A 25.0 cm³ portion of the tablet solution is titrated with standardised permanganate. This is the same method as the standardisation, with the tablet solution in the flask.
[chem15-s1.19.0] 1. Rinse the pipette with deionised water and then with the tablet solution; pipette 25.0 cm³ into a conical flask.
[chem15-s1.19.1] 2. Add about 20 cm³ of dilute sulfuric acid (an excess) to the flask.
[chem15-s1.19.2] 3. Rinse the burette with deionised water and then with the standard KMnO₄ solution; fill it and read the top of the meniscus.
[chem15-s1.19.3] 4. Titrate, swirling, until one drop gives a permanent pale pink colour; no indicator is added.
[chem15-s1.19.4] 5. Do a rough titration, then accurate titrations until two agree within 0.1 cm³; titrate soon after making the solution.
[chem15-s1.19.5] Diagram: burette of purple KMnO₄ above a conical flask of tablet solution on a white tile, labelled with the burette tap and the top of the meniscus.
[chem15-s1.20.b] Each step of the tablet method has a reason. Exams ask for these reasons directly.
[chem15-s1.20.0] Dilute sulfuric acid is used to dissolve the tablets, stopping Fe²⁺ being oxidised to Fe³⁺ by dissolved oxygen; this would make the result too low, because Fe³⁺ does not react with MnO₄⁻.
[chem15-s1.20.1] More dilute sulfuric acid is added before each titration to supply the H⁺ ions needed for MnO₄⁻ to be reduced to Mn²⁺; with too little acid, brown MnO₂ forms and the 1 : 5 ratio no longer holds.
[chem15-s1.20.2] Several tablets are used: the result is an average over tablets that vary slightly in mass and iron content, a larger mass gives a smaller percentage error in weighing, and the solution is concentrated enough to give a reasonable titre.
[chem15-s1.20.3] Grinding increases the surface area so that the tablets dissolve fully and all the iron(II) is extracted.
[chem15-s1.20.4] The solution may be cloudy from the insoluble coating and binder (filler); this does not affect the result, because those substances do not react with the permanganate.
[chem15-s1.20.5] A volumetric flask and pipette are used because the 25.0 cm³ portion must be exactly one-tenth of the total.
[chem15-s1.20.6] No indicator is needed because KMnO₄ is self-indicating: the first excess drop colours the solution pink.
[chem15-s1.21.b] The steps convert the titre into the mass of iron in one tablet, then into a percentage of the tablet's mass.
[chem15-s1.21.0] Data: five tablets have a total mass of 1.80 g, a mean of 0.360 g per tablet; 25.0 cm³ portions of the 250 cm³ tablet solution need a mean titre of 11.6 cm³ of 0.0100 mol L⁻¹ KMnO₄.
[chem15-s1.21.1] Moles MnO₄⁻ = 0.0100 mol L⁻¹ × 0.0116 L = 1.16 × 10⁻⁴ mol.
[chem15-s1.21.2] Moles Fe²⁺ in 25.0 cm³ = 5 × 1.16 × 10⁻⁴ mol = 5.80 × 10⁻⁴ mol, using the 1 : 5 ratio.
[chem15-s1.21.3] Moles Fe²⁺ in 250 cm³ (five tablets) = 5.80 × 10⁻⁴ mol × 10 = 5.80 × 10⁻³ mol, since 25.0 cm³ is one-tenth of 250 cm³; per tablet = 5.80 × 10⁻³ mol ÷ 5 = 1.16 × 10⁻³ mol.
[chem15-s1.21.4] Mass of iron per tablet = 1.16 × 10⁻³ mol × 56 g mol⁻¹ = 0.06496 g = 65.0 mg.
[chem15-s1.21.5] Percentage of iron by mass = 0.06496 g ÷ 0.360 g × 100 = 18.0%.
[chem15-s1.21.6] If the label gives the iron(II) sulfate content instead: mass of FeSO₄ per tablet = 1.16 × 10⁻³ mol × 152 g mol⁻¹ = 0.176 g = 176 mg; iron is 56 ÷ 152 = 36.8% of the mass of FeSO₄.
[chem15-s1.21.7] Conclusion: compare the result with the label value, calculate the percentage difference, and comment on whether the hypothesis is supported.
[chem15-s1.22.b] Besides the acid and permanganate, the tablets themselves are a hazard.
[chem15-s1.22.0] Wear goggles: dilute sulfuric acid is an irritant, and KMnO₄ is an oxidising agent that stains skin and clothes.
[chem15-s1.22.1] Iron tablets are dangerous if swallowed by young children: keep them secure and do not eat them in the laboratory.
[chem15-s1.23.b] Errors push the result in a known direction, so say whether the result would be too low or too high, and why.
[chem15-s1.23.0] Result too low: Fe²⁺ is oxidised to Fe³⁺ by air before titration, because the solution was not acidified or was left standing too long.
[chem15-s1.23.1] Result too low: tablet material is lost while grinding or transferring, so less iron reaches the titration.
[chem15-s1.23.2] Result too high: overshooting the end point, taking a deep purple colour instead of the first permanent pale pink.
[chem15-s1.23.3] Result too high: tablets that also contain vitamin C or another reducing agent, which also reacts with MnO₄⁻.
[chem15-s1.23.4] Other error: too little acid gives brown MnO₂ and an unclear end point.
[chem15-s1.23.5] Other error: KMnO₄ that has partly decomposed since it was standardised.
[chem15-s1.23.6] Other error: a burette or pipette not rinsed with its own solution, as in acid–base titrations.

CANDIDATES chem14-s2: 2. Balancing redox equations and the electrochemical series
[chem14-s2.0.b] Complex redox equations are balanced by making the electrons lost equal the electrons gained. That fixes the key ratio of the equation, and the remaining atoms are then balanced by inspection (by counting atoms and adjusting the numbers in front of the formulas).
[chem14-s2.0.0] Recap: oxidation is an increase in oxidation number (the charge an atom would have if its bonds were ionic) and reduction is a decrease. There is more on the rules for assigning oxidation numbers in Oxidation and reduction.
[chem14-s2.0.1] Step 1: assign oxidation numbers to every atom on both sides, so that you can see which atoms change.
[chem14-s2.0.2] Step 2: identify the elements whose oxidation numbers change. One increases, so the species containing it is oxidised and is the reducing agent (it gives away electrons). One decreases, so that species is reduced and is the oxidising agent (it takes electrons).
[chem14-s2.0.3] Step 3: work out the change per formula unit, which is the change per atom multiplied by the number of those atoms in the formula. This equals the number of electrons lost or gained.
[chem14-s2.0.4] Step 4: choose coefficients (the numbers written in front of formulas) for the oxidising and reducing agents, and their products, so that the total increase equals the total decrease. Use the lowest common multiple, so that electrons lost equal electrons gained.
[chem14-s2.0.5] Step 5: balance the remaining atoms by inspection, leaving oxygen and hydrogen until last, because the other atoms are fixed by the electron step.
[chem14-s2.0.6] Step 5 in acidic solution: add H₂O to balance O, then add H⁺ to balance H.
[chem14-s2.0.7] Step 6: check that each element balances and that the total charge is the same on both sides.
[chem14-s2.1.b] Redox equations often have many atoms and charges, so guessing coefficients rarely works. The method gives a fixed order of working with a built-in check.
[chem14-s2.1.0] Complex redox equations can't easily be balanced by inspection alone, because oxygen, hydrogen and charge all have to come out right at once.
[chem14-s2.1.1] Matching electrons lost and gained fixes the key ratio first, and the charge check at the end catches mistakes.
[chem14-s2.1.2] Write every charge correctly, because one wrong charge makes the equation wrong.
[chem14-s2.2.b] Acidified manganate(VII) (MnO₄⁻, the purple permanganate ion) oxidises iron(II) ions to iron(III). Each step below follows the method in order.
[chem14-s2.2.0] Unbalanced: MnO₄⁻ + Fe²⁺ + H⁺ → Mn²⁺ + Fe³⁺ + H₂O.
[chem14-s2.2.1] Mn goes from +7 to +2, a decrease of 5, so Mn is reduced and MnO₄⁻ is the oxidising agent.
[chem14-s2.2.2] Fe goes from +2 to +3, an increase of 1, so Fe²⁺ is oxidised and is the reducing agent.
[chem14-s2.2.3] Equalise: one MnO₄⁻ gains 5 electrons, so 5 Fe²⁺ are needed to lose 5: MnO₄⁻ + 5Fe²⁺ + H⁺ → Mn²⁺ + 5Fe³⁺ + H₂O.
[chem14-s2.2.4] Balance O: there are 4 O on the left, so 4H₂O on the right.
[chem14-s2.2.5] Balance H: 8 H are in 4H₂O, so 8H⁺ go on the left: MnO₄⁻ + 5Fe²⁺ + 8H⁺ → Mn²⁺ + 5Fe³⁺ + 4H₂O.
[chem14-s2.2.6] Check atoms: Mn 1 = 1, Fe 5 = 5, O 4 = 4, H 8 = 8.
[chem14-s2.2.7] Check charge: left (−1) + 5(+2) + 8(+1) = +17; right (+2) + 5(+3) = +17.
[chem14-s2.3.b] Copper metal is oxidised by the nitrate ion (NO₃⁻) in acid, and the nitrate is reduced to nitrogen monoxide, NO. Here the electron changes, 2 and 3, don't match, so you need the lowest common multiple.
[chem14-s2.3.0] Unbalanced: Cu + NO₃⁻ + H⁺ → Cu²⁺ + NO + H₂O.
[chem14-s2.3.1] Cu goes from 0 to +2, an increase of 2, so copper is oxidised and is the reducing agent.
[chem14-s2.3.2] N goes from +5 in NO₃⁻ to +2 in NO, a decrease of 3, so nitrate is reduced and is the oxidising agent.
[chem14-s2.3.3] The lowest common multiple of 2 and 3 is 6: 3Cu (lose 6) and 2NO₃⁻ (gain 6) give 3Cu + 2NO₃⁻ + H⁺ → 3Cu²⁺ + 2NO + H₂O.
[chem14-s2.3.4] Balance O: 6 O are on the left and 2 are in 2NO, so 4H₂O go on the right.
[chem14-s2.3.5] Balance H: 8 H are in 4H₂O, so 8H⁺ go on the left: 3Cu + 2NO₃⁻ + 8H⁺ → 3Cu²⁺ + 2NO + 4H₂O.
[chem14-s2.3.6] Check charge: left 2(−1) + 8(+1) = +6; right 3(+2) = +6.
[chem14-s2.4.b] Here the species being oxidised contains two atoms that change, so the change per atom must be multiplied up. Ethanedioate is C₂O₄²⁻.
[chem14-s2.4.0] Unbalanced: MnO₄⁻ + C₂O₄²⁻ + H⁺ → Mn²⁺ + CO₂ + H₂O.
[chem14-s2.4.1] Mn goes from +7 to +2, a decrease of 5, so MnO₄⁻ is reduced and is the oxidising agent.
[chem14-s2.4.2] C is +3 in C₂O₄²⁻ (2C + 4(−2) = −2) and +4 in CO₂: an increase of 1 per C atom, so 2 per C₂O₄²⁻ ion. The ethanedioate ion is oxidised and is the reducing agent.
[chem14-s2.4.3] The ethanedioate ion C₂O₄²⁻ is also called oxalate.
[chem14-s2.4.4] The lowest common multiple of 5 and 2 is 10: 2MnO₄⁻ (gain 10) and 5C₂O₄²⁻ (lose 10) give 2Mn²⁺ and 10CO₂.
[chem14-s2.4.5] Balance O: the left has 8 + 20 = 28 O, and 10CO₂ holds 20 O, so 8H₂O go on the right.
[chem14-s2.4.6] Balance H: 16 H⁺ go on the left, to match the 16 H in 8H₂O: 2MnO₄⁻ + 5C₂O₄²⁻ + 16H⁺ → 2Mn²⁺ + 10CO₂ + 8H₂O.
[chem14-s2.4.7] Check charge: left 2(−1) + 5(−2) + 16(+1) = +4; right 2(+2) = +4.
[chem14-s2.5.b] Thiosulfate (S₂O₃²⁻) reacts with iodine, and the sulfur ends up with a fractional oxidation number. This is allowed, because an oxidation number is an average over the atoms of that element in the ion.
[chem14-s2.5.0] Unbalanced: I₂ + S₂O₃²⁻ → I⁻ + S₄O₆²⁻.
[chem14-s2.5.1] I goes from 0 to −1, so each I gains 1 electron and I₂ gains 2: iodine is reduced and is the oxidising agent.
[chem14-s2.5.2] S goes from +2 in S₂O₃²⁻ to +2.5 in S₄O₆²⁻, so each S's oxidation number rises by 0.5 and each S₂O₃²⁻ loses 1 electron: thiosulfate is oxidised and is the reducing agent.
[chem14-s2.5.3] Two S₂O₃²⁻ ions lose 2 electrons, matching one I₂: I₂ + 2S₂O₃²⁻ → 2I⁻ + S₄O₆²⁻.
[chem14-s2.5.4] S₄O₆²⁻ is the tetrathionate ion.
[chem14-s2.5.5] Check atoms: I 2 = 2, S 4 = 4, O 6 = 6.
[chem14-s2.5.6] Check charge: left 2(−2) = −4; right 2(−1) + (−2) = −4.
[chem14-s2.6.b] A P₄ molecule has four atoms that all change, so the electron change per formula is large, and the lowest common multiple is 60. This example also has H₂O and H⁺ on opposite sides.
[chem14-s2.6.0] Unbalanced: P₄ + NO₃⁻ + H₂O → PO₄³⁻ + NO + H⁺.
[chem14-s2.6.1] P goes from 0 in P₄ to +5 in PO₄³⁻, an increase of 5 per atom, so 20 per P₄ molecule: P₄ is oxidised and is the reducing agent.
[chem14-s2.6.2] N goes from +5 to +2, a decrease of 3: nitrate is reduced and is the oxidising agent.
[chem14-s2.6.3] The lowest common multiple of 20 and 3 is 60: 3P₄ (lose 60) and 20NO₃⁻ (gain 60) give 3P₄ + 20NO₃⁻ → 12PO₄³⁻ + 20NO.
[chem14-s2.6.4] Balance O: the left has 60 O, and the right has 48 + 20 = 68, so 8H₂O go on the left.
[chem14-s2.6.5] Balance H: the 8H₂O supply 16 H, so 16H⁺ go on the right: 3P₄ + 20NO₃⁻ + 8H₂O → 12PO₄³⁻ + 20NO + 16H⁺.
[chem14-s2.6.6] Check charge: left 20(−1) = −20; right 12(−3) + 16(+1) = −20.
[chem14-s2.7.b] These equations use the same method with other common oxidising agents. Some are used in titrations (measured reactions between solutions), and you should know them.
[chem14-s2.7.0] Manganate(VII) oxidises chloride to chlorine, which is why hydrochloric acid is not used to acidify permanganate titrations: 2MnO₄⁻ + 10Cl⁻ + 16H⁺ → 2Mn²⁺ + 5Cl₂ + 8H₂O.
[chem14-s2.7.1] In that equation each Mn gains 5 electrons (+7 to +2) and each Cl loses 1 electron (−1 to 0).
[chem14-s2.7.2] Manganate(VII) oxidises iodide to iodine, which is used to make iodine for thiosulfate titrations: 2MnO₄⁻ + 10I⁻ + 16H⁺ → 2Mn²⁺ + 5I₂ + 8H₂O.
[chem14-s2.7.3] Dichromate(VI) oxidises iron(II): Cr₂O₇²⁻ + 6Fe²⁺ + 14H⁺ → 2Cr³⁺ + 6Fe³⁺ + 7H₂O.
[chem14-s2.7.4] Each Cr falls from +6 to +3, so Cr₂O₇²⁻ gains 6 electrons, and each Fe²⁺ loses 1 electron, which is why 6Fe²⁺ are needed.
[chem14-s2.7.5] Only some atoms of an element may change: in Cu + 2H₂SO₄ → CuSO₄ + SO₂ + 2H₂O, one S goes from +6 to +4 (gain 2) and the other stays at +6 in CuSO₄, so only that one is counted.
[chem14-s2.7.6] Copper loses 2 electrons, and the second H₂SO₄ is needed to supply the sulfate for the CuSO₄.
[chem14-s2.7.7] The manganate(VII) and iron(II) equation, and the iodine and thiosulfate equation, are the ones used in Redox titrations.
[chem14-s2.8.b] A half-equation shows only the oxidation or only the reduction, with electrons written in. Building the full equation from two half-equations is an alternative to the oxidation-number method.
[chem14-s2.8.0] The same equation can be built from half-equations: balance the atom that changes, add H₂O to balance O, add H⁺ to balance H, then add electrons to balance charge.
[chem14-s2.8.1] The number of electrons in a half-equation equals the change in oxidation number.
[chem14-s2.8.2] Example: MnO₄⁻ + 8H⁺ + 5e⁻ → Mn²⁺ + 4H₂O, where the 5 electrons match the change from +7 to +2.
[chem14-s2.8.3] The iron half-equation is Fe²⁺ → Fe³⁺ + e⁻.
[chem14-s2.8.4] Multiply the iron half-equation by 5 so that the electrons cancel, then add: MnO₄⁻ + 8H⁺ + 5Fe²⁺ → Mn²⁺ + 5Fe³⁺ + 4H₂O.
[chem14-s2.8.5] Either method earns full marks once oxidation numbers have been assigned, whether or not the question says “hence or otherwise”.
[chem14-s2.9.b] These are the mistakes that most often spoil a balanced equation. Each has a simple rule to prevent it.
[chem14-s2.9.0] Multiply the change per atom by the number of atoms of that element in the formula: 2 C in C₂O₄²⁻, 2 Cr in Cr₂O₇²⁻, 4 P in P₄, 2 I in I₂. Otherwise the electron count, and so the ratio, is wrong.
[chem14-s2.9.1] Balance the electron change before adding H₂O and H⁺; adding them first gives wrong ratios.
[chem14-s2.9.2] Always check charge as well as atoms; an equation can balance in atoms and still be wrong.
[chem14-s2.9.3] Spectator ions (ions that are present but do not change, such as K⁺, SO₄²⁻, Na⁺) are left out of ionic equations.
[chem14-s2.9.4] Bismuth is element 83, so bismuthate (BiO₃⁻) is outside the first 36 elements and outside the specification.
[chem14-s2.10.b] Metals differ in how easily they give up electrons. The electrochemical series ranks them so that you can predict which will react with which.
[chem14-s2.10.0] The electrochemical series is a list of metals (and hydrogen) in order of their tendency to be oxidised, that is, to lose electrons and form positive ions in solution.
[chem14-s2.10.1] Order, most easily oxidised first: potassium, calcium, sodium, magnesium, aluminium, zinc, iron, lead, hydrogen, copper, silver, gold (K, Ca, Na, Mg, Al, Zn, Fe, Pb, H, Cu, Ag, Au).
[chem14-s2.10.2] A metal high in the series loses electrons easily (M → Mⁿ⁺ + ne⁻), so it is a strong reducing agent (a good electron donor). For example Mg(s) → Mg²⁺(aq) + 2e⁻ happens more readily than Cu(s) → Cu²⁺(aq) + 2e⁻.
[chem14-s2.10.3] The ions of metals low in the series gain electrons most easily, so they are the better oxidising agents: Ag⁺ and Cu²⁺ are reduced more readily than Mg²⁺ or Zn²⁺.
[chem14-s2.10.4] Metals near the top corrode most easily; gold and silver at the bottom are found uncombined in nature and resist corrosion, which is why they are used in jewellery.
[chem14-s2.10.5] Hydrogen is included as a reference point: metals above hydrogen displace it from dilute acids, for example Mg(s) + 2H⁺(aq) → Mg²⁺(aq) + H₂(g).
[chem14-s2.10.6] Copper, silver and gold, below hydrogen, don't react with dilute hydrochloric or sulfuric acid. There is more on this in Acids, bases and their reactions.
[chem14-s2.11.b] In a displacement reaction a more easily oxidised metal pushes a less easily oxidised metal out of solution. You can see it happen, because the new metal appears as a deposit and the colour of the solution changes.
[chem14-s2.11.0] A displacement reaction of metals: a metal higher in the series displaces a metal lower in the series from a solution of its ions. The higher metal is oxidised (reducing agent); the lower metal's ions are reduced (oxidising agent).
[chem14-s2.11.1] Zinc in copper(II) sulfate: Zn(s) + Cu²⁺(aq) → Zn²⁺(aq) + Cu(s). The half-equations for this reaction are those given in Oxidation and reduction.
[chem14-s2.11.2] Zinc in copper(II) sulfate: the grey zinc becomes coated with brown copper and the blue colour fades, because blue Cu²⁺ ions are used up.
[chem14-s2.11.3] Magnesium in copper(II) sulfate: Mg(s) + Cu²⁺(aq) → Mg²⁺(aq) + Cu(s); brown copper forms, the blue colour fades and the solution warms, because the reaction is exothermic (it gives out heat).
[chem14-s2.11.4] Iron in copper(II) sulfate: Fe(s) + Cu²⁺(aq) → Fe²⁺(aq) + Cu(s); an iron nail becomes coated with pink-brown copper and the solution turns from blue towards pale green (iron(II) ions).
[chem14-s2.11.5] Copper in silver nitrate: Cu(s) + 2Ag⁺(aq) → Cu²⁺(aq) + 2Ag(s); grey-white crystals of silver grow on the copper and the colourless solution turns blue (copper(II) ions).
[chem14-s2.12.b] The series also tells you when nothing will happen, and how fast a reaction should be.
[chem14-s2.12.0] No reaction happens when the metal is lower than the metal ion: copper in zinc sulfate, or copper in sodium chloride solution, where Cu + 2Na⁺ → Cu²⁺ + 2Na does not happen because copper is below sodium and is less easily oxidised.
[chem14-s2.12.1] The anions (negative ions, such as SO₄²⁻, NO₃⁻) don't change; they are spectator ions and are left out of the ionic equation.
[chem14-s2.12.2] The further apart two metals are in the series, the more vigorous the displacement reaction tends to be: magnesium with copper(II) ions reacts faster and releases more heat than iron with copper(II) ions.
[chem14-s2.13.b] This experiment ranks four metals by testing which of them displace the others from solution. The ranking is then compared with the electrochemical series.
[chem14-s2.13.0] Aim: to place magnesium, zinc, iron and copper in order of their tendency to be oxidised, using displacement reactions, and to compare the order with the electrochemical series.
[chem14-s2.13.1] Independent variable (the thing you change): the metal, and so the metal-ion solution it is placed in.
[chem14-s2.13.2] Dependent variable (the thing you observe): whether a reaction is seen, shown by a deposit, a colour change or a temperature rise.
[chem14-s2.13.3] Controlled variables (kept the same so that the test is fair): the concentration and volume of each solution, the size and surface area of each metal piece, the temperature and the time allowed.
[chem14-s2.14.b] Each metal is added to the solutions of the other metals' ions, and the observations are recorded in a grid.
[chem14-s2.14.0] Step 1: clean each strip of metal (magnesium ribbon, zinc foil, iron nail or wire, copper foil) with sandpaper to remove the oxide layer, which would stop or slow the reaction.
[chem14-s2.14.1] Step 2: place the same volume (e.g. about 5 cm³) of each solution, all at the same concentration, in test tubes (or the wells of a spotting tile, a white tile with small hollows): magnesium sulfate, zinc sulfate, iron(II) sulfate and copper(II) sulfate.
[chem14-s2.14.2] Step 3: add a piece of each metal to each solution other than its own; a metal in a solution of its own ions acts as a control, because no reaction is possible.
[chem14-s2.14.3] Step 4: leave for a set time (e.g. 5 minutes), then record any deposit on the metal, change in colour of the solution and temperature change in a results grid.
[chem14-s2.14.4] Step 5: repeat the experiment to check the observations.
[chem14-s2.15.b] Counting how many displacements each metal causes gives a ranking, which you then compare with the series.
[chem14-s2.15.0] Sample data (expected): magnesium reacts with zinc, iron(II) and copper(II) ions (3 reactions); zinc reacts with iron(II) and copper(II) ions (2); iron reacts with copper(II) ions (1); copper reacts with none (0).
[chem14-s2.15.1] Ranking by number of displacements gives Mg > Zn > Fe > Cu, which matches the order in the electrochemical series: magnesium is the most easily oxidised and the strongest reducing agent of the four.
[chem14-s2.15.2] Write an ionic equation for each reaction seen, e.g. Mg(s) + Zn²⁺(aq) → Mg²⁺(aq) + Zn(s), with a grey zinc deposit on the magnesium.
[chem14-s2.15.3] Zn(s) + Fe²⁺(aq) → Zn²⁺(aq) + Fe(s) gives a dark deposit, and the pale green colour fades.
[chem14-s2.15.4] A larger temperature rise, measured with a thermometer using equal amounts of metal powder and solution, is further evidence that the metals are further apart in the series.
[chem14-s2.15.5] Limitation: no visible change shows only that a reaction is too slow to see in the time allowed; it does not prove that no reaction is possible.
[chem14-s2.16.b] Some of the chemicals are hazardous, and several things can make the results misleading.
[chem14-s2.16.0] Wear goggles. Copper(II) sulfate is harmful if swallowed, an irritant and toxic to aquatic life; silver nitrate stains skin and is corrosive.
[chem14-s2.16.1] Collect metal-ion solutions in a waste container rather than pouring them down the sink, because they are harmful to aquatic life.
[chem14-s2.16.2] Keep magnesium away from flames.
[chem14-s2.16.3] An oxide layer left on a metal can stop a reaction that should happen; aluminium's Al₂O₃ layer makes it appear unreactive.
[chem14-s2.16.4] Deposits are hard to see on dark metals, very slow reactions may be missed, and different surface areas or concentrations make comparisons unfair.
[chem14-s2.16.5] Diagram: a row of test tubes in a rack (or a spotting tile), each containing a metal-ion solution with a strip of metal, labelled with the metal and the solution.
[chem14-s2.16.6] Diagram: a results grid of metals against solutions, with ticks and crosses for reaction and no reaction.
[chem14-s2.17.b] You can also work out the order of metals from results that someone else collected, and you can use the series to predict reactions without doing them.
[chem14-s2.17.0] Secondary data are results collected by others, e.g. a table of displacement observations from another class or a data source; they are analysed in the same way, so a metal that displaces another from solution is higher in the series.
[chem14-s2.17.1] Example: if metal X displaces copper and lead from solutions of their ions but not zinc, X lies between zinc and lead in the series.
[chem14-s2.17.2] The series predicts reactions without doing them: zinc will displace silver from silver nitrate; silver will not displace copper from copper(II) sulfate.
[chem14-s2.17.3] The most reactive metals (potassium, sodium, calcium) are not used in displacement from solution, because they react with the water instead, releasing hydrogen: 2Na + 2H₂O → 2NaOH + H₂.

CANDIDATES chem8-s1: 1. The mole and molar volume
[chem8-s1.0.b] Atoms are far too small to count, so chemists count them by weighing. The mole links a mass that can be weighed to a number of particles.
[chem8-s1.0.0] The mole (symbol mol) is the SI unit (the internationally agreed system of units) of amount of substance, so it measures how much of a substance there is by counting its particles.
[chem8-s1.0.1] One mole contains exactly 6.02214076 × 10²³ elementary entities (atoms, molecules, ions or electrons); this number is the Avogadro constant, NA = 6.02 × 10²³ mol⁻¹.
[chem8-s1.0.2] Before 2019 the mole was defined through carbon-12 (the carbon atom with 6 protons and 6 neutrons): the Avogadro constant was the number of atoms in 12 g of carbon-12.
[chem8-s1.0.3] Since the 2019 SI redefinition the value of the Avogadro constant is exact by definition.
[chem8-s1.0.4] Always state the particle, because a mole of different particles contains different numbers of atoms: one mole of oxygen atoms (O) contains 6.02 × 10²³ atoms.
[chem8-s1.0.5] One mole of oxygen molecules (O₂) contains 6.02 × 10²³ molecules and 1.204 × 10²⁴ atoms, because each O₂ molecule is made of two atoms.
[chem8-s1.0.6] This shows the scale of the mole: one mole of water is only 18 g (about 18 cm³, since water has a density of about 1.00 g cm⁻³) but contains 6.02 × 10²³ molecules.
[chem8-s1.1.b] To weigh out a mole you need to know what one mole of the substance weighs. That comes from the relative atomic masses of its atoms, and density links mass to volume for liquids and solids.
[chem8-s1.1.0] Relative molecular mass (Mr) is the sum of the relative atomic masses (Ar, the mass of an atom compared with one-twelfth of a carbon-12 atom) of all the atoms in a molecule (or formula unit, the simplest ratio of ions in an ionic compound); it has no units.
[chem8-s1.1.1] Relative atomic masses used in these calculations: H 1, C 12, N 14, O 16, Na 23, Mg 24, S 32, Cl 35.5, Ca 40.
[chem8-s1.1.2] Example: Mr(H₂O) = 2(1) + 16 = 18, because water has two hydrogen atoms and one oxygen atom.
[chem8-s1.1.3] Example: Mr(H₂SO₄) = 2(1) + 32 + 4(16) = 98, because there are two H, one S and four O.
[chem8-s1.1.4] Example: Mr(CaCO₃) = 40 + 12 + 3(16) = 100, because there is one Ca, one C and three O.
[chem8-s1.1.5] Molar mass (M) is the mass of one mole of a substance, in grams per mole (g mol⁻¹); it is numerically equal to Ar or Mr, so the molar mass of water is 18 g mol⁻¹.
[chem8-s1.1.6] Density is mass per unit volume (how much mass is packed into a given space): ρ = m ÷ V, in g cm⁻³ or kg m⁻³.
[chem8-s1.2.b] Every mole calculation uses the same few relationships. Each one is a single step, and you can chain them together.
[chem8-s1.2.0] Moles from mass: n = m ÷ M, with m in g and M in g mol⁻¹. Example: 8.8 g of CO₂ (M = 44 g mol⁻¹): n = 8.8 ÷ 44 = 0.20 mol.
[chem8-s1.2.1] Mass from moles: m = n × M, which is the same relationship turned round. Example: 0.25 mol of NaCl (M = 58.5 g mol⁻¹): m = 0.25 × 58.5 = 14.6 g.
[chem8-s1.2.2] Number of particles from moles: N = n × NA, because each mole holds 6.02 × 10²³ particles. Example: 0.20 mol of CO₂: N = 0.20 × 6.02 × 10²³ = 1.20 × 10²³ molecules.
[chem8-s1.2.3] Moles from number of particles: n = N ÷ NA, which reverses the previous step.
[chem8-s1.2.4] Number of atoms: multiply the number of molecules by the number of atoms in each molecule, because each molecule contains that many atoms.
[chem8-s1.2.5] Example: 0.20 mol of CO₂ contains 0.20 × 3 = 0.60 mol of atoms, so 0.60 × 6.02 × 10²³ = 3.61 × 10²³ atoms.
[chem8-s1.3.b] Gases are usually measured by volume and liquids by volume and density, so these also have to be converted to moles. Moles are the middle step in every conversion.
[chem8-s1.3.0] Gas volume from moles: V = n × molar volume (the volume of one mole of gas), using 22.4 L mol⁻¹ at STP (standard temperature and pressure, the agreed reference conditions for gases: 0 °C and 101.3 kPa).
[chem8-s1.3.1] Mass from volume and density: m = ρ × V, then convert the mass to moles with n = m ÷ M.
[chem8-s1.3.2] Example: 10.0 cm³ of ethanol (density 0.789 g cm⁻³): m = 10.0 × 0.789 = 7.89 g.
[chem8-s1.3.3] Continuing the ethanol example (M = 46 g mol⁻¹): n = 7.89 ÷ 46 = 0.172 mol.
[chem8-s1.3.4] Conversion route: mass ⇄ moles ⇄ number of particles, and moles ⇄ gas volume; always go through moles.
[chem8-s1.4.b] Not all atoms of an element have the same mass, so the relative atomic mass in the data tables is an average. That average rests on two ideas: isotopes and mass number.
[chem8-s1.4.0] Isotopes are atoms of the same element with different mass numbers, because they have the same number of protons but different numbers of neutrons.
[chem8-s1.4.1] The mass number of an isotope is the total number of protons and neutrons in its nucleus (the central core of the atom).
[chem8-s1.4.2] Relative atomic mass (Ar) is the weighted average of the isotope masses, using their percentage abundances (the percentage of the element's atoms that are that isotope), relative to one-twelfth of the mass of a carbon-12 atom.
[chem8-s1.5.b] Each isotope contributes to the average in proportion to how abundant it is. Common isotopes count for more than rare ones.
[chem8-s1.5.0] Step 1: multiply each isotope's mass number by its percentage abundance.
[chem8-s1.5.1] Step 2: add the products together, to get the total mass contributed by 100 atoms.
[chem8-s1.5.2] Step 3: divide the total by 100, because the abundances are percentages, so Ar = Σ(mass number × % abundance) ÷ 100.
[chem8-s1.5.3] Quick check: the answer must lie between the lightest and heaviest isotope mass numbers, nearer to the more abundant isotope.
[chem8-s1.6.b] Here the three-step method is applied to chlorine, boron and magnesium, the last with three isotopes.
[chem8-s1.6.0] Chlorine example (75% ³⁵Cl, 25% ³⁷Cl): the products are 35 × 75 = 2625 and 37 × 25 = 925.
[chem8-s1.6.1] Adding the products gives 2625 + 925 = 3550, and 3550 ÷ 100 = 35.5, so Ar(Cl) = 35.5, nearer 35 because ³⁵Cl is three times as abundant as ³⁷Cl.
[chem8-s1.6.2] Boron example (19.9% ¹⁰B, 80.1% ¹¹B): the products are 10 × 19.9 = 199 and 11 × 80.1 = 881.1.
[chem8-s1.6.3] Adding the products gives 199 + 881.1 = 1080.1, and 1080.1 ÷ 100 = 10.8, so Ar(B) = 10.8.
[chem8-s1.6.4] Magnesium example, three isotopes (79.0% ²⁴Mg, 10.0% ²⁵Mg, 11.0% ²⁶Mg): the products are 24 × 79.0 = 1896, 25 × 10.0 = 250 and 26 × 11.0 = 286.
[chem8-s1.6.5] Adding the products gives 1896 + 250 + 286 = 2432, and 2432 ÷ 100 = 24.3, so Ar(Mg) = 24.3.
[chem8-s1.7.b] The method also runs backwards: if you are given the relative atomic mass and the isotopes, you can find how much of each there is, using algebra.
[chem8-s1.7.0] For two isotopes, let the abundance of one isotope be x%; the other is then (100 − x)%, because the two abundances add up to 100%.
[chem8-s1.7.1] Set up Ar = [mass₁ × x + mass₂ × (100 − x)] ÷ 100 and solve for x.
[chem8-s1.7.2] Boron example (Ar = 10.8; isotopes ¹⁰B and ¹¹B): 10x + 11(100 − x) = 1080.
[chem8-s1.7.3] Expanding gives 10x + 1100 − 11x = 1080, so 1100 − x = 1080 and x = 20.
[chem8-s1.7.4] The abundances are therefore about 20% boron-10 and 80% boron-11, matching the 19.9% and 80.1% used in the Ar calculation.
[chem8-s1.7.5] Using whole mass numbers is an approximation, because real isotope masses are not exact whole numbers; data-book isotope masses give more accurate abundances.
[chem8-s1.8.b] Gas volumes can be compared directly, with no need to weigh. Avogadro's law says why: the volume of a gas depends only on how many molecules it holds.
[chem8-s1.8.0] Avogadro's law: equal volumes of all gases, at the same temperature and pressure, contain equal numbers of molecules.
[chem8-s1.8.1] So at fixed temperature and pressure, the volume of a gas is directly proportional to the number of moles: V ∝ n (double the moles and the volume doubles).
[chem8-s1.8.2] Gay-Lussac's law of combining volumes: gases react in volumes that are in simple whole-number ratios to one another and to the volumes of any gaseous products, at the same temperature and pressure.
[chem8-s1.8.3] Example of combining volumes: 2 volumes of hydrogen react with 1 volume of oxygen.
[chem8-s1.8.4] Avogadro's law explains Gay-Lussac's law: the volume ratio equals the mole ratio in the balanced equation, because equal volumes hold equal numbers of molecules.
[chem8-s1.8.5] Example: N₂(g) + 3H₂(g) → 2NH₃(g) reads as 1 volume of nitrogen + 3 volumes of hydrogen → 2 volumes of ammonia.
[chem8-s1.9.b] Gas volumes change with temperature and pressure, so a volume is only useful if the conditions are stated. Chemists agree a standard set of conditions, STP, and quote the molar volume under them.
[chem8-s1.9.0] Standard temperature and pressure (STP) is 0 °C (273 K) and 101.3 kPa (1 atmosphere, 1.013 × 10⁵ Pa).
[chem8-s1.9.1] Temperature in gas calculations must be in kelvin (the absolute temperature scale, where 0 °C is 273 K).
[chem8-s1.9.2] Molar volume is the volume occupied by one mole of any gas at a stated temperature and pressure.
[chem8-s1.9.3] At STP the molar volume is 22.4 L mol⁻¹ (22 400 cm³).
[chem8-s1.9.4] At room temperature (about 20 °C) and pressure, the molar volume is about 24.0 L mol⁻¹, larger than at STP because the gas is warmer.
[chem8-s1.9.5] IUPAC (the international chemistry body) now uses a standard pressure of 100 kPa, at which the molar volume at 273.15 K is 22.7 L mol⁻¹.
[chem8-s1.9.6] Leaving Certificate calculations use 22.4 L mol⁻¹ at STP unless told otherwise.
[chem8-s1.9.7] Molar volume applies to gases only, not to liquids or solids.
[chem8-s1.10.b] The value 22.4 L mol⁻¹ can be calculated from the ideal gas equation, and it can be found by experiment.
[chem8-s1.10.0] From the ideal gas equation PV = nRT (P pressure, V volume, n moles, R the gas constant, T temperature in kelvin), the volume of n moles is V = nRT ÷ P. There is more on this in Gas laws and the ideal gas equation.
[chem8-s1.10.1] For 1 mol at 273 K and 101 325 Pa, with R = 8.314 J K⁻¹ mol⁻¹: V = 1 × 8.314 × 273 ÷ 101 325.
[chem8-s1.10.2] This gives V = 0.0224 m³ = 22.4 L, the molar volume at STP.
[chem8-s1.10.3] Experimentally, weigh a gas cylinder (for example, of oxygen) before and after releasing a known volume of gas into a gas syringe (a calibrated syringe that collects and measures gas volume).
[chem8-s1.10.4] Find the number of moles of gas released from the mass lost, using n = m ÷ M.
[chem8-s1.10.5] Scale the volume to one mole and correct it to STP to obtain the molar volume.
[chem8-s1.10.6] Diagram: a gas cylinder on a balance, connected by tubing to a gas syringe; label the cylinder, the balance, the tubing and the scale of the gas syringe.
[chem8-s1.11.b] At STP every mole of gas occupies 22.4 L, so converting between volume and moles takes one step. Mass and volume are linked through moles.
[chem8-s1.11.0] Moles from gas volume at STP: n = V ÷ 22.4, with V in L. Example: 5.6 L of CO₂ at STP: n = 5.6 ÷ 22.4 = 0.25 mol.
[chem8-s1.11.1] Volume from moles at STP: V = n × 22.4 L. Example: 0.50 mol of O₂ occupies 0.50 × 22.4 = 11.2 L at STP.
[chem8-s1.11.2] Mass to volume goes through moles, because 22.4 L is the volume of a mole: first n = m ÷ M.
[chem8-s1.11.3] Example: 8.0 g of methane (M = 16 g mol⁻¹): n = 8.0 ÷ 16 = 0.50 mol.
[chem8-s1.11.4] Then V = 0.50 × 22.4 = 11.2 L at STP.
[chem8-s1.12.b] Because volume ratios equal mole ratios, the balanced equation tells you the gas volumes directly, with no need to convert to moles.
[chem8-s1.12.0] Example of reacting gases: CH₄ + 2O₂ → CO₂ + 2H₂O.
[chem8-s1.12.1] The volumes of methane and oxygen are in the ratio 50 cm³ : 100 cm³, which is 1 : 2 as in the equation, so all the reactants are used up.
[chem8-s1.12.2] So 50 cm³ of CO₂ is formed, measured at the same temperature and pressure, because the equation gives 1 mole of CO₂ for every 1 mole of methane.
[chem8-s1.13.b] Aim: to determine the relative molecular mass of a volatile liquid by turning a measured mass of it into vapour. The vapour fills a flask of known volume, so its moles can be found from the gas equation.
[chem8-s1.13.0] A volatile liquid evaporates easily, i.e. it has a low boiling point, so it can be turned completely into vapour at 100 °C. Propanone (CH₃COCH₃, boiling point 56 °C) is used.
[chem8-s1.13.1] If the mass (m), volume (V), temperature (T) and pressure (P) of the vapour are measured, the number of moles is n = PV ÷ RT, where R is the gas constant.
[chem8-s1.13.2] Relative molecular mass is then Mr = m ÷ n, giving the molar mass in g mol⁻¹.
[chem8-s1.13.3] The vapour is assumed to behave as an ideal gas (a gas that obeys the gas equation exactly).
[chem8-s1.14.b] The flask holds the vapour, the boiling water bath heats it, and the balance and barometer supply the measurements needed for the calculation.
[chem8-s1.14.0] A clean, dry conical flask (about 250 cm³), a small square of aluminium foil held by a rubber band, and a pin.
[chem8-s1.14.1] A large beaker of boiling water (the water bath) on a hotplate, with a thermometer in the water.
[chem8-s1.14.2] A top-pan balance reading to 0.01 g, a barometer (to measure atmospheric pressure), a graduated (measuring) cylinder, and a dropper of the volatile liquid (propanone).
[chem8-s1.14.3] Diagram: the conical flask with its foil cap, rubber band and pinhole, clamped in a beaker of boiling water on a hotplate; label the water bath and the thermometer.
[chem8-s1.14.4] Diagram: a top-pan balance with the flask on the pan, and a barometer; label the pan and the display or scale.
[chem8-s1.14.5] Diagram: a graduated (measuring) cylinder, read at eye level with the bottom of the meniscus (the curved surface of the water) on the scale.
[chem8-s1.15.b] The aim of the first steps is to fill the flask with pure propanone vapour at a known temperature.
[chem8-s1.15.0] 1. Weigh the dry conical flask with the foil cap and rubber band.
[chem8-s1.15.1] 2. Add about 2–3 cm³ of propanone, replace the foil cap and pierce one small pinhole in it.
[chem8-s1.15.2] 3. Clamp the flask in the boiling water bath so that as much of it as possible is immersed.
[chem8-s1.15.3] 4. Leave it until all the liquid has vaporised; the vapour pushes the air out of the flask through the pinhole and excess vapour escapes.
[chem8-s1.15.4] The flask is then left full of propanone vapour at the temperature of the boiling water.
[chem8-s1.15.5] 5. Record the temperature of the water bath and the atmospheric pressure from the barometer.

[chem8-s1.17.b] The numbers below are sample data for illustration. Every quantity must be in SI units before it goes into the equation.
[chem8-s1.17.0] Sample data: mass of vapour 0.51 g; volume of flask 270 cm³ = 2.70 × 10⁻⁴ m³; temperature 100 °C = 373 K; pressure 101 000 Pa.
[chem8-s1.17.1] Units: P in Pa, V in m³ and T in K; 1 cm³ = 1 × 10⁻⁶ m³.
[chem8-s1.17.2] Moles: n = PV ÷ RT, with R = 8.314 J K⁻¹ mol⁻¹.
[chem8-s1.17.3] Numerator: PV = 101 000 × 2.70 × 10⁻⁴ = 27.27.
[chem8-s1.17.4] Denominator: RT = 8.314 × 373 = 3101.
[chem8-s1.17.5] Moles: n = 27.27 ÷ 3101 = 8.79 × 10⁻³ mol.
[chem8-s1.17.6] Relative molecular mass: Mr = m ÷ n = 0.51 ÷ (8.79 × 10⁻³) = 58.0.
[chem8-s1.17.7] The accepted Mr of propanone (CH₃COCH₃) is 58, so the result agrees with it.
[chem8-s1.18.b] Propanone is flammable and its vapour is harmful to breathe, and the apparatus is hot.
[chem8-s1.18.0] Propanone is highly flammable: use no naked flames, and heat the water bath with a hotplate or electric heater.
[chem8-s1.18.1] Propanone vapour is an irritant: work in a well-ventilated room.
[chem8-s1.18.2] Take care with boiling water and hot glassware, because they can cause burns.
[chem8-s1.19.b] The result depends on measuring the mass of vapour, so anything that changes the measured mass, or makes the vapour differ from the ideal, gives an error.
[chem8-s1.19.0] If not all the liquid vaporises, or the flask is removed too early, the liquid left in it makes the mass too high and so Mr too high.
[chem8-s1.19.1] Wait until no liquid is visible in the flask before removing it from the bath, so that all the liquid has become vapour.
[chem8-s1.19.2] Water left on the outside of the flask when it is reweighed makes the mass too high; dry it carefully.
[chem8-s1.19.3] A pinhole that is too large lets air re-enter as the flask cools, or lets vapour escape before weighing, which affects the measured mass.
[chem8-s1.19.4] Part of the flask is above the water, so the vapour there is cooler than 373 K.
[chem8-s1.19.5] The vapour is not a perfect ideal gas, especially close to its boiling point.
[chem8-s1.19.6] The mass of vapour is small compared with the flask, so a balance reading to 0.01 g (or better) is needed.
[chem8-s1.19.7] Repeat the experiment and average the results, because this reduces the effect of random errors in a single run.
