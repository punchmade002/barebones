TARGET chem25-s5: 5. Redox and acid–base reactions of organic compounds
[chem25-s5.0.b] Organic chemists track redox (electron-transfer) changes by watching oxygen and hydrogen, because carbon compounds rarely show ions. This gives a simple ladder from alcohols up to carboxylic acids.
[chem25-s5.0.0] An organic compound is oxidised when it gains oxygen or loses hydrogen, and reduced when it gains hydrogen or loses oxygen. These are the quick tests to apply to any organic change.
[chem25-s5.0.1] The oxidation number of the carbon involved (the charge it would have if the bonds were fully ionic) rises on oxidation and falls on reduction, which matches the oxygen and hydrogen rule.
[chem25-s5.0.2] The oxidation ladder: primary alcohol (OH on a carbon with one other carbon attached) to aldehyde (–CHO) to carboxylic acid (–COOH); secondary alcohol (OH on a carbon with two other carbons attached) to ketone (C=O inside the chain).
[chem25-s5.0.3] Each step up the ladder is an oxidation, and the reverse of each step is a reduction.
[chem25-s5.0.4] Equations may be written with [O] for oxygen supplied by the oxidising agent (the substance that removes electrons or hydrogen), so the organic change is shown without the reagent: CH₃CH₂OH + [O] → CH₃CHO + H₂O, and CH₃CHO + [O] → CH₃COOH.
[chem25-s5.1.b] Potassium permanganate is a strong oxidising agent that shows its own progress by colour. In acid it removes hydrogen or adds oxygen to alcohols, so it carries organic compounds up the oxidation ladder.
[chem25-s5.1.0] Acidified potassium permanganate, KMnO₄ with dilute sulfuric acid, is a strong oxidising agent. It is reduced from purple MnO₄⁻ to almost colourless Mn²⁺: MnO₄⁻ + 8H⁺ + 5e⁻ → Mn²⁺ + 4H₂O.
[chem25-s5.1.1] It oxidises primary alcohols to aldehydes and then to carboxylic acids: ethanol to ethanal to ethanoic acid, and propan-1-ol to propanal to propanoic acid. The purple colour fades as the permanganate is used up.
[chem25-s5.1.2] Balanced example: 5C₂H₅OH + 2MnO₄⁻ + 6H⁺ → 5CH₃CHO + 2Mn²⁺ + 8H₂O, built from the organic half-equation (the electron-losing half) C₂H₅OH → CH₃CHO + 2H⁺ + 2e⁻ and the permanganate half-equation, so that the electrons cancel.
[chem25-s5.1.3] To stop at the aldehyde, distil it off as it forms (heating so it boils out of the mixture) and do not use the oxidising agent in excess.
[chem25-s5.1.4] This works because ethanal boils at about 21 °C, well below ethanol at 78 °C, so it leaves before it can be oxidised further.
[chem25-s5.2.b] Whether acidified permanganate reacts depends on how many hydrogens sit on the carbon bearing the oxygen. This decides how far each type of alcohol gets.
[chem25-s5.2.0] To make the acid, use an excess of oxidising agent and heat under reflux (boiling with a condenser that returns the vapours to the flask), so the aldehyde stays in the mixture until it is oxidised to the acid.
[chem25-s5.2.1] It oxidises secondary alcohols to ketones: propan-2-ol to propanone, CH₃CH(OH)CH₃ + [O] → CH₃COCH₃ + H₂O.
[chem25-s5.2.2] It does not oxidise tertiary alcohols (OH on a carbon with three other carbons attached), such as 2-methylpropan-2-ol, or ketones. Neither has a hydrogen on the carbon bearing the oxygen, and oxidation would need a C–C bond to break.
[chem25-s5.2.3] Carboxylic acids are not oxidised further.
[chem25-s5.3.b] Permanganate behaves differently in alkaline conditions: it is reduced less far, so a different product and colour result. The alcohol is still oxidised up the ladder.
[chem25-s5.3.0] In basic (alkaline) solution, e.g. with sodium carbonate, permanganate is reduced to brown manganese(IV) oxide instead of Mn²⁺: MnO₄⁻ + 2H₂O + 3e⁻ → MnO₂ + 4OH⁻.
[chem25-s5.3.1] The colour change is from purple to a brown precipitate (an insoluble solid that forms in the solution).
[chem25-s5.3.2] It oxidises a primary alcohol to a carboxylic acid, which is present as its salt in the basic solution because the base neutralises it: phenylmethanol gives benzoate ions, which give benzoic acid on acidifying. There is more on this in Investigation: synthesising benzoic acid.
[chem25-s5.4.b] Fehling's solution is a gentler oxidising agent than permanganate. Because it only oxidises aldehydes, it can tell an aldehyde from a ketone or an alcohol.
[chem25-s5.4.0] Fehling's solution is a mild oxidising agent containing Cu²⁺ ions.
[chem25-s5.4.1] It oxidises aldehydes to carboxylic acids, present as carboxylate salts (the acid's anion) in the alkaline reagent, and is itself reduced to brick-red copper(I) oxide, Cu₂O.
[chem25-s5.4.2] It does not oxidise ketones, and, being mild, it does not oxidise alcohols, so it is used to identify aldehydes. There is more on this in Tests, reaction schemes and preparing an ester.
[chem25-s5.5.b] Reduction is the reverse of the oxidation ladder: hydrogen gas with a nickel catalyst (a solid that speeds up the reaction without being used up) adds hydrogen to a double bond. It turns carbonyl compounds back into alcohols.
[chem25-s5.5.0] Hydrogen gas with a nickel catalyst is the reducing agent: it adds hydrogen across a C=O or C=C double bond.
[chem25-s5.5.1] Aldehydes are reduced to primary alcohols: CH₃CHO + H₂ → CH₃CH₂OH (ethanal to ethanol).
[chem25-s5.5.2] Ketones are reduced to secondary alcohols: CH₃COCH₃ + H₂ → CH₃CH(OH)CH₃ (propanone to propan-2-ol).
[chem25-s5.5.3] Alkenes (hydrocarbons with a C=C bond) are reduced to alkanes: CH₂=CH₂ + H₂ → CH₃CH₃; this is also an addition reaction, because two molecules join to form one product.
[chem25-s5.5.4] Hydrogenation of vegetable oils: oils contain many C=C bonds (they are polyunsaturated), and adding hydrogen over nickel converts some C=C to C–C. This raises the melting point, so the liquid oil becomes a soft solid fat used in margarine.
[chem25-s5.5.5] The degree of hydrogenation (how many C=C bonds are converted) sets how hard the product is.
[chem25-s5.6.b] Carboxylic acids can give away a proton (H⁺), so they react like other acids with bases. Alcohols hold on to their proton much more tightly.
[chem25-s5.6.0] Carboxylic acids are weak acids (only partly ionised in water) that transfer a proton to bases: CH₃COOH + NaOH → CH₃COONa + H₂O.
[chem25-s5.6.1] With carbonates they give carbon dioxide: 2CH₃COOH + Na₂CO₃ → 2CH₃COONa + H₂O + CO₂.
[chem25-s5.6.2] With hydrogencarbonates they also give carbon dioxide: CH₃COOH + NaHCO₃ → CH₃COONa + H₂O + CO₂.
[chem25-s5.6.3] Alcohols are far weaker acids than carboxylic acids, about as weak as water itself, so they do not react with sodium carbonate or usefully with sodium hydroxide. There is more on this in Physical properties and acidity.
[chem25-s5.7.b] An ester (the compound formed from an alcohol and a carboxylic acid) can be split again by water. With a base the hydroxide ion acts as a base, so this is an acid–base reaction, and it goes all the way to completion.
[chem25-s5.7.0] Hydrolysis is the breaking of a compound by reaction with water.
[chem25-s5.7.1] Esters can be hydrolysed by acid, which is reversible and is the reverse of esterification (making an ester from an acid and an alcohol), or by base.
[chem25-s5.7.2] Base hydrolysis: heating an ester with sodium hydroxide gives the sodium salt of the carboxylic acid and the alcohol: CH₃COOC₂H₅ + NaOH → CH₃COONa + C₂H₅OH (ethyl ethanoate to sodium ethanoate and ethanol).
[chem25-s5.7.3] It is an acid–base reaction because the hydroxide ion acts as a base and the carboxylic acid formed is converted to its carboxylate ion (the acid minus its H⁺).
[chem25-s5.7.4] This is why base hydrolysis goes to completion, while acid hydrolysis reaches an equilibrium (a balance in which reactants and products are both present).
[chem25-s5.7.5] Acidifying the product mixture (adding H⁺) converts the carboxylate salt back into the free carboxylic acid: CH₃COO⁻ + H⁺ → CH₃COOH.
[chem25-s5.8.b] Fats and oils are esters with three ester links, made from one glycerol and three fatty acids. Their structure explains why one is solid and the other liquid.
[chem25-s5.8.0] Fats and oils are tri-esters, or triglycerides, of glycerol, propane-1,2,3-triol, HOCH₂CH(OH)CH₂OH (an alcohol with three –OH groups), with three long-chain carboxylic acids called fatty acids. Stearic acid, octadecanoic acid, C₁₇H₃₅COOH, is an example.
[chem25-s5.8.1] Fats, mostly from animals, are solid at room temperature and contain mainly saturated chains (chains with only C–C single bonds).
[chem25-s5.8.2] Oils, mostly from plants, are liquid and contain more unsaturated chains with C=C bonds.
[chem25-s5.8.3] Diagram: a triglyceride, with a three-carbon glycerol backbone, each carbon joined through an ester group (–O–CO–) to a long hydrocarbon chain R; and the products, glycerol with three –OH groups and three RCOO⁻Na⁺ ions.
[chem25-s5.9.b] Heating a fat or oil with sodium hydroxide is base hydrolysis of all three ester links at once. It makes soap and glycerol.
[chem25-s5.9.0] Base hydrolysis of a fat or oil is called saponification, the process of soap-making: fat + 3NaOH → glycerol + 3 sodium salts of fatty acids (soap).
[chem25-s5.9.1] Example: glyceryl tristearate reacts with sodium hydroxide: (C₁₇H₃₅COO)₃C₃H₅ + 3NaOH → 3C₁₇H₃₅COONa + C₃H₅(OH)₃, giving sodium stearate and glycerol.
[chem25-s5.9.2] The mole ratio of fat : NaOH : soap : glycerol is 1 : 3 : 3 : 1, because each fat has three ester links and each needs one NaOH.
[chem25-s5.9.3] Molar masses: glyceryl tristearate 890 g mol⁻¹, sodium stearate 306 g mol⁻¹ and glycerol 92 g mol⁻¹.
[chem25-s5.9.4] Glycerol, the co-product (the second product made alongside the soap), is soluble in water and is used in cosmetics and medicines.

CANDIDATES chem25-s2: 2. Tests, reaction schemes and preparing an ester
[chem25-s2.0.b] Aldehydes and ketones both contain the carbonyl group (a carbon double-bonded to oxygen, C=O), so they look alike. The difference is one hydrogen atom, and that hydrogen decides whether the compound can be oxidised (have oxygen added or hydrogen removed) by a mild reagent.
[chem25-s2.0.0] An aldehyde has the –CHO group: the carbonyl carbon carries a hydrogen atom, e.g. ethanal, CH₃CHO.
[chem25-s2.0.1] A ketone has the C=O group joined to two carbon atoms, so its carbonyl carbon has no hydrogen, e.g. propanone, CH₃COCH₃.
[chem25-s2.0.2] An aldehyde is easily oxidised to a carboxylic acid, because the hydrogen on the carbonyl carbon can be replaced by –OH. This makes aldehydes good reducing agents (substances that get oxidised themselves while reducing something else).
[chem25-s2.0.3] A ketone is not oxidised by mild oxidising agents, because its carbonyl carbon has no hydrogen; oxidising a ketone would mean breaking a C–C bond, which a mild reagent cannot do.
[chem25-s2.0.4] A mild oxidising agent that changes colour on reaction therefore tells them apart: an aldehyde gives a colour change, while a ketone gives none.
[chem25-s2.1.b] Fehling's reagent is the mild oxidising agent the specification uses to test for aldehydes. It is made from two solutions that are kept apart and mixed only when needed.
[chem25-s2.1.0] Fehling's reagent is made fresh by mixing equal volumes of Fehling's A (copper(II) sulfate solution, blue) and Fehling's B (potassium sodium tartrate and sodium hydroxide solution, colourless).
[chem25-s2.1.1] The mixture is a clear deep-blue solution containing Cu²⁺ ions, which are the part that reacts with an aldehyde.
[chem25-s2.1.2] The tartrate keeps the Cu²⁺ ions in solution in the alkaline mixture; without it, copper(II) hydroxide would precipitate (form as an insoluble solid).
[chem25-s2.2.b] The aim is to show which of two unlabelled liquids is the aldehyde (ethanal) and which is the ketone (propanone). Both are tested in the same way so the results can be compared.
[chem25-s2.2.0] Method: put about 2 cm³ of Fehling's reagent into each of two test tubes, so that one can be tested with each liquid for comparison.
[chem25-s2.2.1] Method: add a few drops of ethanal to one test tube and a few drops of propanone to the other.
[chem25-s2.2.2] Method: warm both test tubes in a beaker of hot water (a water bath) for a few minutes; do not heat them with a naked flame, because the liquids are flammable.
[chem25-s2.2.3] With ethanal, the blue solution gives a brick-red precipitate of copper(I) oxide, Cu₂O.
[chem25-s2.2.4] With propanone there is no change: the solution stays blue.
[chem25-s2.2.5] An unknown carbonyl compound that gives a brick-red precipitate is an aldehyde, because only aldehydes reduce the Cu²⁺; one that leaves the reagent blue is a ketone.
[chem25-s2.3.b] Both the reagent and the liquids being tested carry hazards, which is why the heating is done in a water bath.
[chem25-s2.3.0] Wear goggles, because Fehling's B contains sodium hydroxide, which is corrosive.
[chem25-s2.3.1] Ethanal (boiling point about 21 °C) and propanone (boiling point 56 °C) are volatile (they evaporate easily) and flammable, so keep them away from flames and use a water bath.
[chem25-s2.3.2] Diagram: two test tubes standing in a beaker of hot water, labelled Fehling's reagent + ethanal (brick-red precipitate) and Fehling's reagent + propanone (blue).
[chem25-s2.4.b] The colour change is a redox reaction (one where electrons are transferred). The copper ions in the reagent are reduced while the aldehyde is oxidised.
[chem25-s2.4.0] Fehling's reagent is a mild oxidising agent: the Cu²⁺ ions oxidise the aldehyde and are themselves reduced to copper(I).
[chem25-s2.4.1] The aldehyde is oxidised to a carboxylic acid; in the alkaline reagent the acid is present as its salt, so ethanal forms ethanoate ions.
[chem25-s2.4.2] Reduction half-equation: Cu²⁺ + e⁻ → Cu⁺, so the oxidation number of copper falls from +2 to +1.
[chem25-s2.4.3] Full ionic equation for the reduction in alkaline solution: 2Cu²⁺(aq) + 2OH⁻(aq) + 2e⁻ → Cu₂O(s) + H₂O(l).
[chem25-s2.4.4] Overall equation: CH₃CHO + 2Cu²⁺ + 5OH⁻ → CH₃COO⁻ + Cu₂O + 3H₂O.
[chem25-s2.4.5] Fehling's reagent is a weak oxidising agent, so ethanal reducing it shows that aldehydes are very easily oxidised; the reagent is too weak to oxidise a ketone.
[chem25-s2.5.b] Carboxylic acids such as ethanoic acid show the usual properties of an acid. Three reactions (with a metal, a carbonate and a base) and an indicator (a dye that changes colour with acidity) are used to show this.
[chem25-s2.5.0] Aim: to show that ethanoic acid behaves as an acid, by its reactions with a metal, a carbonate and a base, and by its effect on an indicator.
[chem25-s2.5.1] Dilute ethanoic acid turns blue litmus red and has a pH below 7 (about 3, on universal indicator or a pH meter).
[chem25-s2.5.2] Control: repeat the tests with ethanol in place of ethanoic acid; ethanol gives no fizzing with magnesium or sodium carbonate and is neutral to indicator, which shows that the –COOH group, not the –OH group alone, is acidic.
[chem25-s2.6.b] An acid reacts with a reactive metal to give a salt and hydrogen gas, and the gas is identified with a lighted taper.
[chem25-s2.6.0] Add a clean piece of magnesium ribbon to dilute ethanoic acid; bubbles of gas form slowly and the magnesium dissolves.
[chem25-s2.6.1] The gas burns with a pop when a lighted taper is held at the mouth of the tube, so it is hydrogen.
[chem25-s2.6.2] Equation: 2CH₃COOH(aq) + Mg(s) → (CH₃COO)₂Mg(aq) + H₂(g).
[chem25-s2.6.3] Ethanoic acid reacts more slowly with magnesium than hydrochloric acid of the same concentration does, because it is a weak acid that is only partly dissociated (split into ions), so its H⁺ concentration is lower.
[chem25-s2.6.4] Diagram: a test tube of dilute ethanoic acid with magnesium ribbon, and a lighted taper held at the mouth of the tube.
[chem25-s2.7.b] Acids react with carbonates to release carbon dioxide, which gives a clear visible sign of reaction.
[chem25-s2.7.0] Add a little solid sodium carbonate to ethanoic acid; the mixture effervesces (fizzes).
[chem25-s2.7.1] The gas turns limewater milky, so it is carbon dioxide.
[chem25-s2.7.2] Equation: 2CH₃COOH(aq) + Na₂CO₃(s) → 2CH₃COONa(aq) + H₂O(l) + CO₂(g).
[chem25-s2.7.3] Diagram: a test tube of ethanoic acid with sodium carbonate, and a delivery tube bubbling the gas into limewater.
[chem25-s2.8.b] A base neutralises an acid, giving a salt and water. Because this gives no gas, a different way of seeing the reaction is needed.
[chem25-s2.8.0] Sodium hydroxide neutralises ethanoic acid, forming the salt sodium ethanoate and water: CH₃COOH(aq) + NaOH(aq) → CH₃COONa(aq) + H₂O(l).
[chem25-s2.8.1] There is no visible change in this reaction, so add phenolphthalein (an indicator that is pink in alkali) to the acid and add sodium hydroxide until the solution just turns pink; alternatively follow the pH or note the temperature rise.
[chem25-s2.9.b] Concentrated ethanoic acid and the hydrogen test both need care.
[chem25-s2.9.0] Wear goggles, because concentrated (glacial) ethanoic acid is corrosive, so use the dilute acid.
[chem25-s2.9.1] Keep flames away from any flammable liquid when testing for hydrogen, because hydrogen burns with a pop.
[chem25-s2.10.b] A reaction scheme is a map of organic chemistry. Reading one means working out, arrow by arrow, what has changed and what caused the change.
[chem25-s2.10.0] A reaction scheme is a diagram that links organic compounds by arrows, each arrow labelled with the reagent (and sometimes the reaction type) that converts one compound into the next.
[chem25-s2.10.1] A scheme shows how compounds of different families are related, and how a target compound can be made from a simpler starting material in several steps; this is organic synthesis.
[chem25-s2.10.2] To read a scheme, name each compound and its functional group (the atom or group that gives a family its reactions), then for each arrow compare the formulae before and after.
[chem25-s2.10.3] Decide what has been added, removed or swapped at each arrow, then name the reaction type and the reagent.
[chem25-s2.10.4] A formula change of +H₂ across C=C or C=O is addition (two molecules joining with no atoms lost) or reduction; a change of −2H is oxidation; a change of +O is oxidation.
[chem25-s2.10.5] A change of −H₂O or −HCl with a new C=C bond is elimination (a small molecule removed, leaving a double bond); one atom swapped for another is substitution.
[chem25-s2.10.6] A carboxylic acid converted to its sodium salt is an acid–base reaction.
[chem25-s2.11.b] Ethene, the two-carbon alkene (a hydrocarbon with a C=C double bond), sits at the centre of the scheme. Most of its conversions are additions across the double bond.
[chem25-s2.11.0] Ethane to chloroethane: chlorine in ultraviolet light (substitution, because a chlorine atom takes the place of a hydrogen atom).
[chem25-s2.11.1] Ethene to ethane: hydrogen with a nickel catalyst (addition, because H₂ adds across the C=C; also reduction).
[chem25-s2.11.2] Ethene to chloroethane: hydrogen chloride (addition).
[chem25-s2.11.3] Ethene to 1,2-dichloroethane: chlorine (addition).
[chem25-s2.11.4] Ethene to 1,2-dibromoethane: bromine (addition).
[chem25-s2.11.5] Ethene to ethanol: water (addition).
[chem25-s2.11.6] Ethanol to ethene: heated aluminium oxide (elimination, because water is removed and a C=C forms).
[chem25-s2.11.7] Chloroethane to ethene: loss of HCl (elimination, because a hydrogen and a chlorine are removed and a C=C forms).
[chem25-s2.12.b] Ethanol links to the oxidation sequence and to the ester. Here the carbon skeleton stays the same except where two molecules join or split.
[chem25-s2.12.0] Ethanol to ethanal to ethanoic acid: acidified potassium permanganate, as two oxidation steps.
[chem25-s2.12.1] Ethanal to ethanol: hydrogen with a nickel catalyst (reduction, because H₂ adds across the C=O).
[chem25-s2.12.2] Ethanoic acid and ethanol give ethyl ethanoate and water, with concentrated sulfuric acid as catalyst (substitution, esterification).
[chem25-s2.12.3] Ethyl ethanoate and sodium hydroxide give sodium ethanoate and ethanol (base hydrolysis, splitting the ester with the help of water and base).
[chem25-s2.12.4] Ethanoic acid to sodium ethanoate: sodium hydroxide or sodium carbonate (acid–base).
[chem25-s2.13.b] Ethene and its chlorinated relative can also be joined into long chains, giving plastics.
[chem25-s2.13.0] Ethene to poly(ethene) by addition polymerisation (many ethene molecules adding together into a chain).
[chem25-s2.13.1] 1,2-dichloroethane to chloroethene by elimination of HCl, then polymerisation to poly(chloroethene), PVC.
[chem25-s2.13.2] Diagram: the two-carbon scheme as a map, with ethene at the centre and arrows to ethane, chloroethane, 1,2-dichloroethane, ethanol and poly(ethene); ethanol linked to ethanal, ethanoic acid and ethyl ethanoate; each arrow labelled with its reagent and reaction type.
[chem25-s2.14.b] The same reactions apply to longer chains. Whether the alcohol is primary or secondary decides whether oxidation gives an aldehyde and acid or a ketone.
[chem25-s2.14.0] Propan-1-ol (a primary alcohol, with the –OH on an end carbon) to propanal to propanoic acid: acidified potassium permanganate (oxidation).
[chem25-s2.14.1] Propan-2-ol (a secondary alcohol, with the –OH on a middle carbon) to propanone: acidified potassium permanganate (oxidation).
[chem25-s2.14.2] Propanone to propan-2-ol: hydrogen with nickel (reduction).
[chem25-s2.14.3] Propan-2-ol to propene and water (elimination).
[chem25-s2.14.4] Propene to propane: hydrogen with nickel (addition, because H₂ adds across the C=C).
[chem25-s2.14.5] Phenylmethanol to benzoic acid: potassium permanganate under basic conditions (oxidation). There is more on this in Investigation: synthesising benzoic acid.
[chem25-s2.15.b] When a scheme is incomplete, the product tells you what must have come before it, and counting carbon atoms checks your answer.
[chem25-s2.15.0] A scheme that ends in an aldehyde probably began with a primary alcohol; a ketone comes from a secondary alcohol.
[chem25-s2.15.1] An alkene can come from an alcohol by elimination, or go to an alcohol by addition of water.
[chem25-s2.15.2] Carbon atoms are neither gained nor lost in these steps, except in esterification, which joins two molecules, and hydrolysis, which splits them, so the carbon skeleton checks each answer.
[chem25-s2.16.b] To predict a product, recognise the functional group and recall its typical reaction. Multi-step syntheses just chain these together.
[chem25-s2.16.0] To predict a product, identify the functional group and apply its typical reaction.
[chem25-s2.16.1] An alkene with X₂, HX, H₂O or H₂ gives an addition product.
[chem25-s2.16.2] A primary alcohol with an oxidising agent gives an aldehyde, then a carboxylic acid; a carboxylic acid with an alcohol gives an ester; an ester with NaOH gives a salt and an alcohol.
[chem25-s2.16.3] Example: ethene to ethanoic acid in two steps, by adding water to give ethanol (addition), then oxidising with excess acidified potassium permanganate (oxidation).
[chem25-s2.16.4] Example: ethanal to ethyl ethanoate, by reducing part of the ethanal to ethanol with H₂ and nickel, oxidising another part to ethanoic acid with acidified KMnO₄, then esterifying the two with concentrated H₂SO₄.
[chem25-s2.16.5] Example: chloroethane to ethanol in two steps, by eliminating HCl to give ethene, then adding water.
[chem25-s2.16.6] Example: heating propan-1-ol with methanoic acid gives propyl methanoate, HCOOCH₂CH₂CH₃; the alcohol supplies the propyl part and the acid supplies the methanoate part.
[chem25-s2.17.b] The shape around a carbon atom depends on its bonds. Tracking whether a carbon is tetrahedral (four bonds pointing to the corners of a pyramid-like shape) or planar (flat) shows what a reaction does to a molecule.
[chem25-s2.17.0] A carbon atom with four single bonds is tetrahedral, with bond angles of about 109.5°.
[chem25-s2.17.1] A carbon atom in a C=C or C=O double bond is planar (trigonal planar), with bond angles of about 120°.
[chem25-s2.17.2] Ethene has no tetrahedral carbon atom; ethanal and ethanoic acid each have one planar carbon (the C=O carbon) and one tetrahedral carbon.
[chem25-s2.17.3] Ethanol and ethane have only tetrahedral carbon atoms.
[chem25-s2.18.b] A reaction that adds to or removes a double bond changes the carbons involved between planar and tetrahedral. Reactions that leave the C=O in place change nothing.
[chem25-s2.18.0] Addition to C=C, as in ethene to ethane, ethene to ethanol or polymerisation, changes the two carbon atoms from planar to tetrahedral.
[chem25-s2.18.1] Elimination, as in ethanol to ethene, changes the two carbon atoms from tetrahedral to planar.
[chem25-s2.18.2] Oxidising an alcohol to an aldehyde or ketone changes the carbon bearing the oxygen from tetrahedral to planar.
[chem25-s2.18.3] Reducing an aldehyde or ketone to an alcohol changes that carbon from planar to tetrahedral.
[chem25-s2.18.4] Aldehyde to carboxylic acid, and acid to ester, leave the carbonyl carbon planar, so there is no change in geometry.
[chem25-s2.19.b] Each reaction needs a particular feature in the molecule. Where it is missing, the reaction cannot happen, and the reason always comes back to that feature.
[chem25-s2.19.0] Tertiary alcohols (alcohols with the –OH on a carbon bonded to three other carbons), such as 2-methylpropan-2-ol, are not oxidised by acidified potassium permanganate.
[chem25-s2.19.1] The carbon bearing the –OH group of a tertiary alcohol has no hydrogen atom to lose, and oxidation would mean breaking a C–C bond.
[chem25-s2.19.2] Ketones are not oxidised by Fehling's reagent or potassium permanganate, for the same reason: there is no hydrogen on the carbonyl carbon.
[chem25-s2.19.3] Alkanes and poly(ethene) cannot undergo addition, because they are saturated (they have only single bonds) and have no pi bond, so they do not decolourise bromine water.
[chem25-s2.19.4] Methanol cannot be dehydrated to an alkene, because it has only one carbon atom and a C=C bond needs two.
[chem25-s2.19.5] Benzene does not decolourise bromine water as an alkene does: its delocalised pi electrons (shared around the whole ring) make the ring unusually stable, so it resists addition. There is more on this in Shapes, bonding and isomerism.
[chem25-s2.19.6] Ethanoic acid is not oxidised further by potassium permanganate; it is the end product of oxidising a primary alcohol.
[chem25-s2.20.b] An ester is the compound formed when a carboxylic acid reacts with an alcohol. In this investigation methyl propanoate is made from propanoic acid and methanol.
[chem25-s2.20.0] Aim: to prepare a sample of an ester, methyl propanoate, from propanoic acid and methanol by heating under reflux (boiling with a condenser that returns the vapour to the flask).
[chem25-s2.20.1] Equation: CH₃CH₂COOH(l) + CH₃OH(l) ⇌ CH₃CH₂COOCH₃(l) + H₂O(l), with propanoic acid and methanol giving methyl propanoate and water in a mole ratio of 1 : 1 : 1.
[chem25-s2.20.2] Molar masses: propanoic acid 74 g mol⁻¹, methanol 32 g mol⁻¹ and methyl propanoate 88 g mol⁻¹.
[chem25-s2.20.3] The alternative preparation is ethanoic acid with ethanol: CH₃COOH + C₂H₅OH ⇌ CH₃COOC₂H₅ + H₂O, giving ethyl ethanoate.
[chem25-s2.20.4] The specification needs only esters made from monocarboxylic acids and primary alcohols.
[chem25-s2.21.b] Esterification does not go to completion, which shapes the whole purification. Isotope labelling shows exactly which bond forms.
[chem25-s2.21.0] Esterification is slow and reversible: it reaches an equilibrium (the forward and reverse reactions balance) with only part of the acid and alcohol converted.
[chem25-s2.21.1] The product mixture therefore also contains unreacted acid and alcohol, water and the sulfuric acid catalyst.
[chem25-s2.21.2] Isotope evidence: when the alcohol is labelled with oxygen-18 (a heavier form of oxygen used as a tracer), the ¹⁸O is found in the ester, not in the water.
[chem25-s2.21.3] So the –OH lost as water comes from the acid, the H comes from the alcohol, and the bond formed is the C–O bond between the acid's carbonyl carbon and the alcohol's oxygen.
[chem25-s2.22.b] The reactants are mixed with a catalyst and boiled gently for a while. A condenser fitted vertically stops the volatile liquids escaping.
[chem25-s2.22.0] In a fume cupboard, put measured volumes of propanoic acid and methanol (about 10 cm³ of each) into a round-bottomed flask.
[chem25-s2.22.1] Add about 1 cm³ of concentrated sulfuric acid a drop at a time, swirling and cooling the flask under the tap, because mixing is exothermic (gives out heat).
[chem25-s2.22.2] Add a few anti-bumping granules, so that the mixture boils smoothly.
[chem25-s2.22.3] Fit a Liebig condenser vertically on the flask, with cold water entering at the bottom and leaving at the top, and leave the top of the condenser open.
[chem25-s2.22.4] Heat the flask in a water bath, or with a heating mantle, so that the mixture boils gently under reflux for about 20–30 minutes.
[chem25-s2.22.5] Diagram: reflux apparatus, with a round-bottomed flask holding the mixture and granules in a water bath, a vertical Liebig condenser with water in at the bottom and out at the top, and an open top.
[chem25-s2.23.b] After reflux the flask holds a mixture. The ester is separated from the other substances by washing, drying and then distilling.
[chem25-s2.23.0] Cool the flask, then pour the mixture into a separating funnel (a tapped funnel for separating two liquids that do not mix) with some water and shake.
[chem25-s2.23.1] The ester forms the upper layer, because it is less dense than water and only slightly soluble in it; run off and discard the lower aqueous layer, which holds most of the acid, methanol and sulfuric acid.
[chem25-s2.23.2] Add sodium carbonate solution to the ester in the funnel and shake gently, opening the tap often (inverted) to release the carbon dioxide formed; this neutralises the remaining acid.
[chem25-s2.23.3] Run off the lower aqueous layer, which now holds the sodium salt of the acid and the water, so the ester is left on its own.
[chem25-s2.23.4] Run the ester into a dry conical flask and add a few lumps of anhydrous calcium chloride (a drying agent that removes water) to remove water; swirl until the liquid is clear, then decant (pour off) or filter it.
[chem25-s2.23.5] Distil the dry ester and collect the fraction boiling at about 80 °C: methyl propanoate boils at 79.8 °C and ethyl ethanoate at 77 °C.
[chem25-s2.23.6] Identify the product by its boiling point and its pleasant, fruity smell, wafting the vapour towards the nose rather than sniffing directly.
[chem25-s2.23.7] Diagram: separating funnel with the upper ester layer, the lower aqueous layer, the stopper and the tap.
[chem25-s2.24.b] Every choice in the method has a purpose. Exams ask for these reasons directly.
[chem25-s2.24.0] Reflux lets the mixture boil for a long time without losing the volatile reactants and product: vapour rises into the condenser, condenses and drips back into the flask.
[chem25-s2.24.1] Concentrated sulfuric acid is the catalyst, providing H⁺ ions; it also absorbs water, which helps shift the equilibrium towards the ester.
[chem25-s2.24.2] A water bath or heating mantle is used rather than a Bunsen burner, because methanol and the ester are flammable; it also gives gentle, even heating.
[chem25-s2.24.3] Anti-bumping granules give smooth boiling by providing sites for small bubbles to form, which prevents sudden violent boiling (bumping).
[chem25-s2.24.4] The condenser is never stoppered, because heating a closed system would build up pressure.
[chem25-s2.24.5] Sodium carbonate removes acid without destroying the ester: Na₂CO₃ + 2H⁺ → 2Na⁺ + H₂O + CO₂.
[chem25-s2.24.6] The pressure in the separating funnel must be released because carbon dioxide is produced.
[chem25-s2.24.7] Distillation separates the ester from remaining alcohol and other impurities by their boiling points, giving a purer product.
[chem25-s2.25.b] The hazards are corrosive and flammable chemicals. The yield is low because of the equilibrium and because product is lost in handling.
[chem25-s2.25.0] Concentrated sulfuric acid is corrosive: wear goggles and gloves, and add it slowly.
[chem25-s2.25.1] Methanol is toxic and flammable, and propanoic acid is corrosive and has a strong smell; work in a fume cupboard and keep naked flames away.
[chem25-s2.25.2] The yield is well below 100%, because the reaction is an equilibrium.
[chem25-s2.25.3] Some ester dissolves in the aqueous layers and is lost in transfers, and some evaporates.
[chem25-s2.25.4] Percentage yield = actual mass ÷ theoretical mass × 100. There is more on this in Theoretical, actual and percentage yield.
[chem25-s2.25.5] Sources of error include not drying the ester fully, which gives a cloudy product and a low boiling point reading.
[chem25-s2.25.6] Not releasing the pressure in the separating funnel can blow out the stopper and lose product.
[chem25-s2.25.7] Heating too strongly lets vapour escape from the top of the condenser.

CANDIDATES chem25-s1: 1. Types of organic reaction and addition polymers
[chem25-s1.0.b] Organic reactions fall into five types, and the first is addition. It is the characteristic reaction of compounds with a carbon-carbon double bond, because the double bond can open up to take on new atoms.
[chem25-s1.0.0] An addition reaction is one in which two or more molecules react to form a single molecule.
[chem25-s1.0.1] Atoms add across a double (or triple) bond, so an unsaturated compound (one with a C=C or C≡C bond) becomes saturated (only single bonds) and there is only one product.
[chem25-s1.0.2] Ethene and bromine: CH₂=CH₂ + Br₂ → CH₂BrCH₂Br, giving 1,2-dibromoethane. Bromine water changes from orange (red-brown) to colourless, which is the test for unsaturation.
[chem25-s1.0.3] Ethene and chlorine: CH₂=CH₂ + Cl₂ → CH₂ClCH₂Cl, giving 1,2-dichloroethane.
[chem25-s1.0.4] Ethene and hydrogen chloride: CH₂=CH₂ + HCl → CH₃CH₂Cl, giving chloroethane.
[chem25-s1.0.5] Ethene and water: CH₂=CH₂ + H₂O → CH₃CH₂OH, giving ethanol; this is the industrial route to ethanol.
[chem25-s1.0.6] Ethene and hydrogen with a nickel catalyst (a substance that speeds up the reaction without being used up): CH₂=CH₂ + H₂ → CH₃CH₃, giving ethane; this is hydrogenation.
[chem25-s1.0.7] Addition polymerisation, e.g. ethene to poly(ethene), is a series of addition reactions, because each monomer (small molecule) adds on to the growing chain. It is taught in full later in this subsection.
[chem25-s1.1.b] In a substitution reaction one atom or group is swapped for another, so two reactants give two products. Alkanes, which have no double bond to add across, react this way.
[chem25-s1.1.0] A substitution reaction is one in which an atom or group of atoms in a molecule is replaced by another atom or group of atoms; two reactants give two products.
[chem25-s1.1.1] Chlorination of an alkane in ultraviolet light: CH₄ + Cl₂ → CH₃Cl + HCl, where a hydrogen atom is replaced by a chlorine atom.
[chem25-s1.1.2] Ethane gives chloroethane in the same way: C₂H₆ + Cl₂ → C₂H₅Cl + HCl.
[chem25-s1.1.3] Esterification: a carboxylic acid (contains –COOH) and an alcohol (contains –OH) give an ester and water, catalysed by H⁺ from concentrated sulfuric acid, e.g. CH₃COOH + C₂H₅OH ⇌ CH₃COOC₂H₅ + H₂O.
[chem25-s1.1.4] In esterification the –OH of the acid is replaced by the –OC₂H₅ group of the alcohol, which is why it counts as substitution.
[chem25-s1.1.5] Esterification is classed as substitution; it is also called a condensation reaction, because water is lost as two molecules join.
[chem25-s1.1.6] Hydrolysis of an ester (splitting it using water or a base) is also a substitution: the –OR group is replaced by –OH (or by –O⁻ in base), e.g. CH₃COOC₂H₅ + NaOH → CH₃COONa + C₂H₅OH.
[chem25-s1.1.7] Alkanes (saturated hydrocarbons) react by substitution, not addition, because they have no double bond to add across.
[chem25-s1.2.b] Elimination is the reverse of addition. A small molecule is stripped out of a larger one, and the larger molecule is left with a double bond.
[chem25-s1.2.0] An elimination reaction is one in which a small molecule is removed from a larger molecule, leaving a double bond in the larger molecule.
[chem25-s1.2.1] Dehydration of ethanol over heated aluminium oxide (a white solid, used as the catalyst): CH₃CH₂OH → CH₂=CH₂ + H₂O.
[chem25-s1.2.2] Water is the small molecule removed, so this elimination is also called a dehydration.
[chem25-s1.2.3] Propan-2-ol gives propene and water: CH₃CH(OH)CH₃ → CH₃CH=CH₂ + H₂O.
[chem25-s1.2.4] A haloalkane (an alkane with a halogen atom such as Cl) can lose a hydrogen halide: CH₃CH₂Cl → CH₂=CH₂ + HCl.
[chem25-s1.2.5] 1,2-dichloroethane loses HCl to give chloroethene (CH₂=CHCl), the monomer of PVC (the small molecule that is later joined into the polymer PVC).
[chem25-s1.2.6] Elimination is the reverse of addition: one reactant gives two products, and a saturated compound becomes unsaturated.
[chem25-s1.3.b] Redox means reduction and oxidation happening together. In organic chemistry you can usually spot them by counting oxygen and hydrogen atoms.
[chem25-s1.3.0] In organic chemistry, oxidation is the gain of oxygen or the loss of hydrogen.
[chem25-s1.3.1] Reduction is the gain of hydrogen or the loss of oxygen.
[chem25-s1.3.2] Oxidation is also the loss of electrons and an increase in oxidation number (the charge an atom would have if all its bonds were ionic). There is more on this in Oxidation and reduction.
[chem25-s1.3.3] Ethanol is oxidised to ethanal, which loses two H atoms, and then to ethanoic acid, which gains one O atom, using acidified potassium permanganate as the oxidising agent.
[chem25-s1.3.4] Ethanal is reduced to ethanol by hydrogen with a nickel catalyst: CH₃CHO + H₂ → CH₃CH₂OH. This is reduction because the molecule gains hydrogen.
[chem25-s1.3.5] Combustion (burning in oxygen) is a redox reaction in which the carbon and hydrogen are oxidised, e.g. CH₄ + 2O₂ → CO₂ + 2H₂O.
[chem25-s1.4.b] An acid–base reaction is about a proton moving from one species to another. Carboxylic acids show this clearly, while alcohols are barely acidic.
[chem25-s1.4.0] An acid–base reaction is a transfer of a proton (H⁺) from an acid to a base, as defined by Brønsted–Lowry.
[chem25-s1.4.1] Carboxylic acids react with bases: CH₃COOH + NaOH → CH₃COONa + H₂O.
[chem25-s1.4.2] Carboxylic acids react with carbonates: 2CH₃COOH + Na₂CO₃ → 2CH₃COONa + H₂O + CO₂.
[chem25-s1.4.3] Base hydrolysis of esters, including the saponification of fats to make soap, is classed by the specification as an acid–base reaction: the hydroxide ion is the base, and the carboxylic acid formed ends up as its salt.
[chem25-s1.4.4] Alcohols are only very weakly acidic: ethanol is neutral to indicator and does not react with sodium carbonate.
[chem25-s1.4.5] Ethanol does release hydrogen with sodium, forming sodium ethoxide: 2C₂H₅OH + 2Na → 2C₂H₅ONa + H₂; there is more on this in Physical properties and acidity.
[chem25-s1.5.b] At Higher Level you must be able to look at an unfamiliar reaction and say which type or types it is. Work through the checks in a fixed order.
[chem25-s1.5.0] Compare the molecules before and after: two reactants giving one product across a C=C bond is addition; one reactant giving two products with a new C=C bond is elimination; two reactants giving two products with one group swapped for another is substitution.
[chem25-s1.5.1] Then check for redox, by asking whether the organic compound has gained or lost O or H (whether an oxidation number has changed), and for acid–base, by asking whether a proton has moved from an acid to a base.
[chem25-s1.5.2] One reaction can be of more than one type, so give every type that fits: hydrogenation of ethene is addition and reduction.
[chem25-s1.5.3] Esterification is substitution and condensation, and base hydrolysis of an ester is substitution and acid–base.
[chem25-s1.6.b] Higher Level questions also ask which bonds break and form. A pi bond is the second, weaker bond of a C=C double bond, added to the stronger C–C sigma bond that is already there.
[chem25-s1.6.0] Dehydration of ethanol: a C–H bond and a C–O bond are broken and an O–H bond forms in the water; a C=C double bond forms, with a pi bond added to the existing C–C sigma bond.
[chem25-s1.6.1] Addition of water to ethene: the pi bond of C=C and an O–H bond of water are broken; a C–H bond and a C–O bond are formed.
[chem25-s1.6.2] Ethanol to ethanal is oxidation: an O–H bond and a C–H bond are broken and a C=O pi bond forms.
[chem25-s1.6.3] Ethanal to ethanoic acid is oxidation by gain of an O atom: a C–H bond is replaced by C–OH.
[chem25-s1.6.4] Reaction of a carboxylic acid with magnesium shows acidic behaviour but is a redox reaction: Mg is oxidised to Mg²⁺ and H⁺ is reduced to H₂.
[chem25-s1.6.5] The reaction of sodium with ethanol is likewise a redox reaction.
[chem25-s1.7.b] Many everyday plastics are built by joining huge numbers of small molecules into one very long molecule. The words polymer, monomer and repeating unit describe this.
[chem25-s1.7.0] A polymer is a very large molecule, a macromolecule, made of many small units called monomers joined by covalent bonds in a long chain.
[chem25-s1.7.1] A monomer is a small molecule that joins with many others to form a polymer; polymerisation is the reaction in which monomers join to form a polymer.
[chem25-s1.7.2] The repeating unit is the part of the polymer whose repetition makes up the whole chain, apart from the ends.
[chem25-s1.7.3] Polymer chains contain thousands of repeating units, so their relative molecular masses (Mr, the mass of a molecule compared with the carbon-12 standard) are very large and vary from chain to chain.
[chem25-s1.7.4] Example: a poly(ethene) chain of Mr 28 000 contains about 28 000 ÷ 28 = 1000 ethene units, because the Mr of ethene is 28.
[chem25-s1.7.5] Many plastics are polymers, and most addition polymers are made from monomers obtained from crude oil.
[chem25-s1.8.b] Monomers with a C=C double bond can join end to end because each double bond opens up, just as in an ordinary addition reaction. Repeating this thousands of times builds the chain.
[chem25-s1.8.0] An addition polymer is formed when monomers containing a C=C double bond join by addition reactions; usually only one type of monomer is used.
[chem25-s1.8.1] In each addition the pi bond of C=C (the weaker, second bond of the double bond) breaks, and the electrons form new C–C single bonds to neighbouring monomers, giving a long chain of carbon atoms, the backbone.
[chem25-s1.8.2] No other product is formed, so the polymer has the same empirical formula (simplest whole-number ratio of atoms) as the monomer.
[chem25-s1.8.3] General equation: n CH₂=CHX → –(CH₂–CHX)ₙ–, where X is H, Cl or C₆H₅.
[chem25-s1.8.4] The polymer is saturated, with no C=C left, so unlike its monomer it does not decolourise bromine water.
[chem25-s1.8.5] Drawing: show the monomer with its C=C; show the repeating unit with single bonds and a bond sticking out at each end.
[chem25-s1.8.6] To draw two repeating units, show four backbone carbons in a row, with the side groups on alternate carbons and an open bond at each end; do not write atoms at the ends of the open bonds.
[chem25-s1.9.b] Poly(ethene) is the simplest addition polymer. How closely its chains pack decides whether it is soft and flexible or strong and rigid.
[chem25-s1.9.0] The monomer is ethene, CH₂=CH₂, and the repeating unit is –CH₂–CH₂–; poly(ethene) is also called polythene or polyethylene.
[chem25-s1.9.1] Low-density poly(ethene), LDPE, has branched chains that cannot pack closely, so it is soft and flexible.
[chem25-s1.9.2] LDPE is used for plastic bags, food-wrapping film and squeezy bottles.
[chem25-s1.9.3] High-density poly(ethene), HDPE, has unbranched chains that pack closely, so it is stronger and more rigid, with a higher melting point.
[chem25-s1.9.4] HDPE is used for milk crates, bottles, buckets, bowls, toys, pipes and car fuel tanks.
[chem25-s1.9.5] Diagram: two repeating units of poly(ethene), –CH₂–CH₂–CH₂–CH₂–, with open end bonds.
[chem25-s1.10.b] PVC is ethene with one hydrogen replaced by chlorine. The polar C–Cl bond gives it very different properties from poly(ethene).
[chem25-s1.10.0] The monomer is chloroethene, also called vinyl chloride, CH₂=CHCl; the repeating unit is –CH₂–CHCl–.
[chem25-s1.10.1] Poly(chloroethene) is also called polyvinyl chloride, PVC.
[chem25-s1.10.2] The polar C–Cl bonds (the shared electrons sit nearer the chlorine) give permanent dipole–dipole attractions between the chains, so rigid PVC is strong and rigid.
[chem25-s1.10.3] Rigid PVC is used for window frames, doors, gutters, drainpipes, water pipes and bank cards.
[chem25-s1.10.4] Adding plasticisers, small molecules that let the chains slide past each other, makes flexible PVC.
[chem25-s1.10.5] Flexible PVC is used for electrical cable insulation, flooring and raincoats.
[chem25-s1.10.6] Diagram: two repeating units of PVC, –CH₂–CHCl–CH₂–CHCl–, with open end bonds.
[chem25-s1.11.b] Polystyrene is ethene with one hydrogen replaced by a large benzene ring (a ring of six carbon atoms). The bulk of that ring controls the properties.
[chem25-s1.11.0] The monomer is phenylethene, also called styrene, C₆H₅CH=CH₂, which is ethene with one hydrogen replaced by a benzene ring; the repeating unit is –CH₂–CH(C₆H₅)–.
[chem25-s1.11.1] The bulky benzene rings stop the chains moving past each other, so solid polystyrene is rigid and brittle.
[chem25-s1.11.2] Solid polystyrene is used for yoghurt cartons, disposable cups and cutlery, CD cases and ballpoint pen cases.
[chem25-s1.11.3] Expanded polystyrene is blown with gas into a foam, which makes it very light and a good thermal insulator.
[chem25-s1.11.4] Expanded polystyrene is used for packaging, takeaway food boxes, ceiling tiles and building insulation.
[chem25-s1.11.5] Diagram: two repeating units of polystyrene, with a benzene ring on alternate carbon atoms.
[chem25-s1.12.b] Addition polymers are used everywhere because their properties suit the job. The same few polymers turn up in fuel systems, farming, food packaging and the home.
[chem25-s1.12.0] Fuels: HDPE fuel tanks in cars are light, tough and do not corrode; plastic fuel cans and pipes are also used.
[chem25-s1.12.1] Waste plastics can be burned for energy, or broken down by heating (pyrolysis) into liquid fuels.
[chem25-s1.12.2] Food production: poly(ethene) film is used for polytunnels and greenhouses, silage wrap and mulch film on farms.
[chem25-s1.12.3] PVC and poly(ethene) pipes are used for irrigation and water supply.
[chem25-s1.12.4] Food packaging: poly(ethene) film and bags and polystyrene trays keep food clean and moist and keep air out, so food lasts longer and less is wasted.
[chem25-s1.12.5] Household products: buckets, bowls, bottles, bags, toys and containers are made from poly(ethene); pipes, gutters and window frames from PVC; insulation and packaging from polystyrene.
[chem25-s1.12.6] These polymers suit their uses because they are cheap, light, waterproof, durable, easily moulded and unreactive, so they do not react with food, water, fuels or most chemicals.
[chem25-s1.13.b] Biodegradability depends on whether living things can break a substance down. Addition polymers resist this because of their bonding.
[chem25-s1.13.0] A substance is biodegradable if microorganisms, bacteria and fungi, can break it down into simple substances such as carbon dioxide and water.
[chem25-s1.13.1] Addition polymers are non-biodegradable.
[chem25-s1.13.2] The backbone of an addition polymer is a long chain of strong, non-polar C–C single bonds with C–H bonds around it.
[chem25-s1.13.3] Like an alkane, an addition polymer has no pi bonds and no polar functional group (a reactive atom or group) for a reagent to attack, so it is chemically stable and unreactive. There is more on this in Groups of hydrocarbons and their properties.
[chem25-s1.13.4] Microorganisms break down natural polymers, such as starch and proteins, with enzymes (biological catalysts) that attack specific polar linkages.
[chem25-s1.13.5] Addition polymers have no such linkages, so microorganisms have no enzymes able to break them down.
[chem25-s1.14.b] The stability that makes addition polymers so useful is the same property that makes plastic waste a problem.
[chem25-s1.14.0] The same stability that makes addition polymers useful, being durable, waterproof and resistant to chemicals and decay, means they persist in the environment for hundreds of years.
[chem25-s1.14.1] Consequences: plastic litter on land and in oceans, harm to wildlife that eats or becomes tangled in it, and filling of landfill sites.
[chem25-s1.14.2] Sunlight and wear break plastics into small fragments, called microplastics, rather than breaking them down chemically.
[chem25-s1.15.b] Because plastics do not break down, society has to manage them: use less, recycle, burn for energy, or switch to polymers that can degrade.
[chem25-s1.15.0] Responses include reducing use, reusing, and recycling.
[chem25-s1.15.1] Plastics are sorted for recycling by resin identification code, e.g. 2 for HDPE, 3 for PVC, 4 for LDPE and 6 for polystyrene.
[chem25-s1.15.2] Incineration gives energy but releases CO₂, and burning PVC releases hydrogen chloride.
[chem25-s1.15.3] Biodegradable polymers with ester linkages, which microorganisms can hydrolyse (split using water), are being developed. These can break down because the ester linkage gives microorganisms something to attack.
[chem25-s1.15.4] Poly(lactic acid), PLA, is one example: it is made from plant starch and breaks down in industrial composting.

CANDIDATES chem24-s2: 2. Physical properties and acidity
[chem24-s2.0.b] When a solid melts or a liquid boils, the molecules move apart from one another but stay intact. So the energy needed depends on the attractions between molecules, and three kinds of attraction matter for organic compounds.
[chem24-s2.0.0] Melting and boiling separate molecules, so they depend on the intermolecular forces (the attractions between separate molecules), not on the covalent bonds (shared electron pairs) inside the molecules.
[chem24-s2.0.1] The stronger the forces, the more energy is needed to pull the molecules apart, so the higher the melting and boiling points.
[chem24-s2.0.2] London dispersion forces are the weak attractions between all molecules, polar or not. They grow stronger as a molecule has more electrons and more surface.
[chem24-s2.0.3] Permanent dipole–dipole forces act between polar molecules. A polar bond is one where the shared electrons sit nearer the more electronegative atom (the atom that pulls harder on shared electrons), leaving one end δ+ and the other δ−.
[chem24-s2.0.4] Hydrogen bonding is a strong attraction between an H bonded to O, N or F and a lone pair (a non-bonding pair of outer electrons) on O, N or F of a neighbouring molecule. The order of strength is London forces only, then dipole–dipole, then hydrogen bonding.
[chem24-s2.1.b] Which forces a compound has depends on its family, meaning the functional group (the atom or group that gives a family its properties) and whether that group has polar bonds. None of these families can hydrogen bond to its own molecules.
[chem24-s2.1.0] Alkanes and alkenes (hydrocarbons, made only of C and H) are non-polar, so they have London dispersion forces only.
[chem24-s2.1.1] Haloalkanes (alkanes with a halogen such as Cl in place of an H) have a polar C–Cl bond (Cl 3.16, C 2.55 on the electronegativity scale), so they have permanent dipole–dipole forces as well as London forces.
[chem24-s2.1.2] Haloalkanes cannot hydrogen bond, because they have no H bonded to O, N or F.
[chem24-s2.1.3] Aldehydes and ketones (compounds containing the C=O carbonyl group) have a strongly polar C=O bond (Cδ+=Oδ−), giving dipole–dipole forces.
[chem24-s2.1.4] Aldehydes and ketones cannot hydrogen bond with each other, because their hydrogens are bonded to carbon, not oxygen.
[chem24-s2.1.5] Esters have polar C=O and C–O bonds, giving dipole–dipole forces, but no O–H, so there is no hydrogen bonding between ester molecules.
[chem24-s2.2.b] Alcohols and carboxylic acids both contain an O–H group, so unlike the families above they can hydrogen bond to their own kind.
[chem24-s2.2.0] Alcohols have a very polar O–H bond (O 3.44, H 2.20), so alcohol molecules hydrogen bond to each other as well as having London forces.
[chem24-s2.2.1] Carboxylic acids have both O–H and C=O in the –COOH group, so their molecules hydrogen bond strongly.
[chem24-s2.2.2] In the pure liquid, carboxylic acid molecules pair up as dimers (two molecules joined together) held by two hydrogen bonds, the O–H of each molecule bonding to the C=O of the other.
[chem24-s2.2.3] Diagram: two ethanoic acid molecules facing each other, with dashed hydrogen bonds from each O–H hydrogen to the C=O oxygen of the other molecule, forming a dimer.
[chem24-s2.3.b] Size also affects boiling point, because bigger molecules have stronger London forces. To compare the type of force fairly, you need compounds with about the same relative molecular mass (Mr, the mass of a molecule compared with carbon-12).
[chem24-s2.3.0] At similar relative molecular mass, so with similar London forces, boiling point depends on the type of intermolecular force: London forces only give the lowest boiling point, then dipole–dipole forces, then hydrogen bonding.
[chem24-s2.3.1] Butane (Mr 58) boils at −1 °C, propanal (Mr 58) at 48 °C and propanone (Mr 58) at 56 °C.
[chem24-s2.3.2] Methyl methanoate (Mr 60) boils at 32 °C, propan-1-ol (Mr 60) at 97 °C and ethanoic acid (Mr 60) at 118 °C.
[chem24-s2.3.3] Propanal boils far higher than butane because its polar C=O gives dipole–dipole forces in addition to London forces.
[chem24-s2.3.4] Propan-1-ol boils higher than propanal because its O–H groups form hydrogen bonds between molecules, which are stronger than dipole–dipole forces.
[chem24-s2.3.5] Ethanoic acid boils higher than propan-1-ol because each acid molecule has two oxygens taking part, and pairs of molecules form doubly hydrogen-bonded dimers, so more hydrogen bonding must be overcome.
[chem24-s2.4.b] Two compounds can have the same mass, or even the same formula, and still boil at very different temperatures. The difference comes down to whether hydrogen bonding is possible.
[chem24-s2.4.0] Ethanoic acid (bp 118 °C) and methyl methanoate (bp 32 °C) are isomers (different compounds with the same formula), both C₂H₄O₂, and have the same mass.
[chem24-s2.4.1] The acid hydrogen bonds to other acid molecules, but the ester cannot, as it has no O–H.
[chem24-s2.4.2] Ethanol (bp 78 °C) boils far higher than ethane (bp −89 °C) because ethanol has hydrogen bonding, whereas ethane has London forces only.
[chem24-s2.5.b] Within one family the functional group stays the same, so what changes boiling point is chain length. A homologous series (a family whose members differ by one CH₂ unit) shows this clearly.
[chem24-s2.5.0] Within a homologous series, boiling point rises with chain length, because each extra CH₂ adds electrons and surface area, so London forces get stronger.
[chem24-s2.5.1] Alcohols: methanol 65 °C; ethanol 78 °C; propan-1-ol 97 °C; butan-1-ol 117 °C; pentan-1-ol 138 °C; hexan-1-ol 157 °C, a rise of about 20 °C per CH₂.
[chem24-s2.5.2] Carboxylic acids: methanoic acid 101 °C; ethanoic acid 118 °C; propanoic acid 141 °C; butanoic acid 164 °C.
[chem24-s2.5.3] Aldehydes: methanal −19 °C, a gas; ethanal 20 °C; propanal 48 °C; butanal 75 °C.
[chem24-s2.6.b] Haloalkanes follow the same trend, and adding halogens has its own effect. A longer chain also makes the families more alike.
[chem24-s2.6.0] Chloroalkanes: chloromethane −24 °C and chloroethane 12 °C are gases at room temperature.
[chem24-s2.6.1] Chloroalkanes: 1-chloropropane 47 °C and 1-chlorobutane 78 °C are liquids.
[chem24-s2.6.2] Adding more chlorines also raises the boiling point: CH₃Cl −24 °C; CH₂Cl₂ 40 °C; CHCl₃ 61 °C; CCl₄ 77 °C, because more electrons give stronger London forces.
[chem24-s2.6.3] As the chain gets longer, the hydrocarbon part makes up more of the molecule, so the difference between families gets smaller, because the effect of the functional group is diluted.
[chem24-s2.7.b] Molecules with the same formula can have different shapes. A branched molecule is more compact than a straight chain, and that changes how strongly molecules attract one another.
[chem24-s2.7.0] Branched isomers have lower boiling points than straight-chain isomers, because they are more compact, with less surface contact between molecules, so London forces are weaker. There is more on this in Groups of hydrocarbons and their properties.
[chem24-s2.7.1] Isomers of C₄H₉OH: butan-1-ol 117 °C; 2-methylpropan-1-ol 108 °C; butan-2-ol 99 °C; 2-methylpropan-2-ol 82 °C.
[chem24-s2.7.2] The more compact the molecule, and the more crowded the –OH, the lower the boiling point.
[chem24-s2.8.b] Exams give a table or graph of boiling point against number of carbons and ask for the trend, the reason and a prediction. A fixed three-step method covers all three.
[chem24-s2.8.0] Step 1: from a table or graph of boiling point against number of carbons, describe the trend, for example that boiling point rises as the number of carbons rises.
[chem24-s2.8.1] Step 2: explain the trend in terms of chain length, London forces and the functional group.
[chem24-s2.8.2] Step 3: predict the next member by continuing the trend, e.g. continuing the primary alcohols from butan-1-ol, 117 °C, gives about 138 °C for pentan-1-ol.
[chem24-s2.9.b] Melting also depends on intermolecular forces, but a solid is an ordered packing of molecules. So how neatly the molecules fit together matters too.
[chem24-s2.9.0] Melting points depend on the intermolecular forces and also on how well the molecules pack together in the solid, so they follow trends less regularly than boiling points.
[chem24-s2.9.1] Ethanoic acid melts at 17 °C, high for its size, because of strong hydrogen bonding.
[chem24-s2.9.2] On a cold day, pure ethanoic acid freezes to an ice-like solid, so it is called glacial ethanoic acid.
[chem24-s2.9.3] Compact, symmetrical molecules pack well and melt higher: 2-methylpropan-2-ol melts at about 25 °C, but butan-1-ol melts at −89 °C.
[chem24-s2.9.4] Cyclohexane (a ring of six carbons) melts at 6.5 °C, but hexane (the straight chain) melts at −95 °C, again because the compact ring packs better.
[chem24-s2.9.5] Ionic bonding (attraction between oppositely charged ions) gives far higher melting points: sodium ethanoate, an ionic salt of ethanoic acid, is a solid that melts at about 324 °C.
[chem24-s2.10.b] Whether a compound dissolves depends on whether it can attract the solvent (the liquid doing the dissolving) as strongly as it attracts itself. For water, that means hydrogen bonding.
[chem24-s2.10.0] Like dissolves like: a compound dissolves in water if it can form hydrogen bonds with water molecules.
[chem24-s2.10.1] Non-polar compounds dissolve in non-polar solvents such as cyclohexane, where London forces are similar.
[chem24-s2.10.2] Lower alcohols (methanol, ethanol, propan-1-ol and propan-2-ol) are miscible with water, mixing in all proportions, because their –OH groups hydrogen bond with water.
[chem24-s2.10.3] Lower carboxylic acids, from methanoic acid to butanoic acid, are also miscible with water, for the same reason.
[chem24-s2.10.4] Solubility in water falls as the chain gets longer, because the non-polar hydrocarbon part cannot hydrogen bond and disrupts the hydrogen bonding of the water.
[chem24-s2.10.5] Butan-1-ol is only partly soluble in water, and hexan-1-ol is only slightly soluble.
[chem24-s2.10.6] Longer chains dissolve better in non-polar solvents.
[chem24-s2.11.b] These families cannot hydrogen bond to their own molecules, yet small ones still dissolve in water. The reason is that water can hydrogen bond to them.
[chem24-s2.11.0] Small aldehydes and ketones, methanal, ethanal and propanone, are miscible with water: they have no O–H, but water molecules hydrogen bond to the lone pairs on their C=O oxygen.
[chem24-s2.11.1] Propanone dissolves both polar and non-polar substances, so it is a useful solvent.
[chem24-s2.11.2] Butanone is not fully miscible with water; only the smallest ketone, propanone, is.
[chem24-s2.11.3] Small esters are slightly soluble in water for the same reason, as water hydrogen bonds to their oxygens: about 8 g of ethyl ethanoate dissolves in 100 g of water at room temperature.
[chem24-s2.11.4] Small esters dissolve well in organic solvents.
[chem24-s2.12.b] Compounds with no way to hydrogen bond with water stay out of it, while ionic compounds are pulled in by water's charges. These cases explain common solvents and a purification method.
[chem24-s2.12.0] Alkanes, alkenes, benzene and haloalkanes are insoluble in water, as they cannot hydrogen bond with it, but they dissolve in non-polar solvents.
[chem24-s2.12.1] Haloalkanes are good solvents for grease and oil.
[chem24-s2.12.2] Benzoic acid is only slightly soluble in cold water because of its large non-polar benzene ring, but it is much more soluble in hot water, which allows it to be purified by recrystallisation (dissolving in hot water, then cooling so pure crystals form).
[chem24-s2.12.3] There is more on this in Investigation: synthesising benzoic acid.
[chem24-s2.12.4] Ionic salts of carboxylic acids, sodium ethanoate and sodium benzoate, are much more soluble in water than the acids, because their ions are attracted to water molecules (ion–dipole attraction).
[chem24-s2.13.b] Alcohols and carboxylic acids both contain an O–H group, yet carboxylic acids are far more acidic. To see why, first look at why an O–H hydrogen can be lost at all.
[chem24-s2.13.0] An acid, in the Brønsted–Lowry sense, is a proton donor; both alcohols and carboxylic acids can donate the H⁺ of their O–H group.
[chem24-s2.13.1] The O–H bond is polar because oxygen (3.44) is much more electronegative than hydrogen (2.20), so the shared electrons are pulled towards oxygen.
[chem24-s2.13.2] This leaves the hydrogen δ+, so it can be removed as H⁺ by a base (a proton acceptor).
[chem24-s2.13.3] C–H bonds are almost non-polar (C 2.55, H 2.20), so hydrogens bonded to carbon are not acidic.
[chem24-s2.13.4] In ethanoic acid, CH₃COOH, only the hydrogen on oxygen is lost.
[chem24-s2.13.5] How acidic the O–H is depends mainly on how stable the negative ion (the conjugate base, what is left after H⁺ leaves) is.
[chem24-s2.13.6] The more stable the negative ion, the more easily H⁺ is lost and the stronger the acid.
[chem24-s2.14.b] An alcohol can lose H⁺, but the ion it leaves behind is unstable. This is why alcohols are such weak acids.
[chem24-s2.14.0] An alcohol can lose H⁺ to form an alkoxide ion: R–OH ⇌ R–O⁻ + H⁺. Ethanol gives the ethoxide ion, C₂H₅O⁻.
[chem24-s2.14.1] In the alkoxide ion the negative charge is concentrated on one oxygen atom, because it cannot be spread out by resonance (sharing the charge over more than one atom).
[chem24-s2.14.2] The alkoxide ion is therefore unstable and readily takes back H⁺.
[chem24-s2.14.3] The alkyl group (a chain of carbons and hydrogens) is electron-releasing, pushing electron density towards the oxygen, which makes the negative charge on the alkoxide ion even less stable.
[chem24-s2.14.4] Alcohols are therefore weaker acids than water.
[chem24-s2.15.b] Because the alkoxide ion is so unstable, alcohols behave as neutral in water. Their acidic O–H shows up in the reaction with sodium metal.
[chem24-s2.15.0] Alcohols are neutral in water: they do not change the colour of indicators, do not react with sodium carbonate, and do not react to any useful extent with sodium hydroxide.
[chem24-s2.15.1] Alcohols react with sodium metal, giving hydrogen gas and a salt, which is evidence of their acidic O–H.
[chem24-s2.15.2] Equation: 2C₂H₅OH(l) + 2Na(s) → 2C₂H₅ONa + H₂(g), forming sodium ethoxide.
[chem24-s2.15.3] The reaction of ethanol with sodium is gentler than the reaction of sodium with water, showing that ethanol is a weaker acid than water.
[chem24-s2.16.b] A carboxylic acid loses H⁺ much more easily than an alcohol, and the reason lies in the ion it leaves behind. Its negative charge is not stuck on one atom.
[chem24-s2.16.0] A carboxylic acid loses H⁺ to form a carboxylate ion: RCOOH(aq) ⇌ RCOO⁻(aq) + H⁺(aq).
[chem24-s2.16.1] Ethanoic acid gives the ethanoate ion: CH₃COOH ⇌ CH₃COO⁻ + H⁺.
[chem24-s2.16.2] In the carboxylate ion the negative charge is delocalised (spread out), shared over both oxygen atoms through the O–C–O group.
[chem24-s2.16.3] The carboxylate ion is a resonance hybrid (a blend) of two structures, in each of which a different oxygen carries the negative charge.
[chem24-s2.17.b] Bond lengths show that the charge really is shared, and the sharing is what stabilises the ion. This is why a carboxylic acid is a much stronger acid than an alcohol.
[chem24-s2.17.0] The two C–O bonds in a carboxylate ion are the same length, between the length of a C–O single bond and that of a C=O double bond.
[chem24-s2.17.1] In the acid itself, the C=O bond is shorter than the C–O bond.
[chem24-s2.17.2] Spreading the charge over two oxygens stabilises the carboxylate ion.
[chem24-s2.17.3] So a carboxylic acid loses H⁺ much more readily than an alcohol, whose alkoxide ion has no such stabilisation.
[chem24-s2.17.4] Diagram: the two resonance structures of the ethanoate ion, CH₃–C(=O)–O⁻ ↔ CH₃–C(–O⁻)=O, joined by a double-headed arrow, and the hybrid drawn with a dashed bond over O–C–O and a charge of −½ on each oxygen.
[chem24-s2.18.b] A second effect adds to resonance. Atoms in a molecule pull on electrons along the bonds, and this changes how easily the O–H hydrogen leaves.
[chem24-s2.18.0] The inductive effect is the pulling (or pushing) of electron density along sigma bonds (the single bonds that join atoms) by an atom or group, because of its electronegativity.
[chem24-s2.18.1] In the –COOH group, the electronegative oxygen of the C=O pulls electron density away from the O–H bond.
[chem24-s2.18.2] This makes the O–H more polar and the hydrogen easier to lose than in an alcohol.
[chem24-s2.18.3] Electron-withdrawing groups such as chlorine, attached to the carbon chain, pull electron density towards themselves.
[chem24-s2.18.4] This spreads out the negative charge of the carboxylate ion and stabilises it, so the acid is stronger.
[chem24-s2.18.5] More chlorine atoms, or chlorine nearer the –COOH, give a stronger effect.
[chem24-s2.19.b] Alkyl groups have the opposite effect to chlorine, so they weaken an acid. The Ka values (the acid dissociation constant: the larger the Ka, the stronger the acid) confirm the order.
[chem24-s2.19.0] Alkyl groups are electron-releasing; they push electron density towards the –COO⁻ group, destabilising it, so the acid is weaker.
[chem24-s2.19.1] Methanoic acid, with H on the –COOH, is stronger than ethanoic acid, with CH₃ on the –COOH, which is slightly stronger than propanoic acid.
[chem24-s2.19.2] Ka data at 25 °C: methanoic acid 1.8 × 10⁻⁴; ethanoic acid 1.8 × 10⁻⁵; propanoic acid 1.3 × 10⁻⁵.
[chem24-s2.19.3] Ka data at 25 °C: chloroethanoic acid 1.4 × 10⁻³; trichloroethanoic acid about 0.2.
[chem24-s2.19.4] Order of strength: CCl₃COOH > CH₂ClCOOH > HCOOH > CH₃COOH > CH₃CH₂COOH.
[chem24-s2.20.b] Carboxylic acids are much more acidic than alcohols, but they are still weak acids. A weak acid dissociates (splits into H⁺ and its negative ion) only partly, and a short calculation shows how little.
[chem24-s2.20.0] Carboxylic acids are weak acids, only partly dissociated.
[chem24-s2.20.1] Step 1: the degree of dissociation (the fraction of molecules that lose H⁺) is approximately √(Ka ÷ concentration) = √(1.8 × 10⁻⁵ ÷ 0.1) = 0.013, so about 1.3% of 0.1 mol L⁻¹ ethanoic acid is dissociated.
[chem24-s2.20.2] Step 2: [H⁺] = 0.013 × 0.1 mol L⁻¹ = 1.3 × 10⁻³ mol L⁻¹.
[chem24-s2.20.3] Step 3: pH = −log₁₀[H⁺] = −log₁₀(1.3 × 10⁻³) ≈ 2.9.
[chem24-s2.20.4] Carboxylic acids are still far stronger acids than alcohols, because of the resonance-stabilised carboxylate ion and the inductive effect of the C=O.
[chem24-s2.20.5] Ethanol is a much weaker acid, with Ka of the order of 10⁻¹⁶ (pKa about 16), weaker than water.
[chem24-s2.21.b] The difference in acidity shows up in everyday reactions. Carboxylic acids give the typical reactions of an acid, and alcohols mostly do not.
[chem24-s2.21.0] Carboxylic acids show typical acid properties that alcohols do not: they turn blue litmus red, fizz with sodium carbonate giving carbon dioxide, and react with magnesium giving hydrogen.
[chem24-s2.21.1] Carboxylic acids are neutralised by sodium hydroxide, e.g. CH₃COOH + NaOH → CH₃COONa + H₂O. There is more on this in Tests, reaction schemes and preparing an ester.
[chem24-s2.21.2] Sodium metal: an alcohol reacts, giving H₂; an acid reacts, giving H₂.
[chem24-s2.21.3] Sodium hydroxide: an alcohol has no useful reaction; an acid is neutralised.
[chem24-s2.21.4] Sodium carbonate: an alcohol does not react; an acid fizzes, giving CO₂.
[chem24-s2.21.5] Indicator: an alcohol is neutral; an acid is acidic.
[chem24-s2.22.b] Every acidity question has the same logic: the more stable the negative ion left behind, the stronger the acid. A two-step answer follows it.
[chem24-s2.22.0] Step 1 of explaining a difference in acidity: say which anion is more stable and why, referring to resonance and to electron-withdrawing or electron-releasing groups.
[chem24-s2.22.1] Step 2 of explaining a difference in acidity: conclude which acid loses H⁺ more easily.
