TARGET chem23-s1: 1. Hydrocarbons: sources, uses and impact
[chem23-s1.0.b] Organic chemistry is the study of the compounds of carbon, and hydrocarbons are the simplest of them. Knowing what a hydrocarbon is gives you the starting point for every other organic family.
[chem23-s1.0.0] Carbon monoxide, carbon dioxide, carbonates and hydrogencarbonates contain carbon but are classed as inorganic, so they are exceptions to the rule that carbon compounds are organic.
[chem23-s1.0.1] Carbon forms so many compounds because each carbon atom forms four covalent bonds (bonds made by sharing pairs of electrons) and carbon atoms bond strongly to each other. This gives stable chains, straight or branched, and rings of almost any length.
[chem23-s1.0.2] Organic compounds are divided into many groups, or homologous series (families of compounds), each with its own characteristic structure and reactions. There is more on this in Groups of hydrocarbons and their properties.
[chem23-s1.0.3] A hydrocarbon is a compound containing carbon and hydrogen only, which is why hydrocarbons are the simplest organic compounds.
[chem23-s1.0.4] Other organic families, such as alcohols and carboxylic acids, can be thought of as hydrocarbons with one or more hydrogen atoms replaced by other atoms or groups. So hydrocarbons are the basis for understanding and making other organic compounds.
[chem23-s1.1.b] Crude oil, natural gas and coal are the main sources of hydrocarbons, and all three are fossil fuels. They are the starting point for most of the hydrocarbons that industry and society use.
[chem23-s1.1.0] Fossil fuels formed over millions of years from the remains of plants and animals, buried under sediment (layers of mud and sand) and changed by heat and pressure in the absence of air. Crude oil and natural gas formed mainly from tiny marine organisms.
[chem23-s1.1.1] Fossil fuels are finite (non-renewable): they are being used far faster than they form, so they will run out.
[chem23-s1.1.2] Natural gas is mainly methane (CH₄), typically over 80%, with small amounts of ethane, propane and butane.
[chem23-s1.1.3] Crude oil is a mixture of many hydrocarbons, mostly alkanes (hydrocarbons with only single bonds) in chain and ring form. It also holds some aromatic hydrocarbons (ring-shaped hydrocarbons) such as benzene.
[chem23-s1.1.4] Crude oil is separated by fractional distillation (separating a mixture by boiling point) into fractions. Each fraction is a mixture of hydrocarbons with a similar range of boiling points and so of similar molecular size.
[chem23-s1.1.5] The smallest molecules, with the lowest boiling points, are collected at the top of the column and the largest at the bottom. There is more on this in Using the kinetic theory: separation techniques.
[chem23-s1.2.b] Fossil fuels are not the only source of hydrocarbons. Living things make some, and industry and laboratories can build others from different starting materials.
[chem23-s1.2.0] Methane forms when bacteria break down plant and animal material in the absence of air, a process called anaerobic decomposition.
[chem23-s1.2.1] This methane is found in landfill sites (refuse dumps), slurry pits (stores of liquid animal waste), marshes and the digestive systems of cattle and sheep. These are sources of methane that are not linked to the oil and gas industry.
[chem23-s1.2.2] Biogas, made deliberately by anaerobic digestion of farm slurry, food waste or crops, is mainly methane and is a renewable fuel because the plant and animal material can be regrown.
[chem23-s1.2.3] Plants also make hydrocarbons, e.g. natural rubber, which is poly(isoprene) (a hydrocarbon polymer, a very long chain molecule) from the rubber tree, and limonene (C₁₀H₁₆) in citrus-fruit peel oil.
[chem23-s1.2.4] Hydrocarbons can be synthesised (made) from other substances, e.g. ethene is made by dehydrating ethanol (removing water from it). There is more on this in Investigation: preparing ethene.
[chem23-s1.2.5] Ethanol made by fermenting sugar cane (using yeast to turn sugar into ethanol) gives a plant-based, or “bio”, ethene.
[chem23-s1.2.6] The Fischer–Tropsch process makes liquid hydrocarbons from carbon monoxide and hydrogen, so synthetic fuels can be made starting from coal, natural gas or biomass (plant and animal material).
[chem23-s1.3.b] Most hydrocarbons are burned for energy, because they release a lot of energy when they burn in oxygen. Different hydrocarbons and fractions suit different fuel jobs.
[chem23-s1.3.0] Complete combustion (burning with plenty of oxygen) of a hydrocarbon gives carbon dioxide and water. There is more on this in Combustion and heats of combustion.
[chem23-s1.3.1] Methane (natural gas) is used for heating and cooking in homes, and for generating electricity in power stations.
[chem23-s1.3.2] Propane and butane are the main components of LPG (liquefied petroleum gas), sold in cylinders for patio heaters, camping stoves, gas barbecues and some vehicles such as forklift trucks.
[chem23-s1.3.3] Small butane canisters are used in camping stoves and lighters.
[chem23-s1.3.4] Natural gas and LPG have no smell of their own, so a small amount of a strong-smelling sulfur compound, a thiol also called a mercaptan, is added so that leaks can be detected.
[chem23-s1.3.5] Liquid fractions of crude oil are transport fuels: petrol for cars, kerosene for jet aircraft and home heating oil, and diesel (gas oil) for lorries, buses, tractors and cars.
[chem23-s1.3.6] Heavy fuel oil, a thick fraction of crude oil, is used in ships and furnaces.
[chem23-s1.3.7] Ethyne (acetylene) burned in pure oxygen gives a flame above 3000 °C, which is hot enough to be used for cutting and welding metals in the oxyacetylene torch.
[chem23-s1.4.b] A small but important share of hydrocarbons is used as raw materials, or feedstocks (starting materials), for the chemical industry rather than being burned.
[chem23-s1.4.0] Ethene and propene are made into the plastics poly(ethene) and poly(propene). There is more on this in Types of organic reaction and addition polymers.
[chem23-s1.4.1] Ethene is also used to make ethanol and ethane-1,2-diol, the antifreeze, and to ripen fruit, because it is a plant hormone (a chemical that controls plant processes).
[chem23-s1.4.2] Benzene is a feedstock for plastics such as polystyrene, and for detergents, dyes and pharmaceuticals (medicines).
[chem23-s1.4.3] Methane is the main source of hydrogen for making ammonia, which is used to make fertilisers.
[chem23-s1.4.4] Hexane and cyclohexane are used as non-polar solvents (liquids that dissolve non-polar substances such as oils), e.g. hexane to extract vegetable oils from seeds.
[chem23-s1.4.5] The largest molecules give lubricating oils and greases for machinery, paraffin wax for candles, and bitumen, from the residue left at the bottom of the column, for surfacing roads and waterproofing roofs.
[chem23-s1.5.b] The size and bonding of a hydrocarbon decide which use suits it. This links the properties of each group to the uses above.
[chem23-s1.5.0] Small molecules are gases that burn easily, so they make fuels.
[chem23-s1.5.1] Unsaturated alkenes (hydrocarbons with a C=C double bond) react readily, so they make feedstocks for plastics.
[chem23-s1.5.2] Large molecules are viscous (thick and slow-flowing) or solid, so they make lubricants, waxes and bitumen.
[chem23-s1.6.b] The investigation asks for one major impact on society of the continued, extensive use of hydrocarbons, researched from secondary sources (existing reports and data, not your own experiments) and judged on the evidence. You choose one impact and explain the chemistry behind it.
[chem23-s1.6.0] Choose one impact and set a focused question, often about one sector or one country.
[chem23-s1.6.1] Use several reliable sources that agree, judging each for authority (who wrote it), evidence, date and bias, since oil companies and campaign groups may each present one side.
[chem23-s1.6.2] Reliable sources for these topics include peer-reviewed papers (checked by other scientists before publication) and reports of the IPCC (Intergovernmental Panel on Climate Change), the WHO (World Health Organization), the EPA (Environmental Protection Agency, Ireland) and the SEAI (Sustainable Energy Authority of Ireland).
[chem23-s1.6.3] Explain the chemistry with balanced equations, and consider solutions and their limits.
[chem23-s1.6.4] Set every impact against the benefits: hydrocarbons are cheap, energy-dense (a lot of energy in a small mass) and easily transported fuels and raw materials that made modern transport, heating, electricity, plastics and medicines possible.
[chem23-s1.6.5] Justify the conclusion from the evidence, and acknowledge uncertainty and what the sources could not show.
[chem23-s1.7.b] Research question, e.g. “How does burning hydrocarbons for transport in Ireland contribute to climate change, and how could it be reduced?” The chemistry starts with the fact that every hydrocarbon gives carbon dioxide when it burns completely.
[chem23-s1.7.0] Complete combustion of every hydrocarbon releases carbon dioxide, e.g. CH₄(g) + 2O₂(g) → CO₂(g) + 2H₂O(l).
[chem23-s1.7.1] The same happens for octane, a hydrocarbon in petrol: 2C₈H₁₈(l) + 25O₂(g) → 16CO₂(g) + 18H₂O(l).
[chem23-s1.7.2] Scale for 1.00 kg of methane. Moles of CH₄ = mass ÷ molar mass = 1000 g ÷ 16 g mol⁻¹ = 62.5 mol.
[chem23-s1.7.3] The equation gives 1 mol CO₂ for each 1 mol CH₄, so 62.5 mol CO₂ forms. Mass = 62.5 mol × 44 g mol⁻¹ = 2750 g, so 2.75 kg of CO₂ is released.
[chem23-s1.7.4] Scale for 1.00 kg of octane. Moles of C₈H₁₈ = 1000 g ÷ 114 g mol⁻¹ = 8.77 mol.
[chem23-s1.7.5] The equation gives 8 mol CO₂ for each 1 mol C₈H₁₈ (16 for every 2). Mass = 8.77 mol × 8 × 44 g mol⁻¹ = 3088 g, so 3.09 kg of CO₂ is released.
[chem23-s1.8.b] Burning fossil fuels adds carbon dioxide to the air, and the evidence shows its level and the average temperature rising. The extra carbon dioxide traps heat, and that has effects on the climate and the oceans.
[chem23-s1.8.0] Fossil carbon was locked away for millions of years. Burning it moves that carbon into the atmosphere far faster than the carbon cycle (the natural movement of carbon between air, oceans and living things) removes it, so atmospheric CO₂ rises.
[chem23-s1.8.1] CO₂ and methane are greenhouse gases that absorb infrared radiation (heat radiation) emitted by the Earth and so trap heat. There is more on this in The enhanced greenhouse effect.
[chem23-s1.8.2] Methane escapes from gas wells, pipelines and coal mines, which adds to the greenhouse gases in the air.
[chem23-s1.8.3] Atmospheric CO₂ has risen from about 280 ppm (parts per million) before the Industrial Revolution to over 420 ppm today, and global average temperature has risen by more than 1 °C since pre-industrial times.
[chem23-s1.8.4] Consequences include rising sea levels, more extreme weather such as storms, floods and droughts, and ocean acidification (the oceans becoming more acidic) as CO₂ dissolves: CO₂(g) + H₂O(l) ⇌ H₂CO₃(aq).
[chem23-s1.9.b] A good investigation does not stop at the problem. It also assesses how emissions could be cut and what holds each solution back.
[chem23-s1.9.0] Solutions include renewable electricity from wind and solar, electric vehicles and heat pumps (devices that heat buildings using electricity efficiently), energy efficiency, biofuels, hydrogen, and carbon capture and storage (trapping CO₂ before it escapes and storing it).
[chem23-s1.9.1] The limits of these solutions include cost, the supply of materials such as those for batteries, the intermittency of renewables (wind and solar supply stops and starts with the weather), and the time needed to change infrastructure.
[chem23-s1.10.b] Research question, e.g. “How has burning solid fuel and road fuels affected air quality and health in Irish towns?” Burning fuels can release pollutants as well as carbon dioxide, depending on the oxygen supply and the fuel.
[chem23-s1.10.0] Incomplete combustion, with too little oxygen, produces carbon monoxide and soot (tiny particles of carbon), e.g. 2CH₄(g) + 3O₂(g) → 2CO(g) + 4H₂O(l).
[chem23-s1.10.1] Nitrogen and oxygen from the air combine at the high temperatures in engines to form nitrogen oxides: N₂(g) + O₂(g) → 2NO(g); 2NO(g) + O₂(g) → 2NO₂(g).
[chem23-s1.10.2] Sulfur impurities in coal and heavy oils burn to sulfur dioxide: S(s) + O₂(g) → SO₂(g).
[chem23-s1.10.3] Unburned hydrocarbons such as benzene, a carcinogen (a substance that can cause cancer), are released from fuel evaporation and exhausts.
[chem23-s1.11.b] Each pollutant harms the body in a different way, so name the pollutant and its effect together.
[chem23-s1.11.0] Carbon monoxide is toxic: it binds to haemoglobin (the protein in red blood cells that carries oxygen) more strongly than oxygen does, reducing the blood's ability to carry oxygen.
[chem23-s1.11.1] Particulate matter, such as PM2.5 (particles with a diameter below 2.5 μm), from diesel engines and from burning coal and wood is breathed deep into the lungs, because the particles are so small. It is linked to asthma, lung disease and heart disease.
[chem23-s1.11.2] Nitrogen dioxide irritates the lungs and contributes to acid rain and smog.
[chem23-s1.11.3] Sulfur dioxide irritates the airways and causes acid rain.
[chem23-s1.12.b] Real data show whether action on air pollution works. Ireland's smoky coal ban is the evidence to use.
[chem23-s1.12.0] Dublin banned the sale of smoky (bituminous) coal in 1990. Black smoke fell sharply, and later studies linked the ban to fewer deaths from respiratory and heart disease.
[chem23-s1.12.1] The ban on smoky coal was extended to the whole country in October 2022.
[chem23-s1.12.2] Catalytic converters (devices in exhausts that turn harmful gases into less harmful ones), low-sulfur fuels, particulate filters and electric transport also reduce emissions.
[chem23-s1.13.b] Research question, e.g. “What happens to single-use plastic packaging in Ireland, and how could its impact be reduced?” The chemistry explains why plastic waste lasts so long.
[chem23-s1.13.0] Most plastics are made from hydrocarbon feedstocks: ethene and propene are polymerised (joined into long chains) to poly(ethene) and poly(propene). There is more on this in Types of organic reaction and addition polymers.
[chem23-s1.13.1] Addition polymers are long chains held by strong, non-polar C–C and C–H bonds, so microorganisms cannot break them down.
[chem23-s1.13.2] Plastics are therefore non-biodegradable (they cannot be broken down by living organisms) and can persist for hundreds of years.
[chem23-s1.14.b] Because plastics last so long, they build up in the environment and cause harm in several places.
[chem23-s1.14.0] Plastic waste causes litter and fills landfill sites.
[chem23-s1.14.1] Plastic in the sea entangles or is swallowed by marine animals.
[chem23-s1.14.2] Plastics break into microplastics, pieces smaller than 5 mm, which are found in water, food and living things.
[chem23-s1.14.3] Burning plastics recovers energy but releases CO₂, and burning some plastics, such as PVC (poly(chloroethene)), releases toxic gases such as hydrogen chloride unless done in controlled incinerators (furnaces built to burn waste safely).
[chem23-s1.15.b] Solutions work at different points: avoiding waste, reusing material, or changing the plastic itself. Each has limits you should be able to state.
[chem23-s1.15.0] Reducing and reusing plastics avoids the waste altogether.
[chem23-s1.15.1] Recycling involves sorting, washing, shredding, melting and remoulding, but only some plastics can be recycled and only for a limited number of cycles.
[chem23-s1.15.2] Biodegradable and bio-based plastics, deposit-return schemes, bans and levies are other responses; Ireland introduced a plastic bag levy in March 2002.
[chem23-s1.16.b] Research question, e.g. “How does Ireland's dependence on imported oil and gas affect energy prices and security?” Hydrocarbons are built into the economy, so a country that must import them is exposed to events elsewhere.
[chem23-s1.16.0] Modern economies depend on hydrocarbons for transport, heating, electricity generation, farming, where fuel and fertiliser are made from natural gas, and plastics.
[chem23-s1.16.1] Jobs and government revenue depend on the industry.
[chem23-s1.16.2] Fossil fuels are finite and unevenly spread, so countries without their own reserves must import them and are exposed to price shocks (sudden sharp rises in price) and supply cuts.
[chem23-s1.16.3] The 1973 oil crisis was an oil embargo (a ban on supplying oil) by Arab oil-producing countries, and in 2022 gas and electricity prices rose sharply after Russia invaded Ukraine in February 2022.
[chem23-s1.16.4] Ireland imports most of its energy.
[chem23-s1.16.5] Ireland's own natural gas has come from the Kinsale Head gas field, off Co. Cork, which stopped producing in 2020, and from the Corrib gas field off Co. Mayo.
[chem23-s1.17.b] Getting oil and gas out of the ground and moving it around the world carries risks, and accidents have real economic costs.
[chem23-s1.17.0] Extraction and transport carry risks with economic costs.
[chem23-s1.17.1] Oil spills such as the Exxon Valdez (Alaska, 1989), the Prestige (off Galicia, Spain, 2002) and Deepwater Horizon (Gulf of Mexico, 2010) damaged fisheries, tourism and wildlife.
[chem23-s1.18.b] Ireland can cut its reliance on imported fuel, but the options have costs and trade-offs.
[chem23-s1.18.0] Home-produced renewable energy, energy efficiency and electrification (switching from burning fuels to using electricity) reduce imports.
[chem23-s1.18.1] The transition needs investment and takes time.
[chem23-s1.18.2] Hydrocarbons are still needed as feedstocks for plastics, medicines and fertilisers, so some argue they should be conserved for these uses rather than burned.

CANDIDATES chem23-s3: 3. Groups of hydrocarbons and their properties
[chem23-s3.0.b] A hydrocarbon is a compound containing only carbon and hydrogen. Chemists sort hydrocarbons into groups by the kind of carbon–carbon bonds they contain, because the bonding decides how each group behaves.
[chem23-s3.0.0] Hydrocarbons are divided by the type of carbon–carbon bonds present into aliphatic and aromatic hydrocarbons.
[chem23-s3.0.1] Aliphatic hydrocarbons have open chains of carbon atoms, straight or branched, or rings that behave like chains, such as cyclohexane. They are the alkanes, alkenes and alkynes.
[chem23-s3.0.2] Aromatic hydrocarbons contain a benzene ring (a ring of six carbon atoms with special bonding). Benzene, C₆H₆, is the example given in the specification.
[chem23-s3.0.3] A saturated hydrocarbon (one holding as many hydrogens as it can) has only carbon–carbon single bonds, as in alkanes and cycloalkanes (alkanes whose carbons form a ring).
[chem23-s3.0.4] An unsaturated hydrocarbon contains at least one carbon–carbon double or triple bond, as in alkenes and alkynes, so it could take on more atoms by opening that bond.
[chem23-s3.0.5] A homologous series is a family with the same general formula (one formula that fits every member) and similar chemical properties, a gradual change in physical properties, and each member differing from the next by CH₂.
[chem23-s3.1.b] The alkanes are saturated aliphatic hydrocarbons with single bonds only. Having no multiple bonds explains why they are so unreactive.
[chem23-s3.1.0] General formula CₙH₂ₙ₊₂, where n is the number of carbons. The first members are methane CH₄, ethane C₂H₆, propane C₃H₈, butane C₄H₁₀, pentane C₅H₁₂ and hexane C₆H₁₄.
[chem23-s3.1.1] Bonding: only single C–C and C–H covalent bonds are present, so the molecule is saturated.
[chem23-s3.1.2] Structure: each carbon is tetrahedral (its four bonds point to the corners of a tetrahedron), with bond angles of 109.5°. Chains can be branched from butane onwards.
[chem23-s3.1.3] Cyclohexane, C₆H₁₂, is a ring of six CH₂ groups joined by single bonds; it is saturated and aliphatic.
[chem23-s3.1.4] Cyclohexane has two fewer hydrogens than hexane because the ends of the chain are joined to make the ring. Its ring is not flat because each carbon is tetrahedral.
[chem23-s3.1.5] Alkanes are non-polar (their bonds have no significant positive or negative ends) and insoluble in water, and small members burn with a clean blue flame.
[chem23-s3.1.6] Alkanes are otherwise unreactive: they react with halogens (such as chlorine) only in ultraviolet light, by substitution (one atom replaced by another), e.g. CH₄ + Cl₂ → CH₃Cl + HCl.
[chem23-s3.2.b] The alkenes are unsaturated aliphatic hydrocarbons with one carbon–carbon double bond. That double bond makes them far more reactive than alkanes.
[chem23-s3.2.0] General formula CₙH₂ₙ; the members are ethene C₂H₄, propene C₃H₆, butene C₄H₈, pentene C₅H₁₀ and hexene C₆H₁₂.
[chem23-s3.2.1] Bonding: one C=C double bond is present, so the molecule is unsaturated.
[chem23-s3.2.2] Structure: the two carbons of the double bond and the four atoms attached to them lie in one plane, with angles of about 120°, and there is no rotation about the C=C bond.
[chem23-s3.2.3] Alkenes are, like alkanes, non-polar and insoluble in water, but they are much more reactive.
[chem23-s3.2.4] Alkenes undergo addition (other atoms join on across the double bond, which becomes a single bond) with bromine, which decolourises bromine water (turns it from orange to colourless); with hydrogen over a nickel catalyst, e.g. CH₂=CH₂ + H₂ → CH₃CH₃; and with hydrogen chloride, e.g. CH₂=CH₂ + HCl → CH₃CH₂Cl.
[chem23-s3.2.5] Alkenes add to one another to form addition polymers (long chains built from many alkene molecules), e.g. ethene forms poly(ethene). There is more on this in Types of organic reaction and addition polymers.
[chem23-s3.2.6] Alkenes burn with a more luminous (brighter), smokier flame than alkanes because of their higher carbon content.
[chem23-s3.3.b] The alkynes are unsaturated aliphatic hydrocarbons with one carbon–carbon triple bond. Like alkenes, they take part in addition reactions.
[chem23-s3.3.0] General formula CₙH₂ₙ₋₂; the first member is ethyne, C₂H₂, also called acetylene.
[chem23-s3.3.1] Bonding: one C≡C triple bond is present, so the molecule is unsaturated.
[chem23-s3.3.2] Structure: the two carbons of the triple bond and the atoms attached to them lie in a straight line, with an angle of 180°, so ethyne is linear.
[chem23-s3.3.3] Alkynes undergo addition reactions; with excess bromine, ethyne gives 1,1,2,2-tetrabromoethane: C₂H₂ + 2Br₂ → CHBr₂CHBr₂.
[chem23-s3.3.4] Ethyne burns in air with a very smoky, luminous flame.
[chem23-s3.3.5] Ethyne burns in pure oxygen with a very hot flame, which is used for welding: 2C₂H₂(g) + 5O₂(g) → 4CO₂(g) + 2H₂O(l).
[chem23-s3.4.b] Benzene is the aromatic hydrocarbon on the course. It is unsaturated on paper, yet it behaves very differently from an alkene, and its structure explains why.
[chem23-s3.4.0] Benzene, C₆H₆, is a colourless liquid with a boiling point of 80 °C and a sweet smell.
[chem23-s3.4.1] Its structure is a flat, hexagonal ring of six carbon atoms, each bonded to one hydrogen atom, with bond angles of 120°.
[chem23-s3.4.2] All six carbon–carbon bonds are identical, intermediate in length between single and double bonds, because the electrons beyond the single bonds are delocalised (spread) around the whole ring.
[chem23-s3.4.3] This is why benzene is drawn as a hexagon with a circle inside. There is more on this in Shapes, bonding and isomerism.
[chem23-s3.4.4] Although benzene is unsaturated, it is very stable and resists addition: it does not decolourise bromine water or acidified potassium manganate(VII), and it reacts mainly by substitution.
[chem23-s3.4.5] Benzene burns with a very smoky, sooty flame because of its high carbon content, 92.3% by mass, the same as ethyne.
[chem23-s3.4.6] Benzene is toxic and carcinogenic (cancer-causing), causing leukaemia, so it is not used in school laboratories, where safer solvents such as cyclohexane are used instead.
[chem23-s3.4.7] Michael Faraday isolated benzene in 1825, and August Kekulé proposed a ring of alternating single and double bonds in 1865.
[chem23-s3.4.8] The Kekulé structure cannot explain why all the carbon–carbon bonds are the same length, or why benzene does not decolourise bromine water.
[chem23-s3.5.b] Putting the four groups side by side shows a pattern: the type of carbon–carbon bonding decides the shape, the reactivity and the result of the bromine water test.
[chem23-s3.5.0] Alkanes have the general formula CₙH₂ₙ₊₂, single C–C bonds, tetrahedral shape with 109.5° angles around each carbon, and methane as the first member; bromine water shows no change.
[chem23-s3.5.1] Alkenes have the general formula CₙH₂ₙ, one double bond, planar (flat) shape with 120° angles, and ethene as the first member; bromine water is decolourised.
[chem23-s3.5.2] Alkynes have the general formula CₙH₂ₙ₋₂, one triple bond, a linear shape with 180° angles, and ethyne as the first member; bromine water is decolourised.
[chem23-s3.5.3] Benzene has the general formula C₆H₆, a delocalised ring, a planar shape with 120° angles, and benzene as the first member; bromine water shows no change.
[chem23-s3.5.4] All four groups are non-polar, with only London dispersion forces (weak attractions between temporary charges in neighbouring molecules) between their molecules, so all are insoluble in water and soluble in non-polar solvents; small members are gases.
[chem23-s3.5.5] Reactivity follows bonding: single bonds only give unreactive alkanes; a localised double or triple bond gives reactive alkenes and alkynes that undergo addition; a delocalised ring gives benzene, which is stable and undergoes substitution rather than addition.
[chem23-s3.6.b] Names follow the systematic IUPAC rules (the internationally agreed system), and only compounds up to C6 need to be named. Start with the stem and then work through the rules for alkanes.
[chem23-s3.6.0] The stem gives the number of carbons in the parent chain (the main chain): 1 meth-, 2 eth-, 3 prop-, 4 but-, 5 pent-, 6 hex-.
[chem23-s3.6.1] The ending is -ane for alkanes, -ene for alkenes and -yne for alkynes.
[chem23-s3.6.2] Find the longest continuous carbon chain, which may bend on paper; this gives the parent name.
[chem23-s3.6.3] Number the chain from the end that gives the side chains (substituents, groups branching off the main chain) the lowest numbers.
[chem23-s3.6.4] Name side chains as alkyl groups: methyl (–CH₃) and ethyl (–C₂H₅); give each a position number, using di- and tri- for repeats.
[chem23-s3.6.5] Separate numbers from one another with commas, and separate numbers from words with hyphens; list different side chains alphabetically.
[chem23-s3.7.b] Applying the rules to branched alkanes shows how the numbering works.
[chem23-s3.7.0] CH₃CH(CH₃)CH₃ is methylpropane. It needs no number because the methyl group can only be on carbon 2, though 2-methylpropane is also accepted.
[chem23-s3.7.1] CH₃CH(CH₃)CH₂CH₃ is 2-methylbutane, not 3-methylbutane, because numbering from the end that gives the lowest number to the side chain puts it on carbon 2.
[chem23-s3.7.2] C(CH₃)₄ is 2,2-dimethylpropane, with di- showing two methyl groups, both on carbon 2.
[chem23-s3.7.3] CH₃CH₂CH(CH₃)CH₂CH₃ is 3-methylpentane.
[chem23-s3.7.4] (CH₃)₂CHCH(CH₃)₂ is 2,3-dimethylbutane.
[chem23-s3.8.b] Alkenes and alkynes follow the same rules, with one addition: the multiple bond decides the numbering and must sit in the main chain.
[chem23-s3.8.0] For alkenes and alkynes the main chain must contain the multiple bond; number from the end nearer to it, and give the number of its first carbon before -ene or -yne.
[chem23-s3.8.1] CH₂=CHCH₂CH₃ is but-1-ene, CH₃CH=CHCH₃ is but-2-ene, CH₂=C(CH₃)CH₃ is 2-methylpropene, and CH₂=CHCH₂CH₂CH₂CH₃ is hex-1-ene.
[chem23-s3.8.2] HC≡CCH₃ is propyne, and CH₃C≡CCH₃ is but-2-yne.
[chem23-s3.8.3] Ethene, propene, ethyne and propyne need no number, because there is only one position for the multiple bond.
[chem23-s3.8.4] Rings take the prefix cyclo-, as in cyclohexane; benzene keeps its own name.
[chem23-s3.9.b] Three things explain almost every difference in the properties of hydrocarbons: the forces between molecules, the size of the molecules, and the kind of carbon–carbon bond.
[chem23-s3.9.0] Hydrocarbons are non-polar, because C–H bonds have very low polarity (the electrons are shared almost equally), so the only forces between their molecules are London dispersion forces. There is more on this in Bond type and intermolecular forces.
[chem23-s3.9.1] London dispersion forces get stronger as relative molecular mass (Mr, the mass of a molecule compared with the carbon-12 standard) increases, because there are more electrons and larger electron clouds, and as the area of contact between molecules increases.
[chem23-s3.9.2] The stronger the intermolecular forces, the more energy is needed to separate the molecules, so the higher the boiling point and the more likely the substance is a liquid or solid at room temperature.
[chem23-s3.9.3] The nature of the carbon–carbon bonds decides chemical reactivity: single bonds only give unreactive compounds, while a C=C double bond gives reactivity through addition reactions.
[chem23-s3.9.4] Boiling overcomes the forces between molecules; it does not break C–C or C–H bonds.
[chem23-s3.10.b] The data show how boiling point depends on carbon number in the straight-chain alkanes (those with no branches).
[chem23-s3.10.0] Boiling points: methane −162 °C, ethane −89 °C, propane −42 °C, butane −1 °C, pentane 36 °C and hexane 69 °C.
[chem23-s3.10.1] Boiling point rises with carbon number because each extra CH₂ increases the relative molecular mass and the number of electrons, and lengthens the molecule, so London dispersion forces get stronger.
[chem23-s3.10.2] The rise for each extra CH₂ gets smaller: the successive differences are 73, 47, 41, 37 and 33 °C, because each CH₂ is a smaller fraction of the molecule.
[chem23-s3.10.3] At room temperature, about 20 °C, methane to butane are gases, while pentane and hexane are liquids.
[chem23-s3.10.4] Larger alkanes up to about C16 are liquids, and beyond that they are waxy solids.
[chem23-s3.11.b] Other properties either follow from the molecules being non-polar or change gradually with chain length. Reactivity does not change at all.
[chem23-s3.11.0] All alkanes are insoluble in water, because they are non-polar and cannot form hydrogen bonds with water.
[chem23-s3.11.1] All alkanes dissolve in or mix with non-polar solvents such as cyclohexane, following the rule “like dissolves like”.
[chem23-s3.11.2] Liquid alkanes are less dense than water and float on it; hexane has a density of about 0.66 g cm⁻³.
[chem23-s3.11.3] All alkanes burn completely to CO₂ and H₂O, e.g. C₆H₁₄(l) + 9½O₂(g) → 6CO₂(g) + 7H₂O(l); larger alkanes need more oxygen per mole and release more energy per mole.
[chem23-s3.11.4] Larger alkanes burn with a more luminous, smokier flame and are less volatile (they evaporate less easily), so they are harder to ignite.
[chem23-s3.11.5] The percentage of carbon rises from 75.0% in CH₄ to 83.7% in C₆H₁₄, making incomplete combustion (burning in too little oxygen, giving soot and CO) more likely.
[chem23-s3.11.6] There is no difference in reactivity: all straight-chain alkanes are unreactive, reacting only by combustion or by substitution with halogens in ultraviolet light.
[chem23-s3.12.b] Alkanes with the same carbon number but different structures are structural isomers: they have the same molecular formula and Mr, but a different arrangement of the chain. Their boiling points differ even so.
[chem23-s3.12.0] Butane (−1 °C) and methylpropane (−12 °C) are the isomers of C₄H₁₀.
[chem23-s3.12.1] Pentane (36 °C), 2-methylbutane (28 °C) and 2,2-dimethylpropane (10 °C) are the isomers of C₅H₁₂.
[chem23-s3.12.2] Hexane (69 °C), 3-methylpentane (63 °C), 2-methylpentane (60 °C), 2,3-dimethylbutane (58 °C) and 2,2-dimethylbutane (50 °C) are the isomers of C₆H₁₄.
[chem23-s3.12.3] The more branched the molecule, the more compact, or closer to spherical, it is, so the area of contact between neighbouring molecules is smaller.
[chem23-s3.12.4] Smaller contact gives weaker London dispersion forces and a lower boiling point, since the Mr is the same for all the isomers. Unbranched chains lie alongside each other with a large area of contact.
[chem23-s3.13.b] Branching changes the boiling point enough to change the state at room temperature, but it leaves the chemistry unchanged.
[chem23-s3.13.0] 2,2-dimethylpropane, with a boiling point of 10 °C, is a gas at room temperature, although pentane, with the same formula, is a liquid at room temperature because its boiling point is 36 °C.
[chem23-s3.13.1] Isomers show no significant difference in solubility, combustion or reactivity: all are non-polar and insoluble in water, burn to the same products with the same equation, e.g. C₅H₁₂ + 8O₂ → 5CO₂ + 6H₂O, and are equally unreactive.
[chem23-s3.13.2] Diagram: two pentane molecules lying side by side with a large area of contact, beside two 2,2-dimethylpropane molecules touching at only a small area.
[chem23-s3.14.b] The monounsaturated straight-chain alkenes (those with one double bond and no branches) show the same boiling point pattern as the alkanes, but a little lower.
[chem23-s3.14.0] Boiling points: ethene −104 °C, propene −48 °C, but-1-ene −6 °C, pent-1-ene 30 °C and hex-1-ene 63 °C.
[chem23-s3.14.1] Boiling point rises with carbon number for the same reason as in alkanes: a larger Mr, more electrons and stronger London dispersion forces.
[chem23-s3.14.2] Ethene to butene are gases at room temperature, while pentene and hexene are liquids.
[chem23-s3.14.3] Each alkene boils a little lower than the alkane with the same number of carbons, e.g. ethene −104 °C against ethane −89 °C and hex-1-ene 63 °C against hexane 69 °C, because it has two fewer hydrogen atoms, so slightly fewer electrons and weaker London forces.
[chem23-s3.15.b] Alkenes resemble alkanes in solubility, but their double bond makes their reactivity and flame different.
[chem23-s3.15.0] All alkenes are insoluble in water and soluble in non-polar solvents, like alkanes.
[chem23-s3.15.1] All alkenes burn to CO₂ and H₂O, e.g. C₃H₆(g) + 4½O₂(g) → 3CO₂(g) + 3H₂O(l).
[chem23-s3.15.2] Every straight-chain alkene has the same percentage of carbon, 85.7%, since all have the formula CₙH₂ₙ; this is more than in the alkanes, so alkenes burn with smokier flames than alkanes.
[chem23-s3.15.3] All alkenes have one C=C bond, so they all undergo the same addition reactions, e.g. all decolourise bromine water, and they are much more reactive than alkanes.
[chem23-s3.15.4] Changing the chain length or the position of the double bond makes little difference to reactivity.
[chem23-s3.16.b] Use the data tables to predict properties from the trends. Each prediction rests on the three factors: intermolecular forces, Mr and bonding.
[chem23-s3.16.0] To predict a boiling point, use the trend in a table: the value lies between those of the neighbouring members, or of isomers with more and less branching.
[chem23-s3.16.1] Pent-1-ene, with a boiling point of 30 °C, is a liquid at 20 °C but would boil on a warm day or in warm water.
[chem23-s3.16.2] 2-Methylpentane boils below hexane, because both are C₆H₁₄ and the branched isomer has a smaller area of contact.
[chem23-s3.16.3] Hex-1-ene will decolourise bromine water and hexane will not, and both will float on water without dissolving.
[chem23-s3.16.4] A mixture of pentane and hexane can be separated by fractional distillation (separating liquids by their different boiling points), because their boiling points differ, at 36 °C and 69 °C.
[chem23-s3.17.b] Of the aliphatic hydrocarbons, alkanes are the most stable. Stable here has a particular meaning, set out below.
[chem23-s3.17.0] Alkanes are the least reactive aliphatic hydrocarbons: at room temperature they do not react with acids, bases, most oxidising agents such as acidified potassium manganate(VII), or bromine water.
[chem23-s3.17.1] Their old name, paraffins, comes from the Latin parum affinis, meaning little affinity, that is, little reactivity.
[chem23-s3.17.2] “Stable” means unreactive under ordinary conditions, not that alkanes can never react: they burn readily once ignited, and they react with halogens in ultraviolet light.
[chem23-s3.18.b] Three features of alkane bonding explain their stability: they are saturated, their bonds are strong, and their bonds are almost non-polar.
[chem23-s3.18.0] Alkanes are saturated: every carbon already has four single bonds, so nothing can add to the molecule, and a bond must be broken before any reaction can occur.
[chem23-s3.18.1] C–C and C–H bonds are strong, so a great deal of energy is needed to break them, which gives a high activation energy (the minimum energy needed to start a reaction).
[chem23-s3.18.2] The electronegativities (a measure of how strongly an atom pulls on shared electrons) of carbon, 2.55, and hydrogen, 2.20, differ by only 0.35, so C–H bonds are almost non-polar, and C–C bonds are completely non-polar.
[chem23-s3.18.3] With no significant δ⁺ or δ⁻ sites (slightly positive or negative ends), alkane molecules do not attract ions such as OH⁻ or H⁺, or polar molecules, which react by attacking charged sites.
[chem23-s3.18.4] Alkenes are more reactive because the C=C double bond is an electron-rich site that attracts reagents such as bromine and allows addition.
[chem23-s3.19.b] Alkanes are stable, not inert. They react under two sets of conditions: when ignited, and when exposed to ultraviolet light with a halogen.
[chem23-s3.19.0] Combustion: alkanes burn in oxygen once a flame or spark supplies the activation energy, releasing a lot of energy, e.g. CH₄(g) + 2O₂(g) → CO₂(g) + 2H₂O(l).
[chem23-s3.19.1] Substitution with chlorine or bromine needs ultraviolet light, or sunlight, to split the halogen molecule into reactive atoms, called free radicals, e.g. CH₄ + Cl₂ → CH₃Cl + HCl. There is more on this in Reaction mechanisms.
[chem23-s3.19.2] In the dark, an alkane mixed with bromine stays orange; in sunlight the colour slowly fades as substitution happens, which is much slower than the instant decolourisation produced by an alkene.
[chem23-s3.20.b] The unreactivity of alkanes is useful in everyday life, and it also causes an environmental problem.
[chem23-s3.20.0] Alkanes are safe to store and transport as fuels, such as natural gas, LPG and petrol, because they do not react until ignited.
[chem23-s3.20.1] Unreactive liquid alkanes such as hexane and cyclohexane are useful solvents.
[chem23-s3.20.2] Mineral oils and paraffin wax are used as lubricants and protective coatings.
[chem23-s3.20.3] The same chemical stability makes poly(ethene) and other addition polymers, whose chains are alkane-like, slow to break down in the environment. There is more on this in Types of organic reaction and addition polymers.
[chem23-s3.21.b] At Higher Level the stability of alkanes is explained by the type of bond. Single bonds are sigma bonds, while the second bond in an alkene's double bond is a weaker pi bond.
[chem23-s3.21.0] Every bond in an alkane is a sigma (σ) bond, formed by head-on overlap of orbitals (the regions where electrons are found) along the line between the two nuclei.
[chem23-s3.21.1] In a σ bond the shared electron pair is concentrated between the two nuclei, where both nuclei hold it strongly, so σ bonds are strong and their electrons are not exposed to attacking reagents.
[chem23-s3.21.2] An alkene's double bond is one σ bond plus one pi (π) bond; the π electrons lie above and below the plane of the molecule, further from the nuclei and less tightly held, so they are exposed to attack by electron-seeking reagents.
[chem23-s3.21.3] Typical average bond enthalpies (energies needed to break a bond) are C–C 348 kJ mol⁻¹, C=C 614 kJ mol⁻¹ and C–H 412 kJ mol⁻¹, so the π part of the C=C bond adds only about 266 kJ mol⁻¹ to the σ bond.
[chem23-s3.21.4] So alkenes react by breaking the weaker π bond and keeping the σ bond, which is addition, whereas an alkane would have to break a strong σ bond, which needs much more energy.
[chem23-s3.21.5] Diagram: a C–C σ bond with electron density between the nuclei, beside a C=C bond showing the σ bond and the π electron cloud above and below the bond axis.

CANDIDATES chem20-s1: 1. The greenhouse effect
[chem20-s1.0.b] Carbon moves continuously between stores (places where it is held) through natural processes. The balance between sources and sinks decides how much CO₂ is in the air.
[chem20-s1.0.0] Carbon is stored in the atmosphere (mainly as CO₂), in the oceans (dissolved CO₂, hydrogencarbonate and carbonate ions), in living things (carbohydrates, proteins and fats), in soils and peat, and in rocks (limestone and fossil fuels).
[chem20-s1.0.1] Photosynthesis (the process in which plants build sugar from CO₂ and water) removes CO₂ from the air: 6CO₂(g) + 6H₂O(l) → C₆H₁₂O₆(aq) + 6O₂(g). It is powered by sunlight in green plants, algae and phytoplankton (microscopic floating ocean algae).
[chem20-s1.0.2] Respiration, the process in which living things use glucose and oxygen to release energy, returns CO₂ to the air. It happens in plants, animals and microorganisms: C₆H₁₂O₆(aq) + 6O₂(g) → 6CO₂(g) + 6H₂O(l).
[chem20-s1.0.3] Decay is the breakdown of dead matter by microorganisms. It also releases CO₂, and it releases CH₄ (methane) instead where oxygen is absent.
[chem20-s1.0.4] CO₂ dissolves in the oceans and waterways, so the water is also a store: CO₂(g) ⇌ CO₂(aq), and then CO₂(aq) + H₂O(l) ⇌ H₂CO₃(aq), where H₂CO₃ is carbonic acid.
[chem20-s1.0.5] Marine organisms use carbonate to build calcium carbonate shells and skeletons. Over long periods these deposits form limestone, which locks carbon away in rock.
[chem20-s1.1.b] Carbon also returns to the air by burning, and some carbon is held back for very long periods as fuels. This explains how the carbon in fossil fuels reaches the atmosphere.
[chem20-s1.1.0] Combustion (burning) of fuels releases CO₂, for example CH₄(g) + 2O₂(g) → CO₂(g) + 2H₂O(l). Natural sources also include forest fires and volcanic eruptions.
[chem20-s1.1.1] Peat forms from dead organisms buried over thousands of years, while coal, oil and natural gas form over millions of years. In both cases the material is buried without enough oxygen to decay fully, so they are long-term carbon stores.
[chem20-s1.1.2] Burning coal, oil, natural gas or peat therefore returns carbon, stored for a very long time, to the atmosphere as CO₂.
[chem20-s1.1.3] Diagram: the carbon cycle with atmospheric CO₂ at the centre and labelled arrows for photosynthesis, respiration, decay, combustion, dissolving in the oceans, formation of shells and limestone, and formation of fossil fuels.
[chem20-s1.2.b] Each part of the carbon cycle either adds CO₂ to the air or takes it out. When additions and removals match, the CO₂ level stays steady, and human activity has upset that match.
[chem20-s1.2.0] A carbon source adds more CO₂ to the atmosphere than it removes, for example burning fossil fuels, respiration, decay, volcanoes, and heating limestone in cement-making: CaCO₃(s) → CaO(s) + CO₂(g).
[chem20-s1.2.1] A carbon sink removes more carbon from the atmosphere than it releases, and stores it. Examples are growing forests, the oceans, and undisturbed peatlands and wetlands (peat-forming bogs and marshy land).
[chem20-s1.2.2] Waterlogged conditions in undisturbed peatlands and wetlands stop dead plant material from decaying, so the carbon stays stored.
[chem20-s1.2.3] Before industrialisation (the growth of factories and large-scale fuel burning), natural sources and sinks were roughly in balance, so atmospheric CO₂ stayed fairly steady at about 280 ppm (parts per million by volume).
[chem20-s1.2.4] Burning fossil fuels moves carbon from long-term stores into the air far faster than sinks can absorb it. Deforestation (clearing forests) and draining peatlands both remove sinks and release stored carbon.
[chem20-s1.2.5] As a result, atmospheric CO₂ has risen to about 420 ppm in the mid-2020s.
[chem20-s1.2.6] CO₂ is a greenhouse gas, so a carbon cycle out of balance changes the climate. Natural changes in the cycle over thousands of years have caused past climate changes; the present change is human-influenced and much faster.
[chem20-s1.2.7] Sustainability means using resources so that they are not used up and the environment is not damaged for future generations. For carbon this means keeping emissions in balance with what sinks can absorb, for example by using renewable energy and protecting forests and peatlands.
[chem20-s1.3.b] Greenhouse gases in the atmosphere trap some of the heat the Earth radiates, keeping the lower atmosphere warm.
[chem20-s1.3.0] Energy from the Sun reaches the Earth mainly as visible light (shorter wavelength), with some ultraviolet and near-infrared. The atmosphere is largely transparent to visible light, so it passes through.
[chem20-s1.3.1] About 30% of incoming solar radiation is reflected back to space by clouds, ice and the surface. The rest is absorbed and warms the land and oceans.
[chem20-s1.3.2] The warmed Earth radiates energy back out as longer-wavelength infrared (IR) radiation, which is heat. Remember that incoming radiation is mainly visible light and outgoing radiation is infrared.
[chem20-s1.3.3] Greenhouse gases absorb some of this IR and re-emit it in all directions, including back down towards the surface, so heat is kept in the lower atmosphere for longer.
[chem20-s1.3.4] This warming of the Earth's surface and lower atmosphere by naturally occurring greenhouse gases is the natural greenhouse effect.
[chem20-s1.3.5] The main natural greenhouse gases are water vapour, the largest contributor, and carbon dioxide, with smaller amounts of methane and dinitrogen oxide (N₂O); ozone also contributes.
[chem20-s1.3.6] The name is an analogy with a glass greenhouse, which lets sunlight in and keeps heat in. The analogy is not exact, because a real greenhouse works mainly by stopping warm air escaping.
[chem20-s1.3.7] Diagram: the Sun, incoming short-wavelength radiation passing through the atmosphere, some reflected, IR emitted from the surface, some absorbed by a layer of greenhouse gases and re-emitted back down, and some escaping to space.
[chem20-s1.4.b] Not every gas in the air is a greenhouse gas. Whether a gas traps heat depends on the structure of its molecules.
[chem20-s1.4.0] Nitrogen (N₂, about 78% of dry air), oxygen (O₂, about 21%) and argon (about 0.9%) do not absorb IR radiation, so they are not greenhouse gases even though they make up about 99% of dry air.
[chem20-s1.4.1] A molecule absorbs IR when the energy makes its bonds vibrate (stretch or bend) in a way that changes how charge is spread across the molecule.
[chem20-s1.4.2] Molecules of two identical atoms (N₂, O₂) cannot do this, while H₂O, CO₂, CH₄ and N₂O, with three or more atoms and polar bonds (bonds where the electrons are shared unequally), can.
[chem20-s1.4.3] Water vapour contributes most to the natural greenhouse effect because it is by far the most abundant greenhouse gas.
[chem20-s1.4.4] The concentration of water vapour is set by temperature, through evaporation (liquid water turning to vapour) and condensation (vapour turning back to liquid), not directly by human emissions.
[chem20-s1.5.b] The natural greenhouse effect is what makes the Earth habitable, and experiments by John Tyndall in the nineteenth century showed which gases absorb heat radiation.
[chem20-s1.5.0] Without the natural greenhouse effect, the Earth's average surface temperature would be about −18 °C instead of about +15 °C, a difference of about 33 °C.
[chem20-s1.5.1] At −18 °C the oceans would freeze and life as we know it could not exist.
[chem20-s1.5.2] The natural greenhouse effect keeps water liquid over most of the planet and evens out the difference between day and night temperatures.
[chem20-s1.5.3] The natural effect is essential and beneficial. The problem is the extra warming caused by increased greenhouse gas concentrations.
[chem20-s1.5.4] John Tyndall (1820–1893), born in Leighlinbridge, Co. Carlow, used experiments from 1859 at the Royal Institution in London to show which gases absorb heat radiation: water vapour and carbon dioxide do, while nitrogen and oxygen do not.
[chem20-s1.5.5] Tyndall's experiments laid the experimental foundation for understanding the greenhouse effect. The Tyndall National Institute in Cork is named after him.
[chem20-s1.6.b] The natural greenhouse effect is beneficial, but human activity has added greenhouse gases to the air. The extra warming this causes is the enhanced greenhouse effect.
[chem20-s1.6.0] The enhanced greenhouse effect is the extra warming of the Earth's surface and lower atmosphere caused by increased concentrations of greenhouse gases from human (anthropogenic) activities.
[chem20-s1.6.1] The main human activities are burning fossil fuels (for power generation, transport, heating and industry), deforestation and land-use change, agriculture (livestock and fertilisers), cement-making and landfill (waste buried in the ground).
[chem20-s1.6.2] The resulting long-term rise in the Earth's average surface temperature is global warming. Together with the other changes it causes, it is called climate change.
[chem20-s1.7.b] Gases differ in how much they warm the Earth for the same mass emitted. To compare them, scientists use a number called the global warming potential.
[chem20-s1.7.0] Gases differ in their greenhouse effect because they differ in how strongly each molecule absorbs infrared radiation, how abundant they are in the atmosphere, and how long they stay there (atmospheric lifetime).
[chem20-s1.7.1] The greenhouse factor of a gas is its global warming potential (GWP): the warming caused by emitting 1 kg (or 1 tonne) of the gas over a set period, usually 100 years, compared with the warming from the same mass of CO₂. The GWP of CO₂ is 1 by definition.
[chem20-s1.7.2] 100-year GWPs from the IPCC (the Intergovernmental Panel on Climate Change) Fifth Assessment Report (AR5, 2013) are: CO₂ 1; methane (CH₄) 28; dinitrogen oxide (N₂O) 265.
[chem20-s1.7.3] The IPCC Sixth Assessment Report (AR6, 2021) gives slightly different values because the science is refined over time: CH₄ about 27–30, depending on whether the methane is of fossil origin. The AR6 value for N₂O is 273.
[chem20-s1.7.4] Always state which report a GWP comes from, because the values differ between reports.
[chem20-s1.7.5] A high GWP does not make a gas the most important. CO₂ has the lowest GWP of these gases, yet it causes most of the human-caused warming because far more of it is emitted and it stays in the atmosphere for centuries.
[chem20-s1.7.6] Emissions of different gases are compared as CO₂ equivalent, found by multiplying the mass of the gas by its GWP.
[chem20-s1.7.7] Example: 5 tonnes of methane = 5 × 28 = 140 tonnes CO₂ equivalent (AR5 values).
[chem20-s1.8.b] Each greenhouse gas has its own sources and abundance. Abundance is given in ppm, parts per million of the air by volume, and compared with the pre-industrial level.
[chem20-s1.8.0] Carbon dioxide comes from burning fossil fuels, deforestation and cement-making (CaCO₃ → CaO + CO₂).
[chem20-s1.8.1] CO₂ is about 420 ppm in the mid-2020s, up from about 280 ppm before industrialisation, an increase of about 50%.
[chem20-s1.8.2] Much of the extra CO₂ stays in the atmosphere for centuries, and some for thousands of years, which is why its effect builds up.
[chem20-s1.8.3] Methane comes from ruminant livestock (cattle and sheep, which digest food in the stomach), manure, rice paddies, landfill sites, leaks from natural-gas wells and pipelines, and natural wetlands.
[chem20-s1.8.4] Methane is about 1.9 ppm, more than two and a half times the pre-industrial level of about 0.7 ppm, and its atmospheric lifetime is about 12 years.
[chem20-s1.9.b] Dinitrogen oxide is a long-lived greenhouse gas tied to farming. Ireland's emissions show how these gases link to everyday activities.
[chem20-s1.9.0] Dinitrogen oxide (nitrous oxide, N₂O) comes mainly from nitrogen fertilisers and manure on soils. It is about 0.34 ppm, up from about 0.27 ppm.
[chem20-s1.9.1] N₂O stays in the atmosphere for over 100 years (AR5: about 121 years), which is why it is such a potent, long-lived greenhouse gas.
[chem20-s1.9.2] In Ireland agriculture is the largest single source of greenhouse gas emissions, over a third of the total, mainly methane from cattle and N₂O from fertilisers and manure.
[chem20-s1.9.3] In Ireland energy, transport and heating produce most of the CO₂.
[chem20-s1.10.b] Oxides of nitrogen and sulfur form when fuels burn. They matter in a different way from the main greenhouse gases: one group acts indirectly and the other actually cools the climate.
[chem20-s1.10.0] NOx (nitrogen monoxide, NO, and nitrogen dioxide, NO₂) form when nitrogen and oxygen from air combine at the high temperatures in car engines and power stations: N₂(g) + O₂(g) → 2NO(g).
[chem20-s1.10.1] NO then reacts further with oxygen: 2NO(g) + O₂(g) → 2NO₂(g). Lightning also forms NOx.
[chem20-s1.10.2] NOx are short-lived and are not important direct greenhouse gases. They act indirectly, mainly by helping to form ozone, a greenhouse gas, in the lower atmosphere.
[chem20-s1.10.3] NO₂ also causes acid rain (rain made acidic by dissolved pollutant gases) and harms health.
[chem20-s1.10.4] NOx must not be confused with N₂O, the long-lived greenhouse gas from fertilisers.
[chem20-s1.10.5] Sulfur oxides (SO₂ and SO₃) come from burning coal and oil that contain sulfur, S(s) + O₂(g) → SO₂(g), and from volcanoes.
[chem20-s1.10.6] SO₂ forms tiny sulfate particles (aerosols) and droplets in the air that reflect sunlight, so their overall effect on climate is cooling, not warming.
[chem20-s1.10.7] Sulfur oxides also cause acid rain.
[chem20-s1.11.b] Water vapour is the most abundant greenhouse gas, but it behaves differently from the others because temperature, not human emissions, controls how much of it is in the air.
[chem20-s1.11.0] Water vapour is the most abundant greenhouse gas. Its concentration is controlled by temperature, and it stays in the air for only days.
[chem20-s1.11.1] Because of its short stay in the air, human emissions don't change its concentration directly, and water vapour has no GWP.
[chem20-s1.11.2] Water vapour acts as a feedback (a change that reinforces the original change): warming caused by CO₂ and other gases increases evaporation, and the extra water vapour traps more heat, amplifying the warming.
[chem20-s1.11.3] Warmer air holds about 7% more water vapour for each °C of warming (Clausius–Clapeyron relation).
[chem20-s1.12.b] Several independent kinds of measurement show that greenhouse gases have risen and that the planet has warmed. Together they support the conclusion that human activity is the cause.
[chem20-s1.12.0] Continuous measurements at Mauna Loa, Hawaii, begun by Charles David Keeling in 1958 (the “Keeling curve”), show CO₂ rising every year, from about 315 ppm in 1958 to about 420 ppm.
[chem20-s1.12.1] Ice cores (long cylinders of ice drilled from ice sheets): air bubbles trapped in Antarctic ice show that CO₂ stayed between about 180 and 300 ppm for the 800,000 years before industrialisation.
[chem20-s1.12.2] Today's CO₂ level is far above that range, and the rise matches the growth in fossil-fuel use.
[chem20-s1.12.3] The global average surface temperature in 2011–2020 was about 1.1 °C higher than in 1850–1900 (IPCC AR6), with most of the warming since the 1970s. Recent years have been the warmest on record.
[chem20-s1.12.4] Other observed changes include shrinking glaciers and Arctic sea ice, melting ice sheets and warming oceans.
[chem20-s1.12.5] Sea level rose about 20 cm between 1901 and 2018 (IPCC AR6), from melting ice and the expansion of warmer water.
[chem20-s1.12.6] Climate models (computer simulations of the climate) that include only natural factors (changes in the Sun and volcanoes) do not reproduce the warming since the mid-twentieth century. Models that also include human greenhouse gas emissions do.
[chem20-s1.12.7] The IPCC (AR6) concludes that it is unequivocal that human influence has warmed the atmosphere, ocean and land.
[chem20-s1.13.b] Extra warming changes temperature, rainfall, the oceans and living things. The effects are linked: warmer air holds more water vapour, and the oceans absorb extra CO₂.
[chem20-s1.13.0] Global warming brings higher average temperatures, more frequent heatwaves, melting ice and rising sea levels that threaten low-lying coasts and countries, for example Bangladesh, the Netherlands and small island states.
[chem20-s1.13.1] A warmer atmosphere holds more water vapour, so the water cycle intensifies: heavier rainfall and more flooding in some regions, and longer droughts in others.
[chem20-s1.13.2] More winter rain is expected in Ireland.
[chem20-s1.13.3] Other effects include changes to ecosystems (species moving or becoming extinct, coral bleaching), threats to food and water supplies, and more extreme weather events.
[chem20-s1.14.b] The oceans take up part of the CO₂ humans emit. This slows warming in the air, but the dissolved CO₂ makes the sea water less alkaline.
[chem20-s1.14.0] The oceans absorb about a quarter of the CO₂ emitted by humans.
[chem20-s1.14.1] CO₂(aq) + H₂O(l) ⇌ H₂CO₃(aq) ⇌ H⁺(aq) + HCO₃⁻(aq). The H⁺ concentration rises as CO₂ dissolves, so the pH falls.
[chem20-s1.14.2] The average surface ocean pH has fallen by about 0.1, from about 8.2 to about 8.1, since pre-industrial times.
[chem20-s1.14.3] Ocean acidification lowers the pH but does not make the sea acidic, because the pH stays above 7.
[chem20-s1.14.4] The extra H⁺ reacts with carbonate ions, H⁺ + CO₃²⁻ → HCO₃⁻, leaving less carbonate for shell-building organisms such as oysters, mussels, corals and some plankton.
[chem20-s1.14.5] Acidified water can also dissolve calcium carbonate: CaCO₃(s) + CO₂(aq) + H₂O(l) → Ca²⁺(aq) + 2HCO₃⁻(aq).
[chem20-s1.15.b] Reducing the enhanced greenhouse effect means cutting emissions of greenhouse gases and protecting carbon sinks. Each major source has its own response.
[chem20-s1.15.0] Replace fossil fuels with renewable energy (wind, solar, hydroelectric and tidal), and improve energy efficiency by insulating buildings, using heat pumps and buying efficient appliances.
[chem20-s1.15.1] Transport: use public transport, cycling and walking; use electric vehicles charged from renewable electricity; and use hydrogen fuel cells.
[chem20-s1.15.2] Agriculture: use the right amount of fertiliser at the right time, and use clover and other nitrogen-fixing crops.
[chem20-s1.15.3] Agriculture: breed and feed livestock to produce less methane, and capture methane from manure in anaerobic digesters (tanks where microorganisms break down waste without oxygen, giving biogas) for use as a fuel.
[chem20-s1.15.4] Land use: plant forests, stop deforestation, and protect and rewet peatlands so that they act as carbon sinks again.
[chem20-s1.16.b] Some emissions can be captured or avoided in waste and industry, and governments set targets so that action is shared.
[chem20-s1.16.0] Waste: reduce food waste, recycle, and capture landfill methane and burn it to generate electricity. The CO₂ produced has a much smaller warming effect than the methane would have.
[chem20-s1.16.1] Carbon capture and storage (CCS): CO₂ is captured from power-station or factory flue gases (the waste gases from the chimney), compressed to a liquid, piped away and injected into deep rock formations such as depleted oil and gas fields.
[chem20-s1.16.2] Acid rain can be cut by reducing sulfur and NOx emissions: low-sulfur fuels, removing SO₂ from flue gases, and catalytic converters on cars.
[chem20-s1.16.3] International and national action: the Paris Agreement (2015) aims to keep the rise in global temperature well below 2 °C above pre-industrial levels and to pursue efforts to limit it to 1.5 °C.
[chem20-s1.16.4] Ireland's Climate Action and Low Carbon Development (Amendment) Act 2021 sets a 51% cut in emissions by 2030 (compared with 2018) and climate neutrality by 2050.
[chem20-s1.16.5] A carbon footprint is the total greenhouse gas emissions, as CO₂ equivalent, caused by a person, organisation, product or activity. Individuals can reduce theirs through choices in travel, home heating, diet and consumption.
