TARGET chem12-s2: 2. Titration curves, indicators and vinegar
[chem12-s2.0.b] A pH curve is a graph of pH against the volume of one solution added to another. You build it from your own measurements (primary data), and it shows you where the reaction is complete and which indicator would signal that point.
[chem12-s2.0.0] Aim: to plot how the pH changes as an alkali is added to an acid, using primary data (results you measure yourself), and to use the curve to choose an indicator.
[chem12-s2.0.1] First calibrate the pH meter, or the pH sensor and data-logger (a device that records readings automatically), with buffer solutions of known pH, so that its readings can be trusted.
[chem12-s2.0.2] Pipette (measure accurately with a fixed-volume glass pipette) 25.0 cm³ of the acid into a beaker on a magnetic stirrer, or into a beaker that is swirled. Put the pH probe in and record the starting pH.
[chem12-s2.0.3] Add the alkali from a burette (a graduated tube with a tap) in 1 cm³ portions, recording the pH after each.
[chem12-s2.0.4] Near the equivalence point (the point where the acid and alkali have reacted in exactly the amounts in the equation), add 0.1–0.2 cm³ portions, because the pH changes rapidly there. Continue well past it.
[chem12-s2.0.5] Plot pH on the y-axis against the volume of alkali added on the x-axis.
[chem12-s2.0.6] The equivalence point is the midpoint of the steep, vertical section of the curve.
[chem12-s2.0.7] Diagram: a burette above a beaker on a magnetic stirrer, with a pH probe connected to a pH meter or data-logger; label burette, beaker, stirrer, probe and meter.
[chem12-s2.1.b] The shape of a pH curve depends on whether the acid and the base are strong (fully ionised in water) or weak (only partly ionised). Each curve below is for 25 cm³ of acid with base added from a burette, using 0.1 mol L⁻¹ solutions throughout.
[chem12-s2.1.0] Strong acid–strong base (HCl and NaOH): starts at about pH 1; rises slowly, then very steeply from about pH 3 to pH 11, with the equivalence point at pH 7; levels off near pH 13.
[chem12-s2.1.1] Weak acid–strong base (ethanoic acid and NaOH): starts higher, at about pH 3, because the acid is only partly ionised, with a gentler rise. The steep section is from about pH 7 to 11.
[chem12-s2.1.2] In that weak acid–strong base titration the equivalence point is above pH 7 (about 8.7), because the ethanoate ion formed is a weak base and makes the solution slightly alkaline.
[chem12-s2.1.3] Strong acid–weak base (HCl and aqueous ammonia): starts at about pH 1; the steep section is from about pH 3 to 7, and the curve levels off around pH 9–10.
[chem12-s2.1.4] In that strong acid–weak base titration the equivalence point is below pH 7 (about 5), because the ammonium ion formed is a weak acid and makes the solution slightly acidic.
[chem12-s2.1.5] Weak acid–weak base (ethanoic acid and ammonia): has no steep section; the pH changes gradually through the equivalence point, at about pH 7.
[chem12-s2.1.6] Diagram: the four curves sketched on pH–volume axes, with the steep sections and equivalence points marked, and the colour-change ranges of phenolphthalein and methyl orange shaded.
[chem12-s2.2.b] An indicator is a substance that changes colour over a particular pH range, and it is used to show when a titration has reached its end point. The curve tells you which indicator will change colour at the right moment.
[chem12-s2.2.0] An indicator is suitable if its colour-change range lies completely within the steep section of the curve, so that one drop at the equivalence point changes its colour.
[chem12-s2.2.1] Strong acid–strong base: phenolphthalein (pH 8.3–10) or methyl orange (pH 3.1–4.4); both ranges lie within the steep section.
[chem12-s2.2.2] Weak acid–strong base (e.g. vinegar with NaOH): phenolphthalein; methyl orange would change colour too early, before the steep section is reached.
[chem12-s2.2.3] Strong acid–weak base (e.g. HCl with sodium carbonate or ammonia): methyl orange; phenolphthalein would change colour too early, while the solution is still alkaline and before the equivalence point.
[chem12-s2.3.b] Titrating a weak acid with a weak base is a problem case, because the curve has no steep section to signal the equivalence point. The specification asks you to know why.
[chem12-s2.3.0] With no steep section, no indicator changes colour sharply at the equivalence point, so no indicator is suitable and the end point is unclear.
[chem12-s2.3.1] The curve has only a gentle point of inflection (the place where the curve changes its direction of bending), which is hard to locate from the data, and small errors in pH readings move it a lot.
[chem12-s2.3.2] The equivalence point must instead be found from the curve itself, at the point of steepest slope, located with a pH meter and many small additions; or such titrations are avoided.
[chem12-s2.3.3] The specification limits this topic to monoprotic acids and bases (each able to give or accept only one H⁺ per molecule) with a single inflection point.
[chem12-s2.4.b] Many acid–base indicators are themselves weak acids, so their colour depends on the position of an equilibrium (a reversible reaction in which forward and backward rates are equal). Le Châtelier's principle tells you which way that equilibrium moves.
[chem12-s2.4.0] Many indicators are weak acids, written HIn, in which the undissociated molecule HIn and its conjugate base In⁻ (the form left after HIn loses H⁺) have different colours.
[chem12-s2.4.1] In water an equilibrium is set up: HIn(aq) ⇌ H⁺(aq) + In⁻(aq).
[chem12-s2.4.2] Le Châtelier's principle: if a system at equilibrium is disturbed, it shifts to oppose the change.
[chem12-s2.4.3] Adding acid increases [H⁺] (the concentration of hydrogen ions), so the equilibrium shifts to the left: most of the indicator is HIn, and its colour is seen.
[chem12-s2.4.4] Adding base removes H⁺ (H⁺ + OH⁻ → H₂O), so the equilibrium shifts to the right to replace it: most of the indicator is In⁻, and its colour is seen.
[chem12-s2.4.5] Phenolphthalein: HIn is colourless and In⁻ is pink.
[chem12-s2.4.6] Methyl orange: HIn is red and In⁻ is yellow.
[chem12-s2.5.b] A few indicators are weak bases instead. The same reasoning applies, but now the shifting equilibrium involves hydroxide ions, OH⁻, rather than H⁺.
[chem12-s2.5.0] A weak base indicator can be written InOH, with InOH and In⁺ having different colours: InOH(aq) ⇌ In⁺(aq) + OH⁻(aq).
[chem12-s2.5.1] Adding acid removes OH⁻, so the equilibrium shifts to the right to replace it and the In⁺ colour is seen.
[chem12-s2.5.2] Adding base increases [OH⁻], so the equilibrium shifts to the left and the InOH colour is seen.
[chem12-s2.6.b] An indicator does not flip colour at a single pH. It changes gradually over a range, and each indicator has its own range, which is why the choice of indicator matters.
[chem12-s2.6.0] The colour change happens over a range of about two pH units, centred on the pH at which [HIn] = [In⁻].
[chem12-s2.6.1] At the midpoint of the range both colours are present, because both forms are equally abundant; methyl orange looks orange (a mix of its red and yellow).
[chem12-s2.6.2] Different indicators are weak acids of different strengths, so they change colour at different pH values: phenolphthalein at pH 8.3–10 and methyl orange at pH 3.1–4.4.
[chem12-s2.6.3] This is why the indicator must be chosen to match the titration: its range has to fall within the steep section of that titration's curve.
[chem12-s2.6.4] Only a few drops of indicator are used, because the indicator is itself an acid and would react with some of the titrant (the solution added from the burette).
[chem12-s2.7.b] Vinegar is a solution of ethanoic acid, and its strength can be found by titration (adding a solution of known concentration from a burette until a reaction is just complete). The reaction used is a simple 1 : 1 neutralisation.
[chem12-s2.7.0] Aim: to find the concentration of ethanoic acid in a commercial vinegar by titration with a standard solution (one whose concentration is accurately known) of sodium hydroxide.
[chem12-s2.7.1] Equation: CH₃COOH(aq) + NaOH(aq) → CH₃COONa(aq) + H₂O(l), with a mole ratio of 1 : 1, so moles of acid equal moles of NaOH at the equivalence point.
[chem12-s2.7.2] The sodium hydroxide must first be standardised (its concentration found by titrating it), because it is not a primary standard, meaning it cannot be weighed out to give an exactly known concentration.
[chem12-s2.7.3] Commercial vinegar is typically about 4–6% ethanoic acid, often labelled 5%.
[chem12-s2.8.b] The method has two parts: an accurate ten-fold dilution of the vinegar, then a titration of the diluted vinegar against sodium hydroxide. There is more on the rinsing and titration technique in the notes on acid–base titrations.
[chem12-s2.8.0] Dilute the vinegar ten times: pipette 25.0 cm³ of vinegar into a 250 cm³ volumetric flask (a flask with one mark for an exact volume), make up to the mark with deionised water and invert about 20 times to mix.
[chem12-s2.8.1] Rinse the burette with deionised water, then with the standard sodium hydroxide solution, and fill it. Rinsing with the solution itself stops leftover water diluting it.
[chem12-s2.8.2] Rinse a pipette with deionised water, then with the diluted vinegar, and transfer 25.0 cm³ of diluted vinegar to a conical flask.
[chem12-s2.8.3] Add two or three drops of phenolphthalein.
[chem12-s2.8.4] Titrate with the sodium hydroxide, swirling, until the solution turns a permanent pale pink. Do a rough titration, then accurate titrations until two agree within 0.1 cm³.
[chem12-s2.8.5] Diagram: a burette of sodium hydroxide above a conical flask of diluted vinegar with phenolphthalein on a white tile; and the 250 cm³ volumetric flask used for the dilution, labelled with the 25.0 cm³ pipette.
[chem12-s2.9.b] Each step in the vinegar titration has a reason, and exams ask you to give it.
[chem12-s2.9.0] The vinegar is diluted because, undiluted, it would need a very large titre (the volume of solution run in from the burette) of 0.1 mol L⁻¹ sodium hydroxide, more than a burette holds.
[chem12-s2.9.1] Phenolphthalein is used because this is a weak acid–strong base titration, with the equivalence point in the alkaline region (about pH 8.7); methyl orange would change colour too soon.
[chem12-s2.9.2] The sodium hydroxide is placed in the burette, so the colour change is from colourless to pink, which is easy to see.
[chem12-s2.9.3] Brown or coloured vinegar makes the end point hard to see, so white (clear) vinegar is used.
[chem12-s2.10.b] The steps convert a titre into a concentration, then into mass concentration and %w/v (grams of solute per 100 cm³ of solution). The numbers here are sample data.
[chem12-s2.10.0] Mean titre: 20.8 cm³ of 0.100 mol L⁻¹ NaOH is needed for 25.0 cm³ of diluted vinegar.
[chem12-s2.10.1] Moles NaOH = 0.100 × 0.0208 = 2.08 × 10⁻³ mol, which equals the moles of ethanoic acid in 25.0 cm³ of diluted vinegar, because the mole ratio is 1 : 1.
[chem12-s2.10.2] Concentration of diluted vinegar = 2.08 × 10⁻³ mol ÷ 0.0250 L = 0.0832 mol L⁻¹.
[chem12-s2.10.3] Concentration of original vinegar = 0.0832 × 10 = 0.832 mol L⁻¹, because the vinegar was diluted ten times.
[chem12-s2.10.4] Mass concentration = 0.832 mol L⁻¹ × 60 g mol⁻¹ = 49.9 g L⁻¹, using M(CH₃COOH) = 60 g mol⁻¹.
[chem12-s2.10.5] As %w/v: 49.9 g L⁻¹ ÷ 10 = 4.99% w/v, close to the 5% typical of vinegar.
[chem12-s2.11.b] Sodium hydroxide is a hazardous chemical, and several slips in technique shift the result in a predictable direction.
[chem12-s2.11.0] Safety: sodium hydroxide is corrosive, especially to the eyes, so wear goggles and use a pipette filler.
[chem12-s2.11.1] Not rinsing the burette with sodium hydroxide leaves water in it, which dilutes the solution, so the titre is too high and the result is too high.
[chem12-s2.11.2] Overshooting the end point gives too large a titre, so the result is too high.
[chem12-s2.11.3] An air bubble below the burette tap gives a wrong titre, because the reading then does not match the volume actually delivered.
[chem12-s2.11.4] Sodium hydroxide absorbs carbon dioxide from the air over time, changing its concentration, so it should be standardised just before use.

CANDIDATES chem18-s1: 1. Dynamic equilibrium and Kc
[chem18-s1.0.b] Some reactions run to completion; others reach a balance in which the forward and reverse reactions both continue. Telling the two types apart is the starting point for the whole chapter.
[chem18-s1.0.0] Some reactions go to completion: the reactants are converted completely into products and the reaction cannot easily be reversed, e.g. burning magnesium, 2Mg(s) + O₂(g) → 2MgO(s).
[chem18-s1.0.1] A reversible reaction is one in which the products can react to form the reactants again, so both the forward and the reverse reactions take place. It is shown by the sign ⇌, a double arrow.
[chem18-s1.0.2] The forward reaction is the one from left to right as written; the reverse (back) reaction is from right to left. Both are always labelled relative to how the equation is written.
[chem18-s1.0.3] The Haber process, N₂(g) + 3H₂(g) ⇌ 2NH₃(g), makes ammonia from nitrogen and hydrogen. It is named after Fritz Haber (1868–1934); Carl Bosch (1874–1940) developed it industrially.
[chem18-s1.0.4] Hydrogen and iodine react reversibly: H₂(g) + I₂(g) ⇌ 2HI(g), where HI is hydrogen iodide gas.
[chem18-s1.0.5] Esterification (an acid reacting with an alcohol to make an ester and water) is reversible: CH₃COOH + C₂H₅OH ⇌ CH₃COOC₂H₅ + H₂O.
[chem18-s1.0.6] Nitrogen dioxide and dinitrogen tetroxide: 2NO₂(g) ⇌ N₂O₄(g). NO₂ is brown and N₂O₄ is colourless, so the colour of the mixture shows how far the reaction has moved.
[chem18-s1.0.7] Weak acids (acids that only partly split into ions in water) and acid–base indicators (dyes that change colour with acidity) are also reversible, e.g. CH₃COOH(aq) ⇌ CH₃COO⁻(aq) + H⁺(aq) and HIn ⇌ H⁺ + In⁻. There is more on this in How indicators work.
[chem18-s1.1.b] In a reversible reaction in a sealed container, the mixture settles into a balance. At chemical equilibrium both reactions continue, and their rates are equal.
[chem18-s1.1.0] Chemical equilibrium is a state of dynamic balance in a reversible reaction in which the rate of the forward reaction (how fast reactants turn into products) equals the rate of the reverse reaction.
[chem18-s1.1.1] It is called dynamic because both reactions are still happening: reactant particles are still forming products and product particles are still forming reactants, at the same rate.
[chem18-s1.1.2] Because substances are formed and used up at the same rate, their concentrations (amounts per litre) stay constant at equilibrium, so observable properties such as colour, pressure and density stop changing.
[chem18-s1.1.3] The concentrations are constant but not equal: at equilibrium there may be much more product than reactant, or the reverse. It is the rates that are equal, not the amounts.
[chem18-s1.1.4] Static equilibrium is different: nothing moves at all, e.g. a metre stick balanced at its centre.
[chem18-s1.1.5] Analogy: a person walking up a down-escalator at the same speed it moves stays in the same place, although both movements continue. Likewise nothing seems to change at equilibrium, but both reactions carry on.
[chem18-s1.2.b] Equilibrium only forms if nothing can escape. This is why the container matters.
[chem18-s1.2.0] Equilibrium can be reached only in a closed system, where no reactants or products can enter or leave; energy such as heat can still pass to and from the surroundings.
[chem18-s1.2.1] Heating calcium carbonate in a sealed container, CaCO₃(s) ⇌ CaO(s) + CO₂(g): the CO₂ gas builds up and equilibrium is reached.
[chem18-s1.2.2] Heating calcium carbonate in an open container: the CO₂ escapes, so the reverse reaction cannot happen and the calcium carbonate decomposes completely.
[chem18-s1.2.3] A fizzy drink in a closed bottle contains CO₂(g) ⇌ CO₂(aq) at equilibrium (gas above the drink and gas dissolved in it); once the bottle is opened the gas escapes and the drink goes flat.
[chem18-s1.3.b] Equilibrium is not instant. The two rates start far apart and move towards each other until they meet.
[chem18-s1.3.0] At the start, when only reactants are present, the forward rate is high and the reverse rate is zero, because there are no products yet to react back.
[chem18-s1.3.1] As reactants are used up the forward rate falls; as products build up the reverse rate rises.
[chem18-s1.3.2] Equilibrium is reached when the two rates become equal; from then on all concentrations are constant. This is why equilibrium is reached some time after the reactants are mixed.
[chem18-s1.3.3] The same equilibrium is reached from either direction: starting with 1 mol of H₂ and 1 mol of I₂, or with 2 mol of HI only, in the same sealed flask at the same temperature, gives the same equilibrium mixture.
[chem18-s1.3.4] Concentration–time graph for N₂ + 3H₂ ⇌ 2NH₃ starting with N₂ and H₂: the reactant curves fall and the product curve rises from zero, each changing quickly at first because the reactants are most concentrated then.
[chem18-s1.3.5] All three curves level off (become horizontal) at the same moment, when equilibrium is reached. H₂ falls three times as fast as N₂, and NH₃ rises twice as fast as N₂ falls, matching the 1 : 3 : 2 ratio in the equation.
[chem18-s1.3.6] Rate–time graph: the forward-rate curve falls and the reverse-rate curve rises from zero until they meet and continue as one horizontal line at equilibrium.
[chem18-s1.3.7] Diagram: draw (a) concentration against time, with the reactant and product curves levelling off and the moment equilibrium is reached marked; and (b) forward and reverse rates against time, meeting at equilibrium.
[chem18-s1.4.b] At equilibrium the concentrations are not random: for a given reaction at a given temperature they always fit one fixed pattern. That pattern is a number, the equilibrium constant Kc.
[chem18-s1.4.0] The equilibrium law: at equilibrium, at a fixed temperature, the concentrations of products and reactants are related by a constant, the equilibrium constant Kc.
[chem18-s1.4.1] For aA + bB ⇌ cC + dD (where a, b, c and d are the coefficients, the numbers in front of each formula in the balanced equation): Kc = [C]ᶜ[D]ᵈ ÷ [A]ᵃ[B]ᵇ. Square brackets mean the concentration in mol L⁻¹ at equilibrium.
[chem18-s1.4.2] Products go on top and reactants on the bottom; each concentration is raised to the power of its coefficient in the balanced equation, and the concentrations are multiplied, not added.
[chem18-s1.4.3] Example: N₂(g) + 3H₂(g) ⇌ 2NH₃(g) gives Kc = [NH₃]² ÷ [N₂][H₂]³. The 3 in front of H₂ becomes the power 3.
[chem18-s1.4.4] Example: H₂(g) + I₂(g) ⇌ 2HI(g) gives Kc = [HI]² ÷ [H₂][I₂], with HI squared because its coefficient is 2.
[chem18-s1.4.5] Example: PCl₅(g) ⇌ PCl₃(g) + Cl₂(g) gives Kc = [PCl₃][Cl₂] ÷ [PCl₅].
[chem18-s1.4.6] Example: esterification, CH₃COOH + C₂H₅OH ⇌ CH₃COOC₂H₅ + H₂O, gives Kc = [CH₃COOC₂H₅][H₂O] ÷ [CH₃COOH][C₂H₅OH]; water is included because it is a product, not the solvent.
[chem18-s1.4.7] Example: Fe³⁺(aq) + SCN⁻(aq) ⇌ FeSCN²⁺(aq) gives Kc = [FeSCN²⁺] ÷ [Fe³⁺][SCN⁻].
[chem18-s1.5.b] Kc is a ratio of concentrations, so whether it has units depends on how many concentrations are on top and on the bottom.
[chem18-s1.5.0] To find the units, substitute mol L⁻¹ for each concentration in the Kc expression and cancel.
[chem18-s1.5.1] When the total of the powers is the same on top and bottom, as in H₂ + I₂ ⇌ 2HI or esterification, the units cancel and Kc has no units.
[chem18-s1.5.2] For N₂ + 3H₂ ⇌ 2NH₃: (mol L⁻¹)² ÷ (mol L⁻¹)⁴ = (mol L⁻¹)⁻², that is L² mol⁻², because there are fewer powers on top than on the bottom.
[chem18-s1.5.3] For PCl₅ ⇌ PCl₃ + Cl₂: (mol L⁻¹)² ÷ (mol L⁻¹), so the units of Kc are mol L⁻¹.
[chem18-s1.6.b] Many changes alter the composition of an equilibrium mixture, but the constant itself is much harder to move. Only one factor changes its value.
[chem18-s1.6.0] Kc changes only with temperature, so a value of Kc must always be quoted with its temperature.
[chem18-s1.6.1] Changing a concentration does not change Kc: the system shifts until the ratio in the Kc expression has its original value again.
[chem18-s1.6.2] Changing the pressure of a gas mixture does not change Kc: the concentrations change, but the new equilibrium mixture gives the same value of Kc.
[chem18-s1.6.3] A catalyst (a substance that speeds up a reaction without being used up) does not change Kc: it speeds up the forward and reverse reactions equally, so equilibrium is reached sooner with the same composition.
[chem18-s1.6.4] Exothermic forward reaction (one that gives out heat, ΔH negative): raising the temperature decreases Kc, so less product is present at equilibrium; lowering the temperature increases Kc. Example: N₂ + 3H₂ ⇌ 2NH₃, with ΔH = −92 kJ mol⁻¹.
[chem18-s1.6.5] Endothermic forward reaction (one that takes in heat, ΔH positive): raising the temperature increases Kc; lowering it decreases Kc. Example: PCl₅ ⇌ PCl₃ + Cl₂, which is endothermic in the forward direction; PCl₅ decomposes further on heating.
[chem18-s1.6.6] So if Kc rises with temperature the forward reaction is endothermic; if Kc falls with temperature the forward reaction is exothermic.
[chem18-s1.7.b] The size of Kc tells you where the equilibrium lies, and comparing it with the current mixture tells you which way a reaction will go.
[chem18-s1.7.0] A large Kc (much greater than 1) means the equilibrium mixture is mainly products: the equilibrium lies to the right and the reaction goes almost to completion. A small Kc (much less than 1) means mainly reactants: it lies to the left and little product forms.
[chem18-s1.7.1] A Kc near 1 means appreciable amounts of both reactants and products are present at equilibrium. Esterification has Kc ≈ 4 (at room temperature), so starting with 1 mol each of ethanoic acid and ethanol, about two-thirds of a mole of ester forms.
[chem18-s1.7.2] Kc tells how far a reaction goes, not how fast: a reaction with a large Kc may be very slow, e.g. without a catalyst.
[chem18-s1.7.3] The reaction quotient, Q, has the same expression as Kc but uses the concentrations at any moment, not necessarily at equilibrium.
[chem18-s1.7.4] If Q is less than Kc, the mixture has too little product and the reaction proceeds forward (to the right) until Q = Kc; if Q is greater than Kc, it proceeds in reverse (to the left); if Q = Kc, the mixture is at equilibrium.
[chem18-s1.7.5] Worked prediction (sample data): for esterification with Kc = 4.0, a mixture with 0.50 mol each of acid, ethanol, ester and water has Q = (0.50 × 0.50) ÷ (0.50 × 0.50) = 1.0, which is less than Kc, so more ester forms.
[chem18-s1.7.6] This explains Le Châtelier’s principle (a system at equilibrium shifts to oppose a change) mathematically: adding a reactant makes the denominator larger, Q falls below Kc, and the reaction proceeds forward until Q = Kc again.
[chem18-s1.7.7] Writing the equation the other way round inverts Kc: for 2HI ⇌ H₂ + I₂, Kc = [H₂][I₂] ÷ [HI]², which is 1 ÷ Kc for H₂ + I₂ ⇌ 2HI at the same temperature.
[chem18-s1.8.b] Kc calculations follow the same routine every time. Working through a table of amounts keeps the steps in order and avoids the usual mistakes.
[chem18-s1.8.0] 1. Write the balanced equation and the Kc expression.
[chem18-s1.8.1] 2. Convert any masses to moles using n = mass ÷ M, where M is the molar mass in g mol⁻¹.
[chem18-s1.8.2] 3. Set up a table with rows Initial (moles at the start), Change (moles reacting or forming, in the ratio of the coefficients) and Equilibrium (initial + change) for every species.
[chem18-s1.8.3] 4. Divide each equilibrium amount by the volume of the container in litres to get concentrations in mol L⁻¹. When the total number of moles is the same on each side, as in H₂ + I₂ ⇌ 2HI or esterification, the volume cancels, so moles can be used directly.
[chem18-s1.8.4] 5. Substitute the concentrations into the Kc expression and calculate; give the answer to 3 significant figures, with units if asked.
[chem18-s1.8.5] To find equilibrium amounts from Kc: let x be the amount that reacts, write each equilibrium amount in terms of x, substitute into the Kc expression and solve for x.
[chem18-s1.8.6] If both sides of the resulting equation are perfect squares, take the square root of both sides. Otherwise rearrange into a quadratic, ax² + bx + c = 0, and use x = (−b ± √(b² − 4ac)) ÷ 2a.
[chem18-s1.8.7] Choose the root that makes physical sense: no equilibrium amount can be negative, so x cannot exceed the starting amount of a reactant.
[chem18-s1.9.b] The simplest type: every equilibrium amount is given, so convert to concentrations and substitute.
[chem18-s1.9.0] Data: H₂(g) + I₂(g) ⇌ 2HI(g) in a 2.00 L flask at a fixed temperature; at equilibrium there are 0.100 mol H₂, 0.150 mol I₂ and 0.840 mol HI.
[chem18-s1.9.1] [H₂] = 0.100 mol ÷ 2.00 L = 0.0500 mol L⁻¹, because Kc needs concentrations, not moles.
[chem18-s1.9.2] [I₂] = 0.150 mol ÷ 2.00 L = 0.0750 mol L⁻¹.
[chem18-s1.9.3] [HI] = 0.840 mol ÷ 2.00 L = 0.420 mol L⁻¹.
[chem18-s1.9.4] Kc = [HI]² ÷ [H₂][I₂] = (0.420)² ÷ (0.0500 × 0.0750) = 0.1764 ÷ 0.00375 = 47.0 (no units, because the powers on top and bottom match).
[chem18-s1.10.b] Here only one equilibrium amount is given. Use the mole ratio from the equation to work out all the others.
[chem18-s1.10.0] Data: 2.00 mol N₂ and 6.00 mol H₂ are sealed in a 4.00 L vessel; at equilibrium 1.20 mol NH₃ is present, for N₂(g) + 3H₂(g) ⇌ 2NH₃(g).
[chem18-s1.10.1] Change: forming 1.20 mol NH₃ uses 0.600 mol N₂ and 1.80 mol H₂, in the ratio 1 : 3 : 2 from the coefficients.
[chem18-s1.10.2] Equilibrium amounts: N₂ = 2.00 − 0.600 = 1.40 mol; H₂ = 6.00 − 1.80 = 4.20 mol; NH₃ = 1.20 mol.
[chem18-s1.10.3] Concentrations (÷ 4.00 L): [N₂] = 0.350 mol L⁻¹; [H₂] = 1.05 mol L⁻¹; [NH₃] = 0.300 mol L⁻¹.
[chem18-s1.10.4] Kc = [NH₃]² ÷ [N₂][H₂]³ = (0.300)² ÷ (0.350 × 1.05³) = 0.0900 ÷ 0.405 = 0.222 L² mol⁻².
[chem18-s1.11.b] Here the starting amount is a mass, and the extent of reaction is a percentage dissociated (the percentage of the starting compound that has split up into products).
[chem18-s1.11.0] Data: 41.7 g of PCl₅ is heated in a sealed 5.00 L vessel; at equilibrium 40.0% has dissociated, for PCl₅(g) ⇌ PCl₃(g) + Cl₂(g).
[chem18-s1.11.1] Molar mass: M(PCl₅) = 31 + 5 × 35.5 = 208.5 g mol⁻¹.
[chem18-s1.11.2] Moles PCl₅ = 41.7 g ÷ 208.5 g mol⁻¹ = 0.200 mol, using n = mass ÷ M.
[chem18-s1.11.3] Moles dissociated = 40.0% of 0.200 mol = 0.0800 mol, because the percentage applies to the starting amount.
[chem18-s1.11.4] At equilibrium: PCl₅ = 0.120 mol; PCl₃ = 0.0800 mol; Cl₂ = 0.0800 mol, because each PCl₅ that dissociates gives one PCl₃ and one Cl₂.
[chem18-s1.11.5] Concentrations (÷ 5.00 L): [PCl₅] = 0.0240 mol L⁻¹; [PCl₃] = [Cl₂] = 0.0160 mol L⁻¹.
[chem18-s1.11.6] Kc = [PCl₃][Cl₂] ÷ [PCl₅] = (0.0160 × 0.0160) ÷ 0.0240 = 0.0107 mol L⁻¹.
[chem18-s1.12.b] Now Kc is given and you find an amount. When the expression is a perfect square (the same bracket squared on top and bottom), taking a square root avoids a quadratic.
[chem18-s1.12.0] Data: 1.00 mol of ethanoic acid and 1.00 mol of ethanol are mixed and reach equilibrium; Kc = 4.00 for CH₃COOH + C₂H₅OH ⇌ CH₃COOC₂H₅ + H₂O.
[chem18-s1.12.1] Let x mol of ester form: at equilibrium the acid and the ethanol are each (1.00 − x) mol, and the ester and the water are each x mol; the volume cancels.
[chem18-s1.12.2] Kc = x² ÷ (1.00 − x)² = 4.00.
[chem18-s1.12.3] Taking the square root of both sides, which removes the squares, gives x ÷ (1.00 − x) = 2.00.
[chem18-s1.12.4] Rearranging: x = 2.00 − 2.00x, so 3.00x = 2.00 and x = 0.667 mol of ester.
[chem18-s1.12.5] The acid and the ethanol each remain at 1.00 − 0.667 = 0.333 mol, because each mole of ester formed used up one mole of each.
[chem18-s1.12.6] Mass of ester = 0.667 mol × 88 g mol⁻¹ = 58.7 g, using M(CH₃COOC₂H₅) = 88 g mol⁻¹.
[chem18-s1.13.b] When the expression is not a perfect square, it rearranges to a quadratic equation (one containing x²) with two roots, and only one of them is physically possible (the other would need more of a reactant than is present).
[chem18-s1.13.0] Data: 1.00 mol H₂ and 2.00 mol I₂ are placed in a 1.00 L flask; Kc = 49.0 at this temperature, for H₂(g) + I₂(g) ⇌ 2HI(g).
[chem18-s1.13.1] Let x mol of H₂ react: at equilibrium H₂ = (1.00 − x), I₂ = (2.00 − x) and HI = 2x; as the volume is 1.00 L, these amounts are also the concentrations in mol L⁻¹.
[chem18-s1.13.2] 49.0 = (2x)² ÷ [(1.00 − x)(2.00 − x)], so 4x² = 49.0(2.00 − 3.00x + x²).
[chem18-s1.13.3] Rearranging gives 45.0x² − 147x + 98.0 = 0, which is the form ax² + bx + c = 0 needed for the formula.
[chem18-s1.13.4] x = (147 ± √(147² − 4 × 45.0 × 98.0)) ÷ (2 × 45.0) = (147 ± 63.0) ÷ 90.0, so x = 2.33 or x = 0.933.
[chem18-s1.13.5] x = 2.33 is impossible because it exceeds the 1.00 mol of H₂ present, so x = 0.933.
[chem18-s1.13.6] Equilibrium concentrations: [H₂] = 0.0667 mol L⁻¹; [I₂] = 1.07 mol L⁻¹; [HI] = 1.87 mol L⁻¹.
[chem18-s1.13.7] Check: 1.87² ÷ (0.0667 × 1.07) ≈ 49, which matches Kc.

CANDIDATES chem8-s2: 2. Solutions and concentration
[chem8-s2.0.b] Much of chemistry happens in solution, so you need exact words for the parts of one. Every later idea in this subsection, from concentration to dilution, is built on them.
[chem8-s2.0.0] A solution is a uniform (homogeneous) mixture of a solute dissolved in a solvent. Uniform means it looks and behaves the same throughout, with no layers or visible particles.
[chem8-s2.0.1] The solute is the substance that dissolves, and the solvent is the substance it dissolves in. When the solvent is water the solution is called an aqueous solution.
[chem8-s2.1.b] A solvent can only hold so much solute at a given temperature. The names unsaturated, saturated and supersaturated describe how close a solution is to that limit, or beyond it.
[chem8-s2.1.0] An unsaturated solution can still dissolve more solute at that temperature, so extra solute added to it simply dissolves.
[chem8-s2.1.1] A saturated solution contains the maximum amount of solute that can dissolve at that temperature, with undissolved solid present. Any more solute added just sits at the bottom.
[chem8-s2.1.2] A supersaturated solution contains more dissolved solute than a saturated solution holds at that temperature. It is unstable, which means it is easily upset and will not stay as it is.
[chem8-s2.1.3] A supersaturated solution is made by cooling a hot saturated solution carefully. A solid is usually more soluble when hot, so the cooled solution is left holding more than it normally could.
[chem8-s2.1.4] Adding a small seed crystal (a tiny crystal of the solute) to a supersaturated solution makes the excess solute crystallise suddenly, because the crystal gives the dissolved solute a surface to grow on.
[chem8-s2.1.5] Examples of solutes that form supersaturated solutions: sodium thiosulfate and sodium ethanoate.
[chem8-s2.2.b] Saying a solution is strong or weak is not precise enough for chemistry. Concentration gives the amount of solute in a fixed volume, so it can be calculated with and compared.
[chem8-s2.2.0] Concentration is the amount of solute dissolved in a given volume of solution, usually in mol L⁻¹ (molarity, M, meaning moles of solute per litre) or g L⁻¹ (grams of solute per litre).
[chem8-s2.2.1] Concentration (mol L⁻¹) = moles of solute ÷ volume of solution in litres, so c = n ÷ V and n = c × V. Remember that the volume is that of the whole solution, not just the solvent.
[chem8-s2.2.2] Model: the same number of solute particles in a smaller volume gives a higher concentration, because the particles are packed more closely. Diluting spreads the same particles through a larger volume.
[chem8-s2.2.3] A standard solution is a solution whose concentration is accurately known. It is the starting point for finding the concentrations of other solutions.
[chem8-s2.3.b] Here are two calculations. One finds the concentration from a mass of solute, and the other works backwards from a concentration to the mass to weigh out. Convert cm³ to litres first by dividing by 1000.
[chem8-s2.3.0] Finding a concentration: 5.30 g of anhydrous sodium carbonate (Na₂CO₃, M = 106 g mol⁻¹) is dissolved. Anhydrous means it has no water of crystallisation. Moles: n = 5.30 ÷ 106 = 0.0500 mol.
[chem8-s2.3.1] The 0.0500 mol is made up to 500 cm³ (0.500 L). Then c = 0.0500 ÷ 0.500 = 0.100 mol L⁻¹.
[chem8-s2.3.2] Finding a mass needed: for 250 cm³ of 0.100 M sodium hydroxide (M = 40 g mol⁻¹), first find the moles from n = c × V = 0.100 × 0.250 = 0.0250 mol.
[chem8-s2.3.3] Then change moles to mass by multiplying by the molar mass (the mass of one mole): m = 0.0250 × 40 = 1.00 g.
[chem8-s2.4.b] To make a standard solution by weighing, you must trust the mass on the balance to be the true amount of the substance. Only certain substances are reliable enough, and they are called primary standards.
[chem8-s2.4.0] A primary standard is a substance that can be weighed out accurately to make a standard solution directly.
[chem8-s2.4.1] A primary standard is available in a very pure state, so the mass weighed is all the substance and not impurities.
[chem8-s2.4.2] A primary standard is stable in air: it does not absorb water or carbon dioxide, or react with oxygen. If it did, its mass would change while being weighed.
[chem8-s2.4.3] A primary standard has a known formula, so its molar mass and the moles weighed out can be calculated, and it dissolves readily in water.
[chem8-s2.4.4] A primary standard preferably has a high molar mass, which reduces weighing errors. A larger mass is needed for each mole, so a small error on the balance is a smaller fraction of the whole.
[chem8-s2.5.b] Which substance you choose depends on the type of titration (a method that reacts measured volumes of two solutions to find an unknown concentration). Some familiar laboratory chemicals fail the tests for a primary standard.
[chem8-s2.5.0] Anhydrous sodium carbonate is a primary standard used in acid–base titrations (reactions between an acid and a base).
[chem8-s2.5.1] Ammonium iron(II) sulfate, (NH₄)₂Fe(SO₄)₂·6H₂O, dissolved in dilute sulfuric acid, is a primary standard for redox titrations (reactions involving the transfer of electrons).
[chem8-s2.5.2] Sodium hydroxide is not a primary standard because it absorbs water and carbon dioxide from the air, so its mass is not the true mass of sodium hydroxide.
[chem8-s2.5.3] Hydrochloric acid is not a primary standard because its concentration changes as HCl evaporates.
[chem8-s2.5.4] Solutions of sodium hydroxide and hydrochloric acid are standardised, meaning their exact concentration is found, by titration against a primary standard.
[chem8-s2.6.b] Making a standard solution from a weighed primary standard is an investigation with a fixed method. The aim is a solution of accurately known concentration, here 250 cm³ in a volumetric flask (a flask with a single mark showing an exact volume).
[chem8-s2.6.0] 1. Weigh accurately (to 0.01 g or better) the calculated mass of the primary standard on a clock glass or weighing boat.
[chem8-s2.6.1] 2. Transfer the solid to a beaker, and rinse the clock glass into the beaker with deionised water from a wash bottle, so that none of the weighed solid is left behind.
[chem8-s2.6.2] 3. Dissolve the solid in a small volume of deionised water, stirring with a glass rod.
[chem8-s2.6.3] 4. Pour the solution through a funnel into a volumetric flask of the required volume, e.g. 250 cm³.
[chem8-s2.6.4] 5. Rinse the beaker, glass rod and funnel into the flask with deionised water so that no solute is lost.
[chem8-s2.6.5] 6. Add deionised water until the flask is near the mark, then add the last drops with a dropper until the bottom of the meniscus (the curved surface of the liquid) sits on the graduation mark, viewed at eye level.
[chem8-s2.6.6] 7. Stopper the flask and invert it about 20 times so the solution is uniform.
[chem8-s2.7.b] The choice of glassware decides how accurate the final concentration is. The diagrams below show the apparatus used in the method.
[chem8-s2.7.0] Precision and accuracy: the volumetric flask holds an accurately known volume, whereas a beaker or measuring cylinder is not accurate enough.
[chem8-s2.7.1] Diagram: a volumetric flask with its graduation mark, and the meniscus shown at eye level.
[chem8-s2.7.2] Diagram: a funnel in the neck of the volumetric flask during the transfer.
[chem8-s2.7.3] Diagram: a beaker with a glass rod stirring the dissolving solid.
[chem8-s2.8.b] A concentrated solution can be turned into a weaker one by adding solvent. The solute is not changed, only spread through more volume, which gives a simple equation to use.
[chem8-s2.8.0] Diluting adds solvent, so the number of moles of solute stays the same. This gives c₁V₁ = c₂V₂, where c₁ and V₁ are the original solution and c₂ and V₂ the diluted one.
[chem8-s2.8.1] Example: to make 250 cm³ of 0.10 M HCl from 1.0 M HCl, V₁ = (0.10 × 250) ÷ 1.0 = 25 cm³.
[chem8-s2.8.2] Pipette 25 cm³ of the 1.0 M acid into a 250 cm³ volumetric flask and make up to the mark with water.
[chem8-s2.8.3] Use a pipette (a glass tube that measures one exact volume), with a pipette filler to draw up the liquid, for the volume of the original solution, and a volumetric flask for the final volume.
[chem8-s2.8.4] Diagram: a pipette with a pipette filler, filled to the graduation mark.
[chem8-s2.9.b] Sometimes a whole range of concentrations is needed, such as for a set of test solutions. Rather than diluting from the stock solution every time, you dilute step by step.
[chem8-s2.9.0] Serial dilution is a series of dilutions, each using the previous solution, usually by the same factor.
[chem8-s2.9.1] Example: starting from 1.0 M, take 10 cm³ and make it up to 100 cm³ to give 0.10 M. Repeating from that solution gives 0.010 M and then 0.0010 M.
[chem8-s2.9.2] Serial dilution is used to make a range of concentrations accurately.
[chem8-s2.10.b] Concentration can be expressed in several ways depending on the substance and the use. Each unit says how much solute is present in a fixed amount of solution or mixture.
[chem8-s2.10.0] mol L⁻¹ (molarity, M): moles of solute per litre of solution.
[chem8-s2.10.1] g L⁻¹: grams of solute per litre of solution.
[chem8-s2.10.2] %w/v (weight per volume): grams of solute per 100 cm³ of solution. For example, 5% w/v glucose contains 5 g per 100 cm³.
[chem8-s2.10.3] %v/v (volume per volume): cm³ of solute per 100 cm³ of solution, used for a liquid dissolved in a liquid. For example, wine labelled 12% v/v contains 12 cm³ of ethanol in every 100 cm³.
[chem8-s2.10.4] %w/w (weight per weight): grams of solute per 100 g of the whole mixture, used for solids and creams. For example, 1% w/w hydrocortisone cream has 1 g in every 100 g.
[chem8-s2.11.b] The molar mass links moles to grams, and the fact that 100 cm³ is one-tenth of a litre links g L⁻¹ to %w/v. Between them you can move from any one of these units to any other.
[chem8-s2.11.0] mol L⁻¹ to g L⁻¹: multiply by the molar mass. Example: 0.10 M NaOH × 40 g mol⁻¹ = 4.0 g L⁻¹.
[chem8-s2.11.1] g L⁻¹ to mol L⁻¹: divide by the molar mass. Example: 9.8 g L⁻¹ of H₂SO₄ ÷ 98 g mol⁻¹ = 0.10 mol L⁻¹.
[chem8-s2.11.2] g L⁻¹ to %w/v: divide by 10, since 100 cm³ is one-tenth of a litre. Example: 4.0 g L⁻¹ = 0.40% w/v.
[chem8-s2.11.3] To reverse the step, multiply %w/v by 10 to get g L⁻¹.
[chem8-s2.11.4] %w/v to mol L⁻¹: multiply by 10 to get g L⁻¹, then divide by the molar mass. Example: 0.9% w/v physiological saline (NaCl, M = 58.5 g mol⁻¹): 0.9 × 10 = 9.0 g L⁻¹.
[chem8-s2.11.5] Then 9.0 g L⁻¹ ÷ 58.5 g mol⁻¹ = 0.15 mol L⁻¹.
[chem8-s2.12.b] Percentages for liquids and creams tell you how much of the active substance is in a given amount of the product. These calculations turn the percentage back into an actual volume or mass.
[chem8-s2.12.0] %v/v to volume: volume of solute = volume of solution × %v/v ÷ 100.
[chem8-s2.12.1] Example: ethanol in 750 cm³ of 12% v/v wine = 750 × 12 ÷ 100 = 90 cm³.
[chem8-s2.12.2] %v/v to mass, using density (the mass of each cm³ of the substance): mass = volume × density.
[chem8-s2.12.3] Example: 90 cm³ of ethanol × 0.789 g cm⁻³ = 71 g.
[chem8-s2.12.4] %w/w to mass: mass of solute = mass of mixture × %w/w ÷ 100.
[chem8-s2.12.5] Example: 30 g of a 1% w/w cream contains 30 × 1 ÷ 100 = 0.30 g of the active ingredient.
[chem8-s2.13.b] Some solutions are so dilute that mol L⁻¹ gives very tiny numbers. Parts per million is a convenient unit for these, and it is used for pollutants and similar substances.
[chem8-s2.13.0] p.p.m. (parts per million) is the number of parts of solute in one million parts of solution or mixture. It is used for very dilute solutions, such as pollutants and dissolved substances in water.
[chem8-s2.13.1] For dilute aqueous solutions, 1 p.p.m. = 1 mg of solute per litre of solution (1 mg L⁻¹). This holds because 1 L of water has a mass of about 1 kg, which is 10⁶ mg.
[chem8-s2.13.2] For solids, 1 p.p.m. = 1 mg per kg.
[chem8-s2.13.3] Example: 0.0050 g of a substance in 2.5 L of water. First 0.0050 g = 5.0 mg. Then 5.0 mg ÷ 2.5 L = 2.0 p.p.m.
[chem8-s2.13.4] 1 p.p.m. = 0.001 g L⁻¹ = 0.0001% w/v.
[chem8-s2.13.5] Example: 1.0 mg L⁻¹ of fluoride is 1.0 p.p.m. Fluoridated water in Ireland is about 0.6–0.8 p.p.m. (mg L⁻¹).
[chem8-s2.13.6] p.p.m. to mol L⁻¹: convert to g L⁻¹ first, e.g. 2.0 p.p.m. = 2.0 mg L⁻¹ = 0.0020 g L⁻¹, then divide by the molar mass.
