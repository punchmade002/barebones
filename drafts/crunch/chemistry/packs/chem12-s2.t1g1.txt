TARGET chem12-s2: 2. Titration curves, indicators and vinegar
[chem12-s2.0.b] A pH curve is a graph of pH against the volume of one solution added to another. You build it from your own measurements (primary data), and it shows you where the reaction is complete and which indicator would signal that point.
[chem12-s2.0.0] Aim: to plot how the pH changes as an alkali is added to an acid, using primary data (results you measure yourself), and to use the curve to choose an indicator.
[chem12-s2.0.1] First calibrate the pH meter, or the pH sensor and data-logger (a device that records readings automatically), with buffer solutions of known pH, so that its readings can be trusted.
[chem12-s2.0.2] Pipette (measure accurately with a fixed-volume glass pipette) 25.0 cm³ of the acid into a beaker on a magnetic stirrer, or into a beaker that is swirled. Put the pH probe in and record the starting pH.
[chem12-s2.0.3] Add the alkali from a burette (a graduated tube with a tap) in 1 cm³ portions, recording the pH after each.
[chem12-s2.0.4] Near the equivalence point (the point where the acid and alkali have reacted in exactly the amounts in the equation), add 0.1–0.2 cm³ portions, because the pH changes rapidly there. Continue well past it.
[chem12-s2.0.5] Plot pH on the y-axis against the volume of alkali added on the x-axis.
[chem12-s2.0.6] The equivalence point is the midpoint of the steep, vertical section of the curve.
[chem12-s2.0.7] Diagram: a burette above a beaker on a magnetic stirrer, with a pH probe connected to a pH meter or data-logger; label burette, beaker, stirrer, probe and meter.
[chem12-s2.1.b] The shape of a pH curve depends on whether the acid and the base are strong (fully ionised in water) or weak (only partly ionised). Each curve below is for 25 cm³ of acid with base added from a burette, using 0.1 mol L⁻¹ solutions throughout.
[chem12-s2.1.0] Strong acid–strong base (HCl and NaOH): starts at about pH 1; rises slowly, then very steeply from about pH 3 to pH 11, with the equivalence point at pH 7; levels off near pH 13.
[chem12-s2.1.1] Weak acid–strong base (ethanoic acid and NaOH): starts higher, at about pH 3, because the acid is only partly ionised, with a gentler rise. The steep section is from about pH 7 to 11.
[chem12-s2.1.2] In that weak acid–strong base titration the equivalence point is above pH 7 (about 8.7), because the ethanoate ion formed is a weak base and makes the solution slightly alkaline.
[chem12-s2.1.3] Strong acid–weak base (HCl and aqueous ammonia): starts at about pH 1; the steep section is from about pH 3 to 7, and the curve levels off around pH 9–10.
[chem12-s2.1.4] In that strong acid–weak base titration the equivalence point is below pH 7 (about 5), because the ammonium ion formed is a weak acid and makes the solution slightly acidic.
[chem12-s2.1.5] Weak acid–weak base (ethanoic acid and ammonia): has no steep section; the pH changes gradually through the equivalence point, at about pH 7.
[chem12-s2.1.6] Diagram: the four curves sketched on pH–volume axes, with the steep sections and equivalence points marked, and the colour-change ranges of phenolphthalein and methyl orange shaded.
[chem12-s2.2.b] An indicator is a substance that changes colour over a particular pH range, and it is used to show when a titration has reached its end point. The curve tells you which indicator will change colour at the right moment.
[chem12-s2.2.0] An indicator is suitable if its colour-change range lies completely within the steep section of the curve, so that one drop at the equivalence point changes its colour.
[chem12-s2.2.1] Strong acid–strong base: phenolphthalein (pH 8.3–10) or methyl orange (pH 3.1–4.4); both ranges lie within the steep section.
[chem12-s2.2.2] Weak acid–strong base (e.g. vinegar with NaOH): phenolphthalein; methyl orange would change colour too early, before the steep section is reached.
[chem12-s2.2.3] Strong acid–weak base (e.g. HCl with sodium carbonate or ammonia): methyl orange; phenolphthalein would change colour too early, while the solution is still alkaline and before the equivalence point.
[chem12-s2.3.b] Titrating a weak acid with a weak base is a problem case, because the curve has no steep section to signal the equivalence point. The specification asks you to know why.
[chem12-s2.3.0] With no steep section, no indicator changes colour sharply at the equivalence point, so no indicator is suitable and the end point is unclear.
[chem12-s2.3.1] The curve has only a gentle point of inflection (the place where the curve changes its direction of bending), which is hard to locate from the data, and small errors in pH readings move it a lot.
[chem12-s2.3.2] The equivalence point must instead be found from the curve itself, at the point of steepest slope, located with a pH meter and many small additions; or such titrations are avoided.
[chem12-s2.3.3] The specification limits this topic to monoprotic acids and bases (each able to give or accept only one H⁺ per molecule) with a single inflection point.
[chem12-s2.4.b] Many acid–base indicators are themselves weak acids, so their colour depends on the position of an equilibrium (a reversible reaction in which forward and backward rates are equal). Le Châtelier's principle tells you which way that equilibrium moves.
[chem12-s2.4.0] Many indicators are weak acids, written HIn, in which the undissociated molecule HIn and its conjugate base In⁻ (the form left after HIn loses H⁺) have different colours.
[chem12-s2.4.1] In water an equilibrium is set up: HIn(aq) ⇌ H⁺(aq) + In⁻(aq).
[chem12-s2.4.2] Le Châtelier's principle: if a system at equilibrium is disturbed, it shifts to oppose the change.
[chem12-s2.4.3] Adding acid increases [H⁺] (the concentration of hydrogen ions), so the equilibrium shifts to the left: most of the indicator is HIn, and its colour is seen.
[chem12-s2.4.4] Adding base removes H⁺ (H⁺ + OH⁻ → H₂O), so the equilibrium shifts to the right to replace it: most of the indicator is In⁻, and its colour is seen.
[chem12-s2.4.5] Phenolphthalein: HIn is colourless and In⁻ is pink.
[chem12-s2.4.6] Methyl orange: HIn is red and In⁻ is yellow.
[chem12-s2.5.b] A few indicators are weak bases instead. The same reasoning applies, but now the shifting equilibrium involves hydroxide ions, OH⁻, rather than H⁺.
[chem12-s2.5.0] A weak base indicator can be written InOH, with InOH and In⁺ having different colours: InOH(aq) ⇌ In⁺(aq) + OH⁻(aq).
[chem12-s2.5.1] Adding acid removes OH⁻, so the equilibrium shifts to the right to replace it and the In⁺ colour is seen.
[chem12-s2.5.2] Adding base increases [OH⁻], so the equilibrium shifts to the left and the InOH colour is seen.
[chem12-s2.6.b] An indicator does not flip colour at a single pH. It changes gradually over a range, and each indicator has its own range, which is why the choice of indicator matters.
[chem12-s2.6.0] The colour change happens over a range of about two pH units, centred on the pH at which [HIn] = [In⁻].
[chem12-s2.6.1] At the midpoint of the range both colours are present, because both forms are equally abundant; methyl orange looks orange (a mix of its red and yellow).
[chem12-s2.6.2] Different indicators are weak acids of different strengths, so they change colour at different pH values: phenolphthalein at pH 8.3–10 and methyl orange at pH 3.1–4.4.
[chem12-s2.6.3] This is why the indicator must be chosen to match the titration: its range has to fall within the steep section of that titration's curve.
[chem12-s2.6.4] Only a few drops of indicator are used, because the indicator is itself an acid and would react with some of the titrant (the solution added from the burette).
[chem12-s2.7.b] Vinegar is a solution of ethanoic acid, and its strength can be found by titration (adding a solution of known concentration from a burette until a reaction is just complete). The reaction used is a simple 1 : 1 neutralisation.
[chem12-s2.7.0] Aim: to find the concentration of ethanoic acid in a commercial vinegar by titration with a standard solution (one whose concentration is accurately known) of sodium hydroxide.
[chem12-s2.7.1] Equation: CH₃COOH(aq) + NaOH(aq) → CH₃COONa(aq) + H₂O(l), with a mole ratio of 1 : 1, so moles of acid equal moles of NaOH at the equivalence point.
[chem12-s2.7.2] The sodium hydroxide must first be standardised (its concentration found by titrating it), because it is not a primary standard, meaning it cannot be weighed out to give an exactly known concentration.
[chem12-s2.7.3] Commercial vinegar is typically about 4–6% ethanoic acid, often labelled 5%.
[chem12-s2.8.b] The method has two parts: an accurate ten-fold dilution of the vinegar, then a titration of the diluted vinegar against sodium hydroxide. There is more on the rinsing and titration technique in the notes on acid–base titrations.
[chem12-s2.8.0] Dilute the vinegar ten times: pipette 25.0 cm³ of vinegar into a 250 cm³ volumetric flask (a flask with one mark for an exact volume), make up to the mark with deionised water and invert about 20 times to mix.
[chem12-s2.8.1] Rinse the burette with deionised water, then with the standard sodium hydroxide solution, and fill it. Rinsing with the solution itself stops leftover water diluting it.
[chem12-s2.8.2] Rinse a pipette with deionised water, then with the diluted vinegar, and transfer 25.0 cm³ of diluted vinegar to a conical flask.
[chem12-s2.8.3] Add two or three drops of phenolphthalein.
[chem12-s2.8.4] Titrate with the sodium hydroxide, swirling, until the solution turns a permanent pale pink. Do a rough titration, then accurate titrations until two agree within 0.1 cm³.
[chem12-s2.8.5] Diagram: a burette of sodium hydroxide above a conical flask of diluted vinegar with phenolphthalein on a white tile; and the 250 cm³ volumetric flask used for the dilution, labelled with the 25.0 cm³ pipette.
[chem12-s2.9.b] Each step in the vinegar titration has a reason, and exams ask you to give it.
[chem12-s2.9.0] The vinegar is diluted because, undiluted, it would need a very large titre (the volume of solution run in from the burette) of 0.1 mol L⁻¹ sodium hydroxide, more than a burette holds.
[chem12-s2.9.1] Phenolphthalein is used because this is a weak acid–strong base titration, with the equivalence point in the alkaline region (about pH 8.7); methyl orange would change colour too soon.
[chem12-s2.9.2] The sodium hydroxide is placed in the burette, so the colour change is from colourless to pink, which is easy to see.
[chem12-s2.9.3] Brown or coloured vinegar makes the end point hard to see, so white (clear) vinegar is used.
[chem12-s2.10.b] The steps convert a titre into a concentration, then into mass concentration and %w/v (grams of solute per 100 cm³ of solution). The numbers here are sample data.
[chem12-s2.10.0] Mean titre: 20.8 cm³ of 0.100 mol L⁻¹ NaOH is needed for 25.0 cm³ of diluted vinegar.
[chem12-s2.10.1] Moles NaOH = 0.100 × 0.0208 = 2.08 × 10⁻³ mol, which equals the moles of ethanoic acid in 25.0 cm³ of diluted vinegar, because the mole ratio is 1 : 1.
[chem12-s2.10.2] Concentration of diluted vinegar = 2.08 × 10⁻³ mol ÷ 0.0250 L = 0.0832 mol L⁻¹.
[chem12-s2.10.3] Concentration of original vinegar = 0.0832 × 10 = 0.832 mol L⁻¹, because the vinegar was diluted ten times.
[chem12-s2.10.4] Mass concentration = 0.832 mol L⁻¹ × 60 g mol⁻¹ = 49.9 g L⁻¹, using M(CH₃COOH) = 60 g mol⁻¹.
[chem12-s2.10.5] As %w/v: 49.9 g L⁻¹ ÷ 10 = 4.99% w/v, close to the 5% typical of vinegar.
[chem12-s2.11.b] Sodium hydroxide is a hazardous chemical, and several slips in technique shift the result in a predictable direction.
[chem12-s2.11.0] Safety: sodium hydroxide is corrosive, especially to the eyes, so wear goggles and use a pipette filler.
[chem12-s2.11.1] Not rinsing the burette with sodium hydroxide leaves water in it, which dilutes the solution, so the titre is too high and the result is too high.
[chem12-s2.11.2] Overshooting the end point gives too large a titre, so the result is too high.
[chem12-s2.11.3] An air bubble below the burette tap gives a wrong titre, because the reading then does not match the volume actually delivered.
[chem12-s2.11.4] Sodium hydroxide absorbs carbon dioxide from the air over time, changing its concentration, so it should be standardised just before use.

CANDIDATES chem12-s1: 1. Standard solutions and acid–base titrations
[chem12-s1.0.b] Volumetric analysis uses a measured volume of reaction to find an unknown concentration. It is the basis of every titration, so the terms here are used throughout the subsection.
[chem12-s1.0.0] Volumetric analysis (titration) finds the concentration of a solution, the analyte (the solution being analysed), by measuring the volume that reacts exactly with a known volume of another solution of known concentration.
[chem12-s1.0.1] The calculation depends on knowing one concentration exactly, so at least one of the two solutions must be a standard solution (a solution whose concentration is accurately known).
[chem12-s1.0.2] Titre: the volume of solution added from the burette (the long graduated glass tube with a tap, used to add measured volumes of liquid).
[chem12-s1.0.3] End point: the point at which the indicator (a substance that changes colour depending on whether the solution is acidic or basic) changes colour.
[chem12-s1.0.4] Equivalence point: the point at which the reactants have reacted in exactly the mole ratio of the balanced equation (the ratio of the numbers of moles shown by the coefficients).
[chem12-s1.0.5] A good indicator is one whose end point coincides with the equivalence point, so that the colour change tells you the moment the reaction is exactly complete.
[chem12-s1.1.b] A titration answer can only be as good as the solution it is measured against. That is why one special type of substance, the primary standard, is used to fix the concentration.
[chem12-s1.1.0] A primary standard is a very pure, stable substance of known formula that can be weighed accurately and dissolved to give a standard solution directly. Its properties and the method for making up a standard solution are in Solutions and concentration.
[chem12-s1.1.1] Acid–base primary standard: anhydrous sodium carbonate, Na₂CO₃ (anhydrous means it contains no water of crystallisation, so its formula and mass are exact).
[chem12-s1.1.2] Redox primary standard: ammonium iron(II) sulfate, (NH₄)₂Fe(SO₄)₂·6H₂O. Redox titrations are ones where electrons are transferred, and this is mentioned here only as an example.
[chem12-s1.1.3] Every result in volumetric analysis is only as accurate as the standard: an impure or unstable standard gives a wrong concentration, and so a wrong answer for the analyte.
[chem12-s1.2.b] Some reagents cannot be made up directly as standard solutions, because their concentration changes or cannot be known from the mass weighed out. Their concentration must instead be found by titration, a process called standardisation.
[chem12-s1.2.0] Sodium hydroxide absorbs water and carbon dioxide from the air, so the mass weighed out is not all NaOH.
[chem12-s1.2.1] Hydrochloric acid is volatile (it evaporates easily, so hydrogen chloride is lost from the solution).
[chem12-s1.2.2] Potassium manganate(VII) solution slowly decomposes, especially in light and in the presence of organic matter, forming MnO₂.
[chem12-s1.2.3] Sodium thiosulfate crystals have an uncertain water content, so their formula mass, and the number of moles weighed out, is uncertain.
[chem12-s1.2.4] Standardisation is finding the exact concentration of such a solution by titrating it against a primary standard, or against a solution already standardised against one.
[chem12-s1.2.5] Example: hydrochloric acid is standardised by titration against a standard solution of sodium carbonate: Na₂CO₃(aq) + 2HCl(aq) → 2NaCl(aq) + H₂O(l) + CO₂(g).
[chem12-s1.2.6] Example: sodium hydroxide is then standardised against the standardised hydrochloric acid, and potassium manganate(VII) is standardised against ammonium iron(II) sulfate.
[chem12-s1.2.7] A solution standardised in this way is sometimes called a secondary standard.
[chem12-s1.3.b] A titration uses a small set of apparatus, each with a specific job. Knowing what each item is for lets you explain why it is used.
[chem12-s1.3.0] Pipette, used with a pipette filler (a rubber bulb that draws liquid up): delivers an exact volume, e.g. 25.0 cm³, of one solution into a conical flask.
[chem12-s1.3.1] Burette: delivers measured, variable volumes of the other solution, read to 0.05 cm³.
[chem12-s1.3.2] Conical flask: can be swirled without splashing, and holds the pipetted solution during the titration.
[chem12-s1.3.3] White tile: placed under the flask so that the colour change shows clearly.
[chem12-s1.3.4] Funnel: used to fill the burette and then removed.
[chem12-s1.3.5] Wash bottle of deionised water (water with dissolved ions removed): used to rinse the apparatus and to wash down the flask.
[chem12-s1.3.6] Indicator: added to the conical flask.
[chem12-s1.3.7] Diagram: a burette clamped above a conical flask on a white tile, with the pipette and pipette filler shown separately; label burette, tap, pipette, pipette filler, conical flask and white tile.
[chem12-s1.4.b] Each rinse removes water or other solution that would dilute the solution it is about to hold. Following the steps in order, with the reason for each, is what the exam asks for.
[chem12-s1.4.0] Rinse the pipette with deionised water, then with a little of the solution it is to measure, so that any water left in it does not dilute the solution.
[chem12-s1.4.1] Draw the solution up above the mark using a pipette filler, then let it down until the bottom of the meniscus (the curved surface of the liquid) sits on the mark at eye level.
[chem12-s1.4.2] Let the solution run into the conical flask, touching the tip against the inside of the flask at the end; do not blow out the last drop, because the pipette is calibrated (marked to deliver an exact volume) to allow for it.
[chem12-s1.4.3] Rinse the burette with deionised water, then with the solution it is to contain, so that any water left in it does not dilute the solution.
[chem12-s1.4.4] Fill the burette using a funnel, then remove the funnel, because drips from it would change the reading.
[chem12-s1.4.5] Run a little solution out through the tap to fill the part below the tap and remove any air bubble, which would otherwise make the volume delivered inaccurate.
[chem12-s1.4.6] Read the burette at eye level, from the bottom of the meniscus.
[chem12-s1.4.7] Rinsing the conical flask with deionised water is fine, because it does not change the number of moles in the pipetted volume.
[chem12-s1.4.8] The conical flask must not be rinsed with either solution, because that would leave extra moles of reagent in the flask.
[chem12-s1.5.b] A titration is done twice over: a quick rough run to find roughly where the end point is, then careful accurate runs. Each step is there to make the final volume as precise as possible.
[chem12-s1.5.0] Add two or three drops of the indicator to the conical flask.
[chem12-s1.5.1] Add solution from the burette while swirling the flask; first do a rough titration to find the approximate end point.
[chem12-s1.5.2] In the accurate titrations, add the solution quickly to within about 1 cm³ of the rough titre, then add it drop by drop, swirling, until one drop gives a permanent colour change.
[chem12-s1.5.3] Wash the inside walls of the flask with deionised water near the end point, so that all the reagent reacts.
[chem12-s1.5.4] Repeat until two accurate titres agree to within 0.1 cm³, then average them, leaving out the rough titre.
[chem12-s1.6.b] Hydrochloric acid is a strong acid and sodium hydroxide a strong base, meaning each ionises fully in water. This is the simplest titration, with a 1 : 1 mole ratio.
[chem12-s1.6.0] Equation: HCl(aq) + NaOH(aq) → NaCl(aq) + H₂O(l), with a mole ratio of 1 : 1.
[chem12-s1.6.1] Indicator: phenolphthalein, which changes from colourless to pink as NaOH is added from the burette, or methyl orange.
[chem12-s1.6.2] Either indicator works because the pH (the scale of acidity) changes sharply over a wide range at the equivalence point.
[chem12-s1.6.3] Sample data: 25.0 cm³ of NaOH needs a mean titre of 22.5 cm³ of 0.100 mol L⁻¹ HCl.
[chem12-s1.6.4] Moles HCl = 0.100 × 0.0225 = 2.25 × 10⁻³ mol.
[chem12-s1.6.5] Moles NaOH = 2.25 × 10⁻³ mol, because the mole ratio is 1 : 1.
[chem12-s1.6.6] Concentration of NaOH = 2.25 × 10⁻³ mol ÷ 0.0250 L = 0.0900 mol L⁻¹.
[chem12-s1.7.b] Sodium carbonate is the primary standard used to standardise hydrochloric acid. It is a weak base (one that ionises only partly in water), and the mole ratio is not 1 : 1, so the equation must be used in the calculation.
[chem12-s1.7.0] Equation: Na₂CO₃(aq) + 2HCl(aq) → 2NaCl(aq) + H₂O(l) + CO₂(g), with a mole ratio of Na₂CO₃ : HCl of 1 : 2.
[chem12-s1.7.1] Indicator: methyl orange, which is yellow in the sodium carbonate and turns orange-red at the end point as acid is added.
[chem12-s1.7.2] Phenolphthalein is unsuitable because it changes colour in alkaline solution, before the end point.
[chem12-s1.7.3] Sample data: 25.0 cm³ of 0.0500 mol L⁻¹ Na₂CO₃ needs 23.8 cm³ of HCl.
[chem12-s1.7.4] Moles Na₂CO₃ = 0.0500 × 0.0250 = 1.25 × 10⁻³ mol.
[chem12-s1.7.5] Moles HCl = 2 × 1.25 × 10⁻³ = 2.50 × 10⁻³ mol, because the mole ratio is 1 : 2.
[chem12-s1.7.6] Concentration of HCl = 2.50 × 10⁻³ mol ÷ 0.0238 L = 0.105 mol L⁻¹.
[chem12-s1.7.7] Ammonia, a weak base, is titrated with hydrochloric acid in the same way, using methyl orange: NH₃(aq) + HCl(aq) → NH₄Cl(aq).
[chem12-s1.8.b] The solutions used in these titrations can harm skin and eyes, so a few precautions apply every time.
[chem12-s1.8.0] Wear goggles: sodium hydroxide is corrosive (it burns tissue), especially to the eyes, and hydrochloric acid is corrosive or irritant.
[chem12-s1.8.1] Use a pipette filler, never the mouth, so that no solution is swallowed.
[chem12-s1.8.2] Fill the burette while it is below eye level, for example by lowering it or removing it from the stand, so that you do not spill solution towards your face.

CANDIDATES chem11-s2: 2. Acid–base theories and strength
[chem11-s2.0.b] The first successful definition of acids and bases came from Svante Arrhenius in 1887, in his theory of electrolytic dissociation. He defined them by the ions they produce when dissolved in water.
[chem11-s2.0.0] An Arrhenius acid is a substance that dissociates (splits into ions) in water to produce hydrogen ions (H⁺): HCl(aq) → H⁺(aq) + Cl⁻(aq).
[chem11-s2.0.1] An Arrhenius base is a substance that dissociates in water to produce hydroxide ions (OH⁻): NaOH(aq) → Na⁺(aq) + OH⁻(aq).
[chem11-s2.0.2] Neutralisation (an acid and a base cancelling each other's properties) is the combination of hydrogen and hydroxide ions to form water: H⁺ + OH⁻ → H₂O.
[chem11-s2.1.b] The Arrhenius theory works well for simple cases like HCl and NaOH, but it breaks down as soon as a substance or reaction does not fit its water-and-ions picture. These gaps are why a wider theory was needed.
[chem11-s2.1.0] It applies only to aqueous solutions (substances dissolved in water), so it says nothing about acids and bases in other solvents.
[chem11-s2.1.1] It cannot explain substances that act as bases but contain no hydroxide, such as ammonia (NH₃) and sodium carbonate, because it only allows a base to produce OH⁻ itself.
[chem11-s2.1.2] It cannot explain acid–base reactions that happen without water, e.g. ammonia gas and hydrogen chloride gas reacting to form ammonium chloride: NH₃(g) + HCl(g) → NH₄Cl(s).
[chem11-s2.1.3] It ignores the role of water: a bare hydrogen ion (a proton) does not exist in water; it bonds to a water molecule to form the hydronium ion, H₃O⁺.
[chem11-s2.2.b] Johannes Brønsted and Thomas Lowry proposed this theory independently in 1923. It defines acids and bases by the transfer of a proton, so it no longer depends on water or on OH⁻.
[chem11-s2.2.0] A proton is the same particle as a hydrogen ion, H⁺ (a hydrogen atom that has lost its electron, leaving just a proton).
[chem11-s2.2.1] A Brønsted–Lowry acid is a proton donor; a Brønsted–Lowry base is a proton acceptor.
[chem11-s2.2.2] An acid–base reaction is the transfer of a proton from an acid to a base.
[chem11-s2.2.3] HCl(g) + H₂O(l) → H₃O⁺(aq) + Cl⁻(aq): HCl donates a proton, so it is the acid, and water accepts it, so it is the base.
[chem11-s2.2.4] NH₃(aq) + H₂O(l) ⇌ NH₄⁺(aq) + OH⁻(aq): ammonia accepts a proton, so it is the base, and water donates one, so it is the acid. The ⇌ arrow means the reaction is reversible.
[chem11-s2.2.5] Water can act as an acid or a base depending on what it reacts with, so it is described as amphoteric.
[chem11-s2.3.b] A theory is more extensive if it explains a wider range of substances and reactions with fewer restrictions. Brønsted–Lowry does this by fixing each of the Arrhenius limitations.
[chem11-s2.3.0] It includes every Arrhenius acid and base, and more: the hydroxide ion is itself a proton acceptor, so it counts as a base.
[chem11-s2.3.1] It explains bases with no OH⁻, such as ammonia, and the carbonate ion, which accepts a proton: CO₃²⁻ + H⁺ → HCO₃⁻.
[chem11-s2.3.2] It applies to reactions without water, such as ammonia reacting with hydrogen chloride in the gas phase.
[chem11-s2.3.3] It explains the role of water as a proton acceptor when it forms H₃O⁺, which the Arrhenius theory cannot describe.
[chem11-s2.3.4] Acid: Arrhenius gives a substance that produces H⁺ in water; Brønsted–Lowry gives a proton donor, so the definition does not depend on water.
[chem11-s2.3.5] Base: Arrhenius gives a substance that produces OH⁻ in water; Brønsted–Lowry gives a proton acceptor, so it covers bases such as ammonia.
[chem11-s2.3.6] Scope: Arrhenius applies only to aqueous solutions; Brønsted–Lowry applies in any solvent (the liquid doing the dissolving) and in the absence of a solvent.
[chem11-s2.4.b] Because a Brønsted–Lowry reaction passes a proton along, the acid and base each turn into a new species that can pass the proton back. These linked species are called conjugates.
[chem11-s2.4.0] When an acid donates a proton, what is left can accept a proton back: it is the conjugate base of that acid.
[chem11-s2.4.1] When a base accepts a proton, the species formed is the conjugate acid of that base.
[chem11-s2.4.2] A conjugate acid–base pair is two species that differ by one proton (H⁺), e.g. HCl and Cl⁻; H₃O⁺ and H₂O; NH₄⁺ and NH₃.
[chem11-s2.4.3] H₃O⁺ and OH⁻ are not a conjugate pair, because they differ by two protons.
[chem11-s2.4.4] Every Brønsted–Lowry reaction has two conjugate pairs: acid₁ + base₂ ⇌ acid₂ + base₁.
[chem11-s2.5.b] To label the acids, bases and conjugate pairs in any equation, follow the movement of the proton. These four steps work for every equation.
[chem11-s2.5.0] 1. For each reactant, check whether it loses or gains a proton in going to the products.
[chem11-s2.5.1] 2. The reactant that loses a proton is the acid; the reactant that gains a proton is the base.
[chem11-s2.5.2] 3. On the product side, the species formed from the base is its conjugate acid, and the species formed from the acid is its conjugate base.
[chem11-s2.5.3] 4. In a reversible reaction (one that can run in both directions, shown by ⇌), the products act as an acid and a base in the reverse reaction.
[chem11-s2.6.b] Each equation below is labelled using the four steps: find which reactant loses a proton, which gains one, then match each to its product. Notice that the same species can be an acid in one equation and a base in another.
[chem11-s2.6.0] HCl + H₂O → H₃O⁺ + Cl⁻: HCl is the acid and H₂O is the base; the pairs are HCl/Cl⁻ and H₃O⁺/H₂O.
[chem11-s2.6.1] NH₃ + H₂O ⇌ NH₄⁺ + OH⁻: the acids are H₂O and NH₄⁺; the bases are NH₃ and OH⁻; the pairs are NH₄⁺/NH₃ and H₂O/OH⁻.
[chem11-s2.6.2] CH₃COOH + H₂O ⇌ CH₃COO⁻ + H₃O⁺: the acids are CH₃COOH and H₃O⁺; the bases are H₂O and CH₃COO⁻; the pairs are CH₃COOH/CH₃COO⁻ and H₃O⁺/H₂O.
[chem11-s2.6.3] HCO₃⁻ + H₂O ⇌ CO₃²⁻ + H₃O⁺: here HCO₃⁻ acts as an acid because it loses a proton; the pairs are HCO₃⁻/CO₃²⁻ and H₃O⁺/H₂O.
[chem11-s2.6.4] HCO₃⁻ + H₃O⁺ ⇌ H₂CO₃ + H₂O: here HCO₃⁻ acts as a base because it gains a proton; the pairs are H₂CO₃/HCO₃⁻ and H₃O⁺/H₂O.
[chem11-s2.6.5] NH₃ + HCl → NH₄⁺ + Cl⁻ (no water involved): NH₃ is the base and HCl is the acid; the pairs are NH₄⁺/NH₃ and HCl/Cl⁻.
[chem11-s2.7.b] An acid and its conjugate base are linked like a see-saw: the stronger one is, the weaker the other. Some species can sit on either side, depending on what they react with.
[chem11-s2.7.0] The stronger an acid, the weaker its conjugate base: HCl is a strong acid, so Cl⁻ is a very weak base that barely accepts protons.
[chem11-s2.7.1] A weak acid has a relatively strong conjugate base: ethanoic acid is a weak acid, and the ethanoate ion (CH₃COO⁻) readily accepts a proton.
[chem11-s2.7.2] Water and the hydrogencarbonate ion are amphoteric (also called amphiprotic): each can act as either an acid or a base, depending on the reaction, as shown in the worked examples above.
[chem11-s2.8.b] Strength describes how far an acid splits into ions (dissociates, or ionises) when it dissolves in water. This decides how many H⁺ ions it releases.
[chem11-s2.8.0] A strong acid is completely dissociated (ionised) into ions in water, shown with a single arrow: HCl(aq) → H⁺(aq) + Cl⁻(aq).
[chem11-s2.8.1] Strong acids include hydrochloric acid (HCl), nitric acid (HNO₃) and sulfuric acid (H₂SO₄).
[chem11-s2.8.2] A weak acid is only slightly dissociated in water: most molecules stay undissociated and an equilibrium (a balance between the forward and reverse reactions) is set up, e.g. CH₃COOH(aq) ⇌ CH₃COO⁻(aq) + H⁺(aq).
[chem11-s2.8.3] Weak acids include ethanoic acid (vinegar), citric acid, carbonic acid (CO₂ in water) and most organic acids (acids built on carbon chains).
[chem11-s2.9.b] Bases have strength in the same way as acids: it depends on how fully they produce hydroxide ions in water.
[chem11-s2.9.0] A strong base is completely dissociated in water, e.g. sodium hydroxide and potassium hydroxide: NaOH(aq) → Na⁺(aq) + OH⁻(aq).
[chem11-s2.9.1] A weak base reacts only slightly with water to produce hydroxide ions, e.g. ammonia: NH₃(aq) + H₂O(l) ⇌ NH₄⁺(aq) + OH⁻(aq).
[chem11-s2.10.b] Concentration describes how much acid or base is present; strength describes how far it dissociates. The two are separate properties.
[chem11-s2.10.0] Concentration is the amount of acid or base dissolved per litre of solution, measured in mol L⁻¹ (moles per litre; a mole is a fixed counting amount of particles).
[chem11-s2.10.1] A concentrated acid has a large amount of acid per litre, e.g. 10 M HCl; a dilute acid has a small amount per litre, e.g. 0.1 M HCl.
[chem11-s2.10.2] A dilute strong acid, e.g. 0.01 M HCl, is still fully dissociated.
[chem11-s2.10.3] A concentrated weak acid, e.g. glacial ethanoic acid, is still only slightly dissociated.
[chem11-s2.11.b] Compare 0.1 M hydrochloric acid with 0.1 M ethanoic acid, which have the same concentration. Any difference in behaviour must then come from strength, not concentration.
[chem11-s2.11.0] pH (a scale measuring how acidic a solution is; lower means more H⁺): 0.1 M hydrochloric acid has a pH of 1.0, while 0.1 M ethanoic acid has a pH of about 2.9, because it produces far fewer H⁺ ions.
[chem11-s2.11.1] Ethanoic acid has an acid dissociation constant (Ka) of 1.8 × 10⁻⁵, a small value showing that it is only slightly dissociated.
[chem11-s2.11.2] Rate of reaction: magnesium ribbon fizzes much faster in 0.1 M hydrochloric acid than in 0.1 M ethanoic acid, because hydrogen ions are present at a higher concentration.
[chem11-s2.11.3] Conductivity: 0.1 M hydrochloric acid conducts electricity much better than 0.1 M ethanoic acid, because it contains more ions to carry the current.
[chem11-s2.11.4] Equal volumes of the two acids at the same concentration neutralise the same amount of sodium hydroxide and give the same total volume of hydrogen with excess magnesium, because the weak acid's equilibrium shifts to release more H⁺ as H⁺ is used up.
[chem11-s2.11.5] Safety: concentrated acids and alkalis are corrosive, so wear goggles and gloves; dilute an acid by adding it to water.

CANDIDATES chem19-s1: 1. Self-ionisation of water and pH
[chem19-s1.0.b] Even perfectly pure water contains a very small number of ions, made when water molecules pass protons (hydrogen ions, H⁺) to each other. This is the starting point for understanding pH, because it fixes what 'neutral' means.
[chem19-s1.0.0] Pure water conducts electricity very slightly, so it must contain a small number of ions, since only moving charged particles can carry current through a liquid.
[chem19-s1.0.1] These ions come from the self-ionisation (auto-ionisation) of water, in which one water molecule donates a proton to another. This is a Brønsted–Lowry acid–base reaction (an acid gives a proton, a base accepts it): H₂O(l) + H₂O(l) ⇌ H₃O⁺(aq) + OH⁻(aq).
[chem19-s1.0.2] H₃O⁺ is the hydronium ion (a water molecule carrying an extra proton) and OH⁻ is the hydroxide ion. The double arrow shows an equilibrium, a reaction going both ways at the same rate so that amounts stay constant.
[chem19-s1.0.3] In simplified form: H₂O(l) ⇌ H⁺(aq) + OH⁻(aq). This is the same change with the proton written as H⁺ instead of showing it attached to a water molecule.
[chem19-s1.0.4] The equilibrium lies very far to the left, so almost all the water stays as molecules: at 25 °C only about 2 in every 1000 million (1.8 × 10⁻⁹) water molecules are ionised at any moment.
[chem19-s1.0.5] In pure water each ionisation gives one H₃O⁺ and one OH⁻, so [H₃O⁺] = [OH⁻]. Square brackets mean concentration in mol L⁻¹.
[chem19-s1.0.6] A solution is neutral when [H⁺] = [OH⁻], acidic when [H⁺] > [OH⁻], and basic (alkaline) when [H⁺] < [OH⁻]. Even acidic solutions contain some OH⁻ and basic solutions contain some H⁺.
[chem19-s1.1.b] Because water ionises only slightly, its equilibrium constant can be simplified into a single value, the ionic product of water, Kw. Work through it one step at a time.
[chem19-s1.1.0] Step 1: Write the equilibrium constant (Kc, the ratio of product concentrations to reactant concentration at equilibrium) for H₂O ⇌ H⁺ + OH⁻: Kc = [H⁺][OH⁻] ÷ [H₂O].
[chem19-s1.1.1] Step 2: So little water ionises that [H₂O] is effectively constant, because almost every molecule is unchanged: 1000 g of water per litre ÷ 18 g mol⁻¹ = 55.6 mol L⁻¹.
[chem19-s1.1.2] Step 3: Combine the two constants: Kc × [H₂O] = [H⁺][OH⁻]. This new constant is the ionic product of water, Kw.
[chem19-s1.1.3] Definition: Kw = [H₃O⁺][OH⁻], or in simplified form Kw = [H⁺][OH⁻]; it is the product of the hydrogen ion and hydroxide ion concentrations in water or any dilute aqueous solution.
[chem19-s1.1.4] At 25 °C, Kw = 1.0 × 10⁻¹⁴ (units mol² L⁻², often omitted).
[chem19-s1.2.b] Kw links [H⁺] and [OH⁻] in every dilute aqueous solution. It explains why pure water has pH 7 and lets you find one concentration from the other.
[chem19-s1.2.0] Pure water at 25 °C has [H⁺] = [OH⁻], so [H⁺]² = 1.0 × 10⁻¹⁴.
[chem19-s1.2.1] Therefore [H⁺] = √(1.0 × 10⁻¹⁴) = 1.0 × 10⁻⁷ mol L⁻¹.
[chem19-s1.2.2] So pH = −log₁₀(1.0 × 10⁻⁷) = 7.0, which is why pH 7 is neutral at 25 °C.
[chem19-s1.2.3] Kw applies to every dilute aqueous solution, not just pure water: if [H⁺] rises, [OH⁻] falls so that the product stays 1.0 × 10⁻¹⁴ at 25 °C.
[chem19-s1.2.4] Example: a solution with [H⁺] = 1.0 × 10⁻² mol L⁻¹ (pH 2) has [OH⁻] = 1.0 × 10⁻¹⁴ ÷ 1.0 × 10⁻² = 1.0 × 10⁻¹² mol L⁻¹.
[chem19-s1.2.5] Taking −log₁₀ of Kw = [H⁺][OH⁻] gives pH + pOH = 14 at 25 °C, where pOH = −log₁₀[OH⁻]; this is used for bases. There is more on this in pH calculations and dissociation constants.
[chem19-s1.3.b] Kw is an equilibrium constant, so it depends on temperature. The direction of the change follows from whether self-ionisation takes in or gives out heat.
[chem19-s1.3.0] Self-ionisation is endothermic (it takes in heat) in the forward direction, with ΔH about +56 kJ mol⁻¹. It is the reverse of neutralisation, H⁺ + OH⁻ → H₂O, which is exothermic (gives out heat).
[chem19-s1.3.1] By Le Châtelier's principle (a system at equilibrium shifts to oppose a change), raising the temperature shifts the equilibrium to the right, the endothermic direction. This produces more H⁺ and OH⁻, so Kw increases; cooling decreases Kw.
[chem19-s1.3.2] Like every equilibrium constant, Kw changes only with temperature; that is why Kw values are quoted with a temperature.
[chem19-s1.4.b] Because Kw rises with temperature, the pH of pure water falls as it gets hotter, yet the water is still neutral. Neutral is defined by [H⁺] = [OH⁻], not by a fixed pH.
[chem19-s1.4.0] Kw at 0 °C is 1.1 × 10⁻¹⁵, giving a neutral pH of 7.48.
[chem19-s1.4.1] Kw at 25 °C is 1.0 × 10⁻¹⁴, giving a neutral pH of 7.00.
[chem19-s1.4.2] Kw at 50 °C is 5.5 × 10⁻¹⁴, giving a neutral pH of 6.63.
[chem19-s1.4.3] Kw at 100 °C is 5.1 × 10⁻¹³, giving a neutral pH of 6.15.
[chem19-s1.4.4] Hot pure water has a pH below 7 but is still neutral, because [H⁺] and [OH⁻] increase equally and stay equal; neutral means [H⁺] = [OH⁻], not pH = 7.
[chem19-s1.4.5] The neutral pH is therefore lower at higher temperatures and higher at lower temperatures, so pH 7 is the neutral point only at 25 °C.
[chem19-s1.5.b] Concentrations of H⁺ in solutions are often tiny numbers such as 0.0000001 mol L⁻¹. The pH scale turns them into easy numbers, usually between 0 and 14.
[chem19-s1.5.0] pH is defined as pH = −log₁₀[H⁺], where [H⁺] is the concentration of hydrogen (hydronium) ions in mol L⁻¹.
[chem19-s1.5.1] The scale was introduced by the Danish chemist Søren Sørensen in 1909, while working at the Carlsberg Laboratory in Copenhagen, as a convenient way to express very small [H⁺] values.
[chem19-s1.5.2] At 25 °C, pH below 7 is acidic, pH 7 is neutral and pH above 7 is basic (alkaline); the lower the pH, the more acidic the solution.
[chem19-s1.5.3] Where [H⁺] = 10⁻ˣ mol L⁻¹, the pH is x: for example, [H⁺] = 10⁻⁴ mol L⁻¹ gives pH 4.
[chem19-s1.5.4] The scale is logarithmic (each step multiplies by a fixed factor): a change of 1 pH unit is a tenfold change in [H⁺]. A solution of pH 3 has 10 times the [H⁺] of one of pH 4 and 100 times that of one of pH 5.
[chem19-s1.6.b] Putting numbers on familiar substances helps you judge whether a calculated pH is sensible. These values are approximate.
[chem19-s1.6.0] Stomach acid has a pH of about 1–2; lemon juice about 2–3; vinegar about 3. These are acidic.
[chem19-s1.6.1] Pure water has pH 7, and blood 7.35–7.45.
[chem19-s1.6.2] Baking soda solution has a pH of about 8–9; milk of magnesia about 10.5. These are weakly basic.
[chem19-s1.6.3] Household ammonia has a pH of about 11–12; oven cleaner (sodium hydroxide) about 13–14. These are strongly basic.
[chem19-s1.7.b] Exam questions give you either [H⁺] or the pH and ask for the other. Each direction uses the same relationship, worked here with sample numbers.
[chem19-s1.7.0] From [H⁺] to pH: pH = −log₁₀[H⁺]. For example, [H⁺] = 3.5 × 10⁻⁴ mol L⁻¹ gives pH = −log₁₀(3.5 × 10⁻⁴) = 3.46.
[chem19-s1.7.1] For example, [H⁺] = 7.1 × 10⁻⁷ mol L⁻¹ gives pH = 6.15, which is slightly acidic at 25 °C because it is below 7.
[chem19-s1.7.2] From pH to [H⁺]: [H⁺] = 10⁻ᵖᴴ, the antilog (the reverse of log) of −pH. For example, pH 3.7 gives [H⁺] = 10⁻³·⁷ = 2.00 × 10⁻⁴ mol L⁻¹.
[chem19-s1.7.3] For example, pH 8.6 gives [H⁺] = 10⁻⁸·⁶ = 2.51 × 10⁻⁹ mol L⁻¹.
[chem19-s1.7.4] On a calculator, use the log key with a minus sign to find pH, and the 10ˣ key (or inverse log) with −pH to find [H⁺]; give pH to two decimal places unless the question asks otherwise.
[chem19-s1.8.b] The pH scale works well for the dilute aqueous solutions met in most experiments, but it breaks down outside them.
[chem19-s1.8.0] It is normally used only between 0 and 14; very concentrated acids and alkalis go outside this range, where pH is not a convenient measure.
[chem19-s1.8.1] For example, 2.0 M HCl has pH −log₁₀(2.0) = −0.30, a negative value.
[chem19-s1.8.2] It is reliable only for dilute solutions; in concentrated solutions (above about 1 mol L⁻¹) the ions interact and the measured pH no longer matches −log₁₀ of the concentration.
[chem19-s1.8.3] It applies only to aqueous solutions (those with water as the solvent).
[chem19-s1.8.4] pH 7 is neutral only at 25 °C, because Kw and so the neutral pH change with temperature.
[chem19-s1.9.b] The simplest way to find a pH is with an indicator, a dye that changes colour with the pH of the solution. Indicators are quick but not precise.
[chem19-s1.9.0] Universal indicator (solution or paper): add a few drops to the solution, or dip in a strip of paper, and compare the colour with the colour chart. It gives the pH only to about the nearest whole unit. There is more on its colours in Acids, bases and their reactions.
[chem19-s1.9.1] Single indicators such as litmus, methyl orange and phenolphthalein show only whether the pH is above or below their colour-change range, not a pH value.
[chem19-s1.10.b] A pH meter gives a numerical reading, but it must be calibrated (set against known values) and looked after to be accurate.
[chem19-s1.10.0] A pH meter (or pH sensor with a data-logger, a device that records readings automatically) uses a glass electrode probe that responds to [H⁺] and gives a digital reading, usually to 0.01 pH units.
[chem19-s1.10.1] Calibrate the meter before use: place the rinsed probe in buffer solutions of known pH (e.g. pH 4.00 and 7.00, or 7.00 and 10.00) and set the meter to read these values.
[chem19-s1.10.2] A buffer solution resists a change in pH when small amounts of acid or base are added, so its pH is reliably known. This is why buffers, not water or an acid of supposedly known pH, are used to calibrate.
[chem19-s1.10.3] Rinse the probe with deionised water (water with dissolved ions removed) between solutions and blot it dry gently, so one solution does not contaminate the next; store the bulb wet, as directed.
[chem19-s1.11.b] Exams ask for reasons why a meter is preferred, so know each advantage and what it improves.
[chem19-s1.11.0] A pH meter gives a precise numerical value (e.g. 4.72) rather than an estimate to the nearest whole unit.
[chem19-s1.11.1] A pH meter needs no judgement of colour, which varies from person to person and with lighting.
[chem19-s1.11.2] A pH meter works in coloured or cloudy solutions, where an indicator colour cannot be seen.
[chem19-s1.11.3] A pH meter can record pH continuously, which suits following a reaction over time, as in pH titration curves.
[chem19-s1.12.b] The aim is to compare the pH of several substances by two methods and check that they agree.
[chem19-s1.12.0] Aim: to measure and compare the pH of everyday substances, e.g. vinegar, lemon juice, a soft drink, tap water, toothpaste or an antacid tablet mixed with deionised water, and baking soda solution.
[chem19-s1.12.1] Step 1: Put about 20 cm³ of each sample in a clean, labelled beaker.
[chem19-s1.12.2] Step 2: Test each sample with universal indicator paper and record the pH from the colour chart.
[chem19-s1.12.3] Step 3: Measure each sample with a calibrated pH meter, rinsing the probe between samples.
[chem19-s1.12.4] Step 4: Repeat each reading.
[chem19-s1.12.5] Use deionised water to dissolve or mix solids, because tap water may contain dissolved substances that change the pH.
[chem19-s1.12.6] Diagram: a pH probe in a beaker of sample, connected to a meter (or data-logger) showing a digital reading.
[chem19-s1.12.7] Diagram: universal indicator paper beside its colour chart.
[chem19-s1.13.b] After the readings, rank the substances, compare the two methods and take care with hazards and errors.
[chem19-s1.13.0] Results: record both sets of readings in a table and rank the substances from most acidic to most basic.
[chem19-s1.13.1] The meter readings are more precise, and the two methods should agree to about one unit, because universal indicator reads only to about the nearest whole unit.
[chem19-s1.13.2] Safety: wear goggles; treat oven cleaner and other strong alkalis as corrosive (use gloves, small volumes, or omit them); do not taste any sample.
[chem19-s1.13.3] Sources of error: a probe that is not calibrated or not rinsed; a temperature different from the calibration temperature; misjudging indicator colours.
[chem19-s1.14.b] The pH depends on the [H⁺] in the solution, which depends on how far the acid dissociates (splits into ions) and on its concentration. A strong acid dissociates almost completely; a weak acid only partly, measured by its Ka (the acid dissociation constant).
[chem19-s1.14.0] Tendency to dissociate: at the same concentration, a strong acid has a lower pH than a weak acid.
[chem19-s1.14.1] For example, 0.10 M HCl has pH 1.00, but 0.10 M ethanoic acid (Ka 1.8 × 10⁻⁵) has pH 2.87, because the weak acid is only about 1% dissociated.
[chem19-s1.14.2] Concentration: diluting an acid lowers [H⁺] and raises the pH.
[chem19-s1.14.3] Diluting a strong acid ten times raises its pH by 1: 0.10 M HCl has pH 1.00 and 0.010 M HCl has pH 2.00.
[chem19-s1.14.4] Diluting a weak acid ten times raises its pH by only about 0.5: 0.10 M ethanoic acid has pH 2.87 and 0.010 M has pH 3.37.
[chem19-s1.14.5] This is because [H⁺] depends on the square root of the concentration, and dilution makes a weak acid dissociate a larger fraction. There is more on this in pH calculations and dissociation constants.
[chem19-s1.15.b] Temperature changes pH because it changes Kw, Ka and Kb (the dissociation constants of water, weak acids and weak bases).
[chem19-s1.15.0] Temperature: Kw increases with temperature, so the pH of pure water and of neutral solutions falls, to 6.15 at 100 °C.
[chem19-s1.15.1] Ka and Kb also change with temperature, so the pH of weak acids and bases changes; Ka and Kb values are quoted at 25 °C.
[chem19-s1.15.2] For a base, the pH also changes with temperature at fixed [OH⁻], because [H⁺] = Kw ÷ [OH⁻] and Kw changes.
[chem19-s1.15.3] For example, 0.010 M NaOH has pH 12.00 at 25 °C but about 11.3 at 50 °C, because Kw is larger at 50 °C so [H⁺] is larger.
[chem19-s1.16.b] Strength and pH are different ideas. Strength is how far an acid dissociates; pH is the [H⁺] actually present, which also depends on concentration.
[chem19-s1.16.0] pH measures acidity (the [H⁺] in the solution), not the strength of the acid: a very dilute strong acid can have a higher pH than a concentrated weak acid.
[chem19-s1.16.1] For example, 1.0 × 10⁻⁵ M HCl has pH 5.00, while 1.0 M ethanoic acid has pH 2.37.

CANDIDATES chem19-s2: 2. pH calculations and dissociation constants
[chem19-s2.0.b] pH is a number that measures how acidic a solution is: pH = −log₁₀[H⁺], where [H⁺] is the concentration of hydrogen ions in mol L⁻¹ (M means mol L⁻¹). A strong acid ionises completely, so its [H⁺] can be read straight from the concentration of the acid.
[chem19-s2.0.0] A strong acid is fully dissociated (every molecule splits into ions), so [H⁺] = concentration of the acid × number of H⁺ released per formula unit (its basicity, the number of H⁺ each unit of acid can give).
[chem19-s2.0.1] Monoprotic (monobasic) strong acids, which release one H⁺ each, such as HCl and HNO₃, give [H⁺] = [acid], because each molecule supplies exactly one H⁺.
[chem19-s2.0.2] For example, 0.010 M HCl has [H⁺] = 0.010 mol L⁻¹, so pH = −log₁₀(0.010) = 2.00.
[chem19-s2.0.3] For example, 0.025 M HCl has pH = −log₁₀(0.025) = 1.60.
[chem19-s2.0.4] Sulfuric acid is diprotic (it can release two H⁺) and is treated as fully dissociated: H₂SO₄ → 2H⁺ + SO₄²⁻, so [H⁺] = 2 × [H₂SO₄].
[chem19-s2.0.5] For example, 0.050 M H₂SO₄ has [H⁺] = 0.10 mol L⁻¹, so pH = 1.00.
[chem19-s2.1.b] Sometimes a question gives a mass of acid dissolved in a stated volume instead of a concentration. Convert to mol L⁻¹ first, then use the strong acid method one step at a time.
[chem19-s2.1.0] Problem: 5.0 g of sulfuric acid (H₂SO₄ = 98 g mol⁻¹, its molar mass) is dissolved in water and made up to 250 cm³. Find the pH.
[chem19-s2.1.1] Step 1: Concentration in g L⁻¹ = 5.0 ÷ 0.250 = 20 g L⁻¹, because 250 cm³ is 0.250 L.
[chem19-s2.1.2] Step 2: Concentration in mol L⁻¹ = 20 ÷ 98 = 0.204 mol L⁻¹, dividing by the molar mass.
[chem19-s2.1.3] Step 3: [H⁺] = 2 × 0.204 = 0.408 mol L⁻¹, because each H₂SO₄ releases 2H⁺.
[chem19-s2.1.4] Step 4: pH = −log₁₀(0.408) = 0.39.
[chem19-s2.2.b] A strong base is treated like a strong acid, except that it supplies OH⁻ (hydroxide ions) rather than H⁺. So you find [OH⁻] first and then convert it to a pH.
[chem19-s2.2.0] A strong base is fully dissociated, so [OH⁻] = concentration of the base × number of OH⁻ per formula unit: NaOH and KOH give 1 OH⁻, while Ca(OH)₂ gives 2.
[chem19-s2.2.1] Method: find [OH⁻], then pOH = −log₁₀[OH⁻] (pOH is the same scale as pH but for OH⁻); pH = 14 − pOH at 25 °C, because pH + pOH = 14 only at that temperature.
[chem19-s2.2.2] Alternative method: [H⁺] = Kw ÷ [OH⁻] = 1.0 × 10⁻¹⁴ ÷ [OH⁻] (Kw is the ionic product of water, [H⁺][OH⁻]), then pH = −log₁₀[H⁺]; both methods give the same answer.
[chem19-s2.2.3] For example, 0.010 M NaOH has [OH⁻] = 0.010, so pOH = 2.00 and pH = 14 − 2.00 = 12.00.
[chem19-s2.2.4] For example, 0.15 M NaOH has pOH = −log₁₀(0.15) = 0.82, so pH = 13.18.
[chem19-s2.2.5] For example, 0.010 M Ca(OH)₂ dissociates as Ca(OH)₂ → Ca²⁺ + 2OH⁻, so [OH⁻] = 0.020 mol L⁻¹, pOH = 1.70 and pH = 12.30.
[chem19-s2.2.6] Example from a mass: 9.0 g of KOH (56 g mol⁻¹) made up to 500 cm³ gives 18 g L⁻¹ ÷ 56 = 0.321 mol L⁻¹ = [OH⁻].
[chem19-s2.2.7] For the KOH example, pOH = 0.49 and pH = 13.51.
[chem19-s2.3.b] A weak acid dissociates only slightly, so you cannot take [H⁺] equal to its concentration. Instead you use Ka, the acid dissociation constant, a number that measures how far the acid dissociates (the larger the Ka, the stronger the acid).
[chem19-s2.3.0] A weak acid is only slightly dissociated, so [H⁺] is much less than the acid concentration and must be found from Ka.
[chem19-s2.3.1] Formula: [H⁺] = √(Ka × c), where c is the concentration of the acid in mol L⁻¹; this formula must be learned, because it is not in the Formulae and Tables booklet.
[chem19-s2.3.2] It assumes the acid is monoprotic (gives one H⁺ per molecule), that the amount dissociated is very small compared with c (so c − x ≈ c, where x is the amount that dissociates), and that the H⁺ from water is negligible.
[chem19-s2.3.3] The approximation is good: the exact quadratic answer for 0.10 M ethanoic acid is pH 2.88, so the formula is good to about 0.01 pH unit.
[chem19-s2.4.b] Once [H⁺] is found from √(Ka × c), the pH follows with −log₁₀ as for any acid. These examples use Ka values at 25 °C.
[chem19-s2.4.0] For example, 0.10 M ethanoic acid with Ka = 1.8 × 10⁻⁵ has [H⁺] = √(1.8 × 10⁻⁵ × 0.10) = √(1.8 × 10⁻⁶) = 1.34 × 10⁻³ mol L⁻¹.
[chem19-s2.4.1] So its pH = −log₁₀(1.34 × 10⁻³) = 2.87.
[chem19-s2.4.2] For example, 0.50 M ethanoic acid has [H⁺] = √(1.8 × 10⁻⁵ × 0.50) = √(9.0 × 10⁻⁶) = 3.0 × 10⁻³ mol L⁻¹, so pH = 2.52.
[chem19-s2.4.3] For example, 0.10 M methanoic acid with Ka = 1.8 × 10⁻⁴ has [H⁺] = √(1.8 × 10⁻⁵) = 4.24 × 10⁻³ mol L⁻¹, so pH = 2.37.
[chem19-s2.4.4] Methanoic acid has a lower pH than ethanoic acid at the same concentration, because it is the stronger acid (larger Ka).
[chem19-s2.4.5] Example from a mass: 1.48 g of propanoic acid (CH₃CH₂COOH, 74 g mol⁻¹) in 200 cm³ gives moles = 1.48 ÷ 74 = 0.0200 mol.
[chem19-s2.4.6] Concentration c = 0.0200 ÷ 0.200 = 0.100 mol L⁻¹, with the volume converted to litres.
[chem19-s2.4.7] With Ka = 1.3 × 10⁻⁵, [H⁺] = √(1.3 × 10⁻⁵ × 0.100) = 1.14 × 10⁻³ mol L⁻¹, so pH = 2.94.
[chem19-s2.5.b] A weak base reacts only slightly with water to give OH⁻, so it is handled like a weak acid but with Kb, the base dissociation constant (a measure of how far the base dissociates). You find [OH⁻] first.
[chem19-s2.5.0] Formula: [OH⁻] = √(Kb × c), with the same assumptions as for weak acids (the amount dissociated is very small compared with c, and OH⁻ from water is negligible); then pOH = −log₁₀[OH⁻] and pH = 14 − pOH.
[chem19-s2.5.1] For example, 0.10 M ammonia with Kb = 1.8 × 10⁻⁵ has [OH⁻] = √(1.8 × 10⁻⁵ × 0.10) = 1.34 × 10⁻³ mol L⁻¹.
[chem19-s2.5.2] So its pOH = 2.87 and pH = 14 − 2.87 = 11.13.
[chem19-s2.5.3] For example, 0.050 M ammonia has [OH⁻] = √(1.8 × 10⁻⁵ × 0.050) = 9.49 × 10⁻⁴ mol L⁻¹, so pOH = 3.02 and pH = 10.98.
[chem19-s2.6.b] Sometimes the pH is given and you must work backwards to a concentration or to Ka. Undo the log with 10 raised to the power of minus pH.
[chem19-s2.6.0] Concentration of a strong acid from its pH: for a monoprotic acid, [acid] = [H⁺] = 10⁻ᵖᴴ, because pH = −log₁₀[H⁺].
[chem19-s2.6.1] For example, a strong monoprotic acid HX with pH 5.65 has [HX] = 10⁻⁵·⁶⁵ = 2.24 × 10⁻⁶ mol L⁻¹.
[chem19-s2.6.2] [OH⁻] from pH: [OH⁻] = Kw ÷ [H⁺], where Kw is 1.0 × 10⁻¹⁴ at 25 °C.
[chem19-s2.6.3] For example, at pH 5.65, pOH = 14 − 5.65 = 8.35, so [OH⁻] = 10⁻⁸·³⁵ = 4.47 × 10⁻⁹ mol L⁻¹, the same as 1.0 × 10⁻¹⁴ ÷ 10⁻⁵·⁶⁵.
[chem19-s2.6.4] Ka of a weak acid from its pH: [H⁺] = 10⁻ᵖᴴ, then Ka = [H⁺]² ÷ c, which is the formula [H⁺] = √(Ka × c) rearranged.
[chem19-s2.6.5] For example, a 0.10 M weak monoprotic acid with pH 3.00 has [H⁺] = 1.0 × 10⁻³, so Ka = (1.0 × 10⁻³)² ÷ 0.10 = 1.0 × 10⁻⁵.
[chem19-s2.6.6] Concentration of a weak acid from its pH and Ka: c = [H⁺]² ÷ Ka.
[chem19-s2.6.7] For example, ethanoic acid of pH 3.00 has c = (1.0 × 10⁻³)² ÷ 1.8 × 10⁻⁵ = 0.0556 mol L⁻¹.
[chem19-s2.7.b] Every pH calculation goes wrong if the wrong method is chosen or a conversion is skipped. Work through these checks before and after every pH calculation.
[chem19-s2.7.0] First decide: is the species an acid or a base, and is it strong or weak? For a strong species use the concentration directly; for a weak species use √(Ka × c) or √(Kb × c).
[chem19-s2.7.1] For any base, find [OH⁻] first, then convert to pH; do not put [OH⁻] into −log₁₀ and call the result pH.
[chem19-s2.7.2] Convert masses to mol L⁻¹ first: n = m ÷ M (moles = mass ÷ molar mass), then c = n ÷ V, with V in litres.
[chem19-s2.7.3] Check that the answer is sensible: acids have pH below 7, bases have pH above 7, and a weak acid has a higher pH than a strong acid of the same concentration.
[chem19-s2.7.4] Give pH answers to two decimal places.
[chem19-s2.8.b] When an acid or base dissolves in water, it splits into ions; this is called dissociation. Writing the equation for it is the starting point for strength and for the dissociation constants that follow.
[chem19-s2.8.0] A general acid is written HA; in water it dissociates by donating a proton (an H⁺ ion) to water: HA(aq) + H₂O(l) ⇌ H₃O⁺(aq) + A⁻(aq), or simply HA(aq) ⇌ H⁺(aq) + A⁻(aq).
[chem19-s2.8.1] For example, ethanoic acid dissociates as CH₃COOH(aq) ⇌ CH₃COO⁻(aq) + H⁺(aq); A⁻ is the ethanoate ion.
[chem19-s2.8.2] A general base that contains hydroxide is written BOH: BOH(aq) ⇌ B⁺(aq) + OH⁻(aq).
[chem19-s2.8.3] A base such as ammonia, written B, produces OH⁻ by accepting a proton from water: NH₃(aq) + H₂O(l) ⇌ NH₄⁺(aq) + OH⁻(aq).
[chem19-s2.8.4] Here B⁺ is the ammonium ion, NH₄⁺ (BH⁺ in some books).
[chem19-s2.8.5] For a strong acid or base the dissociation goes to completion, shown by a single arrow (→); for a weak acid or base it is an equilibrium (⇌, a reaction that runs both ways) that lies well to the left, so most of the molecules stay undissociated.
[chem19-s2.9.b] The acid dissociation constant Ka comes from the equilibrium constant Kc (products over reactants, each concentration in mol L⁻¹) for the acid reacting with water. Water is then left out, for a reason you can state.
[chem19-s2.9.0] Step 1: Write the equilibrium: HA + H₂O ⇌ H₃O⁺ + A⁻.
[chem19-s2.9.1] Step 2: Write Kc as products over reactants: Kc = [H₃O⁺][A⁻] ÷ ([HA][H₂O]).
[chem19-s2.9.2] Step 3: In a dilute solution [H₂O] is effectively constant (55.6 mol L⁻¹, from 1000 ÷ 18), because so little water reacts; so it is combined with Kc: Ka = Kc × [H₂O].
[chem19-s2.9.3] Result: Ka = [H₃O⁺][A⁻] ÷ [HA], or in simplified form Ka = [H⁺][A⁻] ÷ [HA]; Ka is the acid dissociation constant.
[chem19-s2.9.4] Example: for ethanoic acid, Ka = [CH₃COO⁻][H⁺] ÷ [CH₃COOH] = 1.8 × 10⁻⁵ at 25 °C.
[chem19-s2.9.5] The units of Ka are mol L⁻¹ (concentration² ÷ concentration); they are often omitted.
[chem19-s2.10.b] The base dissociation constant Kb is found by exactly the same steps as Ka, using the equilibrium of a base with water.
[chem19-s2.10.0] For B + H₂O ⇌ B⁺ + OH⁻ (or BOH ⇌ B⁺ + OH⁻), the same steps give the base dissociation constant: Kb = [B⁺][OH⁻] ÷ [B] (or [B⁺][OH⁻] ÷ [BOH]).
[chem19-s2.10.1] [H₂O] is again effectively constant and is absorbed into Kb, so it does not appear in the expression.
[chem19-s2.10.2] Example: for ammonia, Kb = [NH₄⁺][OH⁻] ÷ [NH₃] = 1.8 × 10⁻⁵ at 25 °C.
[chem19-s2.11.b] Ka and Kb are equilibrium constants, so a large value means the equilibrium lies far to the right and a small value means it lies far to the left. This makes them a direct measure of strength.
[chem19-s2.11.0] Ka measures the strength of an acid, its tendency to donate a proton: the larger Ka, the further the equilibrium lies to the right and the stronger the acid.
[chem19-s2.11.1] Likewise, the larger Kb, the stronger the base.
[chem19-s2.11.2] Weak acids have small Ka values (much less than 1): methanoic acid 1.8 × 10⁻⁴, benzoic acid 6.3 × 10⁻⁵, ethanoic acid 1.8 × 10⁻⁵, propanoic acid 1.3 × 10⁻⁵ (strongest to weakest).
[chem19-s2.11.3] Strong acids (HCl, HNO₃, H₂SO₄) and strong bases (NaOH, KOH) dissociate essentially completely, so their Ka or Kb values are very large and the expressions are not used.
[chem19-s2.11.4] Ka and Kb are used for weak acids and bases.
[chem19-s2.11.5] Like all equilibrium constants, Ka and Kb are constant at a given temperature and do not change with concentration; values are quoted at 25 °C.
[chem19-s2.12.b] The weak acid formula comes from the Ka expression plus one approximation. If you can show these four steps, you can state the formula and the assumption behind it.
[chem19-s2.12.0] Step 1: Start with c mol L⁻¹ of a weak acid HA, of which x mol L⁻¹ dissociates. At equilibrium [HA] = c − x, [H⁺] = x and [A⁻] = x, because each HA that dissociates gives one H⁺ and one A⁻.
[chem19-s2.12.1] Step 2: Ka = x × x ÷ (c − x) = x² ÷ (c − x).
[chem19-s2.12.2] Step 3: A weak acid dissociates so little that x is tiny compared with c, so c − x ≈ c and Ka = x² ÷ c.
[chem19-s2.12.3] Step 4: x² = Ka × c, so [H⁺] = √(Ka × c).
[chem19-s2.12.4] The same steps for a weak base give [OH⁻] = √(Kb × c); the formulas are used in the weak acid and weak base calculations earlier in this subsection.
[chem19-s2.13.b] Strength describes how far an acid or base splits into ions. The degree of dissociation puts a number on this, and it is what separates strong from weak.
[chem19-s2.13.0] The degree of dissociation is the fraction of acid (or base) molecules that have dissociated into ions in solution; as a percentage, % dissociation = (concentration dissociated ÷ initial concentration) × 100.
[chem19-s2.13.1] For an acid HA, % dissociation = ([H⁺] at equilibrium ÷ initial [HA]) × 100; for a base, % dissociation = ([OH⁻] ÷ initial [base]) × 100.
[chem19-s2.13.2] Strong acids (HCl, HNO₃, H₂SO₄) and strong bases (NaOH, KOH) are essentially 100% dissociated in dilute solution, because the reaction goes to completion.
[chem19-s2.13.3] Weak acids (e.g. ethanoic acid) and weak bases (e.g. ammonia) are only slightly dissociated, typically a few per cent or less.
[chem19-s2.13.4] For example, 0.10 M ethanoic acid is about 1.3% dissociated, so about 99 out of every 100 molecules remain as CH₃COOH.
[chem19-s2.13.5] So at the same concentration a strong acid gives a much higher [H⁺] than a weak acid, with a lower pH, better conductivity and a faster reaction with magnesium.
[chem19-s2.13.6] In Brønsted–Lowry terms (acids donate protons, bases accept them), a strong acid is a good proton donor whose conjugate base (the ion left after it loses H⁺, e.g. Cl⁻) has almost no tendency to take the proton back, so the equilibrium lies completely to the right.
[chem19-s2.13.7] A weak acid's conjugate base (e.g. CH₃COO⁻) readily takes the proton back, so the equilibrium lies to the left.
[chem19-s2.14.b] Ka and Kb let you rank weak acids and bases and work out their percentage dissociation. Compare them only at the same concentration and temperature.
[chem19-s2.14.0] At the same concentration and temperature, the acid with the larger Ka is dissociated to a greater degree and is the stronger acid; the base with the larger Kb is the stronger base.
[chem19-s2.14.1] For example, methanoic acid (Ka 1.8 × 10⁻⁴) is a stronger acid than ethanoic acid (Ka 1.8 × 10⁻⁵): at 0.10 M, methanoic acid is 4.2% dissociated and ethanoic acid 1.3%.
[chem19-s2.14.2] Formula: degree of dissociation (as a fraction) = [H⁺] ÷ c = √(Ka × c) ÷ c = √(Ka ÷ c); for a weak base, the same form is √(Kb ÷ c).
[chem19-s2.14.3] Worked example: 0.10 M ethanoic acid has [H⁺] = √(1.8 × 10⁻⁵ × 0.10) = 1.34 × 10⁻³ mol L⁻¹.
[chem19-s2.14.4] Its % dissociation = (1.34 × 10⁻³ ÷ 0.10) × 100 = 1.34%.
[chem19-s2.14.5] Worked example: 0.10 M ammonia (Kb 1.8 × 10⁻⁵) has [OH⁻] = 1.34 × 10⁻³ mol L⁻¹, so 1.34% of the ammonia has reacted to form NH₄⁺ and OH⁻.
[chem19-s2.14.6] A very small Ka (or Kb) means a very low degree of dissociation; the Ka values of strong acids are so large that dissociation is complete, which is why Ka is used only to compare weak acids.
[chem19-s2.15.b] Diluting a weak acid does not change Ka, but it does change how much of the acid is dissociated. The percentage dissociation and the [H⁺] move in opposite directions.
[chem19-s2.15.0] Ka is constant at a given temperature, but the degree of dissociation of a weak acid increases as the solution is diluted, because √(Ka ÷ c) gets larger as c gets smaller.
[chem19-s2.15.1] For example, ethanoic acid is 0.42% dissociated at 1.0 M, 1.34% at 0.10 M and 4.2% at 0.010 M.
[chem19-s2.15.2] By Le Châtelier's principle (a system at equilibrium shifts to oppose a change), dilution lowers the concentration of all species, so the equilibrium HA ⇌ H⁺ + A⁻ shifts to the right, the side with more dissolved particles, to oppose the change.
[chem19-s2.15.3] Even though the degree of dissociation rises, [H⁺] still falls on dilution, so the pH still rises.
[chem19-s2.15.4] A strong acid is fully dissociated at every dilute concentration, so its degree of dissociation does not change on dilution.
[chem19-s2.16.b] An acid and the base left after it loses H⁺ are a conjugate acid–base pair. Their dissociation constants are linked through Kw, the ionic product of water.
[chem19-s2.16.0] For a conjugate acid–base pair in water, Ka (of the acid) × Kb (of its conjugate base) = Kw = 1.0 × 10⁻¹⁴ at 25 °C.
[chem19-s2.16.1] So the larger the Ka of an acid, the smaller the Kb of its conjugate base: the stronger the acid, the weaker its conjugate base.
[chem19-s2.16.2] Example: Kb of the ethanoate ion = 1.0 × 10⁻¹⁴ ÷ 1.8 × 10⁻⁵ = 5.6 × 10⁻¹⁰.
[chem19-s2.16.3] Example: Ka of the ammonium ion = 1.0 × 10⁻¹⁴ ÷ 1.8 × 10⁻⁵ = 5.6 × 10⁻¹⁰.
