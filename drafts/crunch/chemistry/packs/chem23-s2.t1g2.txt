TARGET chem23-s2: 2. Investigation: preparing ethene
[chem23-s2.0.b] Ethene is made in the lab by taking the elements of water out of ethanol. The investigation then uses the gas to show what an alkene (a hydrocarbon with a carbon-carbon double bond) is like.
[chem23-s2.0.0] Aim: to prepare ethene by dehydrating ethanol, to observe its physical properties, and to investigate its combustion and show that it is unsaturated (contains a multiple carbon-carbon bond).
[chem23-s2.0.1] Hypothesis: ethene contains a C=C double bond, so it will decolourise bromine water and acidified potassium manganate(VII), while a saturated hydrocarbon (one with only single carbon-carbon bonds) will not.
[chem23-s2.0.2] Ethanol vapour passed over hot aluminium oxide loses a molecule of water: C₂H₅OH(g) → C₂H₄(g) + H₂O(g).
[chem23-s2.0.3] This is a dehydration reaction, a type of elimination reaction in which a small molecule, water, is removed from a larger one and a double bond forms. There is more on this in Five types of organic reaction.
[chem23-s2.0.4] Aluminium oxide, Al₂O₃, a white powder, is the catalyst (a substance that speeds up a reaction), so it is not used up in the reaction.
[chem23-s2.1.b] Heat the aluminium oxide, not the ethanol. The method runs in the order below.
[chem23-s2.1.0] 1. Push some glass wool (a loose plug of glass fibres that soaks up liquid) into the closed end of a hard-glass boiling tube, which withstands strong heating, and soak it with about 2 cm³ of ethanol, using a dropper.
[chem23-s2.1.1] 2. Place a small heap of aluminium oxide about halfway along the tube.
[chem23-s2.1.2] 3. Clamp the tube horizontally and fit a stopper with a delivery tube (a bent tube that carries the gas away) that leads under water in a trough.
[chem23-s2.1.3] 4. Put an inverted test tube full of water over the end of the delivery tube, standing on a beehive shelf (a perforated support that holds the tube mouth under water).
[chem23-s2.1.4] 5. Heat the aluminium oxide strongly with a Bunsen burner; heat conducted along the tube warms the ethanol so that its vapour passes over the hot catalyst.
[chem23-s2.1.5] 6. Collect the gas by displacement of water (the gas bubbles up into the water-filled tube and pushes the water out), and discard the first one or two tubes, then collect several tubes of gas, stoppering each under water so that no gas escapes, and storing it mouth down.
[chem23-s2.1.6] 7. Before removing the heat, lift the delivery tube out of the water, to prevent suck-back.
[chem23-s2.1.7] Diagram: a horizontal boiling tube, clamped, with ethanol-soaked glass wool at the closed end and aluminium oxide in the middle; a Bunsen flame under the aluminium oxide; a delivery tube to an inverted water-filled test tube on a beehive shelf in a trough of water. Label the ethanol on the glass wool, the aluminium oxide, the heat and the ethene collecting.
[chem23-s2.2.b] Each feature of the set-up solves a practical problem. Knowing the reason for each is what the exam tests.
[chem23-s2.2.0] Glass wool holds the liquid ethanol at the end of the tube, so it doesn't run onto the hot catalyst or the hot glass.
[chem23-s2.2.1] The catalyst is heated rather than the ethanol, because aluminium oxide must be hot to work, and ethanol is flammable with a low boiling point of 78 °C, so gentle conducted heat is enough to vaporise it.
[chem23-s2.2.2] Ethene can be collected over water because it is almost insoluble in water, so very little of it dissolves as it bubbles through.
[chem23-s2.2.3] The first tubes are discarded because they contain mainly air pushed out of the apparatus, not ethene.
[chem23-s2.2.4] Suck-back happens if heating stops while the delivery tube is under water: the gas in the hot tube cools and contracts, cold water is drawn back into the hot boiling tube, and the tube can crack or shatter.
[chem23-s2.3.b] Before testing its chemistry, observe the gas itself. Its properties follow from its small, non-polar molecules.
[chem23-s2.3.0] Ethene is a colourless gas with a boiling point of −104 °C and a faint sweet smell, which should be wafted towards the nose, not sniffed directly.
[chem23-s2.3.1] It is almost insoluble in water, shown by its collection over water, because it is non-polar (its molecules have no charged ends) and cannot form hydrogen bonds with water.
[chem23-s2.3.2] It dissolves readily in non-polar solvents such as cyclohexane, because like dissolves like: both have non-polar molecules.
[chem23-s2.3.3] Its density is close to that of air: its relative molecular mass is 28, compared with about 29 for air.
[chem23-s2.4.b] Ethene burns, and the products of burning can be identified. The flame also reveals how much carbon the molecule contains compared with methane.
[chem23-s2.4.0] Remove the stopper and hold the mouth of the tube to a lighted taper or Bunsen flame: ethene burns with a yellow, luminous (bright), slightly smoky flame.
[chem23-s2.4.1] Combustion equation: C₂H₄(g) + 3O₂(g) → 2CO₂(g) + 2H₂O(l).
[chem23-s2.4.2] Add limewater (calcium hydroxide solution) to the tube after burning, stopper and shake; it turns milky because insoluble calcium carbonate forms, showing that carbon dioxide was produced: Ca(OH)₂(aq) + CO₂(g) → CaCO₃(s) + H₂O(l).
[chem23-s2.4.3] Droplets of water may condense on the inside of the tube, which is the other product of combustion.
[chem23-s2.4.4] Methane burns with a clean, non-luminous blue flame, whereas ethene's flame is more luminous and smoky, because ethene has a higher percentage of carbon by mass, 85.7% compared with 75.0% in methane.
[chem23-s2.5.b] A C=C double bond can take in extra atoms, which is why alkenes react with bromine and with manganate(VII) while saturated hydrocarbons do not. Each reagent changes colour when it reacts, so the colour change is the evidence.
[chem23-s2.5.0] Bromine water: add a few drops of dilute bromine water to a tube of ethene, stopper and shake; the orange, or red-brown, colour disappears and the solution becomes colourless.
[chem23-s2.5.1] Bromine adds across the double bond in an addition reaction (the two bromine atoms join on and the double bond becomes a single bond): CH₂=CH₂(g) + Br₂ → CH₂BrCH₂Br, which is 1,2-dibromoethane.
[chem23-s2.5.2] Acidified potassium manganate(VII): add a few drops of dilute KMnO₄ acidified with dilute sulfuric acid, stopper and shake; the purple colour disappears.
[chem23-s2.5.3] In the manganate(VII) test the alkene is oxidised (it is attacked by the oxidising agent), and MnO₄⁻ is reduced (gains electrons) to almost colourless Mn²⁺, which is why the purple fades.
[chem23-s2.5.4] Both decolourisations are positive tests for unsaturation, meaning a C=C or C≡C bond is present.
[chem23-s2.5.5] Control: repeat both tests with a saturated hydrocarbon, e.g. a few drops of cyclohexane or methane from the gas tap; there is no rapid colour change, so the decolourisation is due to the double bond.
[chem23-s2.6.b] The percentage yield compares the ethene actually collected with the most that the ethanol could give. Sample data: 2.0 cm³ of ethanol, with a density of 0.789 g cm⁻³, was used, and 310 cm³ of ethene was collected, taken as if at s.t.p. (standard temperature and pressure).
[chem23-s2.6.0] Mass of ethanol = volume × density = 2.0 cm³ × 0.789 g cm⁻³ = 1.58 g.
[chem23-s2.6.1] Moles of ethanol = mass ÷ molar mass = 1.58 g ÷ 46 g mol⁻¹ = 0.0343 mol.
[chem23-s2.6.2] The mole ratio of ethanol to ethene is 1 : 1, so the theoretical yield (the most ethene the reaction could give) is 0.0343 mol of ethene.
[chem23-s2.6.3] Theoretical volume = 0.0343 mol × 22.4 L mol⁻¹ = 0.768 L = 768 cm³ at s.t.p.
[chem23-s2.6.4] Percentage yield = 310 cm³ ÷ 768 cm³ × 100 = 40.4%.
[chem23-s2.6.5] The yield is low because some ethanol evaporates past the catalyst unreacted and dissolves in the trough water, some ethene is lost in the discarded tubes, and gas is left in the apparatus.
[chem23-s2.7.b] The hazards come from flammable gases, toxic bromine and hot glass. The errors weaken the tests, and the conclusion ties the results back to the aim.
[chem23-s2.7.0] Wear safety glasses; ethanol and ethene are highly flammable, so keep the stock bottle of ethanol away from the flame and add the ethanol before lighting the Bunsen burner.
[chem23-s2.7.1] Bromine water is toxic and corrosive: use small amounts of dilute bromine water in a well-ventilated room or a fume cupboard, and wear gloves.
[chem23-s2.7.2] Potassium manganate(VII) is an oxidiser that stains, and dilute sulfuric acid is an irritant.
[chem23-s2.7.3] Let the apparatus cool before handling it, because the glass is hot, and remove the delivery tube from the water before stopping the heating to avoid suck-back.
[chem23-s2.7.4] Air in the first tubes dilutes the ethene and weakens the tests.
[chem23-s2.7.5] Unreacted ethanol vapour carried over can slowly decolourise acidified manganate(VII) by itself, so the bromine water test and its control are the more convincing evidence.
[chem23-s2.7.6] Bromine water slowly fades in bright light, so the result must be compared with a control.
[chem23-s2.7.7] Conclusion: ethene is a colourless, almost insoluble, flammable gas that decolourises bromine water and acidified manganate(VII), so it is unsaturated.

CANDIDATES chem26-s2: 2. Theoretical, actual and percentage yield
[chem26-s2.0.b] When a chemist makes a substance, the amount collected is almost never the amount the equation predicts. Three linked terms describe the amount made, the amount that was possible, and how the two compare.
[chem26-s2.0.0] Yield is the amount of a product obtained from a reaction.
[chem26-s2.0.1] Theoretical yield is the maximum amount of product that could be formed from the given amounts of reactants. It is calculated from the balanced equation, assuming the reaction goes to completion and all of the limiting reactant (the reactant that runs out first) becomes the desired product.
[chem26-s2.0.2] Actual yield is the amount of product actually obtained, measured in practice, in the laboratory or in industry. It is what you weigh or measure at the end, not what the equation predicts.
[chem26-s2.0.3] Percentage yield = (actual yield ÷ theoretical yield) × 100. It measures how efficiently reactants are converted into the desired product, so 100% would be perfect conversion.
[chem26-s2.0.4] The actual and theoretical yields must be in the same units: both grams, both moles, or both gas volumes at the same temperature and pressure. Gas volumes change with temperature and pressure, so a comparison is only fair under the same conditions.
[chem26-s2.1.b] Every yield question follows the same four steps. The theoretical yield comes from the equation and the amount of starting material; only the last step brings in what was actually collected.
[chem26-s2.1.0] Step 1: Write the balanced equation, because the coefficients (the numbers in front of each formula) give the mole ratio between reactant and product.
[chem26-s2.1.1] Step 2: Find the moles of the reactant that decides the amount of product, the one not in excess (excess means more than enough, so some is left over). Moles = mass ÷ molar mass.
[chem26-s2.1.2] Step 3: Use the mole ratio to find the moles of product, then convert to grams (or a gas volume). This is the theoretical yield.
[chem26-s2.1.3] Step 4: Percentage yield = actual ÷ theoretical × 100.
[chem26-s2.2.b] Ethene is made by dehydrating ethanol (removing the elements of water from it). This example follows the four steps for a reaction with a 1 : 1 ratio and shows the yield worked out from a mass and then from a gas volume.
[chem26-s2.2.0] Question: 4.60 g of ethanol was dehydrated to ethene and 1.96 g of ethene was collected. The equation is C₂H₅OH → C₂H₄ + H₂O, and the preparation is in “Investigation: preparing ethene”.
[chem26-s2.2.1] Moles of ethanol = 4.60 ÷ 46 = 0.100 mol (M of C₂H₅OH is 46 g mol⁻¹). The ratio is 1 : 1, so the theoretical yield is 0.100 mol of ethene.
[chem26-s2.2.2] Theoretical mass of ethene = 0.100 × 28 = 2.80 g (M of C₂H₄ is 28 g mol⁻¹).
[chem26-s2.2.3] Percentage yield = (1.96 ÷ 2.80) × 100 = 70.0%.
[chem26-s2.2.4] A gas yield can also be compared by volume. One mole of gas occupies 22.4 L at STP (standard temperature and pressure), so 0.100 mol of ethene is 2.24 L at STP.
[chem26-s2.2.5] If 1.50 L of ethene (at STP) were collected, the percentage yield would be (1.50 ÷ 2.24) × 100 = 67.0%.
[chem26-s2.3.b] An ester is made by refluxing (boiling with a condenser so vapour returns to the flask) a carboxylic acid with an alcohol and an acid catalyst (a substance that speeds up the reaction without being used up). Here the ethanol is in excess, so the ethanoic acid decides the theoretical yield.
[chem26-s2.3.0] Question: 6.00 g of ethanoic acid was refluxed with excess ethanol and an acid catalyst, and 5.72 g of pure ethyl ethanoate was obtained. The equation is CH₃COOH + C₂H₅OH ⇌ CH₃COOC₂H₅ + H₂O.
[chem26-s2.3.1] Moles of ethanoic acid = 6.00 ÷ 60 = 0.100 mol (M of CH₃COOH is 60 g mol⁻¹), so the theoretical yield is 0.100 mol of ethyl ethanoate (the ratio is 1 : 1).
[chem26-s2.3.2] Theoretical mass = 0.100 × 88 = 8.80 g (M of CH₃COOC₂H₅ is 88 g mol⁻¹).
[chem26-s2.3.3] Percentage yield = (5.72 ÷ 8.80) × 100 = 65.0%.
[chem26-s2.3.4] The yield is well below 100% mainly because esterification (making an ester) is reversible and reaches equilibrium, a balance in which reactants and products are both present. The preparation is in “Tests, reaction schemes and preparing an ester”.
[chem26-s2.4.b] Aspirin is made from salicylic acid in a 1 : 1 ratio, so this is another single-step yield calculation. Here the product was dried and purified before weighing.
[chem26-s2.4.0] Aspirin (C₉H₈O₄, M = 180) is made from salicylic acid (C₇H₆O₃, M = 138). One mole of salicylic acid gives one mole of aspirin.
[chem26-s2.4.1] Moles of salicylic acid = 2.76 ÷ 138 = 0.0200 mol, so the theoretical yield is 0.0200 mol of aspirin.
[chem26-s2.4.2] Theoretical mass of aspirin = 0.0200 × 180 = 3.60 g.
[chem26-s2.4.3] 2.52 g of dry, recrystallised aspirin was obtained (recrystallised means purified by dissolving and letting crystals form again). The percentage yield = (2.52 ÷ 3.60) × 100 = 70.0%.
[chem26-s2.5.b] Sometimes the percentage yield is given and you must find how much reactant to start with, or how much product will actually be collected. The calculation runs in the opposite direction, so the theoretical yield must be found first.
[chem26-s2.5.0] Question: what mass of ethanoic acid is needed to make 5.00 g of ethyl ethanoate if the yield is 65.0%?
[chem26-s2.5.1] Because only 65.0% of the possible product is obtained, the theoretical yield needed is larger: 5.00 ÷ 0.650 = 7.69 g of ethyl ethanoate.
[chem26-s2.5.2] Moles of ethyl ethanoate = 7.692 ÷ 88 = 0.08741 mol, which equals the moles of ethanoic acid needed, since the ratio is 1 : 1.
[chem26-s2.5.3] Mass of ethanoic acid = 0.08741 × 60 = 5.24 g (M of CH₃COOH is 60 g mol⁻¹).
[chem26-s2.5.4] To go from a percentage yield to an actual yield, use actual yield = theoretical yield × (percentage ÷ 100). For example, 15.0% of a theoretical 0.960 L of gas is 0.144 L, which is 144 cm³.
[chem26-s2.6.b] In practice the actual yield is almost always below the theoretical yield. There are several separate causes, some chemical and some to do with handling the product.
[chem26-s2.6.0] The reaction may not go to completion. Many organic reactions are slow or reversible and reach equilibrium, for example esterification, so some reactant is left unconverted.
[chem26-s2.6.1] Side reactions form other, unwanted products from the same reactants. For example, some ethanol forms other products instead of ethene, so less ethene is made.
[chem26-s2.6.2] Product is lost during transfer, filtration, separation and purification. For example, some product stays dissolved in the solvent after recrystallisation, or sticks to glassware and filter paper.
[chem26-s2.6.3] The reactants may be impure, so less of the real reactant is present than the mass suggests. A volatile product or reactant (one that evaporates easily) may also be lost as vapour.
[chem26-s2.6.4] A percentage yield above 100% means the product is impure or not fully dry, for example still containing water or solvent. The true yield can never exceed 100%.
[chem26-s2.7.b] Yield is not just a number in an exam question. It decides how much a process costs and how much waste it creates, and in multi-step syntheses (making a compound through a series of reactions) the losses add up.
[chem26-s2.7.0] In industry, a higher percentage yield means less raw material, energy and cost per kilogram of product, and less waste to treat or dispose of. This links yield to sustainability (making products while using fewer resources and creating less waste).
[chem26-s2.7.1] Pharmaceuticals (medicines) are made in many steps from simpler precursors (the starting compounds), so a low yield at each step makes the overall yield very small. There is more on this in “Organic products and pharmaceuticals”.
[chem26-s2.7.2] Overall yield of a multi-step synthesis = the product of the fractional yields of the steps, because each step only has what the one before delivered. For example, two steps of 80% and 75% give 0.80 × 0.75 = 0.60, an overall yield of 60%.
