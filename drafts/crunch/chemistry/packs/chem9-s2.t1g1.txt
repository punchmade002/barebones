TARGET chem9-s2: 2. Using the kinetic theory: separation techniques
[chem9-s2.0.b] The kinetic theory says matter is made of particles that are always moving. How close the particles are, how strongly they attract one another and how they move explain why solids, liquids and gases behave so differently.
[chem9-s2.0.0] Solids have a fixed shape and volume and cannot be compressed (squeezed into a smaller volume), because the particles are held tightly in fixed positions by strong forces and only vibrate on the spot.
[chem9-s2.0.1] Liquids have a fixed volume but take the shape of their container and flow, because the particles are close together but can slide past one another.
[chem9-s2.0.2] Gases fill their container and are easily compressed, because the particles are far apart, with weak forces between them, and move rapidly and randomly. The empty space between them is what lets them be pushed closer.
[chem9-s2.0.3] Gas pressure is caused by gas particles colliding with the walls of the container. Many fast collisions every second add up to a steady push on the walls.
[chem9-s2.1.b] Each change of state is explained by the energy of the particles and the forces between them. Heating gives particles energy to break free of those forces, and cooling takes energy away so the forces win again.
[chem9-s2.1.0] Melting (solid to liquid): heating makes the particles vibrate more until they have enough energy to overcome some of the forces holding them in place and begin to move past one another. This happens at the melting point.
[chem9-s2.1.1] Freezing (liquid to solid): cooling removes energy, the particles slow down, and the forces pull them back into fixed positions.
[chem9-s2.1.2] Evaporation (liquid to gas, below the boiling point): the fastest-moving particles at the surface escape, and because the fastest (highest-energy) particles leave, the remaining liquid cools.
[chem9-s2.1.3] Boiling (liquid to gas at the boiling point): particles throughout the liquid have enough energy to overcome the forces between them, so bubbles of vapour (the substance as a gas) form within the liquid.
[chem9-s2.1.4] Condensation (gas to liquid): cooling slows the particles so that the attractive forces pull them close together.
[chem9-s2.1.5] Sublimation (solid to gas directly, without melting), e.g. iodine, and solid carbon dioxide (“dry ice”), which sublimes at atmospheric pressure at −78 °C.
[chem9-s2.1.6] Deposition (gas to solid directly) is the reverse of sublimation, e.g. iodine vapour forming crystals on a cold surface.
[chem9-s2.1.7] Diagram: a triangle of the three states with arrows labelled melting, freezing, evaporation and boiling, condensation, sublimation and deposition.
[chem9-s2.2.b] A heating curve plots temperature against time as a pure substance is heated at a steady rate. The flat sections show the changes of state, and they show where the supplied energy goes.
[chem9-s2.2.0] When a pure solid is heated at a steady rate, its temperature rises until it reaches the melting point, then stays constant until it has all melted.
[chem9-s2.2.1] During a change of state the energy supplied is used to overcome the forces between particles, not to increase their kinetic energy (the energy of their movement), so the temperature does not rise even though heat is being supplied.
[chem9-s2.2.2] The temperature then rises again until the boiling point, where it stays constant until all the liquid has boiled.
[chem9-s2.2.3] The boiling plateau (flat section) is longer than the melting plateau because far more energy is needed to separate the particles completely than to loosen them.
[chem9-s2.2.4] Diagram: a heating curve for water (temperature against time) from ice below 0 °C to steam above 100 °C, with the two flat sections labelled melting at 0 °C and boiling at 100 °C. Water melts at 0 °C and boils at 100 °C at standard pressure.
[chem9-s2.3.b] The kinetic theory is a model, a simplified picture of reality. Its assumptions and limitations show when it explains behaviour well and when it does not. The assumptions below are what the model takes to be true about particles.
[chem9-s2.3.0] Assumption: the particles are tiny spheres in constant, random motion.
[chem9-s2.3.1] Assumption: the higher the temperature, the greater the average kinetic energy (energy of movement) of the particles.
[chem9-s2.3.2] Assumption: collisions between particles are perfectly elastic, which means no kinetic energy is lost when they collide.
[chem9-s2.3.3] Assumption: in a gas the volume of the particles themselves is negligible (so small it can be ignored), and there are no forces between the particles except during collisions.
[chem9-s2.4.b] A model is useful only if you know where it fails. The first two limitations come from what the simple model leaves out about the forces between particles.
[chem9-s2.4.0] Limitation: real particles are not hard spheres, and they do attract one another. These forces between particles (intermolecular forces) are what make liquids and solids exist and decide melting and boiling points, which the simple gas model ignores.
[chem9-s2.4.1] Limitation: the model treats all particles as identical, so it does not explain why different substances have different melting and boiling points. That needs the types of bonding and intermolecular forces. There is more on this in Polarity, shape and physical properties.
[chem9-s2.5.b] The model also says nothing about what is inside particles, and it works best only for gases under certain conditions.
[chem9-s2.5.0] Limitation: the model does not describe the structure inside particles or what happens to bonds, and it is qualitative (it describes behaviour in words), giving no values.
[chem9-s2.5.1] Limitation: in real gases at high pressure or low temperature the particles' volume and attractions matter, because the particles are then squeezed close together or moving slowly, so gases deviate from the model. There is more on this in Gas laws and the ideal gas equation.
[chem9-s2.6.b] A mixture is separated by using a physical property in which its components differ. So the first step is always to work out how the components differ, then choose the technique that uses that difference.
[chem9-s2.6.0] Particle size and solubility (whether a substance dissolves) separate by filtration; solubility and volatility (how easily a substance turns to vapour) by evaporation and crystallisation; boiling point by distillation.
[chem9-s2.6.1] Solubility in a solvent and attraction to paper separate by chromatography; immiscibility (the liquids do not mix) and density by a separating funnel.
[chem9-s2.6.2] Heterogeneous mixtures (the components can be seen as separate parts), such as an insoluble solid in a liquid or two immiscible liquids, are separated by filtration, decanting (carefully pouring off the liquid) or a separating funnel.
[chem9-s2.6.3] Homogeneous mixtures (uniform throughout), such as solutions and miscible liquids (liquids that mix completely), need evaporation, crystallisation, distillation or chromatography.
[chem9-s2.7.b] Filtration separates an insoluble solid (one that does not dissolve) from a liquid. The paper has holes small enough to hold back the solid particles but large enough to let the liquid through.
[chem9-s2.7.0] Example: sand is separated from salt solution by filtration, because sand is insoluble and the salt is dissolved in the water.
[chem9-s2.7.1] Step 1: Fold filter paper into a cone and place it in a funnel.
[chem9-s2.7.2] Step 2: Pour the mixture through the filter paper; the solid (residue) stays on the paper and the liquid (filtrate) passes through.
[chem9-s2.7.3] Diagram: filter funnel with filter paper over a conical flask, labelled residue and filtrate.
[chem9-s2.8.b] When a solid is dissolved in a liquid, filtering will not work because the dissolved solid passes through the paper. Instead you remove the solvent (the liquid that does the dissolving) by heating, either completely (evaporation) or only partly (crystallisation).
[chem9-s2.8.0] Evaporation recovers a dissolved solid by heating the solution until all the solvent has gone, e.g. salt from salt water. It is used when the solid does not decompose (break down) on heating.
[chem9-s2.8.1] Step 1 of crystallisation: Heat the solution to evaporate some solvent until it is saturated (cannot dissolve any more solid). A drop on a cold glass rod forms crystals when it is saturated.
[chem9-s2.8.2] Step 2 of crystallisation: Leave the solution to cool slowly so that crystals form, e.g. copper(II) sulfate crystals.
[chem9-s2.8.3] Step 3 of crystallisation: Filter off the crystals, wash them with a little cold solvent and dry them.
[chem9-s2.8.4] Crystallisation gives purer, larger crystals than evaporating to dryness, because soluble impurities stay in the solution.
[chem9-s2.9.b] Distillation separates a liquid from a solution, or two liquids with different boiling points, by boiling and condensing the vapour. The liquid with the lower boiling point boils off first and is collected separately.
[chem9-s2.9.0] Simple distillation separates a solvent from a solution, or two liquids with very different boiling points, e.g. pure water from salt water.
[chem9-s2.9.1] Step 1: Heat the mixture in a flask containing anti-bumping granules (small pieces that make boiling smooth and stop violent bursts of bubbling).
[chem9-s2.9.2] Step 2: The liquid with the lower boiling point boils, and its vapour passes into a water-cooled condenser (a tube that cools the vapour back to liquid).
[chem9-s2.9.3] Step 3: The vapour condenses in the condenser, which has water in at the bottom and out at the top.
[chem9-s2.9.4] Step 4: The distillate (the liquid that has condensed) is collected in a receiving flask.
[chem9-s2.9.5] Step 5: A thermometer at the side arm reads the boiling point of the liquid that is distilling.
[chem9-s2.9.6] Fractional distillation separates miscible liquids with close boiling points, using a fractionating column (a tall column that gives repeated condensing and re-boiling), e.g. ethanol (boiling point 78 °C) from water (boiling point 100 °C).
[chem9-s2.9.7] Crude oil is separated into fractions (groups of compounds with similar boiling points) by fractional distillation.
[chem9-s2.10.b] Distillation heats liquids that may be flammable and produces vapour, so the apparatus and heat source matter as well as the method.
[chem9-s2.10.0] Safety: flammable liquids are heated with a heating mantle or water bath, not a naked flame, so the vapour cannot ignite.
[chem9-s2.10.1] Safety: a closed system is never heated, because the pressure would build up as the vapour expands.
[chem9-s2.10.2] Diagram: a distillation set-up with a round-bottomed flask and granules, the thermometer bulb at the side arm, a Liebig condenser (a straight condenser cooled by a water jacket) with water flow, and a receiving flask.
[chem9-s2.11.b] Paper chromatography separates and identifies small amounts of soluble substances, such as the dyes in an ink or food colouring, or a mixture of indicators. Each substance is carried up the paper by a solvent at its own speed.
[chem9-s2.11.0] Step 1: Draw a pencil line near the bottom of the chromatography paper; use pencil, because ink would run.
[chem9-s2.11.1] Step 2: Put small spots of the mixture and of known substances on the line.
[chem9-s2.11.2] Step 3: Stand the paper in a little solvent in a covered container, with the solvent level below the spots, so the spots are not washed into the solvent.
[chem9-s2.11.3] Step 4: Let the solvent rise up the paper, then remove the paper and mark the solvent front (the highest point the solvent reached).
[chem9-s2.11.4] Principle: components that are more soluble in the solvent (the mobile phase, which moves) and less attracted to the paper (the stationary phase, which stays still) travel further.
[chem9-s2.11.5] Retention factor (Rf) is the distance moved by the spot divided by the distance moved by the solvent.
[chem9-s2.11.6] Components are identified by matching their spots and Rf values with those of known substances.
[chem9-s2.11.7] Diagram: a chromatogram with the pencil start line, the spots, the solvent front and the separated spots.
[chem9-s2.12.b] A separating funnel separates two immiscible liquids of different density. Immiscible liquids settle into two layers, with the denser liquid underneath.
[chem9-s2.12.0] Examples: oil and water, or an organic layer from an aqueous layer (a water-based layer), which is used to purify an ester.
[chem9-s2.12.1] Step 1: Pour the mixture into the separating funnel and let the layers settle.
[chem9-s2.12.2] Step 2: Remove the stopper and run off the lower, denser layer through the tap.
[chem9-s2.12.3] Step 3: Collect the upper layer separately.
[chem9-s2.12.4] Diagram: a separating funnel with two layers, the stopper and the tap labelled.

CANDIDATES chem9-s1: 1. The kinetic theory and its evidence
[chem9-s1.0.b] Everything around you, from air to iron, is matter. Chemistry starts from one idea: matter is built from particles too small to see, and how those particles are arranged decides whether something is a solid, a liquid or a gas.
[chem9-s1.0.0] All matter is made of tiny particles (atoms, molecules or ions) that are constantly moving, with spaces between them.
[chem9-s1.0.1] Solid: the particles are packed closely in a fixed, regular arrangement and vibrate about fixed positions, so a solid keeps its shape.
[chem9-s1.0.2] Liquid: the particles are close together but can move past each other, so a liquid flows and takes the shape of its container.
[chem9-s1.0.3] Gas: the particles are far apart and move rapidly and randomly in all directions, so a gas spreads out to fill any space.
[chem9-s1.0.4] Diagram: three boxes showing the particle arrangement in a solid, a liquid and a gas.
[chem9-s1.1.b] A pure substance is made of only one kind of particle, so it has fixed properties. There are two kinds of pure substance: elements and compounds.
[chem9-s1.1.0] A pure substance contains only one kind of particle (one element or one compound) and has fixed properties, such as a sharp melting point and boiling point (a single exact temperature, not a range).
[chem9-s1.1.1] An element is a substance made of only one kind of atom; it cannot be split into simpler substances by chemical means (that is, by a chemical reaction), e.g. iron (Fe) and oxygen (O₂).
[chem9-s1.1.2] A compound is a substance made of two or more different elements chemically combined (joined by bonds) in a fixed ratio, e.g. water (H₂O) and sodium chloride (NaCl).
[chem9-s1.1.3] A compound has properties that differ from those of its elements, because the elements are bonded together, so it can be separated into them only by a chemical reaction.
[chem9-s1.1.4] A molecule (a group of atoms joined together) can be of an element (O₂, N₂, Cl₂), where all the atoms are the same, or of a compound (H₂O, CO₂), where the atoms are different.
[chem9-s1.2.b] A mixture is two or more substances that are not chemically combined, so each keeps its own properties. Mixtures are grouped by whether they look the same all the way through.
[chem9-s1.2.0] A mixture can have a variable composition (the amounts of each substance can change), and its components keep their own properties.
[chem9-s1.2.1] A mixture can be separated by physical methods (ones that need no chemical reaction), and it has no sharp melting or boiling point: it melts or boils over a range of temperatures. There is more on this in Using the kinetic theory: separation techniques.
[chem9-s1.2.2] A homogeneous mixture has a uniform composition throughout and a single phase (one visible part), e.g. salt solution, air and an alloy (a mixture of metals).
[chem9-s1.2.3] A heterogeneous mixture is non-uniform, with visibly different parts or phases, e.g. sand and water, oil and water, and granite.
[chem9-s1.3.b] Because a pure substance has a fixed melting point and a mixture does not, melting point can be used to check whether a sample is pure.
[chem9-s1.3.0] A pure substance has a sharp melting point that matches the data-book value (the accepted figure listed in reference tables).
[chem9-s1.3.1] Impurities lower the melting point and make the substance melt over a range of temperatures, so a low or spread-out melting point shows the sample is not pure.
[chem9-s1.4.b] Compounds and mixtures both contain more than one element, so they are easy to confuse. The difference is whether the substances are chemically combined.
[chem9-s1.4.0] A compound has a fixed composition; a mixture has a variable composition.
[chem9-s1.4.1] The elements in a compound are chemically combined; the substances in a mixture are not.
[chem9-s1.4.2] A compound has new properties because its elements are chemically combined; a mixture's components keep their own properties because they are not.
[chem9-s1.4.3] A compound can be separated only chemically, because its elements are bonded; a mixture can be separated physically, because nothing is bonded.
[chem9-s1.4.4] Energy is usually taken in or given out when a compound forms, whereas there is little energy change when a mixture is made.
[chem9-s1.4.5] Diagram: particle diagrams of an element (single atoms and diatomic molecules, which are molecules of two atoms), a compound (identical molecules, each made of two kinds of atom) and a mixture (two kinds of particle mixed together).
[chem9-s1.5.b] The kinetic theory states that matter is particles in motion. We cannot see the particles, so the theory rests on effects we can see.
[chem9-s1.5.0] The kinetic theory of matter states that all matter is made of particles that are in constant, random motion.
[chem9-s1.5.1] The particles have kinetic energy (energy of movement), so the higher the temperature, the faster they move.
[chem9-s1.5.2] The average kinetic energy of the particles is proportional to the temperature in kelvin (the temperature scale that starts at absolute zero), so doubling the kelvin temperature doubles the average kinetic energy.
[chem9-s1.5.3] The evidence for the theory comes from effects we can see that are caused by particles we cannot see.
[chem9-s1.6.b] Brownian motion is the continuous, random, zig-zag movement of small visible particles suspended in a gas or liquid. It shows that the invisible particles around them are moving.
[chem9-s1.6.0] Robert Brown, a botanist (a scientist who studies plants), first observed Brownian motion in 1827, using pollen grains in water.
[chem9-s1.6.1] Aim: to observe the motion of smoke particles in air as evidence for the kinetic theory.
[chem9-s1.6.2] Step 1: Fill a small glass cell with smoke and cover it.
[chem9-s1.6.3] Step 2: Light the cell from the side, so the smoke particles scatter light and show up.
[chem9-s1.6.4] Step 3: View the cell through a microscope.
[chem9-s1.6.5] Observation: the smoke particles appear as bright specks that jiggle about randomly and never stop.
[chem9-s1.7.b] The jiggling of the smoke is the evidence. It can be explained only if the air around the smoke is made of moving particles.
[chem9-s1.7.0] Explanation: the smoke particles are bombarded unevenly from all sides by fast-moving, invisible air molecules; at any instant more molecules hit one side than the other, so the particle moves.
[chem9-s1.7.1] The smoke particles are visible solid particles, not air molecules; they are moved by the invisible air molecules.
[chem9-s1.7.2] Conclusion: the air is made of particles in constant, random motion, and the motion becomes more vigorous when the temperature is raised.
[chem9-s1.7.3] Diagram: a smoke cell lit from the side under a microscope, and the zig-zag path of one smoke particle.
[chem9-s1.8.b] Diffusion is the spreading out of particles from a region of high concentration to a region of low concentration, until they are evenly mixed, as a result of their random motion. Gases show it clearly.
[chem9-s1.8.0] Diffusion needs no stirring or currents, because it results from the random motion of the particles alone.
[chem9-s1.8.1] Bromine experiment: a few drops of liquid bromine are placed in a gas jar, giving brown vapour that spreads upwards through a second jar of air placed on top.
[chem9-s1.8.2] The bromine jars are evenly coloured when the vapour has diffused through both jars, which shows the particles have spread out until evenly mixed.
[chem9-s1.8.3] Safety for bromine: bromine is toxic and corrosive, so use a fume cupboard (a ventilated cabinet that removes fumes) and gloves.
[chem9-s1.9.b] This experiment shows that gases diffuse and that lighter gas particles diffuse faster than heavier ones. Two gases start at opposite ends of a tube and meet where they react.
[chem9-s1.9.0] Method: cotton-wool plugs soaked in concentrated ammonia and concentrated hydrochloric acid are placed at opposite ends of a long glass tube, which is sealed with stoppers.
[chem9-s1.9.1] Observation: after a few minutes a white ring of ammonium chloride forms inside the tube where the two gases meet: NH₃(g) + HCl(g) → NH₄Cl(s).
[chem9-s1.9.2] The ring forms nearer the hydrochloric acid end of the tube, because the lighter ammonia travels further.
[chem9-s1.9.3] Explanation: ammonia molecules (Mr 17, relative molecular mass) are lighter than hydrogen chloride molecules (Mr 36.5), so at the same temperature they diffuse faster and travel further before they meet.
[chem9-s1.9.4] Safety: concentrated ammonia and hydrochloric acid give off irritant, corrosive fumes, so use a fume cupboard, goggles, gloves and tongs to handle the plugs.
[chem9-s1.9.5] Diagram: the long tube with the ammonia plug at one end, the hydrochloric acid plug at the other, and the white ring closer to the hydrochloric acid end.
[chem9-s1.10.b] Diffusion is not limited to gases. It also happens in liquids, but far more slowly.
[chem9-s1.10.0] Diffusion also happens in liquids, e.g. a crystal of potassium manganate(VII) slowly colours still water purple.
[chem9-s1.10.1] Diffusion is much slower in liquids than in gases, because the particles are closer together and collide more often.

CANDIDATES chem6-s1: 1. Bond type and intermolecular forces
[chem6-s1.0.b] When two atoms share a pair of electrons in a covalent bond (a bond formed by sharing electrons), the pair is not always shared equally. Electronegativity tells you which atom pulls harder on it. There is more on the continuum from ionic to non-polar covalent bonding in “Types of chemical bonding”.
[chem6-s1.0.0] Electronegativity is the relative attraction that an atom in a molecule has for the shared pair of electrons in a covalent bond. The higher the value, the harder the atom pulls on the pair.
[chem6-s1.0.1] It is measured on the Pauling scale, on which fluorine, the most electronegative element, has the highest value of 3.98 (about 4). Pauling values are relative, so they have no units.
[chem6-s1.0.2] Pauling values, highest to lowest: F 3.98; O 3.44; Cl 3.16; N 3.04; Br 2.96; I 2.66; S 2.58; C 2.55; H 2.20; P 2.19; Si 1.90; Al 1.61; Mg 1.31; Ca 1.00; Li 0.98; Na 0.93; K 0.82.
[chem6-s1.0.3] Non-metals have high electronegativities and metals have low ones, so a non-metal bonded to a metal pulls the electrons strongly towards itself.
[chem6-s1.1.b] Electronegativity follows a pattern in the periodic table. Both halves of the pattern are explained by how close the bonding electrons are to the nucleus (the positive centre of the atom) and how much positive charge pulls on them.
[chem6-s1.1.0] Across a period (a row of the periodic table, left to right) electronegativity increases, for example from Na 0.93 to Cl 3.16.
[chem6-s1.1.1] Across a period the nuclear charge (the positive charge of the nucleus) increases while electrons are added to the same main energy level (shell), so the atomic radius (the size of the atom) decreases.
[chem6-s1.1.2] Because the atomic radius decreases across a period, the bonding electrons are closer to a more highly charged nucleus, so the nucleus attracts them more strongly.
[chem6-s1.1.3] Down a group (a column of the periodic table) electronegativity decreases, for example from F 3.98 to I 2.66.
[chem6-s1.1.4] Down a group each element has an extra energy level, so the atomic radius increases and the bonding electrons are further from the nucleus.
[chem6-s1.1.5] Inner electrons screen (shield) the outer electrons from the nucleus. The bonding electrons are therefore further away and less strongly attracted, even though the nuclear charge is greater.
[chem6-s1.1.6] Noble gases are usually given no electronegativity value because they rarely form bonds, so there is no shared pair to attract.
[chem6-s1.2.b] The difference in electronegativity between two bonded atoms predicts the bond type: the larger the difference, the more ionic the bond. The cut-offs below are guides, not sharp boundaries, because bonding is a continuum (a smooth range from ionic to non-polar covalent).
[chem6-s1.2.0] Step 1: Look up the Pauling electronegativity of each atom.
[chem6-s1.2.1] Step 2: Subtract the smaller value from the larger to find the difference.
[chem6-s1.2.2] Step 3: Compare the difference with the cut-offs below.
[chem6-s1.2.3] A difference greater than 1.7 gives a mainly ionic bond (electrons largely transferred from one atom to the other), for example NaCl: 3.16 − 0.93 = 2.23.
[chem6-s1.2.4] A difference between about 0.4 and 1.7 gives a polar covalent bond (a bond in which the shared pair is pulled unequally), for example H–Cl: 3.16 − 2.20 = 0.96; O–H: 3.44 − 2.20 = 1.24.
[chem6-s1.2.5] A difference less than about 0.4 gives a non-polar (pure) covalent bond, in which the pair is shared almost equally, for example C–H: 2.55 − 2.20 = 0.35.
[chem6-s1.2.6] A bond between identical atoms has a difference of zero, so the pair is shared equally and the bond is non-polar, for example Cl–Cl: 3.16 − 3.16 = 0.
[chem6-s1.2.7] In a polar bond the more electronegative atom carries the partial negative charge (δ−) and the less electronegative atom carries the partial positive charge (δ+).
[chem6-s1.3.b] Ionic compounds are made of oppositely charged ions packed in a lattice (a regular, repeating three-dimensional arrangement). Their properties follow from the strong attraction between those ions and from whether the ions can move.
[chem6-s1.3.0] Ionic compounds are solids at room temperature, for example sodium chloride and magnesium oxide.
[chem6-s1.3.1] Sodium chloride melts at 801 °C and boils at 1413 °C.
[chem6-s1.3.2] The melting and boiling points are high because a lot of energy is needed to overcome the strong electrostatic attractions (attractions between opposite charges) between oppositely charged ions throughout the lattice.
[chem6-s1.3.3] Solid ionic compounds do not conduct electricity, because the ions are held in fixed positions and cannot carry charge.
[chem6-s1.3.4] Molten ionic compounds, and ionic compounds dissolved in water, conduct electricity, because the ions are free to move and carry charge.
[chem6-s1.3.5] Many ionic compounds are soluble in water, because polar water molecules (molecules with a slightly positive end and a slightly negative end) attract and surround the ions.
[chem6-s1.3.6] Ionic compounds are insoluble in non-polar solvents such as cyclohexane, because non-polar molecules have no charged ends to attract the ions.
[chem6-s1.4.b] Covalent compounds are made of separate molecules. Because of this, melting or boiling them only has to separate one molecule from the next, which takes far less energy than melting an ionic lattice.
[chem6-s1.4.0] Many covalent compounds are gases or liquids at room temperature, for example methane, carbon dioxide and water.
[chem6-s1.4.1] Some covalent compounds are solids with low melting points, for example iodine and sugar.
[chem6-s1.4.2] Iodine sublimes (turns straight from solid to gas) on gentle heating and melts at 114 °C.
[chem6-s1.4.3] Covalent compounds have low melting and boiling points, because only the weak forces between molecules have to be overcome.
[chem6-s1.4.4] The covalent bonds inside the molecules do not break when a covalent substance melts or boils: methane boils at −162 °C.
[chem6-s1.5.b] Whether a covalent compound conducts or dissolves depends on whether it has charged particles and on whether its molecules are polar (have a slightly positive end and a slightly negative end).
[chem6-s1.5.0] Covalent compounds do not conduct electricity, because there are no ions or free electrons to carry charge.
[chem6-s1.5.1] Some covalent compounds, such as hydrogen chloride, form ions when they dissolve in water and then conduct.
[chem6-s1.5.2] Non-polar molecules such as methane and iodine are insoluble or only slightly soluble in water, but dissolve in non-polar solvents.
[chem6-s1.5.3] Polar molecules such as ethanol, sugar and ammonia dissolve in water, because they can form hydrogen bonds with it. There is more on this in “Intermolecular forces and physical properties”.
[chem6-s1.5.4] “Like dissolves like” sums up covalent solubility: polar molecules dissolve in water and non-polar molecules dissolve in non-polar solvents.
[chem6-s1.6.b] Putting the two types side by side shows four paired differences, and each has its own reason: melting and boiling points depend on whether strong ionic attractions or only weak forces between molecules must be overcome, and conductivity depends on whether ions are free to move.
[chem6-s1.6.0] Melting and boiling points: ionic compounds are high; covalent compounds are low.
[chem6-s1.6.1] Electrical conductivity: ionic compounds conduct when molten or dissolved; covalent compounds generally do not conduct.
[chem6-s1.6.2] Solubility: ionic compounds are usually soluble in water; covalent compounds are usually soluble in non-polar solvents.
[chem6-s1.6.3] State at room temperature: ionic compounds are solids; covalent compounds are often gases or liquids.
[chem6-s1.6.4] Exception: giant covalent substances such as diamond and graphite have very high melting points, because strong covalent bonds must be broken. There is more on this in “Allotropes of Carbon. Hydrocarbons”.
[chem6-s1.7.b] A simple conductivity test shows whether a substance contains ions that are free to move. Ionic compounds pass the test when molten or dissolved.
[chem6-s1.7.0] Step 1: Melt the substance, or dissolve it in water.
[chem6-s1.7.1] Step 2: Dip two carbon electrodes (the rods that carry current into the liquid) into it, connected to a battery and a bulb (or an ammeter, which measures current).
[chem6-s1.7.2] Step 3: A lit bulb, or a current on the ammeter, shows that ions are present and free to carry charge.
[chem6-s1.7.3] Diagram: a conductivity circuit with a battery, a bulb and two carbon electrodes dipping into a beaker of solution; label the battery, bulb, electrodes and beaker.
[chem6-s1.8.b] Two different kinds of attraction operate in a molecular substance: bonds inside each molecule and forces between molecules. Keeping them apart explains why melting and boiling are easy compared with breaking a molecule.
[chem6-s1.8.0] Intramolecular bonding is the strong covalent bonding within a molecule, holding its atoms together.
[chem6-s1.8.1] Intermolecular forces are the weaker forces of attraction between separate molecules.
[chem6-s1.8.2] Intermolecular forces are much weaker than covalent bonds: boiling water overcomes the forces between H₂O molecules but does not break the O–H bonds.
[chem6-s1.8.3] Van der Waals forces are the collective name for the intermolecular forces: London dispersion forces and permanent dipole–dipole forces (including hydrogen bonding).
[chem6-s1.8.4] Diagram: two water molecules, with the O–H covalent bonds labelled “intramolecular” and the attraction between an H of one molecule and the O of the other labelled “intermolecular (hydrogen bond)”.
[chem6-s1.9.b] Even a molecule with no permanent charged ends still attracts its neighbours weakly. London dispersion forces are the intermolecular force that every molecule has.
[chem6-s1.9.0] London dispersion forces act between all molecules, and are the only intermolecular forces between non-polar molecules, for example H₂, Cl₂, CH₄ and noble gas atoms.
[chem6-s1.9.1] They arise because electrons are always moving: at any instant the electron cloud may be unevenly spread, giving a temporary (instantaneous) dipole (one side slightly negative, the other slightly positive).
[chem6-s1.9.2] The temporary dipole induces a dipole in a neighbouring molecule (pushes its electrons to one side), and the two attract.
[chem6-s1.9.3] London dispersion forces are the weakest intermolecular forces for small molecules, but they get stronger as molecules get larger and have more electrons, because a bigger electron cloud is easier to distort.
[chem6-s1.10.b] Polar molecules have charged ends all the time, not just for an instant. This gives them an extra attraction on top of London dispersion forces.
[chem6-s1.10.0] A polar molecule has a permanent dipole: a δ+ end and a δ− end, which arises from polar bonds. There is more on this in “Electronegativity and bond type”.
[chem6-s1.10.1] Dipole–dipole forces are attractions between the δ+ end of one polar molecule and the δ− end of a neighbouring one, for example between HCl molecules (Hδ+–Clδ−).
[chem6-s1.10.2] Dipole–dipole forces are stronger than London dispersion forces between molecules of similar size, because the charged ends are permanent.
[chem6-s1.11.b] Hydrogen bonding is the strongest of the intermolecular forces. It needs a hydrogen atom attached to one of three very electronegative atoms, F, O or N.
[chem6-s1.11.0] A hydrogen bond is a particularly strong dipole–dipole attraction between a hydrogen atom bonded to a small, very electronegative atom (F, O or N) in one molecule and a lone pair on an F, O or N atom of a neighbouring molecule.
[chem6-s1.11.1] A lone pair is a pair of outer electrons on an atom that is not used in bonding, so it can attract the δ+ hydrogen of a neighbouring molecule.
[chem6-s1.11.2] It is strong because the H–F, O–H and N–H bonds are very polar, leaving the small hydrogen atom strongly δ+.
[chem6-s1.11.3] A hydrogen bond is between molecules, not a covalent bond to hydrogen.
[chem6-s1.11.4] Hydrogen bonding needs H bonded directly to N, O or F: there is no hydrogen bonding between CH₄ molecules or in HCl.
[chem6-s1.11.5] Examples: water (H₂O), ammonia (NH₃), hydrogen fluoride (HF), alcohols such as ethanol, and carboxylic acids.
[chem6-s1.11.6] Hydrogen bonds are the strongest intermolecular forces.
[chem6-s1.11.7] Diagram: water molecules with δ+ and δ− labelled, and hydrogen bonds drawn as dashed lines from H to the lone pair on O of the next molecule.
[chem6-s1.12.b] When an ionic compound dissolves in water, the ions are pulled out of the lattice by the polar water molecules. The attraction between a full charge and a partial charge is called an ion–dipole force.
[chem6-s1.12.0] An ion–dipole force is the attraction between an ion and the oppositely charged end of a polar molecule.
[chem6-s1.12.1] When sodium chloride dissolves in water, the δ− oxygen ends of water molecules are attracted to Na⁺ ions and the δ+ hydrogen ends to Cl⁻ ions.
[chem6-s1.12.2] The ions become surrounded by water molecules (hydrated).
[chem6-s1.12.3] Ion–dipole forces are stronger than dipole–dipole forces; they explain why ionic compounds dissolve in polar solvents such as water.
[chem6-s1.12.4] Diagram: a Na⁺ ion surrounded by water molecules with their O atoms pointing towards it, and a Cl⁻ ion surrounded by water molecules with their H atoms pointing towards it.
[chem6-s1.13.b] Putting the forces in order lets you predict which substance holds its molecules together most strongly, for molecules of similar size.
[chem6-s1.13.0] For molecules of similar size, London dispersion forces are weaker than dipole–dipole forces, which are weaker than hydrogen bonds.
[chem6-s1.13.1] All intermolecular forces are much weaker than covalent and ionic bonds, which is why melting or boiling a molecular substance leaves its molecules intact.

CANDIDATES chem1-s1: 1. How science works: ideas and models
[chem1-s1.0.b] Scientific knowledge is built from observations and evidence, and everyday words such as "theory" and "law" have a much more precise meaning in science. Learning the exact meanings lets you describe how science works.
[chem1-s1.0.0] Observation: information gathered with the senses or with instruments. It is qualitative (a description, such as a colour change) or quantitative (a measurement with a number and a unit).
[chem1-s1.0.1] Hypothesis: a testable, tentative explanation or prediction. It is tentative (not yet confirmed) because evidence from an experiment can support it or contradict it.
[chem1-s1.0.2] Theory: a well-tested explanation of a wide range of observations, supported by a large body of evidence from many investigations. Examples are the kinetic theory of matter (matter is made of moving particles) and collision theory (reactions happen when particles collide).
[chem1-s1.0.3] Law: a statement, often mathematical, that describes what always happens under stated conditions. Examples are the law of conservation of mass (the total mass stays the same during a reaction) and Boyle's law (a law linking the pressure and volume of a gas).
[chem1-s1.0.4] A law describes what happens; a theory explains why it happens. This is why a theory never turns into a law: they do different jobs, and a law is not a "proven theory".
[chem1-s1.0.5] Evidence: data from observation and experiment that are used to support or reject a hypothesis or theory. Scientific knowledge rests on evidence, not on authority or opinion, so a claim is only as good as the data behind it.
[chem1-s1.1.b] Scientists follow a recognisable sequence of steps, from first noticing something to sharing what they found. Each step exists so that the final conclusion rests on evidence that others can check.
[chem1-s1.1.0] 1. Make observations and ask a question about what you have noticed.
[chem1-s1.1.1] 2. Research what is already known, so that you do not repeat work or ignore existing evidence.
[chem1-s1.1.2] 3. Form a hypothesis (a tentative explanation) and use it to make a prediction of what should happen if it is right.
[chem1-s1.1.3] 4. Test the prediction with a fair, controlled experiment, in which only one factor is changed and the others are kept the same.
[chem1-s1.1.4] 5. Analyse the data and draw a conclusion.
[chem1-s1.1.5] 6. Accept, modify or reject the hypothesis, depending on whether the data support it.
[chem1-s1.1.6] 7. Communicate the results so that others can check them.
[chem1-s1.2.b] In practice the steps above are not completed once and then left behind. Real investigations loop back, and they need imagination as well as method.
[chem1-s1.2.0] Scientists repeat, redesign and troubleshoot (find and fix faults in) experiments, so the cycle is iterative, meaning it is repeated and each pass improves on the last.
[chem1-s1.2.1] Unexpected results often lead to new questions and new hypotheses, so a surprising result can open a new line of investigation.
[chem1-s1.2.2] Creativity and imagination, as well as logic, are used to propose hypotheses and to design experiments and models, because no rule produces a good idea automatically.
[chem1-s1.2.3] One reliable, reproducible result (one that others can obtain again) that contradicts a hypothesis can be enough to reject it, whereas agreeing results never prove a hypothesis absolutely.
[chem1-s1.3.b] Scientific ideas are not fixed for ever. They change in a particular way, and that way is what makes science reliable.
[chem1-s1.3.0] Scientific knowledge is tentative (provisional): it is the best explanation of the evidence available now, and it can change when new evidence appears.
[chem1-s1.3.1] Ideas change when new observations do not fit the existing explanation, because an explanation has to account for everything observed.
[chem1-s1.3.2] Ideas change when new technology or instruments allow measurements that were not possible before.
[chem1-s1.3.3] Ideas change when a better explanation is proposed and tested against the evidence.
[chem1-s1.3.4] A theory is refined or replaced only when the new idea explains all the old evidence as well as the new evidence; this is how science progresses.
[chem1-s1.3.5] Long-standing, well-tested theories are durable. They are rarely discarded completely; they are modified or have their limits identified.
[chem1-s1.4.b] The atom shows how ideas change. Each model of the atom was replaced or refined when new evidence did not fit the earlier one. A model here means a picture of the atom that explains what has been observed.
[chem1-s1.4.0] Dalton (early 1800s): matter is made of tiny indivisible atoms (atoms that cannot be split), and atoms of the same element are identical. This was supported by the laws of chemical combination (rules for the masses in which elements combine).
[chem1-s1.4.1] Thomson (1897) discovered the electron, a negatively charged particle, which showed that atoms are not indivisible. He proposed the plum pudding model, with negative electrons embedded in a sphere of positive charge.
[chem1-s1.4.2] Rutherford (1911): his gold-foil experiment, carried out by Geiger and Marsden, showed that most of the atom is empty space with a tiny, dense, positive nucleus (the central core). This is the nuclear model.
[chem1-s1.4.3] Bohr (1913) proposed that electrons move in fixed energy levels around the nucleus, which explained line emission spectra (the separate coloured lines of light that an element gives out when heated).
[chem1-s1.4.4] The current orbital model describes electrons in orbitals, which are regions with a high probability of finding an electron. There is more on this in "Atomic structure: the nuclear model" and "The orbital model and electronic structure".
[chem1-s1.5.b] Scientists are people, and people can unknowingly tilt results. Bias is a tendency, deliberate or not, to collect, interpret or present information so as to favour one outcome.
[chem1-s1.5.0] Confirmation bias: expecting a particular result and then seeing it in the data.
[chem1-s1.5.1] Selecting only the data that support a hypothesis, or using small or unrepresentative samples, introduces bias, because the results then do not reflect the whole picture.
[chem1-s1.5.2] Funding or commercial interests, and personal or political beliefs, can introduce bias, for example when a result would benefit whoever paid for the study.
[chem1-s1.5.3] Scientists reduce bias with fair tests that use controls (a comparison with nothing changed), repeats and random sampling (choosing subjects by chance), and with blind trials (where those taking part do not know which treatment they receive) where possible.
[chem1-s1.5.4] Scientists also publish all results, including negative ones (results that do not support the hypothesis), declare funding and conflicts of interest, and invite independent checks by other scientists. There is more on this in "Science as a global enterprise and researching an issue".
[chem1-s1.6.b] Much of chemistry is about things too small to see, such as atoms and molecules. Scientists use models to think about them. A model is a representation of an object, system, process or idea that helps us understand, explain or predict something we cannot see or handle directly.
[chem1-s1.6.0] Physical models include ball-and-stick and space-filling molecular models, which are three-dimensional builds of a molecule that show how its atoms are arranged.
[chem1-s1.6.1] Diagrams used as models include dot-and-cross diagrams (showing the electrons in bonds), energy-level diagrams and energy profile diagrams (showing how energy changes during a reaction).
[chem1-s1.6.2] Mathematical models are equations, such as PV = nRT, which links the pressure, volume, amount and temperature of a gas.
[chem1-s1.6.3] Symbolic models are chemical formulae and equations, which use symbols to stand for substances and reactions.
[chem1-s1.6.4] Computer or digital simulations show particles and processes moving and changing, which a fixed diagram cannot do.
[chem1-s1.6.5] A theory can also act as a model: the kinetic theory models matter as moving particles, and collision theory models how reactions happen.
[chem1-s1.7.b] Models are used because they do things that direct observation cannot. They give us a way to picture, explain and test ideas.
[chem1-s1.7.0] Models make the very small, the very large and the invisible understandable; a molecular model shows the shape of a molecule that is far too small to see.
[chem1-s1.7.1] Models explain observations: the particle model (matter as tiny moving particles) explains why gases can be compressed but liquids cannot be compressed much.
[chem1-s1.7.2] Models allow predictions that can be tested: the ideal gas equation predicts the volume of a gas at a new temperature and pressure, and VSEPR theory (which treats electron pairs around an atom as repelling one another) predicts molecular shapes.
[chem1-s1.7.3] Models help scientists communicate ideas to each other and to the public, because a shared picture is easier to discuss than an unseen object.
[chem1-s1.8.b] A model is useful but never complete. Knowing where a model stops working is as important as knowing what it explains.
[chem1-s1.8.0] Every model is a simplification: it leaves out detail and rests on assumptions, so it is never a perfect copy of reality.
[chem1-s1.8.1] A model works only within a range of conditions; the ideal gas model fails at high pressure and low temperature, where real gases deviate from it.
[chem1-s1.8.2] Ball-and-stick models show atoms as hard coloured balls and bonds as rigid sticks, and they show the wrong relative sizes and distances, so they can mislead.
[chem1-s1.8.3] Different models of the same thing may each explain some observations but not others, so chemists choose the model that suits the question.
[chem1-s1.8.4] The Bohr model (electrons in fixed energy levels) explains flame colours and line spectra but not the shapes of orbitals.
[chem1-s1.8.5] Models are tested against evidence and are modified or replaced when they fail to explain new observations.
