TARGET chem10-s1: 1. Physical and chemical change
[chem10-s1.0.b] Every substance can be described by what it looks like and does on its own, and by how it behaves when it reacts. Chemists split these descriptions into physical properties and chemical properties, and this split is the basis for telling the two kinds of change apart.
[chem10-s1.0.0] A physical property can be observed or measured without changing the substance into a new substance, so you can test it and still have the same material afterwards.
[chem10-s1.0.1] Physical properties include colour, state (solid, liquid or gas), density (mass per unit volume), melting point, boiling point, solubility (how well it dissolves), electrical conductivity (whether it carries a current) and hardness.
[chem10-s1.0.2] A chemical property describes how a substance reacts to form new substances, so it can only be found out by reacting the substance.
[chem10-s1.0.3] Chemical properties include flammability (whether it burns), reactivity with water, oxygen or acids, whether a substance is acidic or basic, and how it decomposes (breaks down into simpler substances) on heating.
[chem10-s1.1.b] In a physical change the substance looks or behaves differently but is still the same substance. Only how its particles are arranged or spaced has altered.
[chem10-s1.1.0] A physical change is one in which no new substance is formed; the particles are not changed, only their arrangement or spacing.
[chem10-s1.1.1] A physical change is usually easily reversed and involves only a small energy change, because no new substance forms; only the arrangement or spacing of the particles changes.
[chem10-s1.1.2] A change of state is always a physical change: melting, boiling, freezing and condensing only move the same particles further apart or closer together.
[chem10-s1.1.3] Examples of physical change: melting ice, boiling water, dissolving sugar or salt in water, sublimation of iodine (solid turning straight to gas), crushing a solid and magnetising iron.
[chem10-s1.1.4] The mass is unchanged in a physical change, because the same particles are present before and after.
[chem10-s1.2.b] In a chemical change the substances you start with are used up and different substances are made. This happens by rearranging atoms, so the product has properties of its own.
[chem10-s1.2.0] A chemical change, or chemical reaction, is one in which one or more new substances are formed, with different properties from the starting substances.
[chem10-s1.2.1] In a chemical change bonds are broken and new bonds are made, so the atoms are rearranged into new particles.
[chem10-s1.2.2] A chemical change is usually difficult to reverse, because the new substances have to be turned back into the originals by another reaction.
[chem10-s1.2.3] A chemical change usually involves a noticeable energy change, with heat or light given out or taken in, because breaking and making bonds changes the energy stored in the substances.
[chem10-s1.2.4] Total mass is conserved in a chemical change too, if nothing escapes, because the atoms are only rearranged. There is more on this in the investigation on conservation of mass below.
[chem10-s1.3.b] Because a chemical change makes new substances, it often produces something you can see, hear, feel or smell. These are the signs to look for, but they are clues rather than proof.
[chem10-s1.3.0] A gas is given off (effervescence, which is fizzing or bubbling), showing a new gas has been made.
[chem10-s1.3.1] A precipitate forms, meaning an insoluble solid appears in a solution.
[chem10-s1.3.2] A permanent colour change occurs, since the new substance has a different colour from the old one.
[chem10-s1.3.3] The temperature changes, because the reaction gives out or takes in heat.
[chem10-s1.3.4] Light is produced.
[chem10-s1.3.5] A smell is produced.
[chem10-s1.3.6] A colour change alone is not proof of a chemical change: dissolving copper sulfate in water gives a blue solution, which is a physical change.
[chem10-s1.4.b] These are the reactions to quote when asked to classify a change or give an example of a chemical change. In each one a new substance is formed.
[chem10-s1.4.0] Burning magnesium forms white magnesium oxide, a new substance with light and heat given out.
[chem10-s1.4.1] Rusting of iron.
[chem10-s1.4.2] Cooking an egg.
[chem10-s1.4.3] Magnesium fizzing in hydrochloric acid, where the fizzing is gas being given off.
[chem10-s1.4.4] Heating copper(II) carbonate decomposes it: the green solid forms black copper(II) oxide and carbon dioxide.
[chem10-s1.4.5] Neutralisation of an acid by a base is also a chemical change; the equations are given in the section on balancing equations.
[chem10-s1.5.b] Put side by side, the two kinds of change differ on four points. The test is always whether a new substance has been made.
[chem10-s1.5.0] No new substance is formed in a physical change, but a new substance is formed in a chemical change.
[chem10-s1.5.1] A physical change is easily reversed; a chemical change is usually difficult to reverse.
[chem10-s1.5.2] A physical change involves a small energy change; a chemical change usually involves a large energy change.
[chem10-s1.5.3] Particles are unchanged in a physical change, but atoms are rearranged into new particles in a chemical change.
[chem10-s1.5.4] Borderline case: dissolving salt in water is a physical change, because evaporating the water recovers the salt unchanged, even though its ions (charged particles) are separated.
[chem10-s1.6.b] Weighing reactions carefully showed that nothing is lost or gained in the total mass. This is one of the two laws behind all physical and chemical changes.
[chem10-s1.6.0] Law of conservation of mass: in a chemical reaction or physical change, matter is neither created nor destroyed, so the total mass of the products (substances made) equals the total mass of the reactants (substances that react).
[chem10-s1.6.1] Antoine Lavoisier established the law in the late 1700s (published 1789) by careful weighing of reactions in sealed vessels, so that no gas could escape unnoticed.
[chem10-s1.6.2] The number of moles (the amount of substance, counted in particles) or molecules may change in a reaction, but the mass does not.
[chem10-s1.7.b] Energy follows a similar rule to mass. It can change form during a reaction, but the total stays the same.
[chem10-s1.7.0] Law of conservation of energy: energy cannot be created or destroyed, only changed from one form to another.
[chem10-s1.7.1] When a fuel burns, chemical energy (energy stored in the bonds of the fuel) is changed to heat and light.
[chem10-s1.7.2] The total energy of a reaction and its surroundings stays the same, so any energy lost by the reacting substances appears in the surroundings.
[chem10-s1.8.b] The two laws are not just facts to state; they explain two things chemists do every day.
[chem10-s1.8.0] Both laws underpin all physical and chemical changes.
[chem10-s1.8.1] Conservation of atoms, and so of mass, is why chemical equations must be balanced: there must be the same number of each kind of atom on both sides.
[chem10-s1.8.2] Conservation of energy is why energy changes in reactions can be measured: the heat given out or taken in is accounted for in the surroundings.
[chem10-s1.9.b] This experiment tests the law of conservation of mass using your own measurements (primary data). The idea is to weigh a reaction without letting anything escape.
[chem10-s1.9.0] Aim: to verify, using primary data, that mass is conserved in a chemical reaction.
[chem10-s1.9.1] Hypothesis: the total mass before and after the reaction will be the same, because atoms are only rearranged.
[chem10-s1.9.2] Measured variable: the total mass of the stoppered apparatus before and after the reaction; no variable is changed on purpose.
[chem10-s1.10.b] Two solutions that react to form a precipitate are kept apart inside a stoppered flask until the flask is tilted. Because the flask is sealed, any change in mass would show up on the balance.
[chem10-s1.10.0] Apparatus: conical flask, small test tube, stopper, electronic balance (0.01 g), sodium chloride solution and silver nitrate solution.
[chem10-s1.10.1] Diagram: a stoppered conical flask on a balance, containing sodium chloride solution, with a small test tube of silver nitrate solution standing upright inside; label the flask, stopper, test tube and both solutions.
[chem10-s1.10.2] Step 1: Pour some sodium chloride solution into the conical flask.
[chem10-s1.10.3] Step 2: Half-fill the small test tube with silver nitrate solution and lower it carefully, upright, into the flask so the solutions don't mix.
[chem10-s1.10.4] Step 3: Stopper the flask and weigh the whole apparatus.
[chem10-s1.10.5] Step 4: Tilt the flask so the solutions mix; a white precipitate of silver chloride forms: AgNO₃(aq) + NaCl(aq) → AgCl(s) + NaNO₃(aq).
[chem10-s1.10.6] Step 5: Reweigh the stoppered flask, and repeat the investigation three times.
[chem10-s1.11.b] The reweighed flask gives the result, and the precautions come from the chemicals used.
[chem10-s1.11.0] Results: the mass before and after the reaction is the same, within the precision of the balance (0.01 g), so mass is conserved.
[chem10-s1.11.1] Safety: silver nitrate stains the skin and is an irritant, so wear gloves and goggles.
[chem10-s1.11.2] Lead nitrate is not used in this method because it is toxic.
[chem10-s1.12.b] Mass can seem to be lost if a gas is made and escapes. Comparing an open flask with a sealed one shows that the law still holds.
[chem10-s1.12.0] In an open flask, marble chips (calcium carbonate) reacting with hydrochloric acid make the mass on the balance decrease: CaCO₃(s) + 2HCl(aq) → CaCl₂(aq) + H₂O(l) + CO₂(g).
[chem10-s1.12.1] The carbon dioxide escapes, so the apparent loss of mass is gas leaving the flask, not mass being destroyed.
[chem10-s1.12.2] If the same reaction is done in a sealed flask, or with a balloon over the neck to trap the gas, the mass stays the same.
[chem10-s1.12.3] When magnesium burns in air, the product is heavier than the magnesium, because oxygen from the air has combined with it: 2Mg + O₂ → 2MgO.
[chem10-s1.13.b] Models let us explain why mass is conserved. All three below show the same idea: the same atoms are present before and after, just arranged differently.
[chem10-s1.13.0] Particle model: in a reaction the atoms in the reactants are rearranged to form the products; no atoms are created or destroyed, so the total mass stays the same.
[chem10-s1.13.1] A balanced equation is a symbolic model of conservation: the silver nitrate equation has 1 Ag, 1 N, 3 O, 1 Na and 1 Cl on each side.
[chem10-s1.13.2] A physical model, such as molecular model kits, shows the same idea: building the reactant molecules, taking them apart and rebuilding the product molecules uses exactly the same pieces.
[chem10-s1.13.3] Diagram: particle diagrams of 2H₂ + O₂ → 2H₂O, showing four H atoms and two O atoms before the reaction and after it.

CANDIDATES chem9-s1: 1. The kinetic theory and its evidence
[chem9-s1.0.b] Everything around you, from air to iron, is matter. Chemistry starts from one idea: matter is built from particles too small to see, and how those particles are arranged decides whether something is a solid, a liquid or a gas.
[chem9-s1.0.0] All matter is made of tiny particles (atoms, molecules or ions) that are constantly moving, with spaces between them.
[chem9-s1.0.1] Solid: the particles are packed closely in a fixed, regular arrangement and vibrate about fixed positions, so a solid keeps its shape.
[chem9-s1.0.2] Liquid: the particles are close together but can move past each other, so a liquid flows and takes the shape of its container.
[chem9-s1.0.3] Gas: the particles are far apart and move rapidly and randomly in all directions, so a gas spreads out to fill any space.
[chem9-s1.0.4] Diagram: three boxes showing the particle arrangement in a solid, a liquid and a gas.
[chem9-s1.1.b] A pure substance is made of only one kind of particle, so it has fixed properties. There are two kinds of pure substance: elements and compounds.
[chem9-s1.1.0] A pure substance contains only one kind of particle (one element or one compound) and has fixed properties, such as a sharp melting point and boiling point (a single exact temperature, not a range).
[chem9-s1.1.1] An element is a substance made of only one kind of atom; it cannot be split into simpler substances by chemical means (that is, by a chemical reaction), e.g. iron (Fe) and oxygen (O₂).
[chem9-s1.1.2] A compound is a substance made of two or more different elements chemically combined (joined by bonds) in a fixed ratio, e.g. water (H₂O) and sodium chloride (NaCl).
[chem9-s1.1.3] A compound has properties that differ from those of its elements, because the elements are bonded together, so it can be separated into them only by a chemical reaction.
[chem9-s1.1.4] A molecule (a group of atoms joined together) can be of an element (O₂, N₂, Cl₂), where all the atoms are the same, or of a compound (H₂O, CO₂), where the atoms are different.
[chem9-s1.2.b] A mixture is two or more substances that are not chemically combined, so each keeps its own properties. Mixtures are grouped by whether they look the same all the way through.
[chem9-s1.2.0] A mixture can have a variable composition (the amounts of each substance can change), and its components keep their own properties.
[chem9-s1.2.1] A mixture can be separated by physical methods (ones that need no chemical reaction), and it has no sharp melting or boiling point: it melts or boils over a range of temperatures. There is more on this in Using the kinetic theory: separation techniques.
[chem9-s1.2.2] A homogeneous mixture has a uniform composition throughout and a single phase (one visible part), e.g. salt solution, air and an alloy (a mixture of metals).
[chem9-s1.2.3] A heterogeneous mixture is non-uniform, with visibly different parts or phases, e.g. sand and water, oil and water, and granite.
[chem9-s1.3.b] Because a pure substance has a fixed melting point and a mixture does not, melting point can be used to check whether a sample is pure.
[chem9-s1.3.0] A pure substance has a sharp melting point that matches the data-book value (the accepted figure listed in reference tables).
[chem9-s1.3.1] Impurities lower the melting point and make the substance melt over a range of temperatures, so a low or spread-out melting point shows the sample is not pure.
[chem9-s1.4.b] Compounds and mixtures both contain more than one element, so they are easy to confuse. The difference is whether the substances are chemically combined.
[chem9-s1.4.0] A compound has a fixed composition; a mixture has a variable composition.
[chem9-s1.4.1] The elements in a compound are chemically combined; the substances in a mixture are not.
[chem9-s1.4.2] A compound has new properties because its elements are chemically combined; a mixture's components keep their own properties because they are not.
[chem9-s1.4.3] A compound can be separated only chemically, because its elements are bonded; a mixture can be separated physically, because nothing is bonded.
[chem9-s1.4.4] Energy is usually taken in or given out when a compound forms, whereas there is little energy change when a mixture is made.
[chem9-s1.4.5] Diagram: particle diagrams of an element (single atoms and diatomic molecules, which are molecules of two atoms), a compound (identical molecules, each made of two kinds of atom) and a mixture (two kinds of particle mixed together).
[chem9-s1.5.b] The kinetic theory states that matter is particles in motion. We cannot see the particles, so the theory rests on effects we can see.
[chem9-s1.5.0] The kinetic theory of matter states that all matter is made of particles that are in constant, random motion.
[chem9-s1.5.1] The particles have kinetic energy (energy of movement), so the higher the temperature, the faster they move.
[chem9-s1.5.2] The average kinetic energy of the particles is proportional to the temperature in kelvin (the temperature scale that starts at absolute zero), so doubling the kelvin temperature doubles the average kinetic energy.
[chem9-s1.5.3] The evidence for the theory comes from effects we can see that are caused by particles we cannot see.
[chem9-s1.6.b] Brownian motion is the continuous, random, zig-zag movement of small visible particles suspended in a gas or liquid. It shows that the invisible particles around them are moving.
[chem9-s1.6.0] Robert Brown, a botanist (a scientist who studies plants), first observed Brownian motion in 1827, using pollen grains in water.
[chem9-s1.6.1] Aim: to observe the motion of smoke particles in air as evidence for the kinetic theory.
[chem9-s1.6.2] Step 1: Fill a small glass cell with smoke and cover it.
[chem9-s1.6.3] Step 2: Light the cell from the side, so the smoke particles scatter light and show up.
[chem9-s1.6.4] Step 3: View the cell through a microscope.
[chem9-s1.6.5] Observation: the smoke particles appear as bright specks that jiggle about randomly and never stop.
[chem9-s1.7.b] The jiggling of the smoke is the evidence. It can be explained only if the air around the smoke is made of moving particles.
[chem9-s1.7.0] Explanation: the smoke particles are bombarded unevenly from all sides by fast-moving, invisible air molecules; at any instant more molecules hit one side than the other, so the particle moves.
[chem9-s1.7.1] The smoke particles are visible solid particles, not air molecules; they are moved by the invisible air molecules.
[chem9-s1.7.2] Conclusion: the air is made of particles in constant, random motion, and the motion becomes more vigorous when the temperature is raised.
[chem9-s1.7.3] Diagram: a smoke cell lit from the side under a microscope, and the zig-zag path of one smoke particle.
[chem9-s1.8.b] Diffusion is the spreading out of particles from a region of high concentration to a region of low concentration, until they are evenly mixed, as a result of their random motion. Gases show it clearly.
[chem9-s1.8.0] Diffusion needs no stirring or currents, because it results from the random motion of the particles alone.
[chem9-s1.8.1] Bromine experiment: a few drops of liquid bromine are placed in a gas jar, giving brown vapour that spreads upwards through a second jar of air placed on top.
[chem9-s1.8.2] The bromine jars are evenly coloured when the vapour has diffused through both jars, which shows the particles have spread out until evenly mixed.
[chem9-s1.8.3] Safety for bromine: bromine is toxic and corrosive, so use a fume cupboard (a ventilated cabinet that removes fumes) and gloves.
[chem9-s1.9.b] This experiment shows that gases diffuse and that lighter gas particles diffuse faster than heavier ones. Two gases start at opposite ends of a tube and meet where they react.
[chem9-s1.9.0] Method: cotton-wool plugs soaked in concentrated ammonia and concentrated hydrochloric acid are placed at opposite ends of a long glass tube, which is sealed with stoppers.
[chem9-s1.9.1] Observation: after a few minutes a white ring of ammonium chloride forms inside the tube where the two gases meet: NH₃(g) + HCl(g) → NH₄Cl(s).
[chem9-s1.9.2] The ring forms nearer the hydrochloric acid end of the tube, because the lighter ammonia travels further.
[chem9-s1.9.3] Explanation: ammonia molecules (Mr 17, relative molecular mass) are lighter than hydrogen chloride molecules (Mr 36.5), so at the same temperature they diffuse faster and travel further before they meet.
[chem9-s1.9.4] Safety: concentrated ammonia and hydrochloric acid give off irritant, corrosive fumes, so use a fume cupboard, goggles, gloves and tongs to handle the plugs.
[chem9-s1.9.5] Diagram: the long tube with the ammonia plug at one end, the hydrochloric acid plug at the other, and the white ring closer to the hydrochloric acid end.
[chem9-s1.10.b] Diffusion is not limited to gases. It also happens in liquids, but far more slowly.
[chem9-s1.10.0] Diffusion also happens in liquids, e.g. a crystal of potassium manganate(VII) slowly colours still water purple.
[chem9-s1.10.1] Diffusion is much slower in liquids than in gases, because the particles are closer together and collide more often.

CANDIDATES chem2-s1: 1. Atomic structure: the nuclear model
[chem2-s1.0.b] A model of the atom is a picture scientists use to explain the evidence they have. These two early models are background to the nuclear model: each was accepted in its time and then replaced.
[chem2-s1.0.0] Dalton's atomic theory (John Dalton, 1803–1808): all matter is made of tiny indivisible particles called atoms, which means that atoms cannot be split into anything smaller.
[chem2-s1.0.1] Dalton's atoms of the same element are identical, atoms of different elements differ in mass, and atoms combine in simple whole-number ratios to form compounds.
[chem2-s1.0.2] Dalton's atoms are not created or destroyed in chemical reactions, so a reaction only rearranges the atoms that were already there.
[chem2-s1.0.3] Thomson discovered the electron in 1897 using cathode rays (beams of particles given off in a glass tube from which the air has been removed). The electron is a negatively charged particle much lighter than an atom and present in all atoms, so atoms are divisible.
[chem2-s1.0.4] Thomson's plum pudding model: the atom is a sphere of positive charge with negative electrons embedded in it, like plums in a pudding, so the atom is neutral overall because the charges cancel.
[chem2-s1.1.b] The nuclear model came from a scattering experiment, in which a beam of particles is fired at a target and the paths of the deflected particles are studied. The results could not be explained by the plum pudding model.
[chem2-s1.1.0] Gold-foil experiment (1909): Hans Geiger and Ernest Marsden, directed by Rutherford, fired a beam of alpha particles, which are small, fast, positively charged particles, at a very thin sheet of gold foil.
[chem2-s1.1.1] A fluorescent screen (a screen that gives off a flash of light where a particle hits it) was placed around the foil and showed where the particles went after they hit the foil.
[chem2-s1.1.2] Observation: most particles passed straight through the foil, so most of the atom is empty space.
[chem2-s1.1.3] Observation: some particles were deflected through large angles, so there is a concentrated region of positive charge that repels them, because like charges repel.
[chem2-s1.1.4] Observation: a very few particles bounced straight back, so this positive region is very small and very dense, containing almost all of the atom's mass.
[chem2-s1.1.5] This positive region is the nucleus. Rutherford published the nuclear model in 1911: a tiny, dense, positively charged nucleus at the centre of the atom, with electrons moving around it in the mostly empty space outside.
[chem2-s1.1.6] Later work found that the nucleus contains protons (positively charged particles) and neutrons (particles with no charge): Rutherford identified the proton, and Chadwick discovered the neutron in 1932.
[chem2-s1.1.7] Diagram: the gold-foil set-up, labelled with the alpha particle source, the beam, the gold foil and the fluorescent screen, and the paths of particles past the nuclei: straight through, deflected, and bounced back.
[chem2-s1.2.b] Niels Bohr proposed energy levels in 1913 to explain why atoms emit light only at certain frequencies. His model keeps the nucleus but gives the electrons a definite arrangement around it.
[chem2-s1.2.0] Electrons move around the nucleus in fixed circular orbits called energy levels (shells), each with a fixed energy.
[chem2-s1.2.1] An electron in a given energy level does not lose energy, so it stays in that level rather than spiralling inwards.
[chem2-s1.2.2] An electron can move to a higher energy level by absorbing a definite amount of energy.
[chem2-s1.2.3] When the electron falls back to a lower level, it emits light of a definite energy. This explains the lines in emission spectra (the separate coloured lines an element gives out when heated).
[chem2-s1.2.4] Energy levels are numbered n = 1, 2, 3…, with n = 1 closest to the nucleus and lowest in energy.
[chem2-s1.2.5] The energy levels hold up to 2, 8, 18… electrons.
[chem2-s1.3.b] The orbital model is the current model. It describes where electrons are likely to be found, not the path they follow.
[chem2-s1.3.0] Electrons do not follow fixed paths; they occupy orbitals, which are regions of space around the nucleus where there is a high probability of finding an electron.
[chem2-s1.3.1] Main energy levels are divided into sublevels, labelled s, p, d and f; each sublevel is made of orbitals, and each orbital holds up to two electrons. There is more on this in the later subsection on the orbital model.
[chem2-s1.3.2] The model developed from evidence that electrons behave as waves as well as particles, which a fixed circular path cannot describe.
[chem2-s1.3.3] It also rests on the principle that the exact position and the exact path of an electron cannot be known at the same time, which is why only probabilities can be given.
[chem2-s1.4.b] Each model rested on an assumption, and each had a limitation: evidence it could not explain. The first two models fail because the atom has parts and a structure they did not allow for.
[chem2-s1.4.0] Dalton: assumed that atoms are indivisible and that all atoms of an element are identical.
[chem2-s1.4.1] Dalton: limited because subatomic particles (particles smaller than the atom) exist, and because isotopes show that atoms of the same element can differ in mass.
[chem2-s1.4.2] Plum pudding model: assumed that positive charge is spread through the atom.
[chem2-s1.4.3] Plum pudding model: limited because it cannot explain the large deflections and the particles that bounced back in the gold-foil experiment, which need a small, dense positive centre.
[chem2-s1.5.b] The nuclear model fixed the problem of the positive charge but said little about the electrons.
[chem2-s1.5.0] Nuclear model: assumed that electrons orbit the nucleus like planets.
[chem2-s1.5.1] Nuclear model: limited because it does not say how electrons are arranged in the space around the nucleus.
[chem2-s1.5.2] Nuclear model: limited because a charged electron moving in a circle would be expected to lose energy and spiral into the nucleus, so the atom would not be stable.
[chem2-s1.5.3] Nuclear model: limited because it cannot explain line spectra (the separate lines of light an element emits).
[chem2-s1.6.b] The Bohr model fixed the nuclear model's problems for hydrogen but not for other atoms. The orbital model is the best fit for the evidence, but it has its own limitation.
[chem2-s1.6.0] Bohr model: assumed that electrons move in fixed circular orbits with fixed energies.
[chem2-s1.6.1] Bohr model: explains the spectrum of hydrogen, which has one electron, but not the spectra of atoms with more than one electron.
[chem2-s1.6.2] Bohr model: does not account for sublevels or for the wave nature of electrons.
[chem2-s1.6.3] Bohr model: treats the electron's exact path as known, but the exact position and path of an electron cannot be known at the same time.
[chem2-s1.6.4] Orbital model: the best fit for the evidence available, but its limitation is that it is mathematical and abstract.
[chem2-s1.6.5] Orbital model: gives probabilities of where an electron is found, not exact positions, and is hard to picture.
[chem2-s1.7.b] Scientists judge a model by how well it explains the evidence. Replaced models are not useless, because they still work for the things they explain well.
[chem2-s1.7.0] Each model was accepted because it explained the evidence of its time, and was replaced or refined when new evidence did not fit.
[chem2-s1.7.1] The Bohr model is still enough to explain flame colours and simple line spectra.
[chem2-s1.7.2] The Bohr model's arrangement of electrons in shells, such as 2, 8, 8, is still used to predict bonding.
[chem2-s1.7.3] The nuclear model still explains why atoms are mostly empty space.
[chem2-s1.8.b] An atom is made of protons, neutrons and electrons. Their masses and charges are given relative to the proton (that is, as comparisons with it), because the real values are very small numbers.
[chem2-s1.8.0] Proton: relative mass 1, relative charge +1, found in the nucleus.
[chem2-s1.8.1] Neutron: relative mass 1, relative charge 0 (no charge), found in the nucleus.
[chem2-s1.8.2] Electron: relative mass about 1/1840 of a proton, so negligible though not zero; relative charge −1; found outside the nucleus, in energy levels (orbitals).
[chem2-s1.9.b] The relative values hide the real sizes. The actual masses show how tiny the particles are and how much lighter the electron is.
[chem2-s1.9.0] Actual masses: proton 1.6726 × 10⁻²⁷ kg; neutron 1.6749 × 10⁻²⁷ kg; electron 9.109 × 10⁻³¹ kg.
[chem2-s1.9.1] The proton and neutron have almost the same mass, which is why each is given a relative mass of 1.
[chem2-s1.9.2] The charge on a proton is equal in size and opposite in sign to the charge on an electron, so equal numbers of each cancel out.
[chem2-s1.10.b] The particles are not spread evenly. The nucleus is tiny, yet it holds almost all the mass, and the rest of the atom is mostly empty.
[chem2-s1.10.0] Protons and neutrons, collectively called nucleons, make up the nucleus, which holds almost all of the atom's mass because electrons are so light.
[chem2-s1.10.1] The nucleus is tiny compared with the atom: an atom is about 10⁵ (100 000) times the diameter of its nucleus, so most of the atom is empty space.
[chem2-s1.10.2] A neutral atom has equal numbers of protons and electrons, so the positive and negative charges cancel.
[chem2-s1.10.3] The number of protons identifies the element: every atom with 6 protons, for example, is carbon.
[chem2-s1.11.b] Exams ask you to state the numbers of protons, neutrons and electrons in an atom or ion. An ion is an atom that has lost or gained electrons, so it carries a charge. The number of protons never changes in an ion.
[chem2-s1.11.0] Number of protons = atomic number (the number of protons in the nucleus).
[chem2-s1.11.1] Number of neutrons = mass number − atomic number, where the mass number is the total of protons and neutrons.
[chem2-s1.11.2] In a neutral atom, number of electrons = number of protons.
[chem2-s1.11.3] A positive ion has lost electrons, so number of electrons = number of protons − charge, e.g. Na⁺ has 11 protons and 10 electrons.
[chem2-s1.11.4] A negative ion has gained electrons, so number of electrons = number of protons + charge, e.g. Cl⁻ (chlorine-35) has 17 protons, 18 neutrons and 18 electrons; O²⁻ has 8 protons and 10 electrons.
[chem2-s1.12.b] The three particles were found one at a time. The order of discovery follows how easy each is to detect.
[chem2-s1.12.0] The electron was discovered by J. J. Thomson in 1897.
[chem2-s1.12.1] The proton was identified by Rutherford about 1917–1919.
[chem2-s1.12.2] The neutron was discovered by Chadwick in 1932.
[chem2-s1.12.3] The neutron was found last because it has no charge, which makes it hard to detect.
[chem2-s1.13.b] Two whole numbers describe the nucleus of an atom. Together they give the number of each particle and are written beside the element's symbol.
[chem2-s1.13.0] Atomic number (Z): the number of protons in the nucleus of an atom of an element.
[chem2-s1.13.1] Each element has its own atomic number, and the periodic table is arranged in order of atomic number.
[chem2-s1.13.2] Mass number (A): the total number of protons and neutrons in the nucleus of an atom.
[chem2-s1.13.3] Number of neutrons = mass number − atomic number (A − Z).
[chem2-s1.13.4] Nuclear notation writes the mass number at the top left and the atomic number at the bottom left of the element symbol, e.g. ²³₁₁Na has 11 protons, 12 neutrons and (as an atom) 11 electrons.
[chem2-s1.14.b] Atoms of one element are not all identical. They can have different numbers of neutrons, and such atoms are called isotopes.
[chem2-s1.14.0] Isotopes have the same atomic number, so the same number of protons, but different mass numbers because they have different numbers of neutrons.
[chem2-s1.14.1] Hydrogen-1 (¹H) has no neutrons; hydrogen-2 (²H), called deuterium, has one neutron; hydrogen-3 (³H), called tritium, has two.
[chem2-s1.14.2] Chlorine-35 (³⁵Cl) has 18 neutrons and chlorine-37 (³⁷Cl) has 20 neutrons.
[chem2-s1.14.3] Carbon-12, carbon-13 and carbon-14 are isotopes of carbon.
[chem2-s1.14.4] Isotopes of an element have the same chemical properties because they have the same number of electrons and the same electron arrangement, and electrons control how an atom reacts.
[chem2-s1.14.5] Isotopes have slightly different physical properties that depend on mass, such as density and rate of diffusion (how fast a gas spreads out).
[chem2-s1.14.6] Isotopes are written by name and mass number, e.g. chlorine-35, or in nuclear notation, e.g. ³⁵₁₇Cl.
[chem2-s1.15.b] Because an element's isotopes have different masses, one atom's mass number does not describe the element as a whole. Relative atomic mass is an average over its isotopes.
[chem2-s1.15.0] Relative atomic mass (Ar): the average mass of the atoms of an element, taking account of the abundance of its isotopes, relative to one-twelfth of the mass of an atom of carbon-12.
[chem2-s1.15.1] Carbon-12 is the standard: an atom of carbon-12 is defined as having a mass of exactly 12 units.
[chem2-s1.15.2] Ar has no units, because it compares masses.
[chem2-s1.15.3] The mass number of a single isotope is always a whole number; Ar is a weighted average of the isotopes present, so it is usually not whole.
[chem2-s1.15.4] Chlorine is about 75.8% chlorine-35 and 24.2% chlorine-37, giving Ar(Cl) = 35.45 (quoted as 35.5 in school tables). There is more on calculating Ar from isotopic abundance in the later subsection on the mole.
