TARGET chem23-s4: 4. Shapes, bonding and isomerism
[chem23-s4.0.b] There are several ways to write down a hydrocarbon (a compound of only carbon and hydrogen), and each shows a different amount of information. Knowing which one you are looking at tells you what you can and cannot read from it.
[chem23-s4.0.0] A molecular formula gives the number of atoms of each element in one molecule, e.g. C₄H₁₀, but it does not show how the atoms are joined, so it cannot tell two different molecules with the same atoms apart.
[chem23-s4.0.1] An expanded, or displayed, structural formula (a drawing of the structure) shows every atom and every bond as a line, e.g. butane drawn with four carbon atoms in a row, each hydrogen joined by its own line.
[chem23-s4.0.2] A condensed structural formula shows the groups on each carbon in order without drawing the bonds: butane is CH₃CH₂CH₂CH₃ or CH₃(CH₂)₂CH₃, methylpropane is CH₃CH(CH₃)CH₃, and but-1-ene is CH₂=CHCH₂CH₃.
[chem23-s4.0.3] Double and triple bonds are always shown in a condensed formula, so you can still see where they are even though single bonds are left out.
[chem23-s4.0.4] A 3D model shows the real spatial arrangement of the atoms (how they sit in space), which a flat expanded formula cannot: the expanded formula draws the bonds of methane at 90°, but in 3D they point to the corners of a tetrahedron (a triangular-based pyramid).
[chem23-s4.0.5] Two flat drawings can show the same molecule: a four-carbon chain drawn bent or straight is still butane, and building the model shows whether two structures are identical.
[chem23-s4.1.b] Building models is the way to see shapes that flat drawings hide, and it is a practical activity you should be able to describe. A molecular model kit is used, and digital models can do the same job on screen.
[chem23-s4.1.0] Use a molecular model kit: black balls with four holes for carbon, white balls with one hole for hydrogen, short rigid links for single bonds, and flexible links, or springs, for double and triple bonds.
[chem23-s4.1.1] Build methane, ethane, propane and butane; each carbon with four single bonds is tetrahedral, so a straight chain (an unbranched chain) is actually a zig-zag, not a straight line of atoms.
[chem23-s4.1.2] Rotate the two ends of ethane about the C–C bond: rotation is free about a single bond, and the different positions, called conformations, are still the same molecule.
[chem23-s4.1.3] Build ethene with two flexible links: the molecule is flat, and the C=C bond cannot be twisted.
[chem23-s4.1.4] Build ethyne with three flexible links: the four atoms lie in a straight line.
[chem23-s4.1.5] Build all the isomers of C₄H₁₀ and C₅H₁₂ (isomers are different molecules with the same molecular formula); try to build another isomer, and check by rotating it whether it is really new or the same as one already made.
[chem23-s4.1.6] Record each model as an expanded and a condensed formula, and give its IUPAC name (the official systematic name).
[chem23-s4.1.7] Digital models, made with molecule-viewing software or apps, can be rotated on screen and show ball-and-stick and space-filling views.
[chem23-s4.1.8] Ball-and-stick models exaggerate the gaps between atoms and are not to scale; space-filling models show size and shape but hide the bonds; no model shows the electrons, and real molecules are constantly vibrating and rotating.
[chem23-s4.2.b] The three model shapes you build are worth drawing, because they show how the number of bonds between carbons changes the geometry.
[chem23-s4.2.0] Diagram: ball-and-stick models of methane (tetrahedral), ethene (planar) and ethyne (linear), labelled with their bond angles.
[chem23-s4.3.b] The type of bond a carbon forms decides the angles around it and whether the groups attached to it can turn. This is why the molecules you built have the shapes they do.
[chem23-s4.3.0] A carbon with four single bonds is tetrahedral, with bond angles of 109.5°, and the groups around it can rotate about each single bond.
[chem23-s4.3.1] A carbon in a C=C double bond is trigonal planar (its three neighbours lie in one flat triangle around it), with bond angles of 120°; the double bond holds the two carbons and the four atoms attached to them in one plane and prevents rotation.
[chem23-s4.3.2] A carbon in a C≡C triple bond is linear, with bond angles of 180°.
[chem23-s4.3.3] In benzene (a ring-shaped hydrocarbon, C₆H₆) all twelve atoms lie in one flat hexagonal ring.
[chem23-s4.3.4] Spatial arrangement helps predict properties: branched isomers are more compact than straight chains, so they have less contact with neighbouring molecules and lower boiling points. There is more on this in Groups of hydrocarbons and their properties.
[chem23-s4.4.b] Structural isomers are compounds with the same molecular formula but different structural formulae, so the atoms are joined in a different order. Alkanes (hydrocarbons with only single bonds) show this once the chain is long enough to branch.
[chem23-s4.4.0] Isomers can differ in the carbon chain, through branching; in the position of a double bond; or in having a ring instead of a double bond, e.g. cyclohexane and hex-1-ene are both C₆H₁₂.
[chem23-s4.4.1] Methane, ethane and propane have no isomers; isomerism in alkanes starts at C₄, because a chain needs at least four carbons before it can be arranged in more than one way.
[chem23-s4.4.2] C₄H₁₀ has two structural isomers: butane, CH₃CH₂CH₂CH₃, and methylpropane, CH₃CH(CH₃)CH₃.
[chem23-s4.4.3] C₅H₁₂ has three: pentane, CH₃CH₂CH₂CH₂CH₃; 2-methylbutane, CH₃CH(CH₃)CH₂CH₃; and 2,2-dimethylpropane, C(CH₃)₄.
[chem23-s4.4.4] C₆H₁₄ has five. The first three are hexane, CH₃(CH₂)₄CH₃; 2-methylpentane, CH₃CH(CH₃)CH₂CH₂CH₃; and 3-methylpentane, CH₃CH₂CH(CH₃)CH₂CH₃.
[chem23-s4.4.5] The other two isomers of C₆H₁₄ are 2,2-dimethylbutane, CH₃C(CH₃)₂CH₂CH₃, and 2,3-dimethylbutane, CH₃CH(CH₃)CH(CH₃)CH₃.
[chem23-s4.5.b] Alkenes (hydrocarbons with a C=C double bond) have more isomers than alkanes of the same size, because the double bond can sit in different positions as well as the chain being branched.
[chem23-s4.5.0] Isomers of alkenes also first appear at C₄, and propene's only isomer is the ring compound cyclopropane.
[chem23-s4.5.1] The C₄H₈ alkene isomers are but-1-ene, CH₂=CHCH₂CH₃; but-2-ene, CH₃CH=CHCH₃; and methylpropene, CH₂=C(CH₃)CH₃.
[chem23-s4.5.2] The C₅H₁₀ alkene isomers are pent-1-ene, CH₂=CHCH₂CH₂CH₃; pent-2-ene, CH₃CH=CHCH₂CH₃; and 2-methylbut-1-ene, CH₂=C(CH₃)CH₂CH₃.
[chem23-s4.5.3] The other two C₅H₁₀ alkene isomers are 3-methylbut-1-ene, CH₂=CHCH(CH₃)CH₃, and 2-methylbut-2-ene, CH₃C(CH₃)=CHCH₃.
[chem23-s4.5.4] The straight-chain alkene isomers of C₆H₁₂ differ in the position of the double bond: hex-1-ene, hex-2-ene and hex-3-ene.
[chem23-s4.5.5] Check for repeats by numbering the chain from the other end or by rotating the model: 1-methylbutane is really pentane, and pent-4-ene is really pent-1-ene.
[chem23-s4.6.b] Ethane is the simplest molecule with a carbon–carbon single bond, and its shape is the pattern for every carbon with four single bonds.
[chem23-s4.6.0] Ethane, C₂H₆, is CH₃–CH₃: each carbon forms four single bonds, three to hydrogen atoms and one to the other carbon.
[chem23-s4.6.1] The four bonds around each carbon point to the corners of a tetrahedron, with bond angles of about 109.5°, so the molecule is three-dimensional and not flat.
[chem23-s4.6.2] The two CH₃ groups can rotate freely about the C–C single bond.
[chem23-s4.6.3] Diagram: ethane drawn in 3D with wedges and dashes, with the 109.5° angle marked.
[chem23-s4.7.b] Ethene has a carbon–carbon double bond, which changes both the shape and whether the molecule can twist.
[chem23-s4.7.0] Ethene, C₂H₄, is CH₂=CH₂: each carbon forms a double bond to the other carbon and single bonds to two hydrogen atoms.
[chem23-s4.7.1] Each carbon is trigonal planar, with bond angles of about 120°, so all six atoms lie in one plane and the molecule is flat.
[chem23-s4.7.2] There is no rotation about the C=C double bond.
[chem23-s4.7.3] Diagram: flat ethene molecule with the 120° angles marked.
[chem23-s4.8.b] Ethyne has a carbon–carbon triple bond, which gives the most extreme shape of the three: a straight line.
[chem23-s4.8.0] Ethyne, C₂H₂, is HC≡CH: each carbon forms a triple bond to the other carbon and a single bond to one hydrogen atom.
[chem23-s4.8.1] The molecule is linear, with a bond angle of 180°, so all four atoms lie in a straight line.
[chem23-s4.8.2] Diagram: H–C≡C–H in a straight line, with 180° marked.
[chem23-s4.9.b] Putting ethane, ethene and ethyne side by side shows a pattern: each extra bond between the carbons changes the shape around them.
[chem23-s4.9.0] As the carbon–carbon bond changes from single to double to triple, the shape around each carbon changes from tetrahedral, 109.5°, to trigonal planar, 120°, to linear, 180°.
[chem23-s4.9.1] This happens because the number of groups of electrons around each carbon falls from four to three to two, and the groups spread as far apart as possible.
[chem23-s4.9.2] The carbon–carbon bond becomes shorter and stronger from single to double to triple: C–C 0.154 nm, C=C 0.134 nm and C≡C 0.120 nm.
[chem23-s4.9.3] Ethane is three-dimensional, ethene is planar, and ethyne is linear.
[chem23-s4.10.b] At Higher Level you explain the shapes using two kinds of covalent bond (a bond made by sharing electrons), σ and π, which differ in how their orbitals overlap. An orbital is the region where an electron is likely to be found.
[chem23-s4.10.0] A σ (sigma) bond is formed by head-on overlap of orbitals along the line between the nuclei; a π (pi) bond is formed by sideways overlap of parallel p orbitals above and below that line.
[chem23-s4.10.1] A single bond is σ; a double bond is σ + π; a triple bond is σ + 2π.
[chem23-s4.10.2] Only the σ bonds and any lone pairs (pairs of electrons not used in bonding) decide the shape: the σ bonds around each carbon spread as far apart as possible (VSEPR theory, electron-pair repulsion), and the π bonds lie over the σ framework without changing the angles.
[chem23-s4.10.3] In ethane each carbon has four σ bonds around it, giving a tetrahedral shape, and rotation about the σ C–C bond does not change the overlap, so rotation is free.
[chem23-s4.10.4] In ethene each carbon has three σ bonds around it, giving a trigonal planar shape; the p orbital left on each carbon, at right angles to the plane, overlaps sideways with the other to form the π bond above and below the plane.
[chem23-s4.10.5] The π bond prevents rotation: twisting one CH₂ group would turn the p orbitals away from each other and break the π overlap, so the molecule is rigid and planar; this is why cis–trans isomers exist.
[chem23-s4.10.6] In ethyne each carbon has two σ bonds around it, giving a linear shape, and the two p orbitals left on each carbon are at right angles to each other. The two π bonds together form a cylinder of electron density around the C–C axis.
[chem23-s4.10.7] Diagram: σ framework of ethene with the π cloud drawn above and below the plane; ethyne with its two π bonds at right angles around the C–C axis.
[chem23-s4.11.b] Benzene is a flat ring of six carbons whose bonding cannot be described with ordinary single and double bonds. Its electrons are spread over the whole ring instead, which is called delocalised bonding.
[chem23-s4.11.0] Benzene, C₆H₆, has each carbon forming three σ bonds, to two neighbouring carbons and to one hydrogen, so each carbon is trigonal planar with 120° angles and the molecule is a flat, regular hexagon. There are twelve σ bonds in total, six C–C and six C–H.
[chem23-s4.11.1] Each carbon has one remaining p orbital, holding one electron, at right angles to the ring.
[chem23-s4.11.2] The six p orbitals overlap sideways all round the ring, not in three separate pairs, forming a delocalised π system (π electrons shared over the whole ring): one ring of electron density above the plane and one below it, containing six electrons shared by all six carbons.
[chem23-s4.12.b] Delocalisation has measurable effects: the bond lengths are all equal and benzene resists the reactions that alkenes undergo. These are the evidence for the delocalised model.
[chem23-s4.12.0] All six C–C bonds are identical, 0.139 nm long, between a single bond at 0.154 nm and a double bond at 0.134 nm; benzene is drawn as a hexagon with a circle inside to show the delocalised electrons.
[chem23-s4.12.1] Delocalisation makes benzene unusually stable: it does not decolourise bromine water, because addition (joining new atoms on across a double bond) would destroy the delocalised ring, so it reacts by substitution (swapping an atom for another), keeping the ring.
[chem23-s4.12.2] The evidence against the Kekulé structure, with alternating C–C and C=C bonds, is that all the bonds are the same length and benzene does not undergo the addition reactions of alkenes.
[chem23-s4.12.3] Kathleen Lonsdale, born in Newbridge, Co. Kildare, showed in 1929, using hexamethylbenzene, that the benzene ring is flat and a regular hexagon.
[chem23-s4.13.b] Exams ask you to share out the electrons of a benzene molecule between the different kinds of bonding. The total is the starting point, and each group is then counted in turn.
[chem23-s4.13.0] C₆H₆ has 42 electrons in total: 6 × 6 from the carbons and 6 × 1 from the hydrogens.
[chem23-s4.13.1] Of these, 12 electrons are in the six C–H bonds (two per bond), 12 are in the six C–C σ bonds, and 6 are delocalised π electrons.
[chem23-s4.13.2] So 18 electrons are involved in carbon–carbon bonding, and the remaining 12 are the inner (1s) electrons of the carbons, which are not involved in bonding.
[chem23-s4.13.3] Diagram: the planar ring of six C atoms with H atoms, the σ framework, the p orbitals at right angles to the ring, the delocalised π clouds above and below the ring, and the hexagon-with-circle symbol.
[chem23-s4.14.b] Draw a table with one row for each molecule, giving its σ bonds, π bonds, shape and rotation. It pulls the whole section together.
[chem23-s4.14.0] Ethane: 7 σ bonds, no π bonds, tetrahedral with 109.5° angles, and free rotation about the C–C bond.
[chem23-s4.14.1] Ethene: 5 σ bonds, 1 π bond, planar with 120° angles, and no rotation about the C=C bond.
[chem23-s4.14.2] Ethyne: 3 σ bonds, 2 π bonds, and linear with 180° angles.
[chem23-s4.14.3] Benzene: 12 σ bonds and a delocalised π system of 6 electrons, planar with 120° angles, and all C–C bonds equal in length.
[chem23-s4.15.b] There are two ways for molecules to be isomers, and you must be able to tell them apart. The difference is whether the atoms are joined in a different order or only arranged differently in space.
[chem23-s4.15.0] Isomers are compounds with the same molecular formula but different arrangements of atoms.
[chem23-s4.15.1] Structural isomers have the same molecular formula but different structural formulae, so the atoms are joined in a different order; butane and methylpropane (C₄H₁₀), and but-1-ene and but-2-ene (C₄H₈), are examples.
[chem23-s4.15.2] Geometric, or cis–trans, isomers have the same molecular formula and the same structural formula, so the atoms are joined in the same order, but the atoms are arranged differently in space.
[chem23-s4.15.3] If converting one isomer into the other would mean breaking bonds and joining atoms in a different order, they are structural isomers.
[chem23-s4.15.4] If the order of joining is the same and only the positions in space differ, they are geometric isomers.
[chem23-s4.16.b] Geometric isomerism depends on the rigidity of the C=C double bond. Butene, C₄H₈, is the example, in its but-2-ene form.
[chem23-s4.16.0] Geometric isomerism arises in alkenes because there is no rotation about the C=C double bond, since rotation would break the π bond, so groups are fixed on one side or the other of the double bond.
[chem23-s4.16.1] Each carbon of the double bond must carry two different atoms or groups for geometric isomerism to occur.
[chem23-s4.16.2] In cis-but-2-ene the two CH₃ groups are on the same side of the double bond, and the two H atoms are on the same side.
[chem23-s4.16.3] In trans-but-2-ene the two CH₃ groups are on opposite sides of the double bond.
[chem23-s4.17.b] Not every alkene has cis and trans forms. Apply the condition that each double-bond carbon must carry two different groups to decide.
[chem23-s4.17.0] But-1-ene, CH₂=CHCH₂CH₃, has no cis–trans isomers, because one carbon of the double bond carries two identical H atoms.
[chem23-s4.17.1] Methylpropene, CH₂=C(CH₃)₂, has none, because both carbons of the double bond carry identical pairs of groups.
[chem23-s4.17.2] Butene, C₄H₈, therefore has four alkene isomers: but-1-ene, cis-but-2-ene, trans-but-2-ene and methylpropene (three structural isomers, one of which has cis and trans forms).
[chem23-s4.17.3] Alkanes have no geometric isomers, because groups rotate freely about C–C single bonds.
[chem23-s4.17.4] Other alkenes up to C6 with two different groups on each double-bond carbon, e.g. pent-2-ene, hex-2-ene and hex-3-ene, have cis and trans forms.
[chem23-s4.17.5] In a 3D model of but-2-ene the double bond, made of two flexible links, cannot be twisted, so the cis model cannot be turned into the trans model without taking it apart; digital models show the same when rotated.
[chem23-s4.17.6] Diagram: cis-but-2-ene and trans-but-2-ene drawn flat with 120° angles, the CH₃ groups circled on the same and on opposite sides.
[chem23-s4.18.b] Whether isomers react alike depends on whether they have the same types of bond. The same family means the same bonds and so similar chemistry.
[chem23-s4.18.0] Structural isomers of the same family, such as butane and methylpropane, have very similar chemical properties, because they have the same types of bond.
[chem23-s4.18.1] Structural isomers in different families have different chemical properties: hex-1-ene and cyclohexane are both C₆H₁₂, but hex-1-ene, with its C=C bond, decolourises bromine water, and cyclohexane, which is saturated (it has only single bonds), does not.
[chem23-s4.18.2] cis- and trans-but-2-ene have almost the same chemical properties, with the same C=C bond, the same addition reactions and the same combustion: C₄H₈ + 6O₂ → 4CO₂ + 4H₂O.
[chem23-s4.19.b] Isomers have the same atoms but different shapes, and shape affects the physical property of boiling point.
[chem23-s4.19.0] Branched structural isomers have lower boiling points than unbranched ones, e.g. butane, −1 °C, and methylpropane, −12 °C.
[chem23-s4.19.1] The more compact shape of the branched isomer gives less contact between molecules and weaker London dispersion forces (the weak attractions between all molecules), so less energy is needed to separate them. There is more on this in Polarity and symmetry.
[chem23-s4.20.b] Cis and trans isomers react alike but differ in physical properties such as boiling point and melting point. The cause is the difference in polarity and in how the molecules pack.
[chem23-s4.20.0] cis-but-2-ene boils at 4 °C and trans-but-2-ene at 1 °C.
[chem23-s4.20.1] The cis molecule is slightly polar (it has a side with slightly more negative charge and a side with slightly more positive), because both CH₃ groups lie on one side, so it has dipole–dipole attractions as well as London forces.
[chem23-s4.20.2] The symmetrical trans molecule is non-polar, so it has only London forces; this is why cis boils higher than trans.
[chem23-s4.20.3] The trans isomer has the higher melting point, −106 °C compared with −139 °C for cis, because its more symmetrical, straighter shape packs more closely in the solid.
[chem23-s4.21.b] Cis–trans isomerism has a real consequence for diet, because the shape of a fat molecule affects health.
[chem23-s4.21.0] Cis–trans isomerism matters in food: unsaturated fats (fats with C=C bonds in their chains) in natural oils are mainly cis.
[chem23-s4.21.1] Trans fats are formed when vegetable oils are partly hydrogenated (reacted with hydrogen), and they raise the risk of heart disease.

CANDIDATES chem5-s1: 1. Bonding, formulae and shapes
[chem5-s1.0.b] Most atoms are not stable on their own. They join to other atoms because the joined arrangement has less energy, and the force that holds them together is called a chemical bond.
[chem5-s1.0.0] A chemical bond is a force of attraction that holds atoms or ions (atoms that have gained or lost electrons and so carry a charge) together in an element or compound.
[chem5-s1.0.1] Atoms bond to reach a more stable arrangement of electrons, usually a full outer energy level (the outermost shell of electrons) like the nearest noble gas (the unreactive group 18 elements); this is the octet rule, and bonding lowers their energy.
[chem5-s1.0.2] Only the outer (valence) electrons take part in bonding, because the inner electrons are held close to the nucleus (the atom's positively charged centre) and are not available to be lost, gained or shared.
[chem5-s1.1.b] In ionic bonding one atom hands electrons over to another, leaving two oppositely charged particles that attract each other.
[chem5-s1.1.0] An ionic bond is the electrostatic attraction (the attraction between opposite charges) between oppositely charged ions.
[chem5-s1.1.1] It forms when electrons are transferred from one atom, usually a metal that forms a positive ion, to another atom, usually a non-metal that forms a negative ion.
[chem5-s1.1.2] Example: sodium (2,8,1) gives one electron to chlorine (2,8,7): Na → Na⁺ + e⁻ and Cl + e⁻ → Cl⁻. The numbers show the electrons in each energy level.
[chem5-s1.1.3] Both ions have the electron arrangement of a noble gas, which is why the transfer happens: each atom ends up with a full outer energy level.
[chem5-s1.1.4] Ionic compounds form a giant lattice (a huge regular repeating array) of ions, not molecules (separate small groups of atoms).
[chem5-s1.1.5] In sodium chloride each Na⁺ ion is surrounded by six Cl⁻ ions, and each Cl⁻ ion by six Na⁺ ions.
[chem5-s1.1.6] The formula NaCl gives only the ratio of the ions, one Na⁺ to one Cl⁻, because there is no single unit to count.
[chem5-s1.1.7] Diagram: a section of the sodium chloride lattice, drawn as a cube, showing alternating Na⁺ and Cl⁻ ions.
[chem5-s1.2.b] Non-metal atoms usually cannot give electrons away easily, so they share them instead. The shared electrons hold the atoms together.
[chem5-s1.2.0] A covalent bond is a shared pair of electrons between two atoms, usually two non-metals; both nuclei are attracted to the shared pair, which is what holds the atoms together.
[chem5-s1.2.1] A single bond is one shared pair, such as in H–H and Cl–Cl.
[chem5-s1.2.2] A double bond is two shared pairs, as in O=O and C=O in CO₂.
[chem5-s1.2.3] A triple bond is three shared pairs, as in N≡N.
[chem5-s1.2.4] Covalently bonded atoms form molecules (separate small groups of atoms), e.g. H₂O, CH₄ and NH₃.
[chem5-s1.2.5] A few substances have giant covalent structures, in which atoms are linked by covalent bonds throughout instead of forming separate molecules, e.g. diamond.
[chem5-s1.3.b] Not every shared pair is shared fairly. Whether the sharing is equal or unequal depends on how strongly each atom attracts the electrons.
[chem5-s1.3.0] A pure (non-polar) covalent bond shares electrons equally, because both atoms attract them equally, as in H–H and Cl–Cl.
[chem5-s1.3.1] The C–H bond is nearly non-polar.
[chem5-s1.3.2] A polar covalent bond shares electrons unequally, because one atom attracts them more strongly, as in H–Cl and O–H.
[chem5-s1.3.3] The atom that attracts the shared electrons more strongly gets a partial negative charge (δ−), and the other atom gets a partial positive charge (δ+), e.g. Hδ+–Clδ−. The charges are only partial because the electrons are shifted, not fully transferred.
[chem5-s1.4.b] Ionic and covalent bonding are the two ends of a scale, not two separate boxes. Real bonds sit at different points along it, depending on how the electrons are shared.
[chem5-s1.4.0] Bonds are not simply ionic or covalent; they lie on a continuum (a smooth scale) from pure covalent, through polar covalent, to ionic.
[chem5-s1.4.1] At one end, pure covalent bonds share electrons equally, because both atoms attract them equally.
[chem5-s1.4.2] In the middle, polar covalent bonds share electrons unequally, because one atom pulls harder.
[chem5-s1.4.3] At the other end, ionic bonds involve complete transfer of electrons, so ions form.
[chem5-s1.4.4] Examples along the continuum: Cl–Cl (pure covalent), H–Cl (polar covalent), Na⁺Cl⁻ (ionic).
[chem5-s1.4.5] Most bonds have some ionic and some covalent character, so they sit between the ends, not exactly at them.
[chem5-s1.4.6] The difference in electronegativity (how strongly an atom attracts the shared electrons in a bond) between the atoms decides where a bond lies. There is more on this in Bond type and intermolecular forces.
[chem5-s1.4.7] Diagram: a horizontal line labelled pure covalent – polar covalent – ionic, with the electron pair drawn shared equally at the left end, shifted towards one atom in the middle, and fully transferred at the right end.
[chem5-s1.5.b] Setting ionic and covalent bonding side by side shows what each does to the electrons and what kind of structure results. Metals have a third type of bonding.
[chem5-s1.5.0] Ionic bonding transfers electrons and forms ions; covalent bonding shares electrons and forms molecules (mostly).
[chem5-s1.5.1] Ionic bonding occurs between a metal and a non-metal; covalent bonding occurs mostly between two non-metals.
[chem5-s1.5.2] Ionic compounds are giant lattices; covalent bonds are polar or non-polar depending on the atoms involved.
[chem5-s1.5.3] Metallic bonding (assumed): positive metal ions are held together by a sea of delocalised outer electrons (electrons free to move through the whole metal), which explains why metals conduct electricity.
[chem5-s1.6.b] To predict a formula you need to know how many electrons an atom is likely to lose, gain or share. This number is its valency, and the octet rule explains it.
[chem5-s1.6.0] Valency is the number of electrons an atom loses, gains or shares when it bonds; it is the combining power of an element.
[chem5-s1.6.1] The octet rule: atoms tend to lose, gain or share electrons to reach eight electrons in their outer energy level.
[chem5-s1.6.2] Hydrogen follows a different rule: it needs two electrons in its outer level, matching helium (the noble gas with two electrons).
[chem5-s1.6.3] Elements in group 1 and group 17 have a valency of 1, because they lose one electron (group 1) or gain one (group 17) to reach an octet.
[chem5-s1.6.4] Elements in group 2 and group 16 have a valency of 2, because they lose two electrons (group 2) or gain two (group 16).
[chem5-s1.6.5] Elements in group 13 and group 15 have a valency of 3, because they lose three electrons (group 13) or gain three (group 15).
[chem5-s1.6.6] Elements in group 14 have a valency of 4, because they share four electrons to reach an octet.
[chem5-s1.7.b] An ionic compound has no overall charge, so the positive and negative charges must cancel. This gives a simple method for writing any ionic formula.
[chem5-s1.7.0] Step 1: Write the charge on each ion, e.g. Ca²⁺ and Cl⁻.
[chem5-s1.7.1] Step 2: Combine the ions so that the total positive charge equals the total negative charge.
[chem5-s1.7.2] Calcium ions (Ca²⁺) and chloride ions (Cl⁻) give CaCl₂, as one Ca²⁺ balances two Cl⁻.
[chem5-s1.7.3] Aluminium ions (Al³⁺) and oxide ions (O²⁻) give Al₂O₃, as two Al³⁺ balance three O²⁻.
[chem5-s1.7.4] Magnesium ions (Mg²⁺) and nitride ions (N³⁻) give Mg₃N₂, as three Mg²⁺ balance two N³⁻.
[chem5-s1.8.b] Some ions are made of several atoms bonded together that carry one overall charge. They behave as a single unit when you write formulae, so you need to know them.
[chem5-s1.8.0] Negative compound ions: hydroxide OH⁻, nitrate NO₃⁻, hydrogencarbonate HCO₃⁻, carbonate CO₃²⁻, sulfate SO₄²⁻, sulfite SO₃²⁻ and phosphate PO₄³⁻.
[chem5-s1.8.1] Positive compound ion: ammonium NH₄⁺. It is given only as an ion to use in formulae.
[chem5-s1.8.2] Brackets are used round a compound ion when more than one is needed, so that the subscript applies to the whole ion, e.g. Ca(OH)₂ and (NH₄)₂SO₄.
[chem5-s1.9.b] Covalent formulae follow from valency too. Atoms share electron pairs until each has a complete outer level.
[chem5-s1.9.0] Covalent formulae: atoms share enough electron pairs to complete each octet.
[chem5-s1.9.1] Hydrogen (valency 1) and oxygen (valency 2) form H₂O, because two hydrogens are needed to supply the two bonds oxygen can make.
[chem5-s1.9.2] Nitrogen (valency 3) and hydrogen form NH₃, with three hydrogens for nitrogen's three bonds.
[chem5-s1.9.3] Carbon (valency 4) and hydrogen form CH₄, with four hydrogens for carbon's four bonds.
[chem5-s1.9.4] Carbon and oxygen form CO₂, with two double bonds.
[chem5-s1.10.b] A Lewis diagram is a way of drawing bonding that tracks every outer electron. It shows which electrons are transferred, shared or left unused.
[chem5-s1.10.0] A Lewis diagram shows the outer electrons of each atom as dots or crosses; the different symbols let you see which atom each electron came from.
[chem5-s1.10.1] Shared pairs of electrons are drawn between the atoms they join, so you can count the bonds.
[chem5-s1.10.2] Lone pairs are pairs of outer electrons that are not used in bonding.
[chem5-s1.10.3] Ionic compounds: show each ion in square brackets with its charge, e.g. [Na]⁺ [Cl]⁻, with eight electrons around the chloride ion.
[chem5-s1.10.4] Magnesium oxide, an ionic example, is drawn as [Mg]²⁺ [O]²⁻.
[chem5-s1.10.5] Covalent examples to draw: H₂, Cl₂, HCl and H₂O, where water has two lone pairs on oxygen.
[chem5-s1.10.6] Ammonia, NH₃, has one lone pair on nitrogen, because nitrogen has five outer electrons and uses only three in bonds.
[chem5-s1.10.7] O₂ has a double bond, N₂ has a triple bond, and CO₂ has two double bonds.
[chem5-s1.10.8] Diagram: dot-and-cross diagrams of sodium chloride, water and nitrogen, with lone pairs labelled.
[chem5-s1.11.b] Orbitals are the regions of space around a nucleus where an electron is most likely to be found. Covalent bonding can be described as these regions merging.
[chem5-s1.11.0] A covalent bond forms when an orbital of one atom, holding one electron, overlaps an orbital of another atom holding one electron.
[chem5-s1.11.1] The shared pair occupies the overlap region between the two nuclei, which is where both nuclei can attract it.
[chem5-s1.12.b] The way two orbitals overlap decides the type of bond. Overlap can be head-on or sideways, which gives sigma and pi bonds.
[chem5-s1.12.0] A sigma (σ) bond is formed by head-on overlap of orbitals along the line joining the two nuclei.
[chem5-s1.12.1] Sigma bonds are formed by s–s overlap in H₂, s–p overlap in HCl, and end-on p–p overlap in Cl₂.
[chem5-s1.12.2] A pi (π) bond is formed by sideways overlap of two parallel p orbitals, above and below the line joining the nuclei.
[chem5-s1.12.3] Every single bond is a σ bond, so H–H is one σ bond.
[chem5-s1.12.4] A double bond is one σ bond and one π bond; a triple bond is one σ bond and two π bonds, as in N₂.
[chem5-s1.12.5] π bonds are weaker than σ bonds because sideways overlap is less effective.
[chem5-s1.12.6] π bonds prevent rotation about a double bond.
[chem5-s1.12.7] Diagram: head-on overlap of two s orbitals and of two p orbitals (σ); sideways overlap of two p orbitals, showing electron density above and below the axis (π).
[chem5-s1.13.b] Sometimes a bonding pair cannot be pinned between just two atoms. Benzene is the key example, where electrons spread round a whole ring.
[chem5-s1.13.0] In delocalised bonding, electrons are spread over several atoms rather than confined between two.
[chem5-s1.13.1] In benzene (C₆H₆), each carbon uses one p orbital, and the six p orbitals overlap sideways all round the ring.
[chem5-s1.13.2] This forms a ring of delocalised π electrons above and below the plane of the ring.
[chem5-s1.13.3] All six carbon–carbon bonds in benzene are identical, with lengths between those of single and double bonds, because the electrons are shared evenly round the ring. There is more on this in Allotropes of Carbon. Hydrocarbons.
[chem5-s1.14.b] Higher Level students apply the same valency rules past calcium, to the main-group elements up to krypton (element 36).
[chem5-s1.14.0] The same rules apply to elements 21–36 in the main groups.
[chem5-s1.14.1] Potassium bromide is K⁺Br⁻, because group 1 and group 17 both have a valency of 1.
[chem5-s1.14.2] Calcium bromide is CaBr₂, because Ca²⁺ needs two Br⁻ to balance.
[chem5-s1.14.3] Gallium (group 13) forms Ga³⁺ ions, since group 13 has a valency of 3.
[chem5-s1.14.4] Arsenic (group 15) and hydrogen form AsH₃, since group 15 has a valency of 3.
[chem5-s1.14.5] Selenium (group 16) and hydrogen form H₂Se, since group 16 has a valency of 2.
[chem5-s1.15.b] The octet rule and Lewis diagrams are useful but not perfect. Some real molecules break the rule, and the models leave out several properties.
[chem5-s1.15.0] Some molecules are stable with fewer than eight outer electrons on the central atom, e.g. BF₃, with six around boron.
[chem5-s1.15.1] Elements in period 3 and beyond (the third row of the periodic table and below) can have more than eight outer electrons, e.g. PCl₅, with ten around phosphorus.
[chem5-s1.15.2] SF₆ has twelve electrons around sulfur.
[chem5-s1.15.3] The octet rule and Lewis diagrams do not predict the shape of a molecule, how polar a bond is, or bond strength.
[chem5-s1.15.4] They cannot show delocalised bonding, as in benzene.
[chem5-s1.15.5] Predictions from valency assume purely ionic or purely covalent bonding, but real bonds lie on a continuum.
[chem5-s1.16.b] The shape of a molecule follows from a simple idea: electron pairs carry negative charge, so they push each other away. VSEPR turns that idea into a rule for predicting shape.
[chem5-s1.16.0] VSEPR stands for valence shell electron pair repulsion.
[chem5-s1.16.1] The theory applies to molecules of the form ABn (a central atom A bonded to n atoms B), with up to four pairs of electrons around a central atom and single bonds only.
[chem5-s1.16.2] Electron pairs in the outer (valence) shell of the central atom repel each other and arrange themselves as far apart as possible, which fixes the shape of the molecule.
[chem5-s1.16.3] Both bonding pairs and lone pairs count, because both are regions of negative charge that repel.
[chem5-s1.16.4] Lone pairs repel more strongly than bonding pairs: lone pair–lone pair repulsion is greater than lone pair–bond pair repulsion, which is greater than bond pair–bond pair repulsion.
[chem5-s1.16.5] Lone pairs therefore squeeze the bond angles smaller.
[chem5-s1.16.6] The shape is described by the positions of the atoms only, not the lone pairs, so NH₃ (three bonded atoms plus one lone pair) is called pyramidal.
[chem5-s1.17.b] Predicting a shape is a short routine that starts from the Lewis diagram. Follow the same three steps for every molecule.
[chem5-s1.17.0] Step 1: Draw the Lewis (dot-and-cross) diagram of the molecule.
[chem5-s1.17.1] Step 2: Count the electron pairs around the central atom, and how many of them are bonding pairs and how many are lone pairs.
[chem5-s1.17.2] Step 3: Arrange the pairs as far apart as possible, and name the shape from the positions of the atoms, adjusting the angle for lone pairs.
[chem5-s1.18.b] With up to four pairs around the central atom, these five shapes are the ones you need to know. Each depends on how many pairs are bonding and how many are lone.
[chem5-s1.18.0] Two bonding pairs and no lone pairs: linear (a straight line), 180°, e.g. BeCl₂ (gas).
[chem5-s1.18.1] Three bonding pairs and no lone pairs: trigonal planar (three atoms in one flat plane round the centre), 120°, e.g. BF₃.
[chem5-s1.18.2] Four bonding pairs and no lone pairs: tetrahedral (four atoms pointing to the corners of a pyramid-like solid), 109.5°, e.g. CH₄, CCl₄ and SiH₄.
[chem5-s1.18.3] Three bonding pairs and one lone pair: pyramidal (trigonal pyramidal), about 107°, e.g. NH₃. PH₃ is also pyramidal, but its measured angle is much smaller, about 93.5°.
[chem5-s1.18.4] Two bonding pairs and two lone pairs: V-shaped (bent), about 104.5°, e.g. H₂O.
[chem5-s1.18.5] H₂S is also V-shaped, but its measured bond angle is much smaller than water's, about 92°.
[chem5-s1.19.b] Ammonia and water have the same number of electron pairs as methane, yet their bond angles are smaller. Lone pairs are the reason.
[chem5-s1.19.0] NH₃ and H₂O have four electron pairs arranged roughly tetrahedrally, as in CH₄.
[chem5-s1.19.1] The lone pairs push the bonds closer together, because they repel more strongly than bonding pairs, so the bond angle falls as the number of lone pairs rises from 0 to 2: from CH₄ to NH₃ to H₂O.
[chem5-s1.20.b] Molecules are three-dimensional, so a flat drawing needs a convention to show depth. Models let you see the shape directly.
[chem5-s1.20.0] Diagram: 3D shapes drawn with wedges for bonds towards the viewer and dashed lines for bonds away from the viewer, with any lone pairs shown on the central atom.
[chem5-s1.20.1] 3D models: ball-and-stick kits show bond angles, so you can see the shape directly.
[chem5-s1.20.2] Digital models, from molecular-modelling software or apps, can be rotated on screen.
[chem5-s1.20.3] Diagram: the five shapes, each with its example, bond angle and any lone pairs labelled.

CANDIDATES chem23-s3: 3. Groups of hydrocarbons and their properties
[chem23-s3.0.b] A hydrocarbon is a compound containing only carbon and hydrogen. Chemists sort hydrocarbons into groups by the kind of carbon–carbon bonds they contain, because the bonding decides how each group behaves.
[chem23-s3.0.0] Hydrocarbons are divided by the type of carbon–carbon bonds present into aliphatic and aromatic hydrocarbons.
[chem23-s3.0.1] Aliphatic hydrocarbons have open chains of carbon atoms, straight or branched, or rings that behave like chains, such as cyclohexane. They are the alkanes, alkenes and alkynes.
[chem23-s3.0.2] Aromatic hydrocarbons contain a benzene ring (a ring of six carbon atoms with special bonding). Benzene, C₆H₆, is the example given in the specification.
[chem23-s3.0.3] A saturated hydrocarbon (one holding as many hydrogens as it can) has only carbon–carbon single bonds, as in alkanes and cycloalkanes (alkanes whose carbons form a ring).
[chem23-s3.0.4] An unsaturated hydrocarbon contains at least one carbon–carbon double or triple bond, as in alkenes and alkynes, so it could take on more atoms by opening that bond.
[chem23-s3.0.5] A homologous series is a family with the same general formula (one formula that fits every member) and similar chemical properties, a gradual change in physical properties, and each member differing from the next by CH₂.
[chem23-s3.1.b] The alkanes are saturated aliphatic hydrocarbons with single bonds only. Having no multiple bonds explains why they are so unreactive.
[chem23-s3.1.0] General formula CₙH₂ₙ₊₂, where n is the number of carbons. The first members are methane CH₄, ethane C₂H₆, propane C₃H₈, butane C₄H₁₀, pentane C₅H₁₂ and hexane C₆H₁₄.
[chem23-s3.1.1] Bonding: only single C–C and C–H covalent bonds are present, so the molecule is saturated.
[chem23-s3.1.2] Structure: each carbon is tetrahedral (its four bonds point to the corners of a tetrahedron), with bond angles of 109.5°. Chains can be branched from butane onwards.
[chem23-s3.1.3] Cyclohexane, C₆H₁₂, is a ring of six CH₂ groups joined by single bonds; it is saturated and aliphatic.
[chem23-s3.1.4] Cyclohexane has two fewer hydrogens than hexane because the ends of the chain are joined to make the ring. Its ring is not flat because each carbon is tetrahedral.
[chem23-s3.1.5] Alkanes are non-polar (their bonds have no significant positive or negative ends) and insoluble in water, and small members burn with a clean blue flame.
[chem23-s3.1.6] Alkanes are otherwise unreactive: they react with halogens (such as chlorine) only in ultraviolet light, by substitution (one atom replaced by another), e.g. CH₄ + Cl₂ → CH₃Cl + HCl.
[chem23-s3.2.b] The alkenes are unsaturated aliphatic hydrocarbons with one carbon–carbon double bond. That double bond makes them far more reactive than alkanes.
[chem23-s3.2.0] General formula CₙH₂ₙ; the members are ethene C₂H₄, propene C₃H₆, butene C₄H₈, pentene C₅H₁₀ and hexene C₆H₁₂.
[chem23-s3.2.1] Bonding: one C=C double bond is present, so the molecule is unsaturated.
[chem23-s3.2.2] Structure: the two carbons of the double bond and the four atoms attached to them lie in one plane, with angles of about 120°, and there is no rotation about the C=C bond.
[chem23-s3.2.3] Alkenes are, like alkanes, non-polar and insoluble in water, but they are much more reactive.
[chem23-s3.2.4] Alkenes undergo addition (other atoms join on across the double bond, which becomes a single bond) with bromine, which decolourises bromine water (turns it from orange to colourless); with hydrogen over a nickel catalyst, e.g. CH₂=CH₂ + H₂ → CH₃CH₃; and with hydrogen chloride, e.g. CH₂=CH₂ + HCl → CH₃CH₂Cl.
[chem23-s3.2.5] Alkenes add to one another to form addition polymers (long chains built from many alkene molecules), e.g. ethene forms poly(ethene). There is more on this in Types of organic reaction and addition polymers.
[chem23-s3.2.6] Alkenes burn with a more luminous (brighter), smokier flame than alkanes because of their higher carbon content.
[chem23-s3.3.b] The alkynes are unsaturated aliphatic hydrocarbons with one carbon–carbon triple bond. Like alkenes, they take part in addition reactions.
[chem23-s3.3.0] General formula CₙH₂ₙ₋₂; the first member is ethyne, C₂H₂, also called acetylene.
[chem23-s3.3.1] Bonding: one C≡C triple bond is present, so the molecule is unsaturated.
[chem23-s3.3.2] Structure: the two carbons of the triple bond and the atoms attached to them lie in a straight line, with an angle of 180°, so ethyne is linear.
[chem23-s3.3.3] Alkynes undergo addition reactions; with excess bromine, ethyne gives 1,1,2,2-tetrabromoethane: C₂H₂ + 2Br₂ → CHBr₂CHBr₂.
[chem23-s3.3.4] Ethyne burns in air with a very smoky, luminous flame.
[chem23-s3.3.5] Ethyne burns in pure oxygen with a very hot flame, which is used for welding: 2C₂H₂(g) + 5O₂(g) → 4CO₂(g) + 2H₂O(l).
[chem23-s3.4.b] Benzene is the aromatic hydrocarbon on the course. It is unsaturated on paper, yet it behaves very differently from an alkene, and its structure explains why.
[chem23-s3.4.0] Benzene, C₆H₆, is a colourless liquid with a boiling point of 80 °C and a sweet smell.
[chem23-s3.4.1] Its structure is a flat, hexagonal ring of six carbon atoms, each bonded to one hydrogen atom, with bond angles of 120°.
[chem23-s3.4.2] All six carbon–carbon bonds are identical, intermediate in length between single and double bonds, because the electrons beyond the single bonds are delocalised (spread) around the whole ring.
[chem23-s3.4.3] This is why benzene is drawn as a hexagon with a circle inside. There is more on this in Shapes, bonding and isomerism.
[chem23-s3.4.4] Although benzene is unsaturated, it is very stable and resists addition: it does not decolourise bromine water or acidified potassium manganate(VII), and it reacts mainly by substitution.
[chem23-s3.4.5] Benzene burns with a very smoky, sooty flame because of its high carbon content, 92.3% by mass, the same as ethyne.
[chem23-s3.4.6] Benzene is toxic and carcinogenic (cancer-causing), causing leukaemia, so it is not used in school laboratories, where safer solvents such as cyclohexane are used instead.
[chem23-s3.4.7] Michael Faraday isolated benzene in 1825, and August Kekulé proposed a ring of alternating single and double bonds in 1865.
[chem23-s3.4.8] The Kekulé structure cannot explain why all the carbon–carbon bonds are the same length, or why benzene does not decolourise bromine water.
[chem23-s3.5.b] Putting the four groups side by side shows a pattern: the type of carbon–carbon bonding decides the shape, the reactivity and the result of the bromine water test.
[chem23-s3.5.0] Alkanes have the general formula CₙH₂ₙ₊₂, single C–C bonds, tetrahedral shape with 109.5° angles around each carbon, and methane as the first member; bromine water shows no change.
[chem23-s3.5.1] Alkenes have the general formula CₙH₂ₙ, one double bond, planar (flat) shape with 120° angles, and ethene as the first member; bromine water is decolourised.
[chem23-s3.5.2] Alkynes have the general formula CₙH₂ₙ₋₂, one triple bond, a linear shape with 180° angles, and ethyne as the first member; bromine water is decolourised.
[chem23-s3.5.3] Benzene has the general formula C₆H₆, a delocalised ring, a planar shape with 120° angles, and benzene as the first member; bromine water shows no change.
[chem23-s3.5.4] All four groups are non-polar, with only London dispersion forces (weak attractions between temporary charges in neighbouring molecules) between their molecules, so all are insoluble in water and soluble in non-polar solvents; small members are gases.
[chem23-s3.5.5] Reactivity follows bonding: single bonds only give unreactive alkanes; a localised double or triple bond gives reactive alkenes and alkynes that undergo addition; a delocalised ring gives benzene, which is stable and undergoes substitution rather than addition.
[chem23-s3.6.b] Names follow the systematic IUPAC rules (the internationally agreed system), and only compounds up to C6 need to be named. Start with the stem and then work through the rules for alkanes.
[chem23-s3.6.0] The stem gives the number of carbons in the parent chain (the main chain): 1 meth-, 2 eth-, 3 prop-, 4 but-, 5 pent-, 6 hex-.
[chem23-s3.6.1] The ending is -ane for alkanes, -ene for alkenes and -yne for alkynes.
[chem23-s3.6.2] Find the longest continuous carbon chain, which may bend on paper; this gives the parent name.
[chem23-s3.6.3] Number the chain from the end that gives the side chains (substituents, groups branching off the main chain) the lowest numbers.
[chem23-s3.6.4] Name side chains as alkyl groups: methyl (–CH₃) and ethyl (–C₂H₅); give each a position number, using di- and tri- for repeats.
[chem23-s3.6.5] Separate numbers from one another with commas, and separate numbers from words with hyphens; list different side chains alphabetically.
[chem23-s3.7.b] Applying the rules to branched alkanes shows how the numbering works.
[chem23-s3.7.0] CH₃CH(CH₃)CH₃ is methylpropane. It needs no number because the methyl group can only be on carbon 2, though 2-methylpropane is also accepted.
[chem23-s3.7.1] CH₃CH(CH₃)CH₂CH₃ is 2-methylbutane, not 3-methylbutane, because numbering from the end that gives the lowest number to the side chain puts it on carbon 2.
[chem23-s3.7.2] C(CH₃)₄ is 2,2-dimethylpropane, with di- showing two methyl groups, both on carbon 2.
[chem23-s3.7.3] CH₃CH₂CH(CH₃)CH₂CH₃ is 3-methylpentane.
[chem23-s3.7.4] (CH₃)₂CHCH(CH₃)₂ is 2,3-dimethylbutane.
[chem23-s3.8.b] Alkenes and alkynes follow the same rules, with one addition: the multiple bond decides the numbering and must sit in the main chain.
[chem23-s3.8.0] For alkenes and alkynes the main chain must contain the multiple bond; number from the end nearer to it, and give the number of its first carbon before -ene or -yne.
[chem23-s3.8.1] CH₂=CHCH₂CH₃ is but-1-ene, CH₃CH=CHCH₃ is but-2-ene, CH₂=C(CH₃)CH₃ is 2-methylpropene, and CH₂=CHCH₂CH₂CH₂CH₃ is hex-1-ene.
[chem23-s3.8.2] HC≡CCH₃ is propyne, and CH₃C≡CCH₃ is but-2-yne.
[chem23-s3.8.3] Ethene, propene, ethyne and propyne need no number, because there is only one position for the multiple bond.
[chem23-s3.8.4] Rings take the prefix cyclo-, as in cyclohexane; benzene keeps its own name.
[chem23-s3.9.b] Three things explain almost every difference in the properties of hydrocarbons: the forces between molecules, the size of the molecules, and the kind of carbon–carbon bond.
[chem23-s3.9.0] Hydrocarbons are non-polar, because C–H bonds have very low polarity (the electrons are shared almost equally), so the only forces between their molecules are London dispersion forces. There is more on this in Bond type and intermolecular forces.
[chem23-s3.9.1] London dispersion forces get stronger as relative molecular mass (Mr, the mass of a molecule compared with the carbon-12 standard) increases, because there are more electrons and larger electron clouds, and as the area of contact between molecules increases.
[chem23-s3.9.2] The stronger the intermolecular forces, the more energy is needed to separate the molecules, so the higher the boiling point and the more likely the substance is a liquid or solid at room temperature.
[chem23-s3.9.3] The nature of the carbon–carbon bonds decides chemical reactivity: single bonds only give unreactive compounds, while a C=C double bond gives reactivity through addition reactions.
[chem23-s3.9.4] Boiling overcomes the forces between molecules; it does not break C–C or C–H bonds.
[chem23-s3.10.b] The data show how boiling point depends on carbon number in the straight-chain alkanes (those with no branches).
[chem23-s3.10.0] Boiling points: methane −162 °C, ethane −89 °C, propane −42 °C, butane −1 °C, pentane 36 °C and hexane 69 °C.
[chem23-s3.10.1] Boiling point rises with carbon number because each extra CH₂ increases the relative molecular mass and the number of electrons, and lengthens the molecule, so London dispersion forces get stronger.
[chem23-s3.10.2] The rise for each extra CH₂ gets smaller: the successive differences are 73, 47, 41, 37 and 33 °C, because each CH₂ is a smaller fraction of the molecule.
[chem23-s3.10.3] At room temperature, about 20 °C, methane to butane are gases, while pentane and hexane are liquids.
[chem23-s3.10.4] Larger alkanes up to about C16 are liquids, and beyond that they are waxy solids.
[chem23-s3.11.b] Other properties either follow from the molecules being non-polar or change gradually with chain length. Reactivity does not change at all.
[chem23-s3.11.0] All alkanes are insoluble in water, because they are non-polar and cannot form hydrogen bonds with water.
[chem23-s3.11.1] All alkanes dissolve in or mix with non-polar solvents such as cyclohexane, following the rule “like dissolves like”.
[chem23-s3.11.2] Liquid alkanes are less dense than water and float on it; hexane has a density of about 0.66 g cm⁻³.
[chem23-s3.11.3] All alkanes burn completely to CO₂ and H₂O, e.g. C₆H₁₄(l) + 9½O₂(g) → 6CO₂(g) + 7H₂O(l); larger alkanes need more oxygen per mole and release more energy per mole.
[chem23-s3.11.4] Larger alkanes burn with a more luminous, smokier flame and are less volatile (they evaporate less easily), so they are harder to ignite.
[chem23-s3.11.5] The percentage of carbon rises from 75.0% in CH₄ to 83.7% in C₆H₁₄, making incomplete combustion (burning in too little oxygen, giving soot and CO) more likely.
[chem23-s3.11.6] There is no difference in reactivity: all straight-chain alkanes are unreactive, reacting only by combustion or by substitution with halogens in ultraviolet light.
[chem23-s3.12.b] Alkanes with the same carbon number but different structures are structural isomers: they have the same molecular formula and Mr, but a different arrangement of the chain. Their boiling points differ even so.
[chem23-s3.12.0] Butane (−1 °C) and methylpropane (−12 °C) are the isomers of C₄H₁₀.
[chem23-s3.12.1] Pentane (36 °C), 2-methylbutane (28 °C) and 2,2-dimethylpropane (10 °C) are the isomers of C₅H₁₂.
[chem23-s3.12.2] Hexane (69 °C), 3-methylpentane (63 °C), 2-methylpentane (60 °C), 2,3-dimethylbutane (58 °C) and 2,2-dimethylbutane (50 °C) are the isomers of C₆H₁₄.
[chem23-s3.12.3] The more branched the molecule, the more compact, or closer to spherical, it is, so the area of contact between neighbouring molecules is smaller.
[chem23-s3.12.4] Smaller contact gives weaker London dispersion forces and a lower boiling point, since the Mr is the same for all the isomers. Unbranched chains lie alongside each other with a large area of contact.
[chem23-s3.13.b] Branching changes the boiling point enough to change the state at room temperature, but it leaves the chemistry unchanged.
[chem23-s3.13.0] 2,2-dimethylpropane, with a boiling point of 10 °C, is a gas at room temperature, although pentane, with the same formula, is a liquid at room temperature because its boiling point is 36 °C.
[chem23-s3.13.1] Isomers show no significant difference in solubility, combustion or reactivity: all are non-polar and insoluble in water, burn to the same products with the same equation, e.g. C₅H₁₂ + 8O₂ → 5CO₂ + 6H₂O, and are equally unreactive.
[chem23-s3.13.2] Diagram: two pentane molecules lying side by side with a large area of contact, beside two 2,2-dimethylpropane molecules touching at only a small area.
[chem23-s3.14.b] The monounsaturated straight-chain alkenes (those with one double bond and no branches) show the same boiling point pattern as the alkanes, but a little lower.
[chem23-s3.14.0] Boiling points: ethene −104 °C, propene −48 °C, but-1-ene −6 °C, pent-1-ene 30 °C and hex-1-ene 63 °C.
[chem23-s3.14.1] Boiling point rises with carbon number for the same reason as in alkanes: a larger Mr, more electrons and stronger London dispersion forces.
[chem23-s3.14.2] Ethene to butene are gases at room temperature, while pentene and hexene are liquids.
[chem23-s3.14.3] Each alkene boils a little lower than the alkane with the same number of carbons, e.g. ethene −104 °C against ethane −89 °C and hex-1-ene 63 °C against hexane 69 °C, because it has two fewer hydrogen atoms, so slightly fewer electrons and weaker London forces.
[chem23-s3.15.b] Alkenes resemble alkanes in solubility, but their double bond makes their reactivity and flame different.
[chem23-s3.15.0] All alkenes are insoluble in water and soluble in non-polar solvents, like alkanes.
[chem23-s3.15.1] All alkenes burn to CO₂ and H₂O, e.g. C₃H₆(g) + 4½O₂(g) → 3CO₂(g) + 3H₂O(l).
[chem23-s3.15.2] Every straight-chain alkene has the same percentage of carbon, 85.7%, since all have the formula CₙH₂ₙ; this is more than in the alkanes, so alkenes burn with smokier flames than alkanes.
[chem23-s3.15.3] All alkenes have one C=C bond, so they all undergo the same addition reactions, e.g. all decolourise bromine water, and they are much more reactive than alkanes.
[chem23-s3.15.4] Changing the chain length or the position of the double bond makes little difference to reactivity.
[chem23-s3.16.b] Use the data tables to predict properties from the trends. Each prediction rests on the three factors: intermolecular forces, Mr and bonding.
[chem23-s3.16.0] To predict a boiling point, use the trend in a table: the value lies between those of the neighbouring members, or of isomers with more and less branching.
[chem23-s3.16.1] Pent-1-ene, with a boiling point of 30 °C, is a liquid at 20 °C but would boil on a warm day or in warm water.
[chem23-s3.16.2] 2-Methylpentane boils below hexane, because both are C₆H₁₄ and the branched isomer has a smaller area of contact.
[chem23-s3.16.3] Hex-1-ene will decolourise bromine water and hexane will not, and both will float on water without dissolving.
[chem23-s3.16.4] A mixture of pentane and hexane can be separated by fractional distillation (separating liquids by their different boiling points), because their boiling points differ, at 36 °C and 69 °C.
[chem23-s3.17.b] Of the aliphatic hydrocarbons, alkanes are the most stable. Stable here has a particular meaning, set out below.
[chem23-s3.17.0] Alkanes are the least reactive aliphatic hydrocarbons: at room temperature they do not react with acids, bases, most oxidising agents such as acidified potassium manganate(VII), or bromine water.
[chem23-s3.17.1] Their old name, paraffins, comes from the Latin parum affinis, meaning little affinity, that is, little reactivity.
[chem23-s3.17.2] “Stable” means unreactive under ordinary conditions, not that alkanes can never react: they burn readily once ignited, and they react with halogens in ultraviolet light.
[chem23-s3.18.b] Three features of alkane bonding explain their stability: they are saturated, their bonds are strong, and their bonds are almost non-polar.
[chem23-s3.18.0] Alkanes are saturated: every carbon already has four single bonds, so nothing can add to the molecule, and a bond must be broken before any reaction can occur.
[chem23-s3.18.1] C–C and C–H bonds are strong, so a great deal of energy is needed to break them, which gives a high activation energy (the minimum energy needed to start a reaction).
[chem23-s3.18.2] The electronegativities (a measure of how strongly an atom pulls on shared electrons) of carbon, 2.55, and hydrogen, 2.20, differ by only 0.35, so C–H bonds are almost non-polar, and C–C bonds are completely non-polar.
[chem23-s3.18.3] With no significant δ⁺ or δ⁻ sites (slightly positive or negative ends), alkane molecules do not attract ions such as OH⁻ or H⁺, or polar molecules, which react by attacking charged sites.
[chem23-s3.18.4] Alkenes are more reactive because the C=C double bond is an electron-rich site that attracts reagents such as bromine and allows addition.
[chem23-s3.19.b] Alkanes are stable, not inert. They react under two sets of conditions: when ignited, and when exposed to ultraviolet light with a halogen.
[chem23-s3.19.0] Combustion: alkanes burn in oxygen once a flame or spark supplies the activation energy, releasing a lot of energy, e.g. CH₄(g) + 2O₂(g) → CO₂(g) + 2H₂O(l).
[chem23-s3.19.1] Substitution with chlorine or bromine needs ultraviolet light, or sunlight, to split the halogen molecule into reactive atoms, called free radicals, e.g. CH₄ + Cl₂ → CH₃Cl + HCl. There is more on this in Reaction mechanisms.
[chem23-s3.19.2] In the dark, an alkane mixed with bromine stays orange; in sunlight the colour slowly fades as substitution happens, which is much slower than the instant decolourisation produced by an alkene.
[chem23-s3.20.b] The unreactivity of alkanes is useful in everyday life, and it also causes an environmental problem.
[chem23-s3.20.0] Alkanes are safe to store and transport as fuels, such as natural gas, LPG and petrol, because they do not react until ignited.
[chem23-s3.20.1] Unreactive liquid alkanes such as hexane and cyclohexane are useful solvents.
[chem23-s3.20.2] Mineral oils and paraffin wax are used as lubricants and protective coatings.
[chem23-s3.20.3] The same chemical stability makes poly(ethene) and other addition polymers, whose chains are alkane-like, slow to break down in the environment. There is more on this in Types of organic reaction and addition polymers.
[chem23-s3.21.b] At Higher Level the stability of alkanes is explained by the type of bond. Single bonds are sigma bonds, while the second bond in an alkene's double bond is a weaker pi bond.
[chem23-s3.21.0] Every bond in an alkane is a sigma (σ) bond, formed by head-on overlap of orbitals (the regions where electrons are found) along the line between the two nuclei.
[chem23-s3.21.1] In a σ bond the shared electron pair is concentrated between the two nuclei, where both nuclei hold it strongly, so σ bonds are strong and their electrons are not exposed to attacking reagents.
[chem23-s3.21.2] An alkene's double bond is one σ bond plus one pi (π) bond; the π electrons lie above and below the plane of the molecule, further from the nuclei and less tightly held, so they are exposed to attack by electron-seeking reagents.
[chem23-s3.21.3] Typical average bond enthalpies (energies needed to break a bond) are C–C 348 kJ mol⁻¹, C=C 614 kJ mol⁻¹ and C–H 412 kJ mol⁻¹, so the π part of the C=C bond adds only about 266 kJ mol⁻¹ to the σ bond.
[chem23-s3.21.4] So alkenes react by breaking the weaker π bond and keeping the σ bond, which is addition, whereas an alkane would have to break a strong σ bond, which needs much more energy.
