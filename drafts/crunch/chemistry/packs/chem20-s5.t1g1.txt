TARGET chem20-s5: 5. Researching chemistry
[chem20-s5.0.b] The research investigation is a piece of independent work where you take one idea from the course and follow it into the real world. It asks you to use chemistry to explain an issue, not just report on it.
[chem20-s5.0.0] You research one area of the course through one cross-cutting theme (a big topic that links many parts of the course): health, sustainability or technology.
[chem20-s5.0.1] The research must use real chemistry from the course to explain the issue, not just describe it. This means reactions, equations and properties are used to say why something happens.
[chem20-s5.0.2] It should be personal and meaningful: a question you care about, often local or Irish, so that the chemistry connects to something you can see around you.
[chem20-s5.0.3] It shows chemistry as a collaborative, human activity (people working together and building on each other's work) with social and global importance.
[chem20-s5.0.4] Outputs can be a presentation to the class or a short digital report, so you choose the form that suits the audience.
[chem20-s5.0.5] Later research can be more detailed and may include gathering some primary data (data you measure yourself rather than take from a source), for example measuring the pH of local water samples.
[chem20-s5.0.6] The written exam includes questions on applying chemistry to the cross-cutting themes, so the skills and the examples are examinable.
[chem20-s5.1.b] A good investigation follows a fixed path from a question to a justified conclusion. Each step below builds on the one before, so working through them in order stops the research turning into a list of facts.
[chem20-s5.1.0] Step 1: Choose a focused, answerable question that links course chemistry to one theme, for example “Should…?”, “How effective is…?” or “What are the benefits and risks of…?” A focused question keeps the research from becoming too wide to finish.
[chem20-s5.1.1] Step 2: Identify the chemistry involved, such as reactions, equations and properties, so that the research explains why something happens and not only what happens.
[chem20-s5.1.2] Step 3: Find a range of sources: peer-reviewed papers and reviews (articles checked by independent experts before publication), reports from scientific and government bodies (e.g. the IPCC, the EPA, the HSE, WHO and Uisce Éireann), textbooks, and reputable science journalism. Include sources with different viewpoints.
[chem20-s5.1.3] Step 4: Evaluate each source by asking who wrote it and what expertise they have, when it was written (is it current?), and what evidence it gives (data and methods, or just opinion?).
[chem20-s5.1.4] Step 4 also asks whether the source was peer reviewed, and whether the author has a purpose or bias, e.g. funding, selling a product or campaigning.
[chem20-s5.1.5] Step 5: Separate measured data from claims and opinion, compare like with like, and note uncertainties and where scientists disagree. Comparing like with like means the figures you set side by side were measured in the same way.
[chem20-s5.1.6] Step 6: Weigh the evidence on each side, considering benefits, risks, costs and who is affected, locally and globally, now and in the future.
[chem20-s5.1.7] Step 7: Reach a justified conclusion that answers the question, explain the chemistry behind it, state its limitations and suggest further research.
[chem20-s5.1.8] Step 8: Reference all sources consistently, so a reader can find them, and communicate clearly for the audience (a presentation or digital report), using correct chemical terms, equations and units.
[chem20-s5.2.b] This outline shows Steps 1 to 3 applied to a health question. Fluoride is added to Irish public water supplies to protect teeth, and whether that should continue is a real public debate.
[chem20-s5.2.0] Question: “Should Ireland continue to add fluoride to public drinking water?” This is a Step 1 question because it can be answered by weighing evidence.
[chem20-s5.2.1] Chemistry: hexafluorosilicic acid (H₂SiF₆) is added to give 0.6–0.8 mg L⁻¹ of fluoride, a very small concentration. There is more on this in Treating drinking water.
[chem20-s5.2.2] Chemistry: fluoride converts some hydroxyapatite (the mineral that makes up tooth enamel) into fluorapatite, which resists acids made by bacteria in the mouth. This is why fluoride protects teeth from decay.
[chem20-s5.2.3] Sources: Department of Health and HSE information, systematic reviews of the evidence (e.g. Cochrane reviews, which gather and assess many studies together), dental and medical bodies, and the views of groups opposed to fluoridation.
[chem20-s5.2.4] Check the dates of sources, as older studies were done before fluoride toothpaste was common. This matters because the effect of fluoride in water may look different once toothpaste is also supplying it.
[chem20-s5.3.b] Steps 5 to 7 mean setting the evidence for and against side by side and then judging it. For fluoridation, each side has points that must be weighed rather than just listed.
[chem20-s5.3.0] Evidence for: fluoridation reduces tooth decay, with most benefit to people who can't afford dental care, because the fluoride reaches everyone through the water supply.
[chem20-s5.3.1] Evidence for: fluoridation is low cost per person, since one treatment step at the water plant serves a whole population.
[chem20-s5.3.2] Evidence and issues against: dental fluorosis (a change in the appearance of tooth enamel) occurs at higher intakes, so the amount of fluoride matters.
[chem20-s5.3.3] Evidence and issues against: the benefit may be smaller now that fluoride toothpaste is widely used, because teeth already get fluoride from another route.
[chem20-s5.3.4] Evidence and issues against: there are ethical objections to adding a substance to everyone's water without individual consent.
[chem20-s5.3.5] Evidence and issues against: most European countries don't fluoridate water.
[chem20-s5.3.6] Conclusion: a justified judgement weighing the size of the benefit against the risks and ethical issues, based on the most recent reviews.
[chem20-s5.4.b] This outline applies the stages to a sustainability question. The chemistry of what happens to the carbon in an Irish bog depends on whether the peat is wet or drained.
[chem20-s5.4.0] Question: “Is rewetting drained Irish bogs an effective way to reduce greenhouse gas emissions?”
[chem20-s5.4.1] Chemistry: in waterlogged peat (partly decayed plant material), decay is very slow because oxygen is lacking, so carbon builds up in a carbon sink (a store that takes in more carbon than it releases). There is more on this in The natural greenhouse effect and The enhanced greenhouse effect.
[chem20-s5.4.2] Chemistry: when bogs are drained, oxygen gets in and aerobic decay (decay by microorganisms that use oxygen) releases the stored carbon as CO₂.
[chem20-s5.4.3] Chemistry: when the peat is rewetted, the CO₂ release stops, but anaerobic microorganisms (those that live without oxygen) produce some methane (CH₄), which is a stronger greenhouse gas per kg.
[chem20-s5.4.4] Sources: EPA Ireland reports, and Bord na Móna and National Parks and Wildlife Service information.
[chem20-s5.4.5] Sources: peer-reviewed studies of emissions from drained and rewetted bogs, and farming organisations. These groups have different interests, so compare their viewpoints.
[chem20-s5.5.b] Rewetting trades one gas for another, and it also affects people. Weighing it means comparing the climate effects with each other and then with the social ones.
[chem20-s5.5.0] The size of the CO₂ savings is set against the methane emissions. The two are compared using GWPs (global warming potentials, a measure of how strongly each gas warms the atmosphere) and CO₂ equivalent (an amount of any gas expressed as the amount of CO₂ that would warm the same).
[chem20-s5.5.1] Rewetting has benefits for biodiversity and flood control, which are gains beyond the climate effect.
[chem20-s5.5.2] Rewetting affects landowners, jobs and communities that depended on peat, so the people affected must be part of the weighing.
[chem20-s5.5.3] The time scales of the carbon savings and the methane emissions must be considered, because the two may not happen over the same period.
[chem20-s5.5.4] Conclusion: a justified judgement on whether the evidence shows a net climate benefit, and under what conditions.
[chem20-s5.6.b] Public water supplies must be disinfected to kill microorganisms that cause disease. This outline compares two technologies, chemical (chlorine) and physical (UV light), which is a typical technology question.
[chem20-s5.6.0] Question: “Can UV treatment replace chlorine for disinfecting public water supplies?”
[chem20-s5.6.1] Chemistry: chlorine forms hypochlorous acid (Cl₂ + H₂O ⇌ HOCl + HCl), which kills microorganisms and leaves a residual (disinfectant that stays in the water) in the pipes.
[chem20-s5.6.2] Chemistry: UV (ultraviolet light) damages microbial DNA and works against chlorine-resistant Cryptosporidium (a microorganism that causes water-borne disease), but it leaves no residual.
[chem20-s5.6.3] Chemistry: chlorine can react with organic matter in the water to form disinfection by-products, e.g. trihalomethanes. There is more on this in Treating drinking water.
[chem20-s5.6.4] Sources: Uisce Éireann and EPA drinking-water reports, WHO guidelines, and studies of disinfection by-products.
[chem20-s5.6.5] Case study: the 2007 Galway Cryptosporidium outbreak.
[chem20-s5.7.b] Neither method is best on every measure, so the evaluation sets several issues against each other before reaching a conclusion.
[chem20-s5.7.0] Issue: the effectiveness of each method against different microorganisms, since chlorine and UV do not work equally well on all of them.
[chem20-s5.7.1] Issue: the protection each method gives in the distribution pipes, where a residual disinfectant keeps working after the water leaves the plant.
[chem20-s5.7.2] Issue: the health risks of by-products, set against the much larger risk of water-borne disease.
[chem20-s5.7.3] Issue: the energy use of UV lamps, and cost.
[chem20-s5.7.4] Conclusion: UV is a valuable extra barrier but cannot fully replace chlorine, because a residual disinfectant is needed in the pipes. The reasoning must be shown.
[chem20-s5.8.b] The syllabus asks you to see chemistry as something people do together, with effects well beyond the lab. Personal research is one way to appreciate that.
[chem20-s5.8.0] Modern chemistry is done by teams, often international, and builds on the work of others, so no single scientist works alone.
[chem20-s5.8.1] The lithium-ion battery came from the work of three scientists in different countries over decades. Goodenough (USA), Whittingham (UK-born, USA) and Yoshino (Japan) shared the Nobel Prize in Chemistry 2019. There is more on this in The lithium-ion cell.
[chem20-s5.8.2] The IPCC reports are written by hundreds of scientists assessing thousands of studies, which shows collaboration at a global scale.
[chem20-s5.8.3] Chemistry affects society globally through clean water, medicines, energy and materials, and also through pollution and climate change.
[chem20-s5.8.4] Personal research helps students judge claims and take part in public debate, because they can check the chemistry and the evidence behind what they hear.

CANDIDATES chem1-s2: 2. Science as a global enterprise and researching an issue
[chem1-s2.0.b] Science is not done by lone individuals. It is a worldwide effort in which results are shared, so that every scientist can build on what others have found.
[chem1-s2.0.0] Scientists work in universities, industry and government laboratories around the world, sharing results and building on each other's work, so new research starts from what is already known.
[chem1-s2.0.1] Large problems such as climate change, new medicines and clean energy are tackled by international collaborations, because countries working together can share data, equipment and funding.
[chem1-s2.0.2] Scientists publish in journals (magazines that print research reports) and present at conferences (meetings where scientists talk about their work) so that results are available to everyone and can be checked.
[chem1-s2.1.b] A scientific claim is only worth believing if others can look at the evidence behind it. This is why science depends on reports that are complete and clear.
[chem1-s2.1.0] Scientific claims must be supported by evidence that others can examine, so data, methods and conclusions are published together and a reader can judge whether the conclusion follows.
[chem1-s2.1.1] A report must describe the method in enough detail for another scientist to repeat it exactly, because a result can only be checked if the experiment can be redone.
[chem1-s2.1.2] Clear communication uses precise scientific terms, SI units (the international system of units), correct significant figures (the number of meaningful digits in a measurement), labelled tables and graphs, and cited sources (credited references), so the reader cannot misread the results.
[chem1-s2.2.b] Conventions are agreed rules that all scientists use, so that results mean the same everywhere. Without them, a chemist in one country could not be sure what a chemist in another country meant.
[chem1-s2.2.0] IUPAC (the International Union of Pure and Applied Chemistry) sets the rules for naming compounds, so that each compound has one systematic name (a name built by the agreed rules), e.g. but-1-ene, and it agrees chemical symbols and atomic masses.
[chem1-s2.2.1] The SI (Système International) system of units has base units (the basic units from which others are built), including the metre (m), kilogram (kg), second (s), kelvin (K) and mole (mol).
[chem1-s2.2.2] The SI base units also include the ampere (A) and the candela (cd).
[chem1-s2.2.3] Other shared conventions are chemical symbols and formulae, standard conditions for measurements, and international hazard symbols (pictograms, small warning pictures) on chemicals, so that a danger is recognised in any country.
[chem1-s2.3.b] Before research is published, other experts check it. This step, called peer review, is one of the main ways science keeps its standards high.
[chem1-s2.3.0] Peer review is the evaluation of a scientist's work by other independent experts in the same field (peers) before it is published.
[chem1-s2.3.1] 1. The scientist submits a paper (a written report of the research) to a journal.
[chem1-s2.3.2] 2. The editor, who runs the journal, sends the paper to reviewers.
[chem1-s2.3.3] 3. The reviewers check the method, data, analysis and conclusions for errors and originality (whether the work is new), and whether the conclusions are justified by the data.
[chem1-s2.3.4] 4. The paper is accepted, returned for changes, or rejected.
[chem1-s2.3.5] Peer review filters out poor or flawed work, reduces bias (unfair leaning towards one result) and gives readers confidence in published results.
[chem1-s2.3.6] Peer review does not guarantee that a result is correct; results are checked by others repeating the work.
[chem1-s2.4.b] Two similar words describe two different tests of whether a result can be trusted. The difference is who repeats the experiment and how.
[chem1-s2.4.0] Repeatable: the same experimenter, using the same method and equipment, gets the same or very similar results when the experiment is repeated.
[chem1-s2.4.1] Reproducible: a different experimenter, using different equipment or a different method, gets the same results.
[chem1-s2.4.2] A claim is accepted only when its results are reproducible, because results that no one else can reproduce are not accepted.
[chem1-s2.5.b] Researching an issue works best when you plan it. A clear question and a record of where your information came from make the final work easy to check.
[chem1-s2.5.0] Start with a focused research question about the issue, e.g. “What are the environmental effects of lithium mining?”, so you know exactly what you are looking for.
[chem1-s2.5.1] Identify keywords (the important search words) and search systematically rather than reading whatever appears first.
[chem1-s2.5.2] Record every source used: author, title, date, publisher or website, and the date accessed, so others can find and check it.
[chem1-s2.5.3] Take notes in your own words and keep a research log (a running record of what you searched and found).
[chem1-s2.6.b] Data used in a research project come from two places: your own measurements, or someone else's. Knowing which is which matters when you judge how far to trust them.
[chem1-s2.6.0] Primary data are collected first-hand by the investigator, e.g. by experiment or measurement.
[chem1-s2.6.1] Secondary data are collected by someone else, e.g. published results, government statistics, databases or the results of other class groups.
[chem1-s2.6.2] Secondary data are used to compare with primary data, to check or support conclusions, or where an experiment cannot be done in school.
[chem1-s2.7.b] Sources differ in how much you can trust them. Knowing the kind of source gives you a first idea of how reliable it is likely to be.
[chem1-s2.7.0] Peer-reviewed journal articles (reports checked by other experts before publication) are the most reliable sources but are often technical.
[chem1-s2.7.1] Books and textbooks, and reports from government agencies and scientific bodies, are further kinds of source.
[chem1-s2.7.2] The Environmental Protection Agency (EPA) is Ireland's environmental regulator (the body that monitors and enforces environmental rules).
[chem1-s2.7.3] The EPA publishes reports and data on air and water quality.
[chem1-s2.7.4] News media, websites, blogs and social media are accessible but of variable reliability; they may simplify, sensationalise (make more dramatic than the facts) or omit detail.
[chem1-s2.7.5] Company or campaign-group material may be written to sell a product or promote a view, so it may present only what suits that aim.
[chem1-s2.8.b] Once you have found a source, you must judge how far to rely on it. Asking the same set of questions of every source lets you compare them fairly.
[chem1-s2.8.0] Authority: who wrote the source, and are they qualified and named?
[chem1-s2.8.1] Accuracy and evidence: are the claims supported by data and references, and can they be checked against other sources?
[chem1-s2.8.2] Currency: is the source up to date?
[chem1-s2.8.3] Purpose and bias: is it trying to inform, persuade or sell; who funded it; does it present only one side?
[chem1-s2.8.4] Detail: does it explain methods, sample sizes (how many were measured) and uncertainties (how far the values might be off), or does it give only conclusions? A source may lack the detail needed to judge it.
[chem1-s2.8.5] Corroboration (independent confirmation): a claim supported by several independent, reliable sources is more trustworthy than a claim from one source.

CANDIDATES chem1-s6: 6. Chemistry in society and the media
[chem1-s6.0.b] Chemistry has been shaped by individual scientists whose discoveries changed how people live. For each one, know what they did, when, and what the impact on society was, including any downside.
[chem1-s6.0.0] Robert Boyle (born 25 January 1627 at Lismore Castle, Co. Waterford) pioneered experimental chemistry and argued that knowledge should come from experiment, not just from argument or authority.
[chem1-s6.0.1] Boyle's law, published in 1662, relates the pressure and volume of a gas, so it gave chemists a measured, testable rule about how gases behave.
[chem1-s6.0.2] Dmitri Mendeleev published his periodic table (a chart that arranges the elements in order) in 1869, and he left gaps in it for elements not yet discovered.
[chem1-s6.0.3] Mendeleev predicted the properties of those undiscovered elements, which showed the table was more than a list; the periodic table now organises all of chemistry.
[chem1-s6.0.4] Alexander Fleming discovered penicillin, the first antibiotic (a medicine that kills bacteria), in 1928; he shared the Nobel Prize (a major international award) in 1945 with Florey and Chain.
[chem1-s6.0.5] Penicillin was mass-produced from the 1940s, so it became widely available, and antibiotics have since saved very many lives.
[chem1-s6.0.6] Fritz Haber developed the ammonia synthesis from nitrogen and hydrogen (Nobel Prize 1918), and Carl Bosch scaled it up industrially in the early 1900s, so it could be run in large factories.
[chem1-s6.0.7] Ammonia-based fertilisers (chemicals added to soil to help crops grow) help feed much of the world's population, but the same chemistry was used to make explosives: a discovery can have both positive and negative impacts.
[chem1-s6.0.8] John Goodenough, Stanley Whittingham and Akira Yoshino developed the lithium-ion battery (a rechargeable battery), for which they received the 2019 Nobel Prize in Chemistry; it powers phones, laptops and electric vehicles.
[chem1-s6.1.b] Chemistry matters at three levels: to each person's daily life, to society as a whole, and to the planet. Each level has its own examples.
[chem1-s6.1.0] Personal: chemistry provides medicines, clean water, food, cleaning products, cosmetics, and materials for clothing and phones.
[chem1-s6.1.1] Personal: understanding chemical labels and hazards helps people make safe choices, for example when storing or using household products.
[chem1-s6.1.2] Social: chemistry supports public health through water treatment, vaccines (preparations that train the body to fight a disease) and medicines.
[chem1-s6.1.3] Social: chemistry supports agriculture through fertilisers and pesticides (chemicals that kill pests that damage crops), and provides jobs in the chemical and pharmaceutical (medicine-making) industry.
[chem1-s6.1.4] Ireland has a large pharmaceutical industry, so chemistry provides jobs here in particular.
[chem1-s6.1.5] Global: chemistry helps tackle climate change (the warming of the Earth) through cleaner fuels, batteries and carbon capture (trapping carbon dioxide before it enters the air).
[chem1-s6.1.6] Global: chemistry helps tackle pollution, food security (everyone having enough food) and the use of resources.
[chem1-s6.2.b] Science does not happen in isolation. The society around scientists affects which questions get studied and which methods are allowed.
[chem1-s6.2.0] Funding: governments, companies and charities decide what research is funded, often according to social needs or profit, so money steers which problems get studied.
[chem1-s6.2.1] Public concern and urgent problems drive research, for example the search for vaccines during a pandemic (a disease outbreak spreading across the world), or for alternatives to fossil fuels.
[chem1-s6.2.2] Laws and regulations, for example on chemical safety, emissions (gases released into the air) and animal testing, limit or direct research.
[chem1-s6.2.3] The ethical values of society (its beliefs about right and wrong) decide what research is acceptable.
[chem1-s6.3.b] When chemistry is applied to a real issue, the science is not the only consideration. Four kinds of factor influence whether and how it is used, and people can also act on them personally.
[chem1-s6.3.0] Economic factors include the cost of development, production and disposal, jobs and profit, because a process that is too expensive may never be used.
[chem1-s6.3.1] Social factors include effects on health, safety, communities and quality of life.
[chem1-s6.3.2] Sustainability means meeting present needs without compromising future generations, through the use of renewable resources and energy (ones that are not used up) and the management of waste.
[chem1-s6.3.3] Ethical factors include whether an application is right, for example fairness, risks to people or the environment, and informed consent (people agreeing only after being told the risks).
[chem1-s6.3.4] Being personally effective means making informed choices, for example about recycling, energy use and product claims, and taking part in public debate using evidence.
[chem1-s6.4.b] The specification links all its topics through a few big ideas. These help you connect individual pieces of chemistry to the world outside the lab.
[chem1-s6.4.0] The specification's three cross-cutting themes are Health, Sustainability and Technology.
[chem1-s6.4.1] The themes are lenses (ways of looking) for studying how chemistry applies to real life, and the written examination includes questions on them.
[chem1-s6.5.b] News, advertising and social media often report science second-hand rather than as the original research. It is therefore important to check what they say before believing it.
[chem1-s6.5.0] News, advertising and social media may simplify, exaggerate or sensationalise (make more dramatic than the facts justify) science.
[chem1-s6.5.1] They may report a single study as settled fact, or leave out uncertainty and context, so a tentative result sounds like proof.
[chem1-s6.5.2] Headlines are written to attract attention, so they may not match the findings of the study.
[chem1-s6.6.b] A checklist of questions lets you test any claim, whether it is a news report or an advertisement. Each one looks at a different weakness a claim might have.
[chem1-s6.6.0] What exactly is being claimed, and is it a scientific claim, meaning one that can be tested with evidence?
[chem1-s6.6.1] Where does the claim come from: is the original study named, peer-reviewed (checked by independent experts before publication) and available to read?
[chem1-s6.6.2] How strong is the evidence: what is the sample size (how many were tested), were there controls (a comparison group), have the results been reproduced (repeated by others), and was the study done in people, animals or cells?
[chem1-s6.6.3] Is cause being confused with correlation? Two things happening together (correlation) does not prove that one causes the other.
[chem1-s6.6.4] Who benefits: is the source selling a product or promoting a view, and who funded the research?
[chem1-s6.6.5] Do other independent experts and sources agree?
[chem1-s6.6.6] Are scientific words used correctly, or misused to sound convincing, for example “chemical-free” or “natural means safe”?
[chem1-s6.7.b] Once a claim has been checked, you need to discuss it fairly. Good discussion keeps facts and opinions apart.
[chem1-s6.7.0] Separate evidence from opinion, and base conclusions on the weight of the evidence, meaning how much good evidence there is on each side.
[chem1-s6.7.1] Acknowledge uncertainty and the limits of current knowledge, because science can say what the evidence shows so far but cannot always give a final answer.
[chem1-s6.7.2] Consider different perspectives, economic, social, ethical and environmental, while keeping the scientific evidence separate from them.
[chem1-s6.8.b] Some claims appear again and again in advertising and online. Each can be answered with a fact from chemistry.
[chem1-s6.8.0] “Chemical-free”: everything, including water and food, is made of chemicals, so nothing can truly be chemical-free.
[chem1-s6.8.1] “Natural is always safe; synthetic (man-made) is dangerous”: toxicity (how poisonous something is) depends on the substance and the dose, not on whether it is natural.
[chem1-s6.8.2] Some natural substances are highly toxic, and a synthetic substance can be identical to a natural one, so the origin does not change the chemistry.
[chem1-s6.8.3] Alkaline-water and detox claims: the body controls its own pH (the scale that measures how acidic or alkaline a liquid is), and drinking alkaline water does not change blood pH.
[chem1-s6.8.4] Human blood pH is kept between about 7.35 and 7.45.
[chem1-s6.8.5] Stomach acid has a pH of about 1.5 to 3.5, so it is strongly acidic and would quickly neutralise any alkaline water swallowed.

CANDIDATES chem20-s3: 3. Treating drinking water
[chem20-s3.0.b] Water taken from rivers, lakes or the ground is not safe to drink as it comes. Treatment is a series of steps, each removing a different problem, so that the water that reaches your tap is safe.
[chem20-s3.0.0] Drinking water must be free of harmful microorganisms (bacteria, viruses and other living things too small to see) and harmful chemicals, and must be clear, colourless and odourless.
[chem20-s3.0.1] In Ireland, public water supplies are treated by Uisce Éireann (Irish Water) and must meet the limits in the EU Drinking Water Directive (the European law that sets the maximum levels of contaminants).
[chem20-s3.0.2] The usual order of treatment is screening, flocculation (coagulation), sedimentation, filtration (sand and, in some plants, microfiltration), disinfection (chlorination and/or UV), fluoridation and pH adjustment.
[chem20-s3.0.3] The exact steps depend on the source water, so a plant treating a clean source needs fewer steps than one treating a muddy river.
[chem20-s3.0.4] The first steps remove suspended solids (tiny solid particles carried along in the water) by physical means; the later steps kill microorganisms and adjust the chemistry of the water.
[chem20-s3.0.5] Diagram: a flow diagram of a treatment plant showing the intake with screen, flocculation tank (aluminium sulfate added), sedimentation tank (sludge removed), sand filter beds, chlorine and/or UV unit, fluoride and pH adjustment, storage reservoir and distribution pipes.
[chem20-s3.1.b] Screening is the first step. It removes the biggest objects before they can block or damage equipment further along.
[chem20-s3.1.0] Water from a river or lake passes through metal screens (mesh) that remove large floating objects such as branches, leaves, plastic and fish.
[chem20-s3.1.1] No chemicals are added at the screening stage, because it is purely a physical straining of the water.
[chem20-s3.2.b] Very fine particles carry charges that keep them apart and suspended, so they cannot simply be left to settle. Flocculation (also called coagulation) is the chemical trick that makes them clump into pieces big enough to sink.
[chem20-s3.2.0] Very small suspended particles (clay, silt and organic matter) don't settle because they are tiny and carry like, usually negative, charges that make them repel each other.
[chem20-s3.2.1] A flocculating agent (coagulant, a chemical that makes particles clump), usually aluminium sulfate, Al₂(SO₄)₃, is added and stirred in; iron salts and polyelectrolytes (long-chain molecules with many charges) are also used.
[chem20-s3.2.2] The positive Al³⁺ ions neutralise the charges on the particles, so the particles no longer repel each other and clump together.
[chem20-s3.2.3] Aluminium hydroxide also forms as a sticky, jelly-like precipitate (an insoluble solid formed in a reaction) that traps the particles: Al₂(SO₄)₃(aq) + 3Ca(OH)₂(aq) → 2Al(OH)₃(s) + 3CaSO₄(aq).
[chem20-s3.2.4] The particles join into larger clumps called flocs, which are heavy enough to settle.
[chem20-s3.3.b] Once flocculation has made the particles heavy, gravity can do the work of removing them. This step is called sedimentation (settling).
[chem20-s3.3.0] Water flows slowly through large settling tanks, where the flocs and other heavier particles sink to the bottom under gravity, removing most of the suspended matter. The slow flow gives them time to sink.
[chem20-s3.3.1] The sludge (the thick mud of settled solids) that collects at the bottom is removed and disposed of, and the clearer water flows off the top.
[chem20-s3.4.b] Sedimentation leaves behind the smaller particles that did not sink. Filtration strains these out, using sand and, in some plants, membranes as well.
[chem20-s3.4.0] Sand filtration: water passes down through beds of sand on a layer of gravel, and the remaining small particles and flocs are trapped in the spaces between the sand grains.
[chem20-s3.4.1] The filters are cleaned regularly by backwashing, which pumps clean water up through the bed so that the trapped particles are flushed out and the filter does not clog.
[chem20-s3.4.2] Microfiltration forces water through membranes (thin sheets) with very small pores (about 0.1–10 µm), which remove fine particles, bacteria and protozoan parasites (single-celled organisms that cause disease) such as Cryptosporidium.
[chem20-s3.4.3] Microfiltration does not remove dissolved substances or most viruses, because these are smaller than the pores.
[chem20-s3.5.b] Filtering removes particles, but many microorganisms still get through. Chlorination is the disinfection step: adding chlorine to kill them.
[chem20-s3.5.0] Chlorination kills disease-causing microorganisms (bacteria and viruses) so that the water is safe to drink.
[chem20-s3.5.1] Chlorine gas or sodium hypochlorite (NaOCl) is added. Chlorine reacts with water to form hypochlorous acid, the main disinfectant: Cl₂(g) + H₂O(l) ⇌ HOCl(aq) + HCl(aq).
[chem20-s3.5.2] Enough chlorine is added to leave a small residual of free chlorine (a few tenths of a mg L⁻¹, that is, a little left over after disinfection), which keeps the water disinfected as it travels through the pipes to the tap.
[chem20-s3.5.3] Some organisms, especially Cryptosporidium, resist chlorine at the levels used, so another barrier (good filtration or UV) is needed.
[chem20-s3.5.4] Too much chlorine gives an unpleasant taste and smell.
[chem20-s3.5.5] Chlorine reacts with organic matter (material from plants and other living things) in the water to form by-products such as trihalomethanes, whose levels are limited by law.
[chem20-s3.5.6] Removing organic matter first, by flocculation and filtration, keeps trihalomethane levels low because there is less for the chlorine to react with.
[chem20-s3.6.b] Ultraviolet (UV) light is an alternative way to disinfect water. It works on organisms that chlorine struggles with, but it cannot protect the water once it is in the pipes.
[chem20-s3.6.0] The water flows past lamps that emit ultraviolet radiation, which damages the DNA (the genetic material) of microorganisms so that they can't reproduce.
[chem20-s3.6.1] UV treatment is effective against Cryptosporidium, which resists chlorine at the levels used.
[chem20-s3.6.2] UV adds no chemicals and does not change the taste or smell of the water.
[chem20-s3.6.3] UV leaves no residual disinfectant in the pipes, so chlorine is still added afterwards to protect the water on its way to the tap.
[chem20-s3.6.4] The water must be clear first, because suspended particles can shield microorganisms from the UV; the lamps also use electricity.
[chem20-s3.6.5] An outbreak of Cryptosporidium infection in Galway in 2007, from the city's water supply, led to UV treatment being installed at the treatment plant there.
[chem20-s3.7.b] Fluoridation is different from the other steps because it is added for dental health, not to make the water safe from germs or particles. A small amount of a fluoride compound is added to public supplies.
[chem20-s3.7.0] A fluoride compound is added to public water supplies: usually hexafluorosilicic acid (H₂SiF₆) in Ireland, and sodium fluoride (NaF) elsewhere.
[chem20-s3.7.1] Fluoride helps prevent tooth decay: it is taken into tooth enamel (the hard outer layer of the tooth), converting some of the hydroxyapatite (the calcium mineral that enamel is made of) into fluorapatite.
[chem20-s3.7.2] Fluorapatite is more resistant to attack by acids made by bacteria than hydroxyapatite is, so the teeth decay less.
[chem20-s3.7.3] Ireland has fluoridated public water supplies since the 1960s, under the Health (Fluoridation of Water Supplies) Act 1960.
[chem20-s3.7.4] The fluoride level is set at 0.6–0.8 mg L⁻¹ (p.p.m., parts per million), with a legal maximum of 0.8 mg L⁻¹ (since 2007).
[chem20-s3.7.5] Too much fluoride causes dental fluorosis, which shows as white flecks or brown staining of the teeth. This is why the level is limited.
[chem20-s3.7.6] Fluoridation is debated, and most European countries do not fluoridate water.
[chem20-s3.8.b] The last chemical step makes sure the water is neither too acidic nor too alkaline. pH is the scale that measures this, with 7 neutral, below 7 acidic and above 7 alkaline.
[chem20-s3.8.0] The pH of drinking water must be kept within legal limits of 6.5–9.5, usually close to neutral or slightly alkaline.
[chem20-s3.8.1] If the water is too acidic (e.g. from peaty, upland areas), lime (calcium hydroxide, Ca(OH)₂, or calcium oxide, CaO) or sodium carbonate is added to raise the pH, because these are bases that neutralise acid.
[chem20-s3.8.2] If the water is too alkaline, e.g. after lime has been added in treatment, sulfuric acid or carbon dioxide is added to lower the pH, because these give acidic solutions.
[chem20-s3.8.3] Acidic water corrodes (slowly eats away) metal pipes and dissolves lead and copper from household plumbing, which is a health risk.
[chem20-s3.8.4] Very alkaline water tastes bitter and leaves deposits.
[chem20-s3.8.5] The pH also affects how well flocculation and chlorination work.
[chem20-s3.9.b] Treatment uses energy and chemicals, so it is worth asking what it achieves. Clean water has changed human health and still shapes lives around the world.
[chem20-s3.9.0] Clean drinking water is one of the greatest public health advances: water-borne diseases such as cholera, typhoid and dysentery killed huge numbers of people in cities before treatment and disinfection.
[chem20-s3.9.1] In 1854 John Snow traced a cholera outbreak in London to a contaminated water pump in Broad Street, an early piece of evidence that disease spreads through water.
[chem20-s3.9.2] Globally, about 2.2 billion people still lacked safely managed drinking water in 2022 (WHO/UNICEF).
[chem20-s3.9.3] Contaminated water and poor sanitation cause many deaths from diarrhoeal disease, mostly of young children.
[chem20-s3.9.4] Clean, safe water and sanitation for all is UN Sustainable Development Goal 6.
[chem20-s3.9.5] Clean water brings benefits beyond health: less time is spent collecting water, often by women and girls; more children attend school; workers are healthier; and economies develop.
[chem20-s3.9.6] Costs: treatment uses energy and chemicals and produces sludge, so treated water should not be wasted. There is more on this in Water contamination.

CANDIDATES chem1-s5: 5. Reflecting, recording and communicating findings
[chem1-s5.0.b] A log is a portfolio of experimental and research data, kept while the work is done rather than written up afterwards. It is the raw record that everything else in an investigation, from evaluation to the final report, is built from.
[chem1-s5.0.0] A log (laboratory notebook or portfolio) is a dated, ongoing record of all experimental and research work, kept as the work happens, so nothing has to be remembered later.
[chem1-s5.0.1] The log records the aim (what you are trying to find out) and the plan, and any changes made to the plan and why.
[chem1-s5.0.2] It also records the raw data (the actual measurements, before any calculation) and observations, including unexpected ones, plus calculations, sources consulted, problems met and reflections.
[chem1-s5.0.3] The log provides evidence of what was done, so it supports honesty and integrity: anyone can check that the results came from the work.
[chem1-s5.0.4] The log allows the work to be repeated or written up later, because the plan, changes and data are all written down. The Chemistry in Practice Investigation brief asks students to keep one.
[chem1-s5.1.b] An evaluation judges the strengths and weaknesses of the method and the data; it is not a summary of what was done. Each judgement should lead to a specific improvement.
[chem1-s5.1.0] Evaluate the method: was the test fair and valid (did it really test what it set out to test), and which variables (things that could change the result) were hard to control?
[chem1-s5.1.1] Evaluate the data: was there enough of it, over a wide enough range; how much did the repeats vary (a big spread means low reliability); were there anomalous results (ones that do not fit the pattern)?
[chem1-s5.1.2] Identify the main sources of error and their likely effect, stating whether each makes the result larger or smaller than the true value. For example, heat lost to the surroundings makes a measured temperature rise smaller than the true value.
[chem1-s5.1.3] Human error on its own is not an acceptable source of error because it names no specific cause; name the specific mistake, such as misreading a meniscus (the curved surface of a liquid in a measuring cylinder or burette).
[chem1-s5.1.4] Suggest specific improvements, each matched to an error, e.g. collecting a gas in a gas syringe instead of over water, using a data-logger (an electronic sensor that records readings automatically), or insulating a calorimeter (the container in which heat changes are measured) to cut heat loss.
[chem1-s5.1.5] Suggest further investigations that would test the conclusion more fully.
[chem1-s5.1.6] Judge how confident you can be in the conclusion, based on the strengths and weaknesses of the method and the data.
[chem1-s5.2.b] Evaluation looks at the experiment; reflection looks at you as an investigator. It asks how well your own planning and thinking worked.
[chem1-s5.2.0] Reflection asks what went well, what did not, what would be done differently, and what was learned about investigating.
[chem1-s5.2.1] Reflection is honest about difficulties, because a problem that is hidden cannot be learned from.
[chem1-s5.2.2] An investigation is valuable for the process it teaches, not only for getting a right answer, so a result that did not work out still has something to reflect on.
[chem1-s5.3.b] The skills built in practical work are not tied to one experiment. They can be reused when a problem looks completely new.
[chem1-s5.3.0] Skills transfer: the same method of planning, controlling variables and analysing data applies to new problems, because the thinking is the same even when the chemistry differs.
[chem1-s5.3.1] In an unfamiliar problem, identify what is being asked, the chemistry it depends on, and the data given.
[chem1-s5.3.2] Set out a step-by-step approach to the problem, so that each step can be checked.
[chem1-s5.3.3] Check that the answer is sensible, for example that its size and units are reasonable for the situation.
[chem1-s5.4.b] A scientific report follows a fixed order so that any reader can find what they need and judge the work. It is written in enough detail for someone else to repeat the investigation.
[chem1-s5.4.0] A report has a title, followed by an introduction giving the background research and the research question, and then the hypothesis (a testable prediction of the result).
[chem1-s5.4.1] The method gives the equipment, a step-by-step procedure and the safety precautions, in enough detail for the work to be repeated.
[chem1-s5.4.2] The results are presented as tables and graphs, and the analysis gives the calculations and the patterns in the data.
[chem1-s5.4.3] The report ends with a conclusion (what the results show about the hypothesis), an evaluation and references (the list of sources used).
[chem1-s5.4.4] The report is written in the past tense, because the work is already done, and in an impersonal style, with no 'I' or 'we'.
[chem1-s5.5.b] Chemistry has its own precise vocabulary and its own ways of showing information. Using them correctly makes a report exact and unambiguous.
[chem1-s5.5.0] Precise terms are used, e.g. 'dissolves', not 'melts', for a solid going into solution, and 'effervescence' for bubbles of gas, because each word names one particular process.
[chem1-s5.5.1] Correct SI units (the international standard units of measurement) and IUPAC names (the internationally agreed systematic names of compounds) are used throughout.
[chem1-s5.5.2] Representations include balanced chemical equations with state symbols, structural formulae (showing how atoms are bonded), labelled diagrams of apparatus, tables, graphs and energy profile diagrams (which show how energy changes during a reaction).
[chem1-s5.6.b] The same findings have to be presented differently depending on who will read them. What a specialist needs is not what a class or the public needs.
[chem1-s5.6.0] Scientists need full technical detail, the data and references, as in a journal paper or a conference poster, so that they can check and build on the work.
[chem1-s5.6.1] The public, policy-makers or a class need plain language, the key findings stated first, simple graphics, and technical terms explained, because they do not have the specialist background.
[chem1-s5.6.2] The same findings may be communicated as a report, poster, presentation, infographic (a picture-based summary) or article.
[chem1-s5.6.3] The format is chosen for the purpose and the audience.
[chem1-s5.7.b] A scientific opinion has to be backed up, and the evidence behind it has to be traceable. Citing sources lets readers check the evidence and credits the people who produced it.
[chem1-s5.7.0] An argument states a claim, supports it with evidence and reasoning, and considers counter-arguments (points against the claim).
[chem1-s5.7.1] Each source is cited in the text and listed in full in the references, so that readers can check it.
[chem1-s5.7.2] Copying someone else's work without acknowledgement is plagiarism.
[chem1-s5.7.3] When evaluating someone else's argument, check its evidence, reasoning and sources before agreeing or disagreeing, so that your opinion rests on more than a first impression.
