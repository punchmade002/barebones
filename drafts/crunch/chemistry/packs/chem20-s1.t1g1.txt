TARGET chem20-s1: 1. The greenhouse effect
[chem20-s1.0.b] Carbon moves continuously between stores (places where it is held) through natural processes. The balance between sources and sinks decides how much CO₂ is in the air.
[chem20-s1.0.0] Carbon is stored in the atmosphere (mainly as CO₂), in the oceans (dissolved CO₂, hydrogencarbonate and carbonate ions), in living things (carbohydrates, proteins and fats), in soils and peat, and in rocks (limestone and fossil fuels).
[chem20-s1.0.1] Photosynthesis (the process in which plants build sugar from CO₂ and water) removes CO₂ from the air: 6CO₂(g) + 6H₂O(l) → C₆H₁₂O₆(aq) + 6O₂(g). It is powered by sunlight in green plants, algae and phytoplankton (microscopic floating ocean algae).
[chem20-s1.0.2] Respiration, the process in which living things use glucose and oxygen to release energy, returns CO₂ to the air. It happens in plants, animals and microorganisms: C₆H₁₂O₆(aq) + 6O₂(g) → 6CO₂(g) + 6H₂O(l).
[chem20-s1.0.3] Decay is the breakdown of dead matter by microorganisms. It also releases CO₂, and it releases CH₄ (methane) instead where oxygen is absent.
[chem20-s1.0.4] CO₂ dissolves in the oceans and waterways, so the water is also a store: CO₂(g) ⇌ CO₂(aq), and then CO₂(aq) + H₂O(l) ⇌ H₂CO₃(aq), where H₂CO₃ is carbonic acid.
[chem20-s1.0.5] Marine organisms use carbonate to build calcium carbonate shells and skeletons. Over long periods these deposits form limestone, which locks carbon away in rock.
[chem20-s1.1.b] Carbon also returns to the air by burning, and some carbon is held back for very long periods as fuels. This explains how the carbon in fossil fuels reaches the atmosphere.
[chem20-s1.1.0] Combustion (burning) of fuels releases CO₂, for example CH₄(g) + 2O₂(g) → CO₂(g) + 2H₂O(l). Natural sources also include forest fires and volcanic eruptions.
[chem20-s1.1.1] Peat forms from dead organisms buried over thousands of years, while coal, oil and natural gas form over millions of years. In both cases the material is buried without enough oxygen to decay fully, so they are long-term carbon stores.
[chem20-s1.1.2] Burning coal, oil, natural gas or peat therefore returns carbon, stored for a very long time, to the atmosphere as CO₂.
[chem20-s1.1.3] Diagram: the carbon cycle with atmospheric CO₂ at the centre and labelled arrows for photosynthesis, respiration, decay, combustion, dissolving in the oceans, formation of shells and limestone, and formation of fossil fuels.
[chem20-s1.2.b] Each part of the carbon cycle either adds CO₂ to the air or takes it out. When additions and removals match, the CO₂ level stays steady, and human activity has upset that match.
[chem20-s1.2.0] A carbon source adds more CO₂ to the atmosphere than it removes, for example burning fossil fuels, respiration, decay, volcanoes, and heating limestone in cement-making: CaCO₃(s) → CaO(s) + CO₂(g).
[chem20-s1.2.1] A carbon sink removes more carbon from the atmosphere than it releases, and stores it. Examples are growing forests, the oceans, and undisturbed peatlands and wetlands (peat-forming bogs and marshy land).
[chem20-s1.2.2] Waterlogged conditions in undisturbed peatlands and wetlands stop dead plant material from decaying, so the carbon stays stored.
[chem20-s1.2.3] Before industrialisation (the growth of factories and large-scale fuel burning), natural sources and sinks were roughly in balance, so atmospheric CO₂ stayed fairly steady at about 280 ppm (parts per million by volume).
[chem20-s1.2.4] Burning fossil fuels moves carbon from long-term stores into the air far faster than sinks can absorb it. Deforestation (clearing forests) and draining peatlands both remove sinks and release stored carbon.
[chem20-s1.2.5] As a result, atmospheric CO₂ has risen to about 420 ppm in the mid-2020s.
[chem20-s1.2.6] CO₂ is a greenhouse gas, so a carbon cycle out of balance changes the climate. Natural changes in the cycle over thousands of years have caused past climate changes; the present change is human-influenced and much faster.
[chem20-s1.2.7] Sustainability means using resources so that they are not used up and the environment is not damaged for future generations. For carbon this means keeping emissions in balance with what sinks can absorb, for example by using renewable energy and protecting forests and peatlands.
[chem20-s1.3.b] Greenhouse gases in the atmosphere trap some of the heat the Earth radiates, keeping the lower atmosphere warm.
[chem20-s1.3.0] Energy from the Sun reaches the Earth mainly as visible light (shorter wavelength), with some ultraviolet and near-infrared. The atmosphere is largely transparent to visible light, so it passes through.
[chem20-s1.3.1] About 30% of incoming solar radiation is reflected back to space by clouds, ice and the surface. The rest is absorbed and warms the land and oceans.
[chem20-s1.3.2] The warmed Earth radiates energy back out as longer-wavelength infrared (IR) radiation, which is heat. Remember that incoming radiation is mainly visible light and outgoing radiation is infrared.
[chem20-s1.3.3] Greenhouse gases absorb some of this IR and re-emit it in all directions, including back down towards the surface, so heat is kept in the lower atmosphere for longer.
[chem20-s1.3.4] This warming of the Earth's surface and lower atmosphere by naturally occurring greenhouse gases is the natural greenhouse effect.
[chem20-s1.3.5] The main natural greenhouse gases are water vapour, the largest contributor, and carbon dioxide, with smaller amounts of methane and dinitrogen oxide (N₂O); ozone also contributes.
[chem20-s1.3.6] The name is an analogy with a glass greenhouse, which lets sunlight in and keeps heat in. The analogy is not exact, because a real greenhouse works mainly by stopping warm air escaping.
[chem20-s1.3.7] Diagram: the Sun, incoming short-wavelength radiation passing through the atmosphere, some reflected, IR emitted from the surface, some absorbed by a layer of greenhouse gases and re-emitted back down, and some escaping to space.
[chem20-s1.4.b] Not every gas in the air is a greenhouse gas. Whether a gas traps heat depends on the structure of its molecules.
[chem20-s1.4.0] Nitrogen (N₂, about 78% of dry air), oxygen (O₂, about 21%) and argon (about 0.9%) do not absorb IR radiation, so they are not greenhouse gases even though they make up about 99% of dry air.
[chem20-s1.4.1] A molecule absorbs IR when the energy makes its bonds vibrate (stretch or bend) in a way that changes how charge is spread across the molecule.
[chem20-s1.4.2] Molecules of two identical atoms (N₂, O₂) cannot do this, while H₂O, CO₂, CH₄ and N₂O, with three or more atoms and polar bonds (bonds where the electrons are shared unequally), can.
[chem20-s1.4.3] Water vapour contributes most to the natural greenhouse effect because it is by far the most abundant greenhouse gas.
[chem20-s1.4.4] The concentration of water vapour is set by temperature, through evaporation (liquid water turning to vapour) and condensation (vapour turning back to liquid), not directly by human emissions.
[chem20-s1.5.b] The natural greenhouse effect is what makes the Earth habitable, and experiments by John Tyndall in the nineteenth century showed which gases absorb heat radiation.
[chem20-s1.5.0] Without the natural greenhouse effect, the Earth's average surface temperature would be about −18 °C instead of about +15 °C, a difference of about 33 °C.
[chem20-s1.5.1] At −18 °C the oceans would freeze and life as we know it could not exist.
[chem20-s1.5.2] The natural greenhouse effect keeps water liquid over most of the planet and evens out the difference between day and night temperatures.
[chem20-s1.5.3] The natural effect is essential and beneficial. The problem is the extra warming caused by increased greenhouse gas concentrations.
[chem20-s1.5.4] John Tyndall (1820–1893), born in Leighlinbridge, Co. Carlow, used experiments from 1859 at the Royal Institution in London to show which gases absorb heat radiation: water vapour and carbon dioxide do, while nitrogen and oxygen do not.
[chem20-s1.5.5] Tyndall's experiments laid the experimental foundation for understanding the greenhouse effect. The Tyndall National Institute in Cork is named after him.
[chem20-s1.6.b] The natural greenhouse effect is beneficial, but human activity has added greenhouse gases to the air. The extra warming this causes is the enhanced greenhouse effect.
[chem20-s1.6.0] The enhanced greenhouse effect is the extra warming of the Earth's surface and lower atmosphere caused by increased concentrations of greenhouse gases from human (anthropogenic) activities.
[chem20-s1.6.1] The main human activities are burning fossil fuels (for power generation, transport, heating and industry), deforestation and land-use change, agriculture (livestock and fertilisers), cement-making and landfill (waste buried in the ground).
[chem20-s1.6.2] The resulting long-term rise in the Earth's average surface temperature is global warming. Together with the other changes it causes, it is called climate change.
[chem20-s1.7.b] Gases differ in how much they warm the Earth for the same mass emitted. To compare them, scientists use a number called the global warming potential.
[chem20-s1.7.0] Gases differ in their greenhouse effect because they differ in how strongly each molecule absorbs infrared radiation, how abundant they are in the atmosphere, and how long they stay there (atmospheric lifetime).
[chem20-s1.7.1] The greenhouse factor of a gas is its global warming potential (GWP): the warming caused by emitting 1 kg (or 1 tonne) of the gas over a set period, usually 100 years, compared with the warming from the same mass of CO₂. The GWP of CO₂ is 1 by definition.
[chem20-s1.7.2] 100-year GWPs from the IPCC (the Intergovernmental Panel on Climate Change) Fifth Assessment Report (AR5, 2013) are: CO₂ 1; methane (CH₄) 28; dinitrogen oxide (N₂O) 265.
[chem20-s1.7.3] The IPCC Sixth Assessment Report (AR6, 2021) gives slightly different values because the science is refined over time: CH₄ about 27–30, depending on whether the methane is of fossil origin. The AR6 value for N₂O is 273.
[chem20-s1.7.4] Always state which report a GWP comes from, because the values differ between reports.
[chem20-s1.7.5] A high GWP does not make a gas the most important. CO₂ has the lowest GWP of these gases, yet it causes most of the human-caused warming because far more of it is emitted and it stays in the atmosphere for centuries.
[chem20-s1.7.6] Emissions of different gases are compared as CO₂ equivalent, found by multiplying the mass of the gas by its GWP.
[chem20-s1.7.7] Example: 5 tonnes of methane = 5 × 28 = 140 tonnes CO₂ equivalent (AR5 values).
[chem20-s1.8.b] Each greenhouse gas has its own sources and abundance. Abundance is given in ppm, parts per million of the air by volume, and compared with the pre-industrial level.
[chem20-s1.8.0] Carbon dioxide comes from burning fossil fuels, deforestation and cement-making (CaCO₃ → CaO + CO₂).
[chem20-s1.8.1] CO₂ is about 420 ppm in the mid-2020s, up from about 280 ppm before industrialisation, an increase of about 50%.
[chem20-s1.8.2] Much of the extra CO₂ stays in the atmosphere for centuries, and some for thousands of years, which is why its effect builds up.
[chem20-s1.8.3] Methane comes from ruminant livestock (cattle and sheep, which digest food in the stomach), manure, rice paddies, landfill sites, leaks from natural-gas wells and pipelines, and natural wetlands.
[chem20-s1.8.4] Methane is about 1.9 ppm, more than two and a half times the pre-industrial level of about 0.7 ppm, and its atmospheric lifetime is about 12 years.
[chem20-s1.9.b] Dinitrogen oxide is a long-lived greenhouse gas tied to farming. Ireland's emissions show how these gases link to everyday activities.
[chem20-s1.9.0] Dinitrogen oxide (nitrous oxide, N₂O) comes mainly from nitrogen fertilisers and manure on soils. It is about 0.34 ppm, up from about 0.27 ppm.
[chem20-s1.9.1] N₂O stays in the atmosphere for over 100 years (AR5: about 121 years), which is why it is such a potent, long-lived greenhouse gas.
[chem20-s1.9.2] In Ireland agriculture is the largest single source of greenhouse gas emissions, over a third of the total, mainly methane from cattle and N₂O from fertilisers and manure.
[chem20-s1.9.3] In Ireland energy, transport and heating produce most of the CO₂.
[chem20-s1.10.b] Oxides of nitrogen and sulfur form when fuels burn. They matter in a different way from the main greenhouse gases: one group acts indirectly and the other actually cools the climate.
[chem20-s1.10.0] NOx (nitrogen monoxide, NO, and nitrogen dioxide, NO₂) form when nitrogen and oxygen from air combine at the high temperatures in car engines and power stations: N₂(g) + O₂(g) → 2NO(g).
[chem20-s1.10.1] NO then reacts further with oxygen: 2NO(g) + O₂(g) → 2NO₂(g). Lightning also forms NOx.
[chem20-s1.10.2] NOx are short-lived and are not important direct greenhouse gases. They act indirectly, mainly by helping to form ozone, a greenhouse gas, in the lower atmosphere.
[chem20-s1.10.3] NO₂ also causes acid rain (rain made acidic by dissolved pollutant gases) and harms health.
[chem20-s1.10.4] NOx must not be confused with N₂O, the long-lived greenhouse gas from fertilisers.
[chem20-s1.10.5] Sulfur oxides (SO₂ and SO₃) come from burning coal and oil that contain sulfur, S(s) + O₂(g) → SO₂(g), and from volcanoes.
[chem20-s1.10.6] SO₂ forms tiny sulfate particles (aerosols) and droplets in the air that reflect sunlight, so their overall effect on climate is cooling, not warming.
[chem20-s1.10.7] Sulfur oxides also cause acid rain.
[chem20-s1.11.b] Water vapour is the most abundant greenhouse gas, but it behaves differently from the others because temperature, not human emissions, controls how much of it is in the air.
[chem20-s1.11.0] Water vapour is the most abundant greenhouse gas. Its concentration is controlled by temperature, and it stays in the air for only days.
[chem20-s1.11.1] Because of its short stay in the air, human emissions don't change its concentration directly, and water vapour has no GWP.
[chem20-s1.11.2] Water vapour acts as a feedback (a change that reinforces the original change): warming caused by CO₂ and other gases increases evaporation, and the extra water vapour traps more heat, amplifying the warming.
[chem20-s1.11.3] Warmer air holds about 7% more water vapour for each °C of warming (Clausius–Clapeyron relation).
[chem20-s1.12.b] Several independent kinds of measurement show that greenhouse gases have risen and that the planet has warmed. Together they support the conclusion that human activity is the cause.
[chem20-s1.12.0] Continuous measurements at Mauna Loa, Hawaii, begun by Charles David Keeling in 1958 (the “Keeling curve”), show CO₂ rising every year, from about 315 ppm in 1958 to about 420 ppm.
[chem20-s1.12.1] Ice cores (long cylinders of ice drilled from ice sheets): air bubbles trapped in Antarctic ice show that CO₂ stayed between about 180 and 300 ppm for the 800,000 years before industrialisation.
[chem20-s1.12.2] Today's CO₂ level is far above that range, and the rise matches the growth in fossil-fuel use.
[chem20-s1.12.3] The global average surface temperature in 2011–2020 was about 1.1 °C higher than in 1850–1900 (IPCC AR6), with most of the warming since the 1970s. Recent years have been the warmest on record.
[chem20-s1.12.4] Other observed changes include shrinking glaciers and Arctic sea ice, melting ice sheets and warming oceans.
[chem20-s1.12.5] Sea level rose about 20 cm between 1901 and 2018 (IPCC AR6), from melting ice and the expansion of warmer water.
[chem20-s1.12.6] Climate models (computer simulations of the climate) that include only natural factors (changes in the Sun and volcanoes) do not reproduce the warming since the mid-twentieth century. Models that also include human greenhouse gas emissions do.
[chem20-s1.12.7] The IPCC (AR6) concludes that it is unequivocal that human influence has warmed the atmosphere, ocean and land.
[chem20-s1.13.b] Extra warming changes temperature, rainfall, the oceans and living things. The effects are linked: warmer air holds more water vapour, and the oceans absorb extra CO₂.
[chem20-s1.13.0] Global warming brings higher average temperatures, more frequent heatwaves, melting ice and rising sea levels that threaten low-lying coasts and countries, for example Bangladesh, the Netherlands and small island states.
[chem20-s1.13.1] A warmer atmosphere holds more water vapour, so the water cycle intensifies: heavier rainfall and more flooding in some regions, and longer droughts in others.
[chem20-s1.13.2] More winter rain is expected in Ireland.
[chem20-s1.13.3] Other effects include changes to ecosystems (species moving or becoming extinct, coral bleaching), threats to food and water supplies, and more extreme weather events.
[chem20-s1.14.b] The oceans take up part of the CO₂ humans emit. This slows warming in the air, but the dissolved CO₂ makes the sea water less alkaline.
[chem20-s1.14.0] The oceans absorb about a quarter of the CO₂ emitted by humans.
[chem20-s1.14.1] CO₂(aq) + H₂O(l) ⇌ H₂CO₃(aq) ⇌ H⁺(aq) + HCO₃⁻(aq). The H⁺ concentration rises as CO₂ dissolves, so the pH falls.
[chem20-s1.14.2] The average surface ocean pH has fallen by about 0.1, from about 8.2 to about 8.1, since pre-industrial times.
[chem20-s1.14.3] Ocean acidification lowers the pH but does not make the sea acidic, because the pH stays above 7.
[chem20-s1.14.4] The extra H⁺ reacts with carbonate ions, H⁺ + CO₃²⁻ → HCO₃⁻, leaving less carbonate for shell-building organisms such as oysters, mussels, corals and some plankton.
[chem20-s1.14.5] Acidified water can also dissolve calcium carbonate: CaCO₃(s) + CO₂(aq) + H₂O(l) → Ca²⁺(aq) + 2HCO₃⁻(aq).
[chem20-s1.15.b] Reducing the enhanced greenhouse effect means cutting emissions of greenhouse gases and protecting carbon sinks. Each major source has its own response.
[chem20-s1.15.0] Replace fossil fuels with renewable energy (wind, solar, hydroelectric and tidal), and improve energy efficiency by insulating buildings, using heat pumps and buying efficient appliances.
[chem20-s1.15.1] Transport: use public transport, cycling and walking; use electric vehicles charged from renewable electricity; and use hydrogen fuel cells.
[chem20-s1.15.2] Agriculture: use the right amount of fertiliser at the right time, and use clover and other nitrogen-fixing crops.
[chem20-s1.15.3] Agriculture: breed and feed livestock to produce less methane, and capture methane from manure in anaerobic digesters (tanks where microorganisms break down waste without oxygen, giving biogas) for use as a fuel.
[chem20-s1.15.4] Land use: plant forests, stop deforestation, and protect and rewet peatlands so that they act as carbon sinks again.
[chem20-s1.16.b] Some emissions can be captured or avoided in waste and industry, and governments set targets so that action is shared.
[chem20-s1.16.0] Waste: reduce food waste, recycle, and capture landfill methane and burn it to generate electricity. The CO₂ produced has a much smaller warming effect than the methane would have.
[chem20-s1.16.1] Carbon capture and storage (CCS): CO₂ is captured from power-station or factory flue gases (the waste gases from the chimney), compressed to a liquid, piped away and injected into deep rock formations such as depleted oil and gas fields.
[chem20-s1.16.2] Acid rain can be cut by reducing sulfur and NOx emissions: low-sulfur fuels, removing SO₂ from flue gases, and catalytic converters on cars.
[chem20-s1.16.3] International and national action: the Paris Agreement (2015) aims to keep the rise in global temperature well below 2 °C above pre-industrial levels and to pursue efforts to limit it to 1.5 °C.
[chem20-s1.16.4] Ireland's Climate Action and Low Carbon Development (Amendment) Act 2021 sets a 51% cut in emissions by 2030 (compared with 2018) and climate neutrality by 2050.
[chem20-s1.16.5] A carbon footprint is the total greenhouse gas emissions, as CO₂ equivalent, caused by a person, organisation, product or activity. Individuals can reduce theirs through choices in travel, home heating, diet and consumption.

CANDIDATES chem11-s2: 2. Acid–base theories and strength
[chem11-s2.0.b] The first successful definition of acids and bases came from Svante Arrhenius in 1887, in his theory of electrolytic dissociation. He defined them by the ions they produce when dissolved in water.
[chem11-s2.0.0] An Arrhenius acid is a substance that dissociates (splits into ions) in water to produce hydrogen ions (H⁺): HCl(aq) → H⁺(aq) + Cl⁻(aq).
[chem11-s2.0.1] An Arrhenius base is a substance that dissociates in water to produce hydroxide ions (OH⁻): NaOH(aq) → Na⁺(aq) + OH⁻(aq).
[chem11-s2.0.2] Neutralisation (an acid and a base cancelling each other's properties) is the combination of hydrogen and hydroxide ions to form water: H⁺ + OH⁻ → H₂O.
[chem11-s2.1.b] The Arrhenius theory works well for simple cases like HCl and NaOH, but it breaks down as soon as a substance or reaction does not fit its water-and-ions picture. These gaps are why a wider theory was needed.
[chem11-s2.1.0] It applies only to aqueous solutions (substances dissolved in water), so it says nothing about acids and bases in other solvents.
[chem11-s2.1.1] It cannot explain substances that act as bases but contain no hydroxide, such as ammonia (NH₃) and sodium carbonate, because it only allows a base to produce OH⁻ itself.
[chem11-s2.1.2] It cannot explain acid–base reactions that happen without water, e.g. ammonia gas and hydrogen chloride gas reacting to form ammonium chloride: NH₃(g) + HCl(g) → NH₄Cl(s).
[chem11-s2.1.3] It ignores the role of water: a bare hydrogen ion (a proton) does not exist in water; it bonds to a water molecule to form the hydronium ion, H₃O⁺.
[chem11-s2.2.b] Johannes Brønsted and Thomas Lowry proposed this theory independently in 1923. It defines acids and bases by the transfer of a proton, so it no longer depends on water or on OH⁻.
[chem11-s2.2.0] A proton is the same particle as a hydrogen ion, H⁺ (a hydrogen atom that has lost its electron, leaving just a proton).
[chem11-s2.2.1] A Brønsted–Lowry acid is a proton donor; a Brønsted–Lowry base is a proton acceptor.
[chem11-s2.2.2] An acid–base reaction is the transfer of a proton from an acid to a base.
[chem11-s2.2.3] HCl(g) + H₂O(l) → H₃O⁺(aq) + Cl⁻(aq): HCl donates a proton, so it is the acid, and water accepts it, so it is the base.
[chem11-s2.2.4] NH₃(aq) + H₂O(l) ⇌ NH₄⁺(aq) + OH⁻(aq): ammonia accepts a proton, so it is the base, and water donates one, so it is the acid. The ⇌ arrow means the reaction is reversible.
[chem11-s2.2.5] Water can act as an acid or a base depending on what it reacts with, so it is described as amphoteric.
[chem11-s2.3.b] A theory is more extensive if it explains a wider range of substances and reactions with fewer restrictions. Brønsted–Lowry does this by fixing each of the Arrhenius limitations.
[chem11-s2.3.0] It includes every Arrhenius acid and base, and more: the hydroxide ion is itself a proton acceptor, so it counts as a base.
[chem11-s2.3.1] It explains bases with no OH⁻, such as ammonia, and the carbonate ion, which accepts a proton: CO₃²⁻ + H⁺ → HCO₃⁻.
[chem11-s2.3.2] It applies to reactions without water, such as ammonia reacting with hydrogen chloride in the gas phase.
[chem11-s2.3.3] It explains the role of water as a proton acceptor when it forms H₃O⁺, which the Arrhenius theory cannot describe.
[chem11-s2.3.4] Acid: Arrhenius gives a substance that produces H⁺ in water; Brønsted–Lowry gives a proton donor, so the definition does not depend on water.
[chem11-s2.3.5] Base: Arrhenius gives a substance that produces OH⁻ in water; Brønsted–Lowry gives a proton acceptor, so it covers bases such as ammonia.
[chem11-s2.3.6] Scope: Arrhenius applies only to aqueous solutions; Brønsted–Lowry applies in any solvent (the liquid doing the dissolving) and in the absence of a solvent.
[chem11-s2.4.b] Because a Brønsted–Lowry reaction passes a proton along, the acid and base each turn into a new species that can pass the proton back. These linked species are called conjugates.
[chem11-s2.4.0] When an acid donates a proton, what is left can accept a proton back: it is the conjugate base of that acid.
[chem11-s2.4.1] When a base accepts a proton, the species formed is the conjugate acid of that base.
[chem11-s2.4.2] A conjugate acid–base pair is two species that differ by one proton (H⁺), e.g. HCl and Cl⁻; H₃O⁺ and H₂O; NH₄⁺ and NH₃.
[chem11-s2.4.3] H₃O⁺ and OH⁻ are not a conjugate pair, because they differ by two protons.
[chem11-s2.4.4] Every Brønsted–Lowry reaction has two conjugate pairs: acid₁ + base₂ ⇌ acid₂ + base₁.
[chem11-s2.5.b] To label the acids, bases and conjugate pairs in any equation, follow the movement of the proton. These four steps work for every equation.
[chem11-s2.5.0] 1. For each reactant, check whether it loses or gains a proton in going to the products.
[chem11-s2.5.1] 2. The reactant that loses a proton is the acid; the reactant that gains a proton is the base.
[chem11-s2.5.2] 3. On the product side, the species formed from the base is its conjugate acid, and the species formed from the acid is its conjugate base.
[chem11-s2.5.3] 4. In a reversible reaction (one that can run in both directions, shown by ⇌), the products act as an acid and a base in the reverse reaction.
[chem11-s2.6.b] Each equation below is labelled using the four steps: find which reactant loses a proton, which gains one, then match each to its product. Notice that the same species can be an acid in one equation and a base in another.
[chem11-s2.6.0] HCl + H₂O → H₃O⁺ + Cl⁻: HCl is the acid and H₂O is the base; the pairs are HCl/Cl⁻ and H₃O⁺/H₂O.
[chem11-s2.6.1] NH₃ + H₂O ⇌ NH₄⁺ + OH⁻: the acids are H₂O and NH₄⁺; the bases are NH₃ and OH⁻; the pairs are NH₄⁺/NH₃ and H₂O/OH⁻.
[chem11-s2.6.2] CH₃COOH + H₂O ⇌ CH₃COO⁻ + H₃O⁺: the acids are CH₃COOH and H₃O⁺; the bases are H₂O and CH₃COO⁻; the pairs are CH₃COOH/CH₃COO⁻ and H₃O⁺/H₂O.
[chem11-s2.6.3] HCO₃⁻ + H₂O ⇌ CO₃²⁻ + H₃O⁺: here HCO₃⁻ acts as an acid because it loses a proton; the pairs are HCO₃⁻/CO₃²⁻ and H₃O⁺/H₂O.
[chem11-s2.6.4] HCO₃⁻ + H₃O⁺ ⇌ H₂CO₃ + H₂O: here HCO₃⁻ acts as a base because it gains a proton; the pairs are H₂CO₃/HCO₃⁻ and H₃O⁺/H₂O.
[chem11-s2.6.5] NH₃ + HCl → NH₄⁺ + Cl⁻ (no water involved): NH₃ is the base and HCl is the acid; the pairs are NH₄⁺/NH₃ and HCl/Cl⁻.
[chem11-s2.7.b] An acid and its conjugate base are linked like a see-saw: the stronger one is, the weaker the other. Some species can sit on either side, depending on what they react with.
[chem11-s2.7.0] The stronger an acid, the weaker its conjugate base: HCl is a strong acid, so Cl⁻ is a very weak base that barely accepts protons.
[chem11-s2.7.1] A weak acid has a relatively strong conjugate base: ethanoic acid is a weak acid, and the ethanoate ion (CH₃COO⁻) readily accepts a proton.
[chem11-s2.7.2] Water and the hydrogencarbonate ion are amphoteric (also called amphiprotic): each can act as either an acid or a base, depending on the reaction, as shown in the worked examples above.
[chem11-s2.8.b] Strength describes how far an acid splits into ions (dissociates, or ionises) when it dissolves in water. This decides how many H⁺ ions it releases.
[chem11-s2.8.0] A strong acid is completely dissociated (ionised) into ions in water, shown with a single arrow: HCl(aq) → H⁺(aq) + Cl⁻(aq).
[chem11-s2.8.1] Strong acids include hydrochloric acid (HCl), nitric acid (HNO₃) and sulfuric acid (H₂SO₄).
[chem11-s2.8.2] A weak acid is only slightly dissociated in water: most molecules stay undissociated and an equilibrium (a balance between the forward and reverse reactions) is set up, e.g. CH₃COOH(aq) ⇌ CH₃COO⁻(aq) + H⁺(aq).
[chem11-s2.8.3] Weak acids include ethanoic acid (vinegar), citric acid, carbonic acid (CO₂ in water) and most organic acids (acids built on carbon chains).
[chem11-s2.9.b] Bases have strength in the same way as acids: it depends on how fully they produce hydroxide ions in water.
[chem11-s2.9.0] A strong base is completely dissociated in water, e.g. sodium hydroxide and potassium hydroxide: NaOH(aq) → Na⁺(aq) + OH⁻(aq).
[chem11-s2.9.1] A weak base reacts only slightly with water to produce hydroxide ions, e.g. ammonia: NH₃(aq) + H₂O(l) ⇌ NH₄⁺(aq) + OH⁻(aq).
[chem11-s2.10.b] Concentration describes how much acid or base is present; strength describes how far it dissociates. The two are separate properties.
[chem11-s2.10.0] Concentration is the amount of acid or base dissolved per litre of solution, measured in mol L⁻¹ (moles per litre; a mole is a fixed counting amount of particles).
[chem11-s2.10.1] A concentrated acid has a large amount of acid per litre, e.g. 10 M HCl; a dilute acid has a small amount per litre, e.g. 0.1 M HCl.
[chem11-s2.10.2] A dilute strong acid, e.g. 0.01 M HCl, is still fully dissociated.
[chem11-s2.10.3] A concentrated weak acid, e.g. glacial ethanoic acid, is still only slightly dissociated.
[chem11-s2.11.b] Compare 0.1 M hydrochloric acid with 0.1 M ethanoic acid, which have the same concentration. Any difference in behaviour must then come from strength, not concentration.
[chem11-s2.11.0] pH (a scale measuring how acidic a solution is; lower means more H⁺): 0.1 M hydrochloric acid has a pH of 1.0, while 0.1 M ethanoic acid has a pH of about 2.9, because it produces far fewer H⁺ ions.
[chem11-s2.11.1] Ethanoic acid has an acid dissociation constant (Ka) of 1.8 × 10⁻⁵, a small value showing that it is only slightly dissociated.
[chem11-s2.11.2] Rate of reaction: magnesium ribbon fizzes much faster in 0.1 M hydrochloric acid than in 0.1 M ethanoic acid, because hydrogen ions are present at a higher concentration.
[chem11-s2.11.3] Conductivity: 0.1 M hydrochloric acid conducts electricity much better than 0.1 M ethanoic acid, because it contains more ions to carry the current.
[chem11-s2.11.4] Equal volumes of the two acids at the same concentration neutralise the same amount of sodium hydroxide and give the same total volume of hydrogen with excess magnesium, because the weak acid's equilibrium shifts to release more H⁺ as H⁺ is used up.
[chem11-s2.11.5] Safety: concentrated acids and alkalis are corrosive, so wear goggles and gloves; dilute an acid by adding it to water.

CANDIDATES chem5-s1: 1. Bonding, formulae and shapes
[chem5-s1.0.b] Most atoms are not stable on their own. They join to other atoms because the joined arrangement has less energy, and the force that holds them together is called a chemical bond.
[chem5-s1.0.0] A chemical bond is a force of attraction that holds atoms or ions (atoms that have gained or lost electrons and so carry a charge) together in an element or compound.
[chem5-s1.0.1] Atoms bond to reach a more stable arrangement of electrons, usually a full outer energy level (the outermost shell of electrons) like the nearest noble gas (the unreactive group 18 elements); this is the octet rule, and bonding lowers their energy.
[chem5-s1.0.2] Only the outer (valence) electrons take part in bonding, because the inner electrons are held close to the nucleus (the atom's positively charged centre) and are not available to be lost, gained or shared.
[chem5-s1.1.b] In ionic bonding one atom hands electrons over to another, leaving two oppositely charged particles that attract each other.
[chem5-s1.1.0] An ionic bond is the electrostatic attraction (the attraction between opposite charges) between oppositely charged ions.
[chem5-s1.1.1] It forms when electrons are transferred from one atom, usually a metal that forms a positive ion, to another atom, usually a non-metal that forms a negative ion.
[chem5-s1.1.2] Example: sodium (2,8,1) gives one electron to chlorine (2,8,7): Na → Na⁺ + e⁻ and Cl + e⁻ → Cl⁻. The numbers show the electrons in each energy level.
[chem5-s1.1.3] Both ions have the electron arrangement of a noble gas, which is why the transfer happens: each atom ends up with a full outer energy level.
[chem5-s1.1.4] Ionic compounds form a giant lattice (a huge regular repeating array) of ions, not molecules (separate small groups of atoms).
[chem5-s1.1.5] In sodium chloride each Na⁺ ion is surrounded by six Cl⁻ ions, and each Cl⁻ ion by six Na⁺ ions.
[chem5-s1.1.6] The formula NaCl gives only the ratio of the ions, one Na⁺ to one Cl⁻, because there is no single unit to count.
[chem5-s1.1.7] Diagram: a section of the sodium chloride lattice, drawn as a cube, showing alternating Na⁺ and Cl⁻ ions.
[chem5-s1.2.b] Non-metal atoms usually cannot give electrons away easily, so they share them instead. The shared electrons hold the atoms together.
[chem5-s1.2.0] A covalent bond is a shared pair of electrons between two atoms, usually two non-metals; both nuclei are attracted to the shared pair, which is what holds the atoms together.
[chem5-s1.2.1] A single bond is one shared pair, such as in H–H and Cl–Cl.
[chem5-s1.2.2] A double bond is two shared pairs, as in O=O and C=O in CO₂.
[chem5-s1.2.3] A triple bond is three shared pairs, as in N≡N.
[chem5-s1.2.4] Covalently bonded atoms form molecules (separate small groups of atoms), e.g. H₂O, CH₄ and NH₃.
[chem5-s1.2.5] A few substances have giant covalent structures, in which atoms are linked by covalent bonds throughout instead of forming separate molecules, e.g. diamond.
[chem5-s1.3.b] Not every shared pair is shared fairly. Whether the sharing is equal or unequal depends on how strongly each atom attracts the electrons.
[chem5-s1.3.0] A pure (non-polar) covalent bond shares electrons equally, because both atoms attract them equally, as in H–H and Cl–Cl.
[chem5-s1.3.1] The C–H bond is nearly non-polar.
[chem5-s1.3.2] A polar covalent bond shares electrons unequally, because one atom attracts them more strongly, as in H–Cl and O–H.
[chem5-s1.3.3] The atom that attracts the shared electrons more strongly gets a partial negative charge (δ−), and the other atom gets a partial positive charge (δ+), e.g. Hδ+–Clδ−. The charges are only partial because the electrons are shifted, not fully transferred.
[chem5-s1.4.b] Ionic and covalent bonding are the two ends of a scale, not two separate boxes. Real bonds sit at different points along it, depending on how the electrons are shared.
[chem5-s1.4.0] Bonds are not simply ionic or covalent; they lie on a continuum (a smooth scale) from pure covalent, through polar covalent, to ionic.
[chem5-s1.4.1] At one end, pure covalent bonds share electrons equally, because both atoms attract them equally.
[chem5-s1.4.2] In the middle, polar covalent bonds share electrons unequally, because one atom pulls harder.
[chem5-s1.4.3] At the other end, ionic bonds involve complete transfer of electrons, so ions form.
[chem5-s1.4.4] Examples along the continuum: Cl–Cl (pure covalent), H–Cl (polar covalent), Na⁺Cl⁻ (ionic).
[chem5-s1.4.5] Most bonds have some ionic and some covalent character, so they sit between the ends, not exactly at them.
[chem5-s1.4.6] The difference in electronegativity (how strongly an atom attracts the shared electrons in a bond) between the atoms decides where a bond lies. There is more on this in Bond type and intermolecular forces.
[chem5-s1.4.7] Diagram: a horizontal line labelled pure covalent – polar covalent – ionic, with the electron pair drawn shared equally at the left end, shifted towards one atom in the middle, and fully transferred at the right end.
[chem5-s1.5.b] Setting ionic and covalent bonding side by side shows what each does to the electrons and what kind of structure results. Metals have a third type of bonding.
[chem5-s1.5.0] Ionic bonding transfers electrons and forms ions; covalent bonding shares electrons and forms molecules (mostly).
[chem5-s1.5.1] Ionic bonding occurs between a metal and a non-metal; covalent bonding occurs mostly between two non-metals.
[chem5-s1.5.2] Ionic compounds are giant lattices; covalent bonds are polar or non-polar depending on the atoms involved.
[chem5-s1.5.3] Metallic bonding (assumed): positive metal ions are held together by a sea of delocalised outer electrons (electrons free to move through the whole metal), which explains why metals conduct electricity.
[chem5-s1.6.b] To predict a formula you need to know how many electrons an atom is likely to lose, gain or share. This number is its valency, and the octet rule explains it.
[chem5-s1.6.0] Valency is the number of electrons an atom loses, gains or shares when it bonds; it is the combining power of an element.
[chem5-s1.6.1] The octet rule: atoms tend to lose, gain or share electrons to reach eight electrons in their outer energy level.
[chem5-s1.6.2] Hydrogen follows a different rule: it needs two electrons in its outer level, matching helium (the noble gas with two electrons).
[chem5-s1.6.3] Elements in group 1 and group 17 have a valency of 1, because they lose one electron (group 1) or gain one (group 17) to reach an octet.
[chem5-s1.6.4] Elements in group 2 and group 16 have a valency of 2, because they lose two electrons (group 2) or gain two (group 16).
[chem5-s1.6.5] Elements in group 13 and group 15 have a valency of 3, because they lose three electrons (group 13) or gain three (group 15).
[chem5-s1.6.6] Elements in group 14 have a valency of 4, because they share four electrons to reach an octet.
[chem5-s1.7.b] An ionic compound has no overall charge, so the positive and negative charges must cancel. This gives a simple method for writing any ionic formula.
[chem5-s1.7.0] Step 1: Write the charge on each ion, e.g. Ca²⁺ and Cl⁻.
[chem5-s1.7.1] Step 2: Combine the ions so that the total positive charge equals the total negative charge.
[chem5-s1.7.2] Calcium ions (Ca²⁺) and chloride ions (Cl⁻) give CaCl₂, as one Ca²⁺ balances two Cl⁻.
[chem5-s1.7.3] Aluminium ions (Al³⁺) and oxide ions (O²⁻) give Al₂O₃, as two Al³⁺ balance three O²⁻.
[chem5-s1.7.4] Magnesium ions (Mg²⁺) and nitride ions (N³⁻) give Mg₃N₂, as three Mg²⁺ balance two N³⁻.
[chem5-s1.8.b] Some ions are made of several atoms bonded together that carry one overall charge. They behave as a single unit when you write formulae, so you need to know them.
[chem5-s1.8.0] Negative compound ions: hydroxide OH⁻, nitrate NO₃⁻, hydrogencarbonate HCO₃⁻, carbonate CO₃²⁻, sulfate SO₄²⁻, sulfite SO₃²⁻ and phosphate PO₄³⁻.
[chem5-s1.8.1] Positive compound ion: ammonium NH₄⁺. It is given only as an ion to use in formulae.
[chem5-s1.8.2] Brackets are used round a compound ion when more than one is needed, so that the subscript applies to the whole ion, e.g. Ca(OH)₂ and (NH₄)₂SO₄.
[chem5-s1.9.b] Covalent formulae follow from valency too. Atoms share electron pairs until each has a complete outer level.
[chem5-s1.9.0] Covalent formulae: atoms share enough electron pairs to complete each octet.
[chem5-s1.9.1] Hydrogen (valency 1) and oxygen (valency 2) form H₂O, because two hydrogens are needed to supply the two bonds oxygen can make.
[chem5-s1.9.2] Nitrogen (valency 3) and hydrogen form NH₃, with three hydrogens for nitrogen's three bonds.
[chem5-s1.9.3] Carbon (valency 4) and hydrogen form CH₄, with four hydrogens for carbon's four bonds.
[chem5-s1.9.4] Carbon and oxygen form CO₂, with two double bonds.
[chem5-s1.10.b] A Lewis diagram is a way of drawing bonding that tracks every outer electron. It shows which electrons are transferred, shared or left unused.
[chem5-s1.10.0] A Lewis diagram shows the outer electrons of each atom as dots or crosses; the different symbols let you see which atom each electron came from.
[chem5-s1.10.1] Shared pairs of electrons are drawn between the atoms they join, so you can count the bonds.
[chem5-s1.10.2] Lone pairs are pairs of outer electrons that are not used in bonding.
[chem5-s1.10.3] Ionic compounds: show each ion in square brackets with its charge, e.g. [Na]⁺ [Cl]⁻, with eight electrons around the chloride ion.
[chem5-s1.10.4] Magnesium oxide, an ionic example, is drawn as [Mg]²⁺ [O]²⁻.
[chem5-s1.10.5] Covalent examples to draw: H₂, Cl₂, HCl and H₂O, where water has two lone pairs on oxygen.
[chem5-s1.10.6] Ammonia, NH₃, has one lone pair on nitrogen, because nitrogen has five outer electrons and uses only three in bonds.
[chem5-s1.10.7] O₂ has a double bond, N₂ has a triple bond, and CO₂ has two double bonds.
[chem5-s1.10.8] Diagram: dot-and-cross diagrams of sodium chloride, water and nitrogen, with lone pairs labelled.
[chem5-s1.11.b] Orbitals are the regions of space around a nucleus where an electron is most likely to be found. Covalent bonding can be described as these regions merging.
[chem5-s1.11.0] A covalent bond forms when an orbital of one atom, holding one electron, overlaps an orbital of another atom holding one electron.
[chem5-s1.11.1] The shared pair occupies the overlap region between the two nuclei, which is where both nuclei can attract it.
[chem5-s1.12.b] The way two orbitals overlap decides the type of bond. Overlap can be head-on or sideways, which gives sigma and pi bonds.
[chem5-s1.12.0] A sigma (σ) bond is formed by head-on overlap of orbitals along the line joining the two nuclei.
[chem5-s1.12.1] Sigma bonds are formed by s–s overlap in H₂, s–p overlap in HCl, and end-on p–p overlap in Cl₂.
[chem5-s1.12.2] A pi (π) bond is formed by sideways overlap of two parallel p orbitals, above and below the line joining the nuclei.
[chem5-s1.12.3] Every single bond is a σ bond, so H–H is one σ bond.
[chem5-s1.12.4] A double bond is one σ bond and one π bond; a triple bond is one σ bond and two π bonds, as in N₂.
[chem5-s1.12.5] π bonds are weaker than σ bonds because sideways overlap is less effective.
[chem5-s1.12.6] π bonds prevent rotation about a double bond.
[chem5-s1.12.7] Diagram: head-on overlap of two s orbitals and of two p orbitals (σ); sideways overlap of two p orbitals, showing electron density above and below the axis (π).
[chem5-s1.13.b] Sometimes a bonding pair cannot be pinned between just two atoms. Benzene is the key example, where electrons spread round a whole ring.
[chem5-s1.13.0] In delocalised bonding, electrons are spread over several atoms rather than confined between two.
[chem5-s1.13.1] In benzene (C₆H₆), each carbon uses one p orbital, and the six p orbitals overlap sideways all round the ring.
[chem5-s1.13.2] This forms a ring of delocalised π electrons above and below the plane of the ring.
[chem5-s1.13.3] All six carbon–carbon bonds in benzene are identical, with lengths between those of single and double bonds, because the electrons are shared evenly round the ring. There is more on this in Allotropes of Carbon. Hydrocarbons.
[chem5-s1.14.b] Higher Level students apply the same valency rules past calcium, to the main-group elements up to krypton (element 36).
[chem5-s1.14.0] The same rules apply to elements 21–36 in the main groups.
[chem5-s1.14.1] Potassium bromide is K⁺Br⁻, because group 1 and group 17 both have a valency of 1.
[chem5-s1.14.2] Calcium bromide is CaBr₂, because Ca²⁺ needs two Br⁻ to balance.
[chem5-s1.14.3] Gallium (group 13) forms Ga³⁺ ions, since group 13 has a valency of 3.
[chem5-s1.14.4] Arsenic (group 15) and hydrogen form AsH₃, since group 15 has a valency of 3.
[chem5-s1.14.5] Selenium (group 16) and hydrogen form H₂Se, since group 16 has a valency of 2.
[chem5-s1.15.b] The octet rule and Lewis diagrams are useful but not perfect. Some real molecules break the rule, and the models leave out several properties.
[chem5-s1.15.0] Some molecules are stable with fewer than eight outer electrons on the central atom, e.g. BF₃, with six around boron.
[chem5-s1.15.1] Elements in period 3 and beyond (the third row of the periodic table and below) can have more than eight outer electrons, e.g. PCl₅, with ten around phosphorus.
[chem5-s1.15.2] SF₆ has twelve electrons around sulfur.
[chem5-s1.15.3] The octet rule and Lewis diagrams do not predict the shape of a molecule, how polar a bond is, or bond strength.
[chem5-s1.15.4] They cannot show delocalised bonding, as in benzene.
[chem5-s1.15.5] Predictions from valency assume purely ionic or purely covalent bonding, but real bonds lie on a continuum.
[chem5-s1.16.b] The shape of a molecule follows from a simple idea: electron pairs carry negative charge, so they push each other away. VSEPR turns that idea into a rule for predicting shape.
[chem5-s1.16.0] VSEPR stands for valence shell electron pair repulsion.
[chem5-s1.16.1] The theory applies to molecules of the form ABn (a central atom A bonded to n atoms B), with up to four pairs of electrons around a central atom and single bonds only.
[chem5-s1.16.2] Electron pairs in the outer (valence) shell of the central atom repel each other and arrange themselves as far apart as possible, which fixes the shape of the molecule.
[chem5-s1.16.3] Both bonding pairs and lone pairs count, because both are regions of negative charge that repel.
[chem5-s1.16.4] Lone pairs repel more strongly than bonding pairs: lone pair–lone pair repulsion is greater than lone pair–bond pair repulsion, which is greater than bond pair–bond pair repulsion.
[chem5-s1.16.5] Lone pairs therefore squeeze the bond angles smaller.
[chem5-s1.16.6] The shape is described by the positions of the atoms only, not the lone pairs, so NH₃ (three bonded atoms plus one lone pair) is called pyramidal.
[chem5-s1.17.b] Predicting a shape is a short routine that starts from the Lewis diagram. Follow the same three steps for every molecule.
[chem5-s1.17.0] Step 1: Draw the Lewis (dot-and-cross) diagram of the molecule.
[chem5-s1.17.1] Step 2: Count the electron pairs around the central atom, and how many of them are bonding pairs and how many are lone pairs.
[chem5-s1.17.2] Step 3: Arrange the pairs as far apart as possible, and name the shape from the positions of the atoms, adjusting the angle for lone pairs.
[chem5-s1.18.b] With up to four pairs around the central atom, these five shapes are the ones you need to know. Each depends on how many pairs are bonding and how many are lone.
[chem5-s1.18.0] Two bonding pairs and no lone pairs: linear (a straight line), 180°, e.g. BeCl₂ (gas).
[chem5-s1.18.1] Three bonding pairs and no lone pairs: trigonal planar (three atoms in one flat plane round the centre), 120°, e.g. BF₃.
[chem5-s1.18.2] Four bonding pairs and no lone pairs: tetrahedral (four atoms pointing to the corners of a pyramid-like solid), 109.5°, e.g. CH₄, CCl₄ and SiH₄.
[chem5-s1.18.3] Three bonding pairs and one lone pair: pyramidal (trigonal pyramidal), about 107°, e.g. NH₃. PH₃ is also pyramidal, but its measured angle is much smaller, about 93.5°.
[chem5-s1.18.4] Two bonding pairs and two lone pairs: V-shaped (bent), about 104.5°, e.g. H₂O.
[chem5-s1.18.5] H₂S is also V-shaped, but its measured bond angle is much smaller than water's, about 92°.
[chem5-s1.19.b] Ammonia and water have the same number of electron pairs as methane, yet their bond angles are smaller. Lone pairs are the reason.
[chem5-s1.19.0] NH₃ and H₂O have four electron pairs arranged roughly tetrahedrally, as in CH₄.
[chem5-s1.19.1] The lone pairs push the bonds closer together, because they repel more strongly than bonding pairs, so the bond angle falls as the number of lone pairs rises from 0 to 2: from CH₄ to NH₃ to H₂O.
[chem5-s1.20.b] Molecules are three-dimensional, so a flat drawing needs a convention to show depth. Models let you see the shape directly.
[chem5-s1.20.0] Diagram: 3D shapes drawn with wedges for bonds towards the viewer and dashed lines for bonds away from the viewer, with any lone pairs shown on the central atom.
[chem5-s1.20.1] 3D models: ball-and-stick kits show bond angles, so you can see the shape directly.
[chem5-s1.20.2] Digital models, from molecular-modelling software or apps, can be rotated on screen.
[chem5-s1.20.3] Diagram: the five shapes, each with its example, bond angle and any lone pairs labelled.

CANDIDATES chem21-s5: 5. Combustion and heats of combustion
[chem21-s5.0.b] Burning is the everyday reaction that powers fires, cookers and engines. Chemists define it precisely, and then measure how much heat it gives out per mole of fuel, a quantity called the heat of combustion.
[chem21-s5.0.0] Combustion is the reaction of a substance with oxygen, giving out heat (and usually light). It is always exothermic, which means it releases energy to the surroundings, so the temperature around it rises.
[chem21-s5.0.1] Complete combustion happens in plenty of oxygen: all the carbon becomes carbon dioxide and all the hydrogen becomes water. For hydrocarbons (compounds of only carbon and hydrogen) and alcohols the only products are CO₂ and H₂O.
[chem21-s5.0.2] Incomplete combustion happens in limited oxygen. It also gives carbon monoxide (a toxic gas) and soot (carbon), and releases less energy because less of the fuel is fully oxidised, e.g. 2CH₄(g) + 3O₂(g) → 2CO(g) + 4H₂O(l).
[chem21-s5.0.3] Standard heat of combustion, ΔHc°: the enthalpy change (the heat change at constant pressure) when one mole of a substance is completely burned in excess oxygen under standard conditions (25 °C, 101.3 kPa). It is always negative, because combustion is exothermic.
[chem21-s5.0.4] Because it is per mole of the substance burned, the equation for a heat of combustion has 1 mol of fuel, so fractions of O₂ may be needed, e.g. C₂H₆(g) + 3½O₂(g) → 2CO₂(g) + 3H₂O(l), ΔHc = −1560 kJ mol⁻¹.
[chem21-s5.0.5] The same equation doubled, 2C₂H₆ + 7O₂ → 4CO₂ + 6H₂O, has ΔH = −3120 kJ, because twice as many moles of fuel burn and so twice as much heat is released.
[chem21-s5.1.b] Every complete combustion equation can be balanced by the same five steps. The order matters: carbon first, hydrogen second, and oxygen last, because oxygen appears in more than one product.
[chem21-s5.1.0] Step 1: write the formula of the fuel and the products CO₂ and H₂O.
[chem21-s5.1.1] Step 2: balance carbon. The number of CO₂ equals the number of C atoms in the fuel, since each CO₂ holds one carbon.
[chem21-s5.1.2] Step 3: balance hydrogen. The number of H₂O equals half the number of H atoms in the fuel, since each H₂O holds two hydrogens.
[chem21-s5.1.3] Step 4: balance oxygen last. Count the O atoms in the products, subtract any O already in the fuel (one for an alcohol), and divide by two for the number of O₂, since each O₂ molecule holds two O atoms.
[chem21-s5.1.4] Step 5: double everything if you want whole numbers, for example to remove a ½ in front of O₂.
[chem21-s5.1.5] Check: count each type of atom on both sides, and add state symbols (fuel, O₂(g), CO₂(g), H₂O(l) under standard conditions). The (l) for water is because standard heats of combustion are measured with water as a liquid.
[chem21-s5.2.b] Each family of compounds follows a pattern, so one general equation covers every member. Here n is the number of carbon atoms in the molecule. The range examined is hydrocarbons with no more than one double or triple bond, and primary alcohols (alcohols with the OH group on a carbon at the end of the chain) up to C6.
[chem21-s5.2.0] Alkanes (hydrocarbons with only single bonds): CₙH₂ₙ₊₂ + ((3n + 1)/2) O₂ → nCO₂ + (n + 1)H₂O.
[chem21-s5.2.1] Alkenes (one C=C, a carbon-carbon double bond): CₙH₂ₙ + (3n/2) O₂ → nCO₂ + nH₂O.
[chem21-s5.2.2] Alkynes (one C≡C, a carbon-carbon triple bond): CₙH₂ₙ₋₂ + ((3n − 1)/2) O₂ → nCO₂ + (n − 1)H₂O.
[chem21-s5.2.3] Primary alcohols: CₙH₂ₙ₊₁OH + (3n/2) O₂ → nCO₂ + (n + 1)H₂O.
[chem21-s5.3.b] These are the complete combustion equations for the alkanes, alkenes and alkyne you may be asked for, with water as a liquid. Where the fuel needs a half-molecule of oxygen, the doubled form gives whole numbers.
[chem21-s5.3.0] Methane: CH₄(g) + 2O₂(g) → CO₂(g) + 2H₂O(l).
[chem21-s5.3.1] Propane: C₃H₈(g) + 5O₂(g) → 3CO₂(g) + 4H₂O(l), because 3 carbons give 3 CO₂ and 8 hydrogens give 4 H₂O.
[chem21-s5.3.2] Butane: C₄H₁₀(g) + 6½O₂(g) → 4CO₂(g) + 5H₂O(l), or 2C₄H₁₀(g) + 13O₂(g) → 8CO₂(g) + 10H₂O(l).
[chem21-s5.3.3] Ethene: C₂H₄(g) + 3O₂(g) → 2CO₂(g) + 2H₂O(l).
[chem21-s5.3.4] Propene: C₃H₆(g) + 4½O₂(g) → 3CO₂(g) + 3H₂O(l).
[chem21-s5.3.5] Ethyne: C₂H₂(g) + 2½O₂(g) → 2CO₂(g) + H₂O(l), or 2C₂H₂(g) + 5O₂(g) → 4CO₂(g) + 2H₂O(l).
[chem21-s5.4.b] Each alcohol already contains one oxygen atom in its OH group, so it needs less O₂ from the air than the alkane with the same number of carbons. Step 4 above (subtract the O in the fuel) is what gives these numbers.
[chem21-s5.4.0] Methanol: CH₃OH(l) + 1½O₂(g) → CO₂(g) + 2H₂O(l), or 2CH₃OH(l) + 3O₂(g) → 2CO₂(g) + 4H₂O(l).
[chem21-s5.4.1] Ethanol: C₂H₅OH(l) + 3O₂(g) → 2CO₂(g) + 3H₂O(l), because the products hold 7 oxygen atoms, one of which comes from the ethanol, so 6 atoms (3 O₂) come from the air.
[chem21-s5.4.2] Propan-1-ol: C₃H₇OH(l) + 4½O₂(g) → 3CO₂(g) + 4H₂O(l).
[chem21-s5.4.3] Butan-1-ol: C₄H₉OH(l) + 6O₂(g) → 4CO₂(g) + 5H₂O(l).
[chem21-s5.4.4] Pentan-1-ol: C₅H₁₁OH(l) + 7½O₂(g) → 5CO₂(g) + 6H₂O(l).
[chem21-s5.4.5] Hexan-1-ol: C₆H₁₃OH(l) + 9O₂(g) → 6CO₂(g) + 7H₂O(l).
[chem21-s5.5.b] These are the data values you are given and must be able to use to spot trends. Values are in kJ mol⁻¹ (kilojoules per mole) at 25 °C, with water as a liquid. All are negative because combustion is exothermic.
[chem21-s5.5.0] Alkanes: methane −890; ethane −1560; propane −2220; butane −2877. For example, each is more negative than the one before it.
[chem21-s5.5.1] Primary alcohols: methanol −726; ethanol −1367; propan-1-ol −2021; butan-1-ol −2676; pentan-1-ol −3331; hexan-1-ol −3984.
[chem21-s5.5.2] Two-carbon compounds: ethane −1560; ethene −1411; ethyne −1300; ethanol −1367.
[chem21-s5.5.3] Three-carbon compounds: propane −2220; propene −2058.
[chem21-s5.6.b] In a homologous series (a family of compounds in which each member differs from the next by one CH₂ group), the heat of combustion changes in a very regular way. The reason lies in the bonds broken and formed for each extra CH₂.
[chem21-s5.6.0] ΔHc becomes more negative as the number of carbon atoms increases, by an almost constant amount for each extra CH₂, so more heat is released per mole of a longer molecule.
[chem21-s5.6.1] For alkanes the increase is about 650–670 kJ mol⁻¹, e.g. 1560 − 890 = 670 and 2877 − 2220 = 657.
[chem21-s5.6.2] For primary alcohols the increase is about 640–655 kJ mol⁻¹, e.g. 1367 − 726 = 641 and 2021 − 1367 = 654.
[chem21-s5.6.3] Each extra CH₂ group adds one C–C and two C–H bonds to break, and needs 1½ more O₂ molecules. It forms one more CO₂ (two C=O bonds) and one more H₂O (two O–H bonds).
[chem21-s5.6.4] More energy is released by the new bonds formed than is needed to break the extra bonds, so each extra CH₂ makes ΔHc more negative.
[chem21-s5.6.5] Because exactly the same extra bonds are broken and formed for every CH₂ added, the increase is nearly constant, so a graph of ΔHc against number of carbon atoms is a straight line.
[chem21-s5.7.b] Bond enthalpies (the energy needed to break one mole of a given bond) let you put a number on the increase per CH₂. The regular straight-line trend also lets you predict a missing value.
[chem21-s5.7.0] Bond enthalpy estimate per CH₂, using C–C 348, C–H 412, O=O 498, C=O 799 and O–H 463 kJ mol⁻¹: broken 348 + 2(412) + 1½(498) = 1919 kJ; formed 2(799) + 2(463) = 2524 kJ; difference −605 kJ.
[chem21-s5.7.1] This estimate is for gaseous water. The measured increase is larger because the water formed is liquid, which means extra heat is released when the vapour condenses.
[chem21-s5.7.2] The straight-line trend can be used to predict values, e.g. hexan-1-ol ≈ −3331 − 655 = −3986 kJ mol⁻¹, close to the data value of −3984 kJ mol⁻¹.
[chem21-s5.8.b] An alcohol and the alkane with the same number of carbon atoms give the same CO₂ and H₂O, yet the alcohol releases less heat. The difference comes from the oxygen already inside the alcohol.
[chem21-s5.8.0] A primary alcohol has a less negative ΔHc than the alkane with the same number of carbon atoms, e.g. ethanol −1367 against ethane −1560 kJ mol⁻¹, and methanol −726 against methane −890 kJ mol⁻¹.
[chem21-s5.8.1] The alcohol molecule already contains an oxygen atom bonded to carbon and hydrogen (C–O–H), so it is already partly oxidised (it has already combined with some oxygen).
[chem21-s5.8.2] It therefore needs less oxygen (ethanol 3 O₂, ethane 3½ O₂), and less energy is released on complete oxidation, although the same numbers of CO₂ and H₂O are formed.
[chem21-s5.9.b] Comparing compounds with the same number of carbon atoms but different numbers of hydrogens shows a second pattern. Fewer hydrogens means less water formed.
[chem21-s5.9.0] For the same number of carbon atoms, ΔHc becomes less negative in the order alkane, then alkene, then alkyne: ethane −1560, ethene −1411, ethyne −1300 kJ mol⁻¹.
[chem21-s5.9.1] The alkene has two fewer hydrogen atoms than the alkane, and the alkyne four fewer, so fewer water molecules (fewer O–H bonds) are formed and less energy is released.
[chem21-s5.9.2] The main reason is the fewer O–H bonds formed, not the C=C bond being harder to break.
[chem21-s5.10.b] You can estimate a heat of combustion in the lab by burning an alcohol under a can of water and measuring how much the water warms up. This investigation uses primary alcohols to test how heat of combustion changes along a series.
[chem21-s5.10.0] Aim: to estimate the heats of combustion of a series of primary alcohols (methanol, ethanol, propan-1-ol and butan-1-ol) using spirit burners (small lamps that burn liquid alcohol from a wick), and to compare them with standard values.
[chem21-s5.10.1] Heat of combustion: the enthalpy change when one mole of a substance is completely burned in excess oxygen.
[chem21-s5.10.2] Principle: the heat released by burning a measured mass of alcohol is absorbed by a known mass of water, so Q = m(water) × c × ΔT.
[chem21-s5.10.3] Here c is the specific heat capacity of water (the heat needed to warm a given mass by one degree) and ΔT is the temperature rise, so Q is the heat the water has gained.
[chem21-s5.10.4] ΔHc = −Q ÷ moles of alcohol burned. The negative sign is there because heat released by the burning alcohol means a negative enthalpy change.
[chem21-s5.10.5] Hypothesis (the prediction being tested): the heat of combustion becomes more negative by a roughly constant amount for each extra CH₂ in the alcohol.
[chem21-s5.11.b] For a fair test, only one thing is changed, one thing is measured, and everything else is kept the same.
[chem21-s5.11.0] Independent variable (the one you change): the alcohol, i.e. the number of carbon atoms.
[chem21-s5.11.1] Dependent variable (the one you measure): the heat released per mole, calculated from the mass of alcohol burned and the temperature rise of the water.
[chem21-s5.11.2] Controlled (kept the same): the same volume (mass) of water, at the same starting temperature, so that the same heat gives the same temperature rise each time.
[chem21-s5.11.3] Controlled: the same copper can, the same distance from wick to can, and the same wick length, so that the share of heat reaching the can is similar for every alcohol.
[chem21-s5.11.4] Controlled: the same temperature rise (e.g. 20 °C), and the draught shield in the same position.
[chem21-s5.12.b] The method is the same for every alcohol: heat a fixed mass of water with the burner, and find out from the change in the burner's mass how much alcohol was burned.
[chem21-s5.12.0] Step 1: measure 100 cm³ (100 g) of water with a graduated cylinder into a copper can (calorimeter, a container used to measure heat changes).
[chem21-s5.12.1] Step 2: clamp the can a fixed distance (about 2–3 cm) above the wick of the spirit burner, and surround both with a draught shield.
[chem21-s5.12.2] Step 3: weigh the spirit burner containing the alcohol, with its cap on, on a balance reading to 0.01 g.
[chem21-s5.12.3] Step 4: record the initial temperature of the water.
[chem21-s5.12.4] Step 5: light the burner and stir the water gently with the thermometer or a stirrer.
[chem21-s5.12.5] Step 6: when the temperature has risen by about 20 °C, put out the flame by replacing the cap, and record the highest temperature reached.
[chem21-s5.12.6] Step 7: reweigh the burner with its cap at once. The mass burned is the initial mass minus the final mass.
[chem21-s5.12.7] Step 8: repeat with each alcohol, using fresh water each time, and repeat each alcohol to check reproducibility (that you get similar results each time).
[chem21-s5.13.b] The apparatus has several parts, and each one has a job in the method, so know every part and where it sits.
[chem21-s5.13.0] Diagram: a copper can of water held by a clamp above a spirit burner, a thermometer and stirrer in the water, a draught shield around the burner and can, the burner on a heat-proof mat, and a balance for weighing the burner.
[chem21-s5.14.b] Every feature of the set-up either gets more of the heat into the water or keeps the measurement of the alcohol burned accurate.
[chem21-s5.14.0] Copper is a good conductor of heat, so heat passes quickly from the flame into the water.
[chem21-s5.14.1] The draught shield reduces heat lost by air currents and keeps the flame steady.
[chem21-s5.14.2] Capping the burner as soon as the flame is out, and weighing immediately, stops the volatile alcohol (one that evaporates easily) evaporating from the wick, which would otherwise be counted as burned.
[chem21-s5.14.3] Stirring spreads the heat evenly, so the thermometer reading represents all the water.
[chem21-s5.14.4] The same temperature rise and set-up for each alcohol make the heat losses similar, so the comparison between alcohols is fair even if the absolute values are low.
[chem21-s5.14.5] A temperature rise of about 20 °C is large enough to measure accurately, but not so large that heat losses, which increase as the water gets hotter, become very large.
[chem21-s5.15.b] The calculation has two parts: the heat absorbed by the water, then that heat divided by the moles of alcohol burned. Here it is for ethanol, using sample data.
[chem21-s5.15.0] For each alcohol: 100 g (0.100 kg) of water heated through 20.0 °C gives Q = 0.100 × 4.18 × 20.0 = 8.36 kJ, using c(water) = 4.18 kJ kg⁻¹ K⁻¹.
[chem21-s5.15.1] Ethanol (M = 46 g mol⁻¹, the molar mass): mass burned 0.63 g, so moles = 0.63 ÷ 46 = 0.0137 mol.
[chem21-s5.15.2] Heat per mole = 8.36 ÷ (0.63 ÷ 46) = 610 kJ mol⁻¹, so ΔHc(experimental) = −610 kJ mol⁻¹ to 3 significant figures.
[chem21-s5.15.3] Percentage error = (standard − experimental) ÷ standard × 100, e.g. ethanol (1367 − 610) ÷ 1367 × 100 = 55%.
[chem21-s5.16.b] Repeating the calculation for all four alcohols gives a set of experimental values to compare with the standard ones.
[chem21-s5.16.0] Methanol (M = 32 g mol⁻¹): 0.82 g burned, giving ΔHc = −326 kJ mol⁻¹.
[chem21-s5.16.1] Propan-1-ol (M = 60 g mol⁻¹): 0.55 g burned, giving ΔHc = −912 kJ mol⁻¹.
[chem21-s5.16.2] Butan-1-ol (M = 74 g mol⁻¹): 0.51 g burned, giving ΔHc = −1210 kJ mol⁻¹ to 3 significant figures.
[chem21-s5.16.3] Standard values are −726, −1367, −2021 and −2676 kJ mol⁻¹; each experimental value is about 45% of the standard value.
[chem21-s5.16.4] A graph of experimental ΔHc against number of carbon atoms is a straight line, like the standard values but less steep, so the trend is confirmed even though the values are too low.
[chem21-s5.17.b] The experimental values come out far below the standard ones. A good answer says why each error makes the value too low, not just that it is a problem.
[chem21-s5.17.0] Heat loss: much of the heat from the flame goes to the surrounding air rather than into the water; this is the main reason the values are much less negative than standard values.
[chem21-s5.17.1] Heat absorbed by the copper can, thermometer and clamp is not included in Q, so less heat is measured in the water and the value is too small. It could be added as m(copper) × c(copper) × ΔT, with c(copper) ≈ 0.385 J g⁻¹ K⁻¹.
[chem21-s5.17.2] Incomplete combustion: with limited air at the wick, some carbon forms soot, seen on the base of the can, and carbon monoxide, releasing less energy than complete combustion.
[chem21-s5.17.3] Evaporation of alcohol from the wick: alcohol lost without burning is counted as burned, making the heat per mole too small.
[chem21-s5.17.4] Non-standard conditions: standard values assume products at 25 °C with water as a liquid; in the experiment the water leaves as vapour, so its heat of condensation is not released into the can.
[chem21-s5.17.5] Improvements to reduce heat loss: insulate the can and add a lid, reduce the distance between flame and can, and use a draught shield.
[chem21-s5.17.6] Improvements to reduce other errors: ensure a good air supply for complete combustion, cap and weigh the burner immediately, and include the heat absorbed by the can.
[chem21-s5.17.7] Accurate values are measured in a sealed calorimeter, which is not required here.
[chem21-s5.18.b] Alcohols burn easily and methanol is poisonous, so the main precautions are about fire and fumes.
[chem21-s5.18.0] Alcohols are highly flammable: keep bottles stoppered and away from flames, never refill a burner while it is lit or hot, and refill in a fume cupboard or away from flames.
[chem21-s5.18.1] Methanol is toxic by swallowing, by skin contact and by inhaling its vapour; avoid skin contact and work in a well-ventilated room.
[chem21-s5.18.2] Wear safety glasses, tie back long hair, stand the burner on a heat-proof mat, and let the copper can cool before handling it.
