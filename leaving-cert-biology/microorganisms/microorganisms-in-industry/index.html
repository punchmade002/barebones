Why microorganisms are used in industry

  • Biotechnology is the use of living organisms or their parts, such as cells and enzymes, to make useful products or provide services.
  • Microorganisms are the most used organisms in biotechnology.
  • Microorganisms reproduce very rapidly: some bacteria divide every 20 minutes in ideal conditions, so large amounts of product are made quickly.
  • They can be grown on cheap nutrients, including waste materials from other industries, e.g. whey from cheese-making and molasses from sugar refining.
  • They can be grown in any climate or season in a controlled vessel that takes little space.
  • They carry out a huge range of chemical reactions at low temperatures and normal pressure, saving energy compared with chemical manufacture.
  • They are easy to modify genetically, so they can be made to produce human proteins (see “Genetic engineering”).
  • Many secrete their products into the medium, which makes the products easier to collect.
  • Products are either the cells themselves (e.g. yeast for baking, mycoprotein for food), substances the cells make (e.g. ethanol, antibiotics, enzymes, insulin), or changes the cells make to a material (e.g. milk to yogurt, slurry to biogas, sewage treatment).

Bioreactors

  • A bioreactor, or fermenter, is a vessel in which microorganisms, cells or enzymes are grown or used on a large scale under carefully controlled conditions to make a product (enzyme bioreactors are in “Industrial enzymes”).
  • The vessel is made of stainless steel: it is strong, does not corrode, and is easy to sterilise with steam.
  • Inlets allow sterile nutrient medium and the inoculum, a pure starter culture, to enter the vessel.
  • A sterile air supply is bubbled in at the bottom through a sparger, giving oxygen to aerobic microorganisms; the air is filtered so that no contaminating microorganisms enter.
  • A motor-driven stirrer (impeller) keeps the cells, nutrients and oxygen evenly mixed and spreads heat.
  • A water jacket, or cooling coils, with cold water flowing through it removes the heat released by the microorganisms' respiration, keeping the temperature at the optimum.
  • Probes monitor temperature, pH, dissolved oxygen and foam level, and are linked to a computer.
  • Inlets for acid or alkali correct the pH.
  • A gas outlet releases CO₂ and excess air.
  • An outlet at the base, the harvest tap, removes the culture and product.
  • Before use, the vessel, pipes and medium are sterilised with steam, so that no contaminating microorganisms compete with the culture, use up nutrients or spoil the product.
  • This is aseptic technique on an industrial scale.
  • Downstream processing: the product is separated from the cells and medium by filtering or centrifuging, then purified, concentrated and packaged.
  • If the product is inside the cells, the cells are first broken open.
  • Diagram a labelled bioreactor showing the vessel, nutrient and inoculum inlets, sterile air inlet and sparger, stirrer and motor, water jacket with cold water in and warm water out, temperature, pH and oxygen probes, acid/alkali inlet, gas outlet and harvest outlet.

Pharmaceutical industry

  • Antibiotics are chemicals made by microorganisms that kill or inhibit bacteria.
  • Penicillin is made by the fungus Penicillium; Alexander Fleming discovered its effect in 1928, and Howard Florey and Ernst Chain purified it and showed it cured infections from 1940 (see “Science, scientists and society”).
  • Penicillium is now grown in bioreactors, using strains selected and mutated to give far more penicillin than the original.
  • Streptomycin, tetracycline and many other antibiotics are made by soil bacteria of the genus Streptomyces.
  • The gene for human insulin is inserted into bacteria (E. coli) or yeast, which are grown in bioreactors; the insulin is then extracted and purified.
  • Human insulin was the first genetically engineered medicine approved for use, in 1982.
  • Before that, people with diabetes injected insulin extracted from the pancreases of pigs and cattle, which was in shorter supply and caused allergic reactions in some people (see “Genetic engineering”).
  • Human growth hormone, for children who make too little, is made by genetically modified bacteria.
  • The hepatitis B vaccine is made by genetically modified yeast, which produces a hepatitis B virus surface protein (the antigen) without the virus.
  • Other vaccines contain killed or weakened microorganisms grown in culture (see “Acquired immunity, prevention and treatment”).

Medical uses

  • Diagnosis: the enzyme glucose oxidase, made by the fungus Aspergillus niger, is used in the test strips with which people with diabetes measure their blood glucose.
  • Diagnosis: Taq polymerase, a heat-stable enzyme from the bacterium Thermus aquaticus, which lives in hot springs, is used in PCR.
  • PCR detects pathogens, e.g. in COVID-19 tests, and is used in DNA profiling (see “Generating a DNA profile”).
  • Treatment: faecal microbiota transplant, in which gut microorganisms from a healthy donor are placed in a patient's gut, is used to treat repeated infections with the bacterium Clostridioides difficile that antibiotics have failed to clear (see “The microbiome”).
  • Treatment: probiotics, live microorganisms taken as food or supplements, are used to try to restore gut microorganisms after antibiotics; the evidence is mixed (see “The microbiome”).
  • Treatment: the toxin of the bacterium Clostridium botulinum, in tiny purified doses (botulinum toxin, “Botox”), relaxes overactive muscles and is used to treat some muscle spasms and other conditions.
  • Medicines and vaccines are made in the pharmaceutical industry, so the medical uses here are those in diagnosis and treatment in hospitals and clinics.

Agricultural industry

  • Nitrogen fixation: seeds of legumes such as clover, peas and beans can be coated with nitrogen-fixing bacteria (Rhizobium) before sowing.
  • The crop then forms root nodules and needs less nitrogen fertiliser (mutualism in “Biotic factors and relationships”).
  • Biological pest control: the soil bacterium Bacillus thuringiensis (Bt) makes a protein that is toxic to the larvae of certain insects, e.g. caterpillars, but harmless to humans and most other animals.
  • Bt is sprayed on crops as a biopesticide.
  • The Bt toxin gene has been put into some genetically modified crops, e.g. Bt maize, which then make their own insecticide.
  • Genetic modification of plants: the soil bacterium Agrobacterium tumefaciens naturally transfers part of a plasmid into plant cells.
  • Biotechnologists use Agrobacterium as a vector to insert useful genes into crop plants (see “Genetic engineering”).
  • Silage: grass stored airtight, in a pit covered with plastic or in wrapped bales, is fermented by lactic acid bacteria, which convert its sugars to lactic acid.
  • The low pH stops other microorganisms rotting the silage, so it keeps as winter feed for cattle and sheep; silage-making is widespread on Irish farms.
  • Anaerobic digestion: animal slurry, food waste and crop waste are broken down by microorganisms in sealed tanks without oxygen.
  • Methane-producing microorganisms (archaea) produce biogas, which is mainly methane and is burned for heat or electricity; the remaining digestate is spread as fertiliser.
  • Composting: aerobic decomposition of plant waste by bacteria and fungi produces compost, which returns humus and minerals to soil.

Food production industry

  • Bread: baker's yeast (Saccharomyces cerevisiae) ferments sugars in dough, producing CO₂, which makes the dough rise.
  • The ethanol evaporates during baking (see “Respiration as a catabolic process”).
  • Beer and wine: yeast ferments the sugars in malted barley (beer) or grape juice (wine) to ethanol and CO₂, under anaerobic conditions.
  • Yogurt: milk is pasteurised, cooled and inoculated with lactic acid bacteria, e.g. Lactobacillus and Streptococcus species.
  • The inoculated milk is incubated warm, at about 40–45 °C, for several hours.
  • The bacteria ferment lactose to lactic acid, which lowers the pH, making the milk proteins thicken and giving the sour taste.
  • The low pH also preserves the yogurt.
  • Cheese: lactic acid bacteria sour the milk, and rennet clots it into curds; chymosin, the main ingredient of rennet, is now mostly made by genetically modified microorganisms (see “Industrial enzymes”).
  • Bacteria and moulds ripen the cheese and develop its flavour; the blue veins of blue cheeses are the mould Penicillium roqueforti.
  • Mycoprotein: the fungus Fusarium venenatum is grown in large continuous fermenters on glucose and mineral salts.
  • Its hyphae are harvested, heated and pressed to make mycoprotein, sold as Quorn, a high-protein, high-fibre, low-fat meat substitute.
  • Vinegar is made when bacteria (Acetobacter) oxidise the ethanol in wine, cider or beer to acetic (ethanoic) acid.
  • Soy sauce is made by fermenting soya beans and wheat with the mould Aspergillus oryzae.
  • Citric acid, a flavouring and preservative in soft drinks, is made by the mould Aspergillus niger.

Other industries

  • Sewage treatment: in the secondary stage, aerobic bacteria and protists in aerated tanks (activated sludge) feed on the organic matter in sewage, so the water can be returned to rivers without causing oxygen depletion; the sludge can be anaerobically digested to biogas.
  • Bioremediation: microorganisms that break down pollutants, e.g. bacteria that digest oil, are used to clean up oil spills and contaminated land.
  • Biofuels: yeast ferments sugars from crops, e.g. sugar cane or maize, to ethanol, which is blended with petrol (see “Climate change and biological strategies”).

Discussing the importance of microorganisms in industry

  • Health, for: antibiotics have made most bacterial infections curable and have made surgery much safer.
  • Health, for: insulin, vaccines and other medicines from microorganisms are cheap, pure and available in large amounts.
  • Health, for: human insulin from microorganisms avoids reliance on animal pancreases and the reactions some people had to animal insulin.
  • Health, against: overuse of antibiotics in medicine and in farm animals has selected for resistant bacteria, so some antibiotics no longer work (see “Emergence and spread of infectious disease”).
  • Health, against: new antibiotics are expensive and slow to develop.
  • Food and agriculture, for: fermentation preserves food and creates many foods.
  • Food and agriculture, for: mycoprotein and other microbial protein need far less land and water than livestock.
  • Food and agriculture, for: biopesticides and Rhizobium inoculants reduce the use of chemical pesticides and fertilisers, and silage gives winter feed.
  • Food and agriculture, against: genetically modified microorganisms and crops raise concerns about safety, the environment and who controls the technology (see “Ethics of genetic technologies”).
  • Food and agriculture, against: insects can evolve resistance to the Bt toxin, and some people object to GM ingredients.
  • Environment and economy, for: microorganisms turn wastes, such as whey, slurry, sewage and food waste, into useful products, reducing pollution.
  • Environment and economy, for: biogas and bioethanol are renewable energy, and biological processes run at low temperatures, using less energy than chemical manufacture.
  • Environment and economy, for: biotechnology and pharmaceutical manufacturing are major employers and exporters in Ireland.
  • Environment and economy, against: bioreactors need sterile conditions, energy for stirring and cooling, and careful disposal of waste culture.
  • Environment and economy, against: contamination of a batch can waste the whole product, and growing crops for biofuel uses land that could grow food.
  • Judgement: on balance, microorganisms are essential to modern medicine, food production and waste treatment, and their importance is growing as biotechnology replaces fossil-fuel-based chemical processes (see “The cross-cutting themes” on health, sustainability and technology).
  • This judgement holds provided that antibiotic use and genetic modification are regulated.

The growth curve and its phases (Higher Level only)

  • A growth curve shows how the number of living cells in a population of microorganisms changes over time when they are grown in a closed system: a fixed volume of medium, with no nutrients added and no wastes removed (batch culture).
  • The number of cells is usually plotted as a logarithm, log₁₀ of the number of cells, because numbers rise from thousands to billions; on a log scale the exponential phase is a straight line.
  • Diagram a growth curve with log number of living cells on the y-axis and time on the x-axis, with the five phases labelled in order: lag, log (exponential), stationary, decline (death) and survival.
  • Lag phase: little or no increase in cell number.
  • During the lag phase the cells adjust to the new medium: they take in water and nutrients, switch on genes, make the enzymes needed to use the nutrients present, and grow in size before dividing.
  • The lag phase is longer if the new conditions differ greatly from those the cells came from.
  • Log (exponential) phase: the cells divide at their maximum rate, as nutrients are plentiful and wastes are low.
  • The number doubles in each generation time, e.g. every 20–30 minutes, so the population grows exponentially.
  • This is when the cells grow fastest and make most of the products linked to growth, the primary metabolites (see primary metabolites below).
  • Stationary phase: the rate of cell division equals the rate of cell death, so the number of living cells stays constant.
  • The stationary phase is caused by nutrients running out, toxic waste products such as acids and ethanol building up, oxygen possibly running short, and changes in pH.
  • Many microorganisms make secondary metabolites, such as antibiotics, in this phase; some bacteria form endospores.
  • Decline (death) phase: the death rate is greater than the rate of division, so the number of living cells falls, because nutrients are exhausted and wastes are at toxic levels.
  • Dead cells may break open in the decline phase.
  • Survival phase: a small number of cells survive for a long time, with the number falling only slowly or levelling off.
  • Cells survive the survival phase by forming endospores (in some bacteria), by becoming inactive, or by using nutrients released from dead cells that have broken open.

Growth-curve calculations (Higher Level only)

  • Number of cells after n generations of the log phase = starting number × 2ⁿ.
  • Worked example: one bacterium dividing every 20 minutes, for 2 hours: n = 120 ÷ 20 = 6 generations; number = 1 × 2⁶ = 64 cells.
  • Worked example: with 1000 starting cells and a generation time of 30 minutes, after 3 hours: n = 6, number = 1000 × 64 = 64 000.
  • Working out generation time from a graph or table: in the log phase the number rose from 1 × 10⁴ to 8 × 10⁴ cells in 90 minutes.
  • 8 = 2³, so there were 3 generations in 90 minutes; generation time = 90 ÷ 3 = 30 minutes.
  • Reading a log-scale graph: each step of 1 on a log₁₀ axis is a tenfold increase, e.g. from log 4 = 10 000 cells to log 5 = 100 000 cells.

Batch and continuous culture and increasing yields (Higher Level only)

  • Batch culture: the bioreactor is filled with a fixed amount of sterile medium, inoculated, and left to grow for a set time, with temperature, pH and oxygen controlled.
  • In batch culture nothing is added except air, acid or alkali, and nothing is removed until the end, when the whole contents are harvested.
  • The vessel is then emptied, cleaned and sterilised for the next batch, and the population passes through the lag, log and stationary phases.
  • Batch culture advantages: it is simpler to set up and control, and if a batch is contaminated only that batch is lost; it is best for products made in the stationary phase.
  • Batch culture disadvantages: the vessel is idle while being emptied, cleaned and refilled, and for much of each run the cells are not in the log phase, so output per vessel is lower.
  • Primary metabolites are substances the cells make as part of normal growth, mostly in the log phase, e.g. ethanol by yeast, amino acids, enzymes, and the cells themselves as mycoprotein.
  • Secondary metabolites are not needed for growth and are made mainly in the stationary phase, e.g. the antibiotic penicillin.
  • Continuous culture: fresh sterile nutrient medium is added continuously, and culture (cells, medium and product) is removed at the same rate, so the volume stays constant.
  • Nutrients never run out and wastes do not build up, so the population is held in the log phase for weeks.
  • Continuous culture advantages: high output from a smaller vessel, no idle time between batches, and a steady, uniform product.
  • Continuous culture disadvantages: it is harder to control; if contamination enters, a long run is lost; mutations can arise over many generations; and it suits only products made during growth, e.g. mycoprotein.
  • Shorten the lag phase by inoculating with a large volume of actively growing cells taken from the log phase of a culture grown in the same medium and at the same temperature, so the cells do not need to adjust.
  • Keep the log phase going as long as possible by holding temperature, pH and oxygen at the optimum with the probes, and by supplying fresh nutrients and removing wastes (continuous culture, for primary metabolites).
  • Harvest at the right phase: primary metabolites at the end of the log phase or continuously, and secondary metabolites, e.g. penicillin, in batch culture during the stationary phase, before the decline phase reduces yield.
  • Delay the stationary and decline phases by removing toxic products, e.g. ethanol, or by adding nutrients gradually (fed-batch culture, a batch to which nutrients are added during the run).

Key terms

Biotechnology
The use of living organisms or their parts, such as cells and enzymes, to make useful products or provide services.
Bioreactor
A vessel in which microorganisms, cells or enzymes are grown or used on a large scale under controlled conditions.
Sparger
A device at the bottom of a bioreactor that bubbles sterile air into the culture.
Downstream processing
The separation, purification, concentration and packaging of a product after it has been made in a bioreactor.
Silage
Grass stored airtight and fermented by lactic acid bacteria, so that it keeps as winter feed.
Anaerobic digestion
The breakdown of waste by microorganisms in sealed tanks without oxygen, producing biogas.
Bioremediation
The use of microorganisms that break down pollutants to clean up contaminated land or water.
Mycoprotein
A high-protein, high-fibre meat substitute made from harvested hyphae of the fungus Fusarium venenatum.
Growth curve
A graph of the number of living cells in a closed culture against time, showing the lag, log, stationary, decline and survival phases.
Lag phase
The phase of little or no increase in cell number while the cells adjust to a new medium.
Log phase
The exponential phase in which cells divide at their maximum rate.
Stationary phase
The phase in which the rate of cell division equals the rate of cell death, so the number of living cells stays constant.
Decline phase
The phase in which the death rate exceeds the rate of division, so the number of living cells falls.
Survival phase
The phase in which a small number of cells survive for a long time, often as endospores or inactive cells.
Generation time
The time taken for a population of cells to double in number during the log phase.
Batch culture
A closed culture in which a fixed amount of medium is inoculated and harvested only at the end.
Continuous culture
A culture in which fresh medium is added and culture removed at the same rate, holding the population in the log phase.
Fed-batch culture
A batch culture to which nutrients are added during the run to extend the log or stationary phase.
Primary metabolite
A substance made by cells as part of normal growth, mostly during the log phase, e.g. ethanol.
Secondary metabolite
A substance not needed for growth, made mainly in the stationary phase, e.g. the antibiotic penicillin.
