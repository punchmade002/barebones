What a seed is

  • A seed is a fertilised, mature ovule. It contains an embryo (a new plant in miniature), a food store and a protective coat, the testa. It is the product of sexual reproduction in flowering plants.
  • As the seed matures it loses most of its water and becomes dry and hard, and the activity of the embryo slows almost to a stop.

Seed structures and their functions

  • Testa: the seed coat, formed from the outer layers (integuments) of the ovule. It protects the embryo from mechanical damage, from attack by insects, fungi and bacteria, and from drying out.
  • Embryo: the young plant, which developed from the zygote by mitosis. It is made of the plumule, the radicle and one or two cotyledons.
  • Plumule: the embryonic shoot of the embryo; it will grow into the shoot (stem and leaves) of the new plant.
  • Radicle: the embryonic root of the embryo; it will grow into the first root of the new plant. It points towards the micropyle.
  • Cotyledon: a seed leaf, part of the embryo. In some seeds the cotyledons absorb and store the food; in others they pass food from the endosperm to the growing embryo.
  • Endosperm: a food store outside the embryo, formed by double fertilisation. It supplies the embryo with food, mainly starch and also oils and proteins in some seeds, when it starts to grow into a seedling, before the seedling's leaves can photosynthesise.
  • Micropyle: a tiny pore in the testa, where the pollen tube entered the ovule. Hilum: the scar where the seed was attached to the inside of the fruit.
  • Food stored in seeds: starch (e.g. cereal grains and beans), oils (e.g. sunflower seeds and oilseed rape) and protein (e.g. peas and beans). These food stores make seeds important human foods, such as cereals, pulses and vegetable oils (see Metabolic roles and nutritional sources).

Two seed types compared

  • Monocotyledons have one cotyledon; dicotyledons have two. Endospermic seeds keep the endosperm as the food store when mature; non-endospermic seeds have absorbed the endosperm into the cotyledons as the seed developed.
  • Maize (a monocot): one cotyledon; a large endosperm is the food store; the cotyledon passes food from the endosperm to the embryo. The maize grain is strictly a fruit, because its thin fruit wall is fused to the testa.
  • Broad bean (a dicot): two large cotyledons store the food; there is no endosperm in the mature seed.

Seeds and growth regulators in fruit growth

  • A growth regulator (plant hormone) is a chemical made in small amounts in one part of a plant that controls growth or development elsewhere. Developing seeds make growth regulators, mainly auxins (e.g. indoleacetic acid, IAA) and gibberellins.
  • After fertilisation the developing seeds release these growth regulators into the surrounding tissues. They stimulate the cells of the ovary wall (or, in some fruits, the receptacle) to divide and enlarge, so the fruit grows. Without fertilised seeds, most flowers simply wither and fall without forming a fruit.
  • Evidence from strawberry: the fleshy part of a strawberry is the swollen receptacle, and the pips on its surface are tiny dry fruits, each with one seed. In experiments by J. P. Nitsch (published 1950), removing the pips stopped the receptacle growing; applying auxin to the de-seeded receptacle restored its growth; and removing pips from only one strip gave a strawberry that grew only where seeds remained.
  • The number and spread of seeds affect the size and shape of the fruit: fruits with few or unevenly spread fertilised seeds are small or misshapen, e.g. poorly pollinated strawberries and apples, which is one reason good pollination matters to growers.
  • Technology link: seedless fruits (parthenocarpy, fruit formation without fertilisation). Growers spray unpollinated flowers with auxins (e.g. tomatoes) or gibberellins so that the fruit grows without seeds. Gibberellins are sprayed on seedless grape varieties to make the grapes larger. Some plants form fruit naturally without seeds, e.g. cultivated bananas, which are sterile (triploid) plants.

The role of seeds in plant reproduction

  • A seed carries the next generation: the embryo is a new individual formed by sexual reproduction, with half its genes from each parent, so seeds give genetic variation within the species (see Mitosis and meiosis).
  • The testa protects the embryo, and the food store supplies it until the seedling can photosynthesise.
  • Seeds, within fruits, allow the plant to spread to new places, away from the parent (see Fruit and seed dispersal).
  • Dry seeds with a tough testa can survive conditions that would kill the parent plant, such as winter or drought, so they carry the species through to a new growing season.
  • Developing seeds make the growth regulators that cause the fruit to form, and the fruit in turn protects and disperses the seeds.

Key terms

Seed
A fertilised, mature ovule containing an embryo, a food store and a protective testa.
Testa
The seed coat, formed from the outer layers of the ovule, which protects the embryo.
Embryo
The young plant inside a seed, formed from the zygote by mitosis and made of the plumule, radicle and cotyledons.
Plumule
The embryonic shoot of a seed, which will grow into the shoot of the new plant.
Radicle
The embryonic root of a seed, which will grow into the first root of the new plant.
Cotyledon
A seed leaf that is part of the embryo; it stores food or passes food from the endosperm to the embryo.
Endosperm
A food store outside the embryo, formed by double fertilisation, that supplies the embryo with food.
Micropyle
A tiny pore in the testa where the pollen tube entered the ovule.
Hilum
The scar on a seed where it was attached to the inside of the fruit.
Monocotyledon
A flowering plant, or its seed, with one cotyledon.
Dicotyledon
A flowering plant, or its seed, with two cotyledons.
Growth regulator
A chemical made in small amounts in one part of a plant that controls growth or development elsewhere.
Auxin
A growth regulator made by developing seeds that stimulates the ovary wall or receptacle to grow and so forms the fruit.
Gibberellin
A growth regulator made by developing seeds that stimulates fruit growth; sprayed on seedless grapes to make them larger.
Parthenocarpy
Fruit formation without fertilisation, so that the fruit is seedless.
