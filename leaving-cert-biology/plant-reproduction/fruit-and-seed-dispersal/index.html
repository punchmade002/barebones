Dispersal and why it matters

Dispersal is the carrying of fruits and seeds away from the parent plant. Plants cannot move, so dispersal is how they spread their offspring.

  • Dispersal reduces competition between the young plants and the parent, and among the young plants themselves, for light, water, minerals and space.
  • Dispersal lets the species colonise new areas and new habitats.
  • Dispersal spreads the population, so a local disaster, such as disease, fire or flooding, is less likely to destroy all of it.
  • Seeds that fall directly under the parent are usually shaded and short of water and minerals, so few survive. Plants produce many seeds because most are lost during dispersal or eaten.
  • Sustainability link: the ability of some plants to spread their seeds widely makes them invasive when introduced to new countries, e.g. Himalayan balsam, an invasive plant along Irish rivers with exploding seed pods.

Fruits

  • A fruit is a mature ovary containing seeds; its wall is the pericarp. It protects the seeds and is adapted to disperse them. Botanically, many foods called vegetables are fruits, e.g. tomatoes, cucumbers and pea pods.
  • Fleshy (succulent) fruits have a soft, juicy pericarp, often brightly coloured and sweet when ripe, e.g. blackberry, tomato and cherry; they are mostly dispersed by animals that eat them.
  • Dry fruits have a hard or papery pericarp, e.g. pea pod, hazelnut and sycamore; they are dispersed by wind, water, animals carrying them, or by splitting open.
  • A false fruit is one in which other parts of the flower, not only the ovary, form the fruit, e.g. strawberry, where the fleshy part is the receptacle.

Wind dispersal

  • Adaptations: fruits or seeds are small and light, or have wings or parachutes that increase their surface area, so they fall slowly and are carried further by the wind.
  • Parachutes: dandelion and thistle fruits each carry a parachute of fine hairs that lets them float long distances.
  • Wings: sycamore has paired winged fruits and ash has single winged fruits. The wing makes the fruit spin like a helicopter blade as it falls, slowing it so the wind carries it away from the parent.
  • Censer (pepper-pot) mechanism: the poppy's dry fruit, a capsule, has a ring of pores near the top. When the wind sways the stalk, the tiny seeds are shaken out a few at a time.
  • Dust-like seeds: orchid seeds are so tiny and light that they are blown like dust.

Water dispersal

  • Adaptations: fruits or seeds that float, because they contain air spaces or have a light, waterproof covering, so they can be carried by streams, rivers or the sea.
  • Coconut: the fruit has a thick, fibrous, air-filled husk and a waterproof outer layer, so it floats for long periods and is carried by ocean currents to other shores and islands.
  • Alder: a tree of riverbanks whose small fruits contain air and float, and are carried downstream to settle on new banks.

Animal dispersal: internal

  • Fleshy fruits are eaten by birds and mammals. The animal digests the fleshy pericarp, but the seeds have a tough testa (or a hard stone) that resists digestion. They pass out in the faeces, often far from the parent, and the droppings act as a supply of minerals.
  • Adaptations: bright colours (often red, black or purple) when ripe, attracting birds; sweet, juicy, nutritious flesh as a reward; green and sour fruit while the seeds are unripe, so the fruit isn't eaten too early.
  • Examples: blackberry, holly berries and rowan berries (eaten by birds), and tomato.
  • Animal caching: squirrels and jays bury acorns and hazelnuts as winter stores and fail to recover some, which then grow into new trees.

Animal dispersal: external

  • Fruits have hooks or sticky surfaces that catch on the fur or feathers of passing animals (or on human clothing). They fall or are brushed off later, away from the parent.
  • Examples: burdock has hooked burs; goosegrass (cleavers) has small fruits covered in hooked hairs.
  • Technology link: the hooked burs of burdock inspired Velcro, invented by the Swiss engineer George de Mestral after he found burs stuck to his dog's coat.

Self-dispersal

  • Some dry fruits split open forcefully as they dry, flinging the seeds out: gorse and pea pods twist and burst in dry weather, and Himalayan balsam pods explode when touched.

Summary of dispersal methods

  • Wind: wings, parachutes and light seeds (sycamore, ash, dandelion, poppy). Water: floating, air-filled fruits (coconut, alder). Animal, internal: fleshy, coloured, edible fruits with indigestible seeds (blackberry, holly). Animal, external: hooks (burdock, goosegrass). Self-dispersal: exploding pods (gorse, pea).
  • Diagram labelled drawings of one fruit for each method: sycamore (wing and seed), dandelion (parachute of hairs and the fruit containing a seed), burdock bur (hooks) and blackberry (fleshy fruit and seeds). A coconut in section (fibrous, air-filled husk and seed) is optional.

Key terms

Dispersal
The carrying of fruits and seeds away from the parent plant.
Pericarp
The wall of a fruit, developed from the wall of the ovary.
Fleshy fruit
A fruit with a soft, juicy pericarp, often brightly coloured and sweet when ripe, usually dispersed by animals that eat it.
Dry fruit
A fruit with a hard or papery pericarp, dispersed by wind, water, animals or by splitting open.
False fruit
A fruit in which parts of the flower other than the ovary form the fruit, such as the receptacle of a strawberry.
Parachute
A tuft of fine hairs on a fruit that lets it float on the wind over long distances, as in the dandelion.
Censer mechanism
The shaking out of small seeds through pores in a dry fruit when the wind sways its stalk, as in the poppy.
Caching
The burying of seeds or nuts by animals as stores, some of which are not recovered and then grow into new plants.
Invasive plant
A plant that spreads widely when introduced to a new country, such as Himalayan balsam in Ireland.
