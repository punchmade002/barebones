Adaptations of insect- and wind-pollinated flowers

Each point gives the insect-pollinated flower first and the wind-pollinated flower second. The flower parts are named as in Flower structure and pollination.

  • Petals: insect-pollinated flowers have large, brightly coloured petals, often with nectar guides, to attract insects; wind-pollinated flowers have small, green or dull petals, or none, as there is no animal to attract and petals would block pollen in the wind.
  • Scent and nectar: insect-pollinated flowers are usually scented and have nectaries that produce nectar as a reward; wind-pollinated flowers have no scent and no nectaries.
  • Position of anthers: insect-pollinated anthers are inside the flower, held firmly on short or stiff filaments where a visiting insect brushes against them; wind-pollinated anthers hang outside the flower on long, thin, flexible filaments, loosely attached and often swinging freely, so the wind shakes pollen out.
  • Stigma: insect-pollinated stigmas are small, sticky and inside the flower, where the insect's body touches them; wind-pollinated stigmas are large, feathery (branched) and hang outside the flower, giving a big surface area to catch pollen from the air.
  • Amount of pollen: insect-pollinated flowers produce relatively small amounts, because insects carry pollen directly from flower to flower; wind-pollinated flowers produce very large amounts, because most pollen carried by the wind never reaches a stigma of the same species.
  • Pollen grains: insect pollen grains are larger, often with a sticky or oily coat and a rough or spiky surface that clings to insect hairs; wind pollen grains are small, light, dry and smooth, so they are carried easily and far by the wind and don't clump.
  • Flowers as a whole: insect-pollinated flowers are usually showy, single or in visible clusters; wind-pollinated flowers are small and inconspicuous, often in catkins or spikes held high above the leaves. Many wind-pollinated trees, e.g. hazel, flower before their leaves open, so the leaves don't block the pollen.
  • Examples: insect-pollinated plants include buttercup, wallflower, foxglove and primrose; wind-pollinated plants include grasses (e.g. ryegrass), hazel, oak, birch and plantain.
  • Diagram side-by-side labelled drawings of an insect-pollinated flower (a half-flower of buttercup or wallflower, showing the large petal, nectary, anther inside the flower and small sticky stigma) and a wind-pollinated grass flower (feathery stigmas and anthers on long filaments hanging outside small green bracts). Label the bracts only as bracts (small green scales).
  • Diagram an insect pollen grain (larger, with a spiky or sculptured surface) drawn beside a wind pollen grain (smaller and smooth), as seen under the microscope.

Investigation: aim, hypothesis and planning

This is a comparative observation study, not a controlled experiment. No factor is changed, so there is no treatment and no control group; the two flower types are compared with each other.

  • Aim: to compare the structures of an insect-pollinated flower and a wind-pollinated flower, and relate each structure to its function in pollination.
  • Example hypothesis: “The insect-pollinated flower will have larger, coloured petals, nectaries and a sticky stigma inside the flower; the wind-pollinated flower will have small or no petals, anthers and feathery stigmas hanging outside the flower, and smaller, smoother pollen grains.”
  • What differs and what is recorded: the factor being compared is the method of pollination (insect or wind). Qualitative features recorded are colour, scent, presence of nectar, whether the stigma is sticky or feathery, whether anthers and stigma are inside or outside the flower, and pollen surface. Quantitative features recorded are the number of each part, the lengths of filaments and styles, petal size and pollen grain diameter.
  • Keeping the comparison fair: use fully open, mature flowers in which the anthers are shedding pollen; examine several flowers of each species (at least three) and average the measurements; measure every flower in the same way, with the same instruments and microscope magnification.
  • Equipment: fresh insect-pollinated flowers (e.g. buttercup or wallflower) and wind-pollinated flowers (e.g. a flowering grass, or hazel catkins in late winter); white tile; scalpel or sharp blade; forceps; mounted needle; hand lens (×10) or stereo (dissecting) microscope; ruler (mm) or graph paper; microscope slides and coverslips; dropper with water; compound light microscope; clear sticky tape; gloves; pencil and paper.

Investigation: method

  • 1. Examine each whole flower with the hand lens. Record its colour, size and whether it has a scent. Note whether the anthers and stigmas are inside the flower or hang outside it.
  • 2. Remove the parts of the insect-pollinated flower from the outside in, using forceps, laying each ring (sepals, petals, stamens, carpels) in order on the white tile. Count the parts in each ring and record them.
  • 3. Look at the base of the petals for nectaries (shiny or moist patches). Touch the stigma lightly with a mounted needle to see if it is sticky.
  • 4. Measure the length of the filaments, style and petals with a ruler (or on graph paper) for each flower. Repeat for at least three flowers of each species and calculate the means.
  • 5. Repeat steps 1 to 4 with the wind-pollinated flower, using the stereo microscope for the small grass flowers. Note the bracts, the long hanging filaments and the feathery stigmas.
  • 6. Cut a vertical section of the insect-pollinated flower with the scalpel on the tile (a half-flower) and draw it, labelled.
  • 7. Pollen: tap a ripe anther over a drop of water on a clean slide, or press clear sticky tape onto the anther and stick the tape on a slide. Add a coverslip and examine under the light microscope at low, then high, power. Draw a few grains of each type at the same magnification and compare their size and surface; estimate their diameter (e.g. against the field of view or an eyepiece scale). Note how easily pollen comes off the anther and whether it clumps (sticky) or spreads (dry).
  • 8. Record all observations and measurements in a results table, and make labelled drawings with the magnification stated.

Investigation: safety

  • Cut away from the body on a white tile with a sharp scalpel; carry and store blades safely.
  • Pollen can trigger hay fever or asthma. Students with pollen allergies should avoid handling wind-pollinated flowers or wear a mask and gloves, and the room should be ventilated.
  • Some plants are poisonous or irritant: foxglove is poisonous if eaten, and buttercup sap can irritate the skin. Wear gloves, don't put plant material near the mouth, and wash hands after the practical.
  • Collect only common plants, with the landowner's permission; don't pick protected or rare wild flowers.
  • Microscope slides and coverslips are fragile glass; dispose of breakages in a glass bin.

Investigation: results

  • Results table: one column for each species (insect-pollinated and wind-pollinated), with rows for petal colour and size, scent, nectaries, number of stamens, filament length, anther position, stigma type and position, style length, amount of pollen released, and pollen size and surface.
  • Expected primary data for the insect-pollinated flower: large coloured petals, nectaries, scent, anthers on short or stiff filaments inside the flower, a small sticky stigma, and larger, sticky, spiky or sculptured pollen grains.
  • Expected primary data for the wind-pollinated flower: small green bracts or no petals, no nectar or scent, anthers dangling outside the flower on long filaments that shed clouds of dry pollen when tapped, feathery stigmas outside the flower, and smaller, smooth, dry pollen grains.

Investigation: conclusion and secondary data

  • Conclusion: relate each observed structure to its function, using the adaptations in the first block. For example, the feathery stigma of the grass gives a large surface to catch airborne pollen, and the sticky stigma of the buttercup collects pollen rubbed off an insect's body. State whether the results support the hypothesis.
  • Secondary data used to support the conclusions: scanning electron micrographs (SEM images) of pollen grains, which show surface detail that a light microscope can't resolve, such as spikes and ridges on insect pollen and smooth surfaces on grass pollen; published pollen counts, which show the huge numbers of grass pollen grains in the air in early summer; labelled diagrams and descriptions of other species in textbooks or plant identification guides.
  • Primary data are collected by the student through observing and measuring; secondary data are collected by others (see Producing and analysing data).

Investigation: sources of error and limitations

  • Only a few flowers of one or two species are examined, so the results may not represent all insect- or wind-pollinated plants; examine more flowers and more species, and compare with secondary data.
  • Flowers at different stages (bud, open, withering) differ in size and pollen release; use flowers at the same stage.
  • Grass flowers are very small, so measurements with a ruler are imprecise; use a stereo microscope or graph paper.
  • Pollen size estimated against the field of view is approximate; a calibrated eyepiece scale gives more precise sizes. The light microscope can't show fine surface detail, hence the use of SEM images.
  • Judgements of colour, scent and stickiness are qualitative and subjective; have two people record them.
  • Damage during dissection can break parts or knock off pollen; handle flowers gently with forceps.

Key terms

Bract
A small green scale that replaces petals in many wind-pollinated flowers, such as grasses.
Nectar guide
A line or patch on an insect-pollinated petal that leads the insect to the nectar.
Catkin
A hanging, cylindrical flower cluster, such as the male flowers of hazel.
Insect-pollinated flower
A flower with large, coloured petals, often scented, with nectaries, a small sticky stigma inside the flower and small numbers of larger, sticky pollen grains.
Wind-pollinated flower
A flower with small or no petals, no scent or nectar, anthers and feathery stigmas outside the flower, and very large amounts of small, light, dry pollen.
Primary data
Data that the student collects by observing and measuring.
Secondary data
Data collected by others, such as published pollen counts or scanning electron micrographs, used to support conclusions.
Scanning electron micrograph (SEM)
An image of a specimen made with an electron microscope, showing surface detail that a light microscope cannot resolve.
