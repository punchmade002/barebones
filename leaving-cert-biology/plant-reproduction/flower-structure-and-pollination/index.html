Reproductive structures of a flowering plant

Flowering plants reproduce sexually by means of flowers, which produce the gametes and bring about pollination and fertilisation.

  • The flower is a short shoot whose parts are modified leaves arranged in rings (whorls) on the receptacle. Its job is to produce gametes, bring about pollination and fertilisation, and then produce seeds and fruits.
  • The flower makes the male and female gametes and brings about pollination. The seed, a fertilised and developed ovule, contains the embryo (a new plant) with a food store and a protective coat. The fruit, a developed ovary, protects the seeds and helps to disperse them. Seed detail is in Seeds; dispersal is in Fruit and seed dispersal.
  • Many flowers are bisexual (hermaphrodite), with both male and female parts, e.g. buttercup. Some plants have separate male and female flowers on the same plant, e.g. hazel, whose male flowers hang in catkins and whose small female flowers have red stigmas; in some species male and female flowers are on different plants, e.g. holly.

Flower structures and their functions

The parts of a flower are listed from the outside inwards. The male parts are the stamens and the female parts are the carpels.

  • Receptacle: the swollen tip of the flower stalk, to which all the other flower parts are attached. In strawberry it swells to form the fleshy part of the fruit.
  • Sepal: a small, usually green, leaf-like part in the outer ring; together the sepals form the calyx. Sepals enclose and protect the flower while it is a bud; some photosynthesise.
  • Petal: inside the sepals; together the petals form the corolla. In insect-pollinated flowers the petals are large, coloured and often scented, attracting insects; many have lines or patches (nectar guides) that lead insects to the nectar. In wind-pollinated flowers petals are small, dull or absent.
  • Nectary: a gland, usually at the base of the petals, that secretes nectar, a sugary liquid that rewards visiting insects. Pollen is also eaten by some insects, e.g. bees collect it to feed their larvae.
  • Stamen: the male part, made of an anther on a stalk, the filament. The stamens together form the androecium.
  • Anther: produces pollen grains by meiosis, so they are haploid. Each pollen grain contains the male gamete nuclei. When ripe the anther splits open to release the pollen.
  • Filament: the stalk that holds the anther in position (where insects brush against it, or out in the wind) and carries water and nutrients to the anther through a small vascular strand.
  • Carpel: the female part, made of the stigma, style and ovary. A flower may have one carpel or many; the carpels together form the gynoecium.
  • Stigma: the tip of the carpel that receives (catches) pollen. It is sticky in insect-pollinated flowers and large and feathery in wind-pollinated grasses.
  • Style: the stalk joining the stigma to the ovary. It holds the stigma in a position to receive pollen, and the pollen tube grows down through it to the ovary.
  • Ovary: the swollen base of the carpel. It contains one or more ovules and protects them. After fertilisation the ovary develops into the fruit.
  • Ovule: a structure inside the ovary that contains the egg cell (female gamete), made by meiosis. After fertilisation the ovule develops into the seed. The micropyle is a tiny pore at one end of the ovule through which the pollen tube usually enters.

Pollination

Pollination must happen before fertilisation can take place. Flowers can be self-pollinated or cross-pollinated, and several features favour cross-pollination.

  • Pollination is the transfer of pollen from an anther to a stigma of a flower of the same species.
  • Self-pollination: pollen is transferred to a stigma of the same flower or of another flower on the same plant. Cross-pollination: pollen is transferred to a stigma of a flower on a different plant of the same species.
  • Cross-pollination combines genes from two different parents, so the offspring show more genetic variation; the species is better able to adapt to change, such as new diseases or climate, and avoids the harmful effects of inbreeding.
  • Self-pollination needs no second plant or pollinator and is more certain, but it gives less variation; it suits isolated plants and plants in places with few pollinators.
  • Ways flowers favour cross-pollination: the stamens and stigmas of a flower ripen at different times; self-incompatibility, where pollen landing on a stigma of the same plant fails to grow a pollen tube; separate male and female flowers (hazel) or separate male and female plants (holly).
  • In primrose two flower forms exist: pin flowers, with a long style and low anthers, and thrum flowers, with a short style and high anthers. Insects carry pollen between the two forms.
  • Agents of pollination: insects (most showy flowers), wind (grasses and many trees, e.g. hazel, oak and birch), and less often other animals (birds and bats in other parts of the world) or water. The comparison of insect- and wind-pollinated flowers is in Investigation: insect- and wind-pollinated plants.

The role of flowers in pollination

The flower's structure matches the agent of pollination, whether an animal or the wind.

  • In insect-pollinated plants the flower attracts the pollinator (colour, scent, nectar guides), rewards it (nectar and pollen), and places its anthers and stigma where the insect's body touches them. Pollen sticks to the insect at one flower and is rubbed onto the sticky stigma of the next flower of the same species it visits.
  • Many insects visit only one species on a foraging trip, having learned the flower they are faithful to, so pollen is carried to the right species. Plant and pollinator both benefit: this is a mutualism (detail in Biotic factors and relationships).
  • In wind-pollinated plants the flower's job is to release very large amounts of pollen into moving air and to catch pollen on a large stigma. There is nothing to attract animals, so there are no showy petals, scent or nectar.
  • Sustainability link: many crops (e.g. apples, strawberries and oilseed rape) and wild plants depend on insect pollinators, especially bees. Bee numbers are falling through habitat loss, pesticides and disease. Ireland's response is the All-Ireland Pollinator Plan, first published in 2015 and coordinated by the National Biodiversity Data Centre, which encourages councils, farmers, schools and gardeners to provide food and nesting places for pollinators, e.g. by leaving wildflowers and mowing less often.
  • Health link: wind-pollinated plants, especially grasses, release huge numbers of small, light pollen grains that cause hay fever (allergic rhinitis) in early summer.

From pollination to seed and fruit

  • Fertilisation in five steps: 1. A pollen grain of the same species lands on the stigma.
  • 2. It grows a pollen tube down through the style to the ovary, digesting a path with enzymes.
  • 3. The tube enters an ovule, usually through the micropyle, and carries the male gamete nuclei to it.
  • 4. Fertilisation: a male gamete nucleus fuses with the egg cell nucleus to form a diploid zygote.
  • 5. The zygote divides by mitosis to form the embryo.
  • Double fertilisation: a second male nucleus fuses with two other nuclei in the ovule (the polar nuclei) to form the endosperm nucleus, which divides to form the endosperm food store.
  • After fertilisation the ovule becomes the seed, and its outer layers (integuments) become the testa. The ovary becomes the fruit, and its wall becomes the fruit wall (pericarp). The petals, stamens and usually the sepals wither and fall off.

Key terms

Receptacle
The swollen tip of the flower stalk to which all the other flower parts are attached.
Sepal
A small, usually green, leaf-like part of the outer ring of a flower that protects the bud; the sepals together form the calyx.
Petal
A part inside the sepals that forms the corolla; large and coloured in insect-pollinated flowers and small or absent in wind-pollinated ones.
Nectary
A gland, usually at the base of the petals, that secretes nectar to reward visiting insects.
Stamen
The male part of a flower, made of an anther on a filament; the stamens together form the androecium.
Anther
The part of a stamen that produces pollen grains by meiosis and releases them when ripe.
Filament
The stalk that holds the anther in position and carries water and nutrients to it.
Carpel
The female part of a flower, made of the stigma, style and ovary; the carpels together form the gynoecium.
Stigma
The tip of the carpel that receives pollen.
Style
The stalk joining the stigma to the ovary, through which the pollen tube grows.
Ovary
The swollen base of the carpel, containing the ovules; it develops into the fruit after fertilisation.
Ovule
A structure inside the ovary containing the egg cell; after fertilisation it develops into the seed.
Micropyle
A tiny pore at one end of the ovule through which the pollen tube usually enters.
Pollination
The transfer of pollen from an anther to a stigma of a flower of the same species.
Self-pollination
The transfer of pollen to a stigma of the same flower or of another flower on the same plant.
Cross-pollination
The transfer of pollen to a stigma of a flower on a different plant of the same species.
Nectar guide
A line or patch on a petal that leads an insect to the nectar.
Pollen tube
A tube grown by a pollen grain down through the style to the ovary, carrying the male gamete nuclei to the ovule.
Zygote
The diploid cell formed when a male gamete nucleus fuses with the egg cell nucleus.
Double fertilisation
The process in flowering plants in which a second male nucleus fuses with the polar nuclei to form the endosperm food store.
