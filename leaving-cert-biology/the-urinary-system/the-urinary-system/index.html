Excretion and the kidney's two jobs

  • Excretion is the removal of the waste products of metabolism (see Characteristics of living things).
  • The kidneys excrete urea, excess water, excess salts and excess hydrogen ions in urine.
  • The lungs excrete carbon dioxide; sweat removes some water, salts and a little urea.
  • Urea is made in the liver from excess amino acids by deamination (see Absorption, transport and storage). It is carried dissolved in the blood plasma to the kidneys.
  • Urea is toxic if it builds up, so it must be removed continuously.
  • Transport between environments: blood (the internal environment) carries wastes to the kidneys, the kidneys transfer them from the blood into urine, and urine is passed out of the body (the external environment).
  • The kidneys also return useful substances (glucose, amino acids, most of the water and most of the salts) to the blood, so nutrients are not lost.
  • The kidneys have two roles: excretion, and osmoregulation and homeostasis, which regulate the volume, water content, salt concentration and pH of the body fluids.
  • Both roles use the same two processes: filtration of the blood, then reabsorption of what the body needs. What is not reabsorbed is urine.

The kidneys and their blood supply

  • The kidneys are a pair of bean-shaped organs at the back of the abdomen, one on each side of the vertebral column, just below the diaphragm; the lowest ribs partly protect them.
  • The right kidney is slightly lower than the left because the liver lies above it.
  • Each kidney is embedded in a layer of fat that cushions it against physical damage and helps hold it in place, and is covered by a tough outer membrane, the renal capsule.
  • The adrenal glands sit on top of the kidneys (see Glands, locations and hormones).
  • Each kidney receives blood from a renal artery, a short wide branch of the aorta, so blood arrives at high pressure and in large volume, which suits filtration.
  • Filtered blood leaves by the renal vein, which joins the vena cava.
  • Blood in the renal vein has less urea, less oxygen and less glucose than blood in the renal artery, and more carbon dioxide because the kidney cells respire; it is the blood vessel with the lowest urea concentration in the body.

Ureters, bladder and urethra

  • There are two ureters: narrow muscular tubes, one from the renal pelvis of each kidney down to the bladder.
  • The ureter walls contain smooth muscle that moves urine by peristalsis (waves of contraction), so urine reaches the bladder whatever the body position, not just by gravity.
  • The urinary bladder is a hollow muscular bag in the lower abdomen (pelvis) that stores urine.
  • The bladder wall is elastic and folded, so it can stretch to hold a large volume and contract to empty.
  • Two rings of sphincter muscle at the bladder outlet keep it closed: an inner one that works automatically (involuntary) and an outer one under conscious (voluntary) control, which young children learn to control.
  • The urethra is the single tube that carries urine from the bladder out of the body.
  • In males the urethra is longer, runs through the penis and also carries semen; in females it is short and opens just in front of the vagina.

Regions of the kidney

  • A kidney cut lengthways shows three regions.
  • Cortex: the dark outer layer, under the renal capsule.
  • Medulla: the paler inner layer, divided into cone-shaped renal pyramids.
  • Renal pelvis: a funnel-shaped space on the inner (concave) side that collects urine and leads into the ureter. No filtration happens in the pelvis and urine is not changed there; it is only a collecting space.

Nephrons and the sites of filtration and reabsorption

  • Each kidney contains about one million nephrons, microscopic tubules that are the kidney's filtering units. Each runs from the cortex down into the medulla and back, and the tubules drain into collecting ducts that run through the pyramids and open into the renal pelvis.
  • Filtration takes place in the cortex: blood is forced at high pressure through knots of capillaries (glomeruli) into the cup-shaped tops of the nephrons (Bowman's capsules).
  • Small molecules (water, glucose, amino acids, salts and urea) are filtered out of the blood; blood cells and plasma proteins are too large and stay in the blood. The filtered liquid is called the filtrate.
  • Reabsorption takes place in the cortex (in the coiled tubules) and in the medulla (in the loops of Henle and the collecting ducts).
  • All the glucose and amino acids, most of the salts and most of the water are taken back into the blood; the remaining liquid, carrying urea and excess salts and water, is urine.
  • Diagram longitudinal section of a kidney labelled renal capsule, cortex, medulla, renal pyramid, renal pelvis, ureter, renal artery and renal vein, with one nephron drawn in from cortex into medulla to show where filtration and reabsorption happen.

Urine and regulation of body fluids

  • Urine is mostly water, with urea, salts (mainly sodium chloride) and other wastes dissolved in it.
  • Healthy urine contains no protein and no glucose, because protein is not filtered and all the glucose is reabsorbed.
  • After drinking a lot of water, a large volume of dilute (pale) urine is produced.
  • When water is short (little drinking, heavy sweating in exercise or hot weather, or diarrhoea), a small volume of concentrated (dark yellow) urine is produced.
  • The control is by the hormone ADH (detail in ADH and osmoregulation); this note gives the effect only.
  • Salt balance: after a salty meal the kidneys excrete more salt; when salt is short they reabsorb more.
  • Keeping the salt and water concentration of the blood steady stops body cells from gaining or losing water by osmosis (see Water).
  • pH: the kidneys remove excess hydrogen ions from the blood in urine, helping to keep blood pH at about 7.4 (buffers are covered in Minerals).
  • This is homeostasis: the kidneys keep the internal environment constant whatever is eaten or drunk (negative feedback is taught in Homeostasis as a stimulus-response model).

Path of urine from kidney to urethra

  • 1. Urine forms continuously in the nephrons and drains from the collecting ducts into the renal pelvis.
  • 2. Peristalsis in the ureter carries it to the bladder in small spurts.
  • 3. The bladder fills and its wall stretches; stretch receptors in the wall send nerve impulses that give the urge to urinate.
  • 4. To urinate, the bladder's muscular wall contracts and the sphincters relax; the outer sphincter relaxes under voluntary control.
  • 5. Urine leaves the body through the urethra.

Relating structure to function

  • Fat layer around each kidney: protects it from physical damage.
  • Renal artery branching directly from the aorta: delivers high-pressure blood for filtration.
  • About one million nephrons: a huge filtering surface.
  • Cortex full of capillary knots: the site of filtration.
  • Long tubules and loops in the medulla: the site of reabsorption.
  • Funnel-shaped renal pelvis: collects urine.
  • Muscular ureters: move urine to the bladder by peristalsis.
  • Elastic bladder with sphincters: stores urine and allows controlled release.
  • Urethra: the exit for urine from the body.

Urine testing as a health link

  • Glucose in urine is a sign of diabetes, because blood glucose is so high that not all of it can be reabsorbed.
  • Protein in urine is a sign of damage to the kidney's filters.
  • Urine tests are also used to detect drugs, e.g. in sport.

Kidney failure and its treatment

  • If the kidneys stop working, urea, salts and water build up in the blood and the person will die without treatment.
  • Treatment is by dialysis or by a kidney transplant.
  • A transplant gives a working kidney, but the patient must take drugs to stop the immune system rejecting it (see Immunity).

Dialysis as a technology

  • The patient's blood is pumped through a machine where it flows past a selectively permeable membrane, with dialysis fluid on the other side.
  • Urea and excess salts diffuse out of the blood into the fluid.
  • The fluid contains glucose and salts at normal blood concentrations, so these are not lost from the blood; blood cells and proteins are too large to cross the membrane.
  • Dialysis must be repeated several times a week.

Key terms

Excretion
The removal of the waste products of metabolism from the body.
Urea
A toxic nitrogen waste made in the liver from excess amino acids and removed from the blood by the kidneys.
Osmoregulation
The control of the water and salt concentration of the body fluids.
Renal capsule
The tough outer membrane that covers each kidney.
Cortex
The dark outer region of the kidney, where filtration takes place.
Medulla
The paler inner region of the kidney, divided into renal pyramids, where reabsorption takes place.
Renal pelvis
The funnel-shaped space at the inner side of the kidney that collects urine and leads into the ureter.
Ureter
A narrow muscular tube that carries urine from the renal pelvis of a kidney to the bladder by peristalsis.
Urethra
The tube that carries urine from the bladder out of the body.
Sphincter
A ring of muscle that closes an opening; the bladder has an involuntary inner sphincter and a voluntary outer sphincter.
Glomerulus
A knot of capillaries in the cortex where blood is filtered under high pressure.
Bowman's capsule
The cup-shaped top of a nephron that surrounds a glomerulus and receives the filtrate.
Filtrate
The liquid that is filtered out of the blood into the nephron, containing water, glucose, amino acids, salts and urea but not blood cells or plasma proteins.
Dialysis
A treatment for kidney failure in which urea and excess salts diffuse out of the blood across a selectively permeable membrane into a dialysis fluid.
