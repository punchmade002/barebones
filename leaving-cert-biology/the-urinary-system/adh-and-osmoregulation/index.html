Why water balance must be controlled (Higher Level only)

  • Osmoregulation (defined in Water) in humans is mainly done by the kidneys, by changing how much water is reabsorbed from the collecting ducts. The concentration of the blood plasma is the variable being controlled.
  • Water is gained from drink, food and respiration, and lost in urine, sweat, breath (as water vapour) and faeces. Gains and losses change from hour to hour, so the kidney must adjust the urine to match.
  • If the blood becomes too concentrated, body cells lose water by osmosis and shrink; if it becomes too dilute, cells gain water and swell. Either upsets cell function, especially in the brain.

ADH and the collecting duct (Higher Level only)

  • ADH stands for antidiuretic hormone. Diuresis is the production of a large volume of dilute urine, so ADH works against it and reduces urine volume. ADH is released from the pituitary gland (see Glands, locations and hormones).
  • The target cells are the cells lining the collecting ducts and the last part of the distal convoluted tubule. ADH binds to receptors on these cells and causes water channel proteins, called aquaporins, to be inserted into their membranes, so the duct wall becomes more permeable to water. Channel proteins are described in Metabolic roles and nutritional sources.
  • Because the collecting duct passes through the salty medulla (see The nephron), water moves out of the more permeable duct by osmosis into the medulla and is carried away in the blood capillaries. More ADH means more water reabsorbed, less water left in the duct, and a small volume of concentrated urine.
  • Less ADH means fewer water channels, a less permeable duct wall, less water reabsorbed, and a large volume of dilute urine.
  • ADH changes water reabsorption only; it does not change the amount of salt or urea excreted.

Feedback when the body is short of water (Higher Level only)

  • 1. Stimulus: the water concentration of the blood falls (its solute concentration rises), e.g. after heavy sweating, not drinking enough, eating salty food, or diarrhoea.
  • 2. Receptors: osmoreceptors, special cells in the hypothalamus of the brain, detect the change.
  • 3. The hypothalamus causes the pituitary gland to release more ADH into the blood.
  • 4. ADH travels in the blood to the kidneys.
  • 5. The collecting ducts become more permeable to water, so more water is reabsorbed into the blood.
  • 6. A small volume of concentrated, dark-yellow urine is produced.
  • 7. The water concentration of the blood rises back towards normal, and ADH release falls again: this is negative feedback.
  • At the same time the hypothalamus triggers thirst, so the person drinks more. Drinking is the only way to replace lost water; the kidneys can only reduce further loss.

Feedback when the body has too much water (Higher Level only)

  • 1. Stimulus: after drinking a large volume of water, the water concentration of the blood rises (it becomes more dilute).
  • 2. The hypothalamus detects the change.
  • 3. The pituitary releases less ADH.
  • 4. The collecting ducts become less permeable to water, so less water is reabsorbed.
  • 5. A large volume of dilute, pale urine is produced.
  • 6. The blood's water concentration falls back to normal and ADH release returns to its usual level.

Examples of ADH in action (Higher Level only)

  • On a hot day or during exercise, much water is lost in sweat, so more ADH is released and the urine is small in volume and dark.
  • A high salt intake raises the solute concentration of the blood, so more ADH is released, more water is reabsorbed and a smaller volume of urine is produced; the extra salt is excreted in this smaller volume, and the person also feels thirsty.
  • Alcohol reduces the release of ADH, so the collecting ducts reabsorb less water and a large volume of dilute urine is produced; this causes dehydration, which contributes to the headache and thirst of a hangover.

Key terms

ADH (antidiuretic hormone)
A hormone released from the pituitary gland when the blood is too concentrated; it makes the collecting ducts more permeable to water, so less urine is produced.
Diuresis
The production of a large volume of dilute urine.
Aquaporin
A water channel protein that is inserted into the cell membranes of the collecting duct when ADH acts on them.
Osmoreceptor
A special cell in the hypothalamus that detects the water concentration of the blood.
Osmoregulation
The control of the water concentration of the blood and body fluids.
Hypothalamus
The part of the brain that detects changes in blood water concentration and causes the pituitary gland to release ADH.
Negative feedback
A control in which a change triggers a response that reverses the change, returning the level to normal.
