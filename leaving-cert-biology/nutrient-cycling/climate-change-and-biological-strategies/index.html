The greenhouse effect

  • Step 1: sunlight, mostly visible and short-wave radiation, passes through the atmosphere and warms the Earth's surface.
  • Step 2: the warm surface gives off long-wave infrared radiation, which is heat.
  • Step 3: greenhouse gases in the atmosphere (water vapour, carbon dioxide, methane and nitrous oxide) absorb some of this infrared and re-emit it in all directions, including back down to the surface.
  • Step 4: as a result, the lower atmosphere and the surface are warmer than they would otherwise be.
  • Without the natural greenhouse effect the Earth's average surface temperature would be about −18 °C instead of about 15 °C, which is too cold for most life.
  • The enhanced greenhouse effect is the extra warming caused by human increases in greenhouse gases.
  • Climate change is the long-term change in the Earth's climate, including temperature, rainfall and extreme weather. It is now driven mainly by the enhanced greenhouse effect.
  • Global warming is the rise in average surface temperature, which is one part of climate change.

Carbon dioxide, methane and climate change

Carbon dioxide and methane both add to the greenhouse effect. Each is discussed here with its sources, its evidence and the uncertainties in the link.

  • Carbon dioxide: atmospheric CO₂ has risen from about 280 parts per million before the Industrial Revolution to over 420 parts per million today. This comes mainly from burning fossil fuels and from deforestation (sources and stores are in “The carbon cycle”).
  • CO₂ is the largest single cause of human-caused warming because so much is released and it stays in the atmosphere for centuries.
  • Methane (CH₄): its concentration has more than doubled since before the Industrial Revolution.
  • Methane sources include anaerobic microorganisms (archaea) in the stomachs (rumens) of cattle and sheep, released mainly by belching; manure and slurry stores; rice paddies; landfill sites; natural wetlands and bogs; and leaks from natural-gas wells and pipelines.
  • Kilogram for kilogram, methane traps far more heat than CO₂: over 100 years about 30 times as much, and over 20 years about 80 times as much.
  • Methane lasts only about 12 years in the air before it is broken down to CO₂, so cutting methane slows warming quickly.
  • Nitrous oxide (N₂O) from fertilised and waterlogged soils and from manure, formed by denitrification (see “The nitrogen cycle”), is a third biological greenhouse gas. Kilogram for kilogram it is about 270 times as powerful as CO₂ over 100 years.
  • Ireland: agriculture is the largest single source of Ireland's greenhouse gas emissions, mainly methane from cattle and nitrous oxide from fertiliser and manure, because Ireland has a large, grass-fed cattle herd.
  • Ireland's law, the Climate Action and Low Carbon Development (Amendment) Act 2021, sets a 51% cut in greenhouse gas emissions by 2030 compared with 2018, and climate neutrality by 2050. Agriculture's target is a 25% cut by 2030.
  • Evidence: greenhouse gases absorb infrared, a property measured in the laboratory since the nineteenth century.
  • Evidence: direct measurements show CO₂ and methane rising together with global temperature.
  • Evidence: air bubbles trapped in ancient ice cores show that CO₂ and temperature rose and fell together over hundreds of thousands of years.
  • Evidence: the observed warming pattern, in which the lower atmosphere warms while the upper atmosphere cools, fits greenhouse gases and not a brighter Sun.
  • The Intergovernmental Panel on Climate Change (IPCC) concluded in 2021 that it is unequivocal that human influence has warmed the climate, by about 1.1 °C for 2011–2020 compared with 1850–1900.
  • The Paris Agreement (2015) aims to keep warming well below 2 °C and to pursue efforts to limit it to 1.5 °C. Scientific consensus is covered in “Evaluating evidence”.
  • Uncertainty: correlation alone does not prove cause. Here the physics of infrared absorption supplies the mechanism.
  • Uncertainty: exactly how much warming a given rise in CO₂ causes, and how regional rainfall will change, are less certain than the direction of change. Feedbacks are hard to model.
  • Feedback: warming speeds up decomposition by soil microorganisms, releasing more CO₂ (see “The microbiome” for soil microbiomes and warming).
  • Feedback: thawing permafrost, which is frozen soil in the Arctic, lets stored organic matter decompose, releasing CO₂ and methane.
  • Feedback: warmer oceans dissolve less CO₂.
  • Feedback: drought and fires turn forests from sinks into sources.
  • Biological effect: species' ranges move towards the poles or uphill, and species that cannot move or adapt face extinction (see “Biodiversity loss and conservation”).
  • Biological effect: the timing of events such as flowering, breeding and migration changes, so species may get out of step with their food.
  • Biological effect: more CO₂ dissolving in the oceans makes them more acidic (ocean acidification), which makes it harder for corals, shellfish and some phytoplankton to build calcium carbonate shells.
  • Biological effect: warmer seas cause coral bleaching.
  • Biological effect: insects that spread disease can spread to new areas (see “Emergence and spread of infectious disease”).

Carbon sinks compared

This block builds on “Photosynthesis as an anabolic process” and “The carbon cycle”, and does not redefine carbon sinks.

  • Forests and other land plants: trees store carbon in wood for decades or centuries, and in roots and soil. The sink is strong while trees grow. A mature forest takes in roughly what it releases. The store is lost quickly if the forest burns, dies or is felled.
  • Phytoplankton and algae: microscopic algae and cyanobacteria in the oceans carry out about half of all photosynthesis on Earth.
  • When phytoplankton die or are eaten, some of their carbon sinks to the deep ocean and sea floor, which is the biological pump. There it can stay for centuries or longer.
  • Seaweeds such as kelp, and seagrass meadows and saltmarshes, also store carbon, which is called blue carbon.
  • Bogs and other peatlands: waterlogged, acidic, low-oxygen conditions stop decomposition (see “The carbon cycle”), so a healthy bog accumulates peat very slowly over thousands of years.
  • Peatlands cover only about 3% of the world's land but hold more carbon than all the world's forests. A bog is a sink only while it stays wet.
  • Soils: soil organic matter (humus) stores more carbon than all plants and the atmosphere together. Grassland and undisturbed soils keep it, while ploughing and draining release it.
  • Comparison: bogs store the most carbon per hectare and for the longest time, but gain it very slowly. Forests take up carbon fastest but store it less securely. Oceans are the largest active sink, but they are becoming more acidic and absorb less as they warm.
  • All natural sinks together absorb only about half of the CO₂ humans release each year, so sinks can slow the rise but cannot replace cutting emissions.

Strategies to reduce CO₂

Each strategy is evaluated by how it works biologically, the case for it, the case against it, and a judgement.

  • Protecting existing forests, bogs and wetlands works by keeping carbon in place.
  • For protecting existing forests, bogs and wetlands: The quickest and cheapest way to avoid emissions, with large benefits for biodiversity and water.
  • Against protecting existing forests, bogs and wetlands: Land is valuable for farming or development, and protection needs enforcement and payment to landowners (the ethics are covered in “Ethics and sustainability of nutrient cycling”).
  • Judgement on protecting existing forests, bogs and wetlands: The most effective first step.
  • Afforestation (planting new forests) and reforestation (replanting cleared land) work by photosynthesis fixing CO₂ into wood.
  • For afforestation and reforestation: They take up carbon for decades. Native broadleaved woodland also supports biodiversity, reduces flooding, and gives timber that can replace concrete and steel.
  • Against afforestation and reforestation: They are slow, taking decades to reach full uptake. Fire, storms and disease can release the carbon. They need land that could grow food.
  • Against afforestation and reforestation: Conifers planted on deep peat can release more carbon from the drained peat than the trees take up.
  • Against afforestation and reforestation: Dense plantations of one non-native species, in Ireland mostly Sitka spruce, support less wildlife.
  • Ireland's forest cover is about 11%, among the lowest in the EU.
  • Judgement on afforestation and reforestation: Valuable if the right trees are planted in the right places, but slow.
  • Rewetting and restoring bogs works by blocking drains so the water table rises. Conditions become anaerobic again, decomposition stops, and sphagnum moss starts forming peat.
  • For rewetting and restoring bogs: It quickly stops the large CO₂ release from drained peat, restores rare habitat, and holds water to reduce flooding. Bord na Móna's cutaway bogs are being rewetted on a large scale.
  • Against rewetting and restoring bogs: Rewetted bogs give off some methane from anaerobic decomposition. New peat forms very slowly, so the gain is mainly avoided CO₂ loss rather than fast uptake. The land can no longer be used for turf or farming.
  • Judgement on rewetting and restoring bogs: Strongly beneficial for Ireland because so much peat is drained.
  • Algae and phytoplankton: seaweed farming and algae grown in tanks fix CO₂ fast, and the algae can be made into food, feed or biofuel.
  • For algae and phytoplankton: Algae grow much faster than land plants and need no farmland or fresh water.
  • Against algae and phytoplankton: If the algae are eaten or burned, the carbon returns to the air, so the sink lasts only if the carbon is stored.
  • Against algae and phytoplankton: Adding iron to the ocean to cause phytoplankton blooms has been tested, but little of the carbon was shown to reach the deep ocean, and it risks harming marine ecosystems, for example by creating low-oxygen zones. It is therefore not an accepted strategy.
  • Judgement on algae and phytoplankton: Promising for fuels and food, but unproven as large-scale storage.
  • Increasing soil carbon: keeping grassland unploughed, adding manure and compost, growing cover crops over winter, reduced tillage and planting hedgerows add organic matter to soil. This is also for improved soil fertility and water-holding.
  • Against increasing soil carbon: Soils fill up (saturate) after some decades, the gain is hard to measure, and it is lost again if the land is ploughed.
  • Bioenergy in place of fossil fuels: biofuels (see “Industrial enzymes”) and wood or other biomass burned in power stations release CO₂ that the plants recently took in, so in principle they add little CO₂. Capturing and storing the CO₂ from burning biomass could even remove carbon.
  • Against bioenergy: Fuel crops compete with food for land, clearing land for them releases carbon, and growing, harvesting and transporting them uses fossil fuel. Biofuels are not carbon-neutral without this qualification.

Strategies to reduce methane

  • Changes to cattle diet and breeding: feed additives (e.g. the chemical 3-NOP, and some seaweeds) inhibit the methane-producing microorganisms in the rumen, cutting methane in trials.
  • Changes to cattle diet and breeding: better-quality feed and earlier finishing of cattle mean less methane per kilogram of meat or milk. Breeding cattle that naturally produce less methane is being researched.
  • For changes to cattle diet and breeding: They tackle Ireland's largest methane source directly without cutting production.
  • Against changes to cattle diet and breeding: Additives are hard to give to cattle that graze outdoors for most of the year. Long-term effects and costs are still being studied. Some seaweeds contain a compound (bromoform) with health concerns, and breeding is slow.
  • For fewer livestock and dietary change: Fewer cattle produce less methane and need less land, which could be used for forests or bogs. Eating less red meat and dairy reduces demand.
  • Against fewer livestock and dietary change: Farming is central to Ireland's rural economy and exports. Cutting the Irish herd may only move production to countries with higher emissions per animal, which is called carbon leakage. Meat and milk are good sources of protein, iron and calcium.
  • Judgement on fewer livestock and dietary change: A fair transition needs support for farmers.
  • Anaerobic digestion of slurry, food waste and grass: microorganisms break down the waste without oxygen in a sealed tank, producing biogas, which is mainly methane. The biogas is burned for heat or electricity, or upgraded to replace natural gas, and the digestate is used as fertiliser.
  • For anaerobic digestion: It captures methane that would otherwise escape from open slurry stores and landfill, replaces a fossil fuel, and recycles nutrients.
  • Against anaerobic digestion: The plants are expensive, leaks release methane, and burning the biogas still releases CO₂, although it is recent carbon rather than fossil carbon.
  • Other methods: capping landfill sites and collecting their gas; composting food waste aerobically instead of sending it to landfill; reducing food waste; and covering slurry stores.
  • Rewetted bogs and rice paddies show that wet, anaerobic soil makes methane, which is a trade-off with storing carbon.
  • Nitrous oxide: using clover in grassland, applying fertiliser precisely, and using “protected” urea fertiliser, which releases nitrogen more slowly, all reduce nitrous oxide. They also reduce nitrate leaching (see “Ethics and sustainability of nutrient cycling”).

Biology in a post-fossil-fuel world

  • A post-fossil-fuel world is one in which energy, fuels, materials and chemicals no longer come from coal, oil and gas. Biology contributes by providing renewable replacements made from recently fixed carbon, and by protecting the sinks that absorb CO₂.
  • Biofuels and biogas: bioethanol is made by fermentation of sugars by yeast (fermentation is covered in “Respiration as a catabolic process”), and enzymes that break down cellulose for fuel are covered in “Industrial enzymes”.
  • Biofuels and biogas: biodiesel is made from plant oils and waste cooking oil. Biogas comes from anaerobic digestion (see the methane strategies above), and fuels are also made from algae.
  • Bio-based materials: bioplastics are made from plant starch or sugars, such as polylactic acid (PLA), and some are biodegradable. Wood is used in construction, and plant fibres replace oil-based fibres.
  • Microorganisms as factories, or biomanufacturing: bacteria, yeasts and algae grown in fermenters (bioreactors) can make medicines, enzymes, food proteins and chemicals that are now made from oil. They use sugars or waste as raw materials (see “Microorganisms in industry”; genetic modification is covered in “Genetic engineering”).
  • Food and farming: breeding crops, including by genetic technologies, that tolerate drought, heat and new pests and need less fertiliser.
  • Food and farming: using legumes and soil microbiomes instead of manufactured fertiliser, which is made with fossil energy (see “The nitrogen cycle”), and reducing food waste.
  • Biology also monitors and understands change, by measuring sinks, tracking species' ranges and predicting the spread of disease.
  • Limits: biomass cannot replace all fossil fuels, because land is needed for food and nature. Bio-based products are not automatically sustainable, since they depend on how the raw material is grown. Biology therefore works alongside other renewables, such as wind and solar, and alongside using less energy.

Key terms

Greenhouse effect
The warming of the lower atmosphere and surface because greenhouse gases absorb infrared radiation from the Earth and re-emit some of it back down.
Enhanced greenhouse effect
The extra warming caused by human increases in greenhouse gases in the atmosphere.
Climate change
The long-term change in the Earth's climate, including temperature, rainfall and extreme weather.
Global warming
The rise in average surface temperature, which is one part of climate change.
Feedback
A process in which a change in the climate causes further change, such as warming releasing more CO₂ from soil.
Ocean acidification
The increase in the acidity of the oceans as they absorb more CO₂, which makes it harder for organisms to build calcium carbonate shells.
Biological pump
The sinking of dead phytoplankton and their carbon to the deep ocean and sea floor, where it can be stored for centuries or longer.
Blue carbon
Carbon stored by seaweeds, seagrass meadows and saltmarshes.
Afforestation
Planting new forests on land that has not recently been forested.
Rewetting
Blocking the drains of a bog so that the water table rises, conditions become anaerobic again and peat forms.
Biomanufacturing
Using bacteria, yeasts or algae grown in fermenters to make medicines, enzymes, foods and chemicals.
