Investigation: aim, measurement and equipment

  • Aim: to investigate how temperature, the concentration gradient and surface area affect the rate of osmosis across a semi-permeable membrane.
  • How the rate is measured: water entering a bag of sucrose solution increases its mass, so the rate of osmosis is the change in mass per unit time (g per minute), or the percentage change in mass in a fixed time. For potato tissue the percentage change in mass in a fixed time is used, because pieces start at slightly different masses. An alternative for Visking bags is the rise of liquid up a capillary tube attached to the bag (an osmometer), measured in mm per minute.
  • Equipment and materials: Visking tubing; thread or tubing clips; sucrose solutions (made up as below) and distilled water; syringes or measuring cylinders; beakers or boiling tubes; thermostatically controlled water baths (or beakers of water heated and topped up, with ice for low temperatures) and thermometers; top-pan balance reading to 0.01 g; paper towels; stopwatch; scissors; white tile, scalpel, ruler and cork borer for potato; a fresh potato; labels; safety glasses.
  • Sucrose solutions: a 1.0 M (mol per litre) solution contains 342 g of sucrose made up to 1 litre with distilled water. A range, e.g. 0.2, 0.4, 0.6, 0.8 and 1.0 M, is made by diluting it with distilled water, e.g. 2 cm³ of 1.0 M plus 8 cm³ of water gives 0.2 M. Detail of dilution is in Investigation: reducing sugars, quantitatively.
  • Replicates: set up three bags (or three sets of potato pieces) for each value of the factor and calculate the mean. Repeat the whole investigation to check repeatability.

Making a Visking tubing bag

  • 1. Cut pieces of Visking tubing of the same length, e.g. 15 cm, and soak them in water for a few minutes until soft.
  • 2. Rub one end between finger and thumb to open the tube.
  • 3. Tie one end tightly with thread, or knot the tubing itself.
  • 4. Using a syringe, add the same volume, e.g. 10 cm³, of sucrose solution to each bag.
  • 5. Squeeze out the air and tie the other end tightly, leaving the bag slightly slack so it can swell.
  • 6. Rinse the outside with distilled water to remove any sucrose, blot it dry with paper towel in the same way every time, and weigh it.
  • 7. Record this starting mass.
  • Visking tubing is semi-permeable: its tiny pores let water molecules through easily but hold back larger molecules. Sucrose molecules pass only very slowly, so over a short experiment the bag behaves as a membrane permeable to water but not to sucrose, as cell membranes do. Starch is held back completely.
  • Diagram a Visking tubing bag tied at both ends, containing sucrose solution, in a beaker of distilled water, with an arrow showing water entering; and an osmometer, a bag tied to a capillary tube clamped upright in a beaker of distilled water, with a ruler beside the tube. Label: Visking tubing, sucrose solution, distilled water, thread, capillary tube and scale.

Investigation: concentration gradient

  • Hypothesis: the greater the concentration gradient between the solution inside the bag and the water outside, the faster the rate of osmosis, because the difference in water concentration across the membrane is greater, so the net movement of water is faster.
  • Variables: the independent variable is the concentration of sucrose inside the bag (0.2 to 1.0 M), with distilled water outside, so the gradient increases with concentration. The dependent variable is the rate of mass gain (g per minute). Controlled variables are temperature (all at room temperature, or in one water bath, e.g. 25 °C), length and width of tubing (surface area), volume of solution in the bag, volume of distilled water outside, time intervals and blotting method.
  • Control: a bag containing distilled water, placed in distilled water (no concentration gradient). Its mass should not change, showing that any change in the other bags is due to osmosis and not to handling, leaking or water left on the surface.
  • Method: 1. Make one bag for each concentration (three of each), plus the control (see the block on making a Visking tubing bag above).
  • 2. Weigh each bag.
  • 3. Place each bag in its own labelled beaker of distilled water, enough to cover it, and start the stopwatch.
  • 4. Every 10 minutes, for 40 to 60 minutes, remove each bag, blot it dry in the same way, weigh it, and return it to the water.
  • 5. Calculate the mean change in mass for each concentration and the rate in g per minute.
  • Second method, potato tissue: 1. Use a cork borer to cut cylinders from one potato, remove the skin, and cut them all to the same length, e.g. 3 cm, on a tile.
  • 2. Blot and weigh each cylinder.
  • 3. Place three cylinders in each of a range of sucrose solutions (0.0 M, i.e. distilled water, and 0.2, 0.4, 0.6, 0.8 and 1.0 M) of the same volume, for the same time (e.g. at least 30 minutes; often left overnight) at the same temperature.
  • 4. Remove them, blot in the same way, reweigh, and calculate the percentage change in mass.
  • 5. Plot percentage change in mass against sucrose concentration.
  • Third method (optional demonstration): an egg whose shell has been dissolved in vinegar over a day or two is held in by its membrane. Placed in distilled water it gains mass, and in concentrated sugar syrup it loses mass. It shows the direction of osmosis clearly, but it is slow and gives only one result per egg.

Investigation: temperature

  • Hypothesis: the higher the temperature, the faster the rate of osmosis, because water molecules have more kinetic energy and move faster, so more cross the membrane each second.
  • Variables: the independent variable is the temperature, e.g. 10, 20, 30, 40 and 50 °C. The dependent variable is the rate of mass gain (g per minute) of the Visking bags. Controlled variables are sucrose concentration (e.g. 0.5 M in every bag), volume of solution, length and width of tubing, volume of distilled water, time intervals and blotting method.
  • Control: at each temperature, a bag of distilled water in distilled water, which should show no mass change.
  • Method: 1. Set water baths to each temperature (use iced water for 10 °C) and check each with a thermometer.
  • 2. Place beakers of distilled water, and boiling tubes of the 0.5 M sucrose solution, in each bath until they reach its temperature.
  • 3. Make the bags with the warmed or cooled solution (see the block on making a Visking tubing bag above) and weigh them.
  • 4. Place three bags in the beaker of distilled water in each bath.
  • 5. Every 10 minutes for 40 minutes, remove, blot, weigh and return each bag, checking the bath temperature each time.
  • 6. Calculate the mean rate (g per minute) at each temperature.
  • Visking tubing is used rather than potato for temperature, because high temperatures damage the cell membranes of living tissue, making them fully permeable, so the cells would no longer show osmosis.

Investigation: surface area

  • Hypothesis: the larger the surface area of membrane in contact with the solution, the faster the rate of osmosis, because more water molecules can cross the membranes at the same time.
  • Variables: the independent variable is the surface area of potato tissue, changed by cutting the same volume into more pieces. The dependent variable is the percentage change in total mass in a fixed time. Controlled variables are the total volume (and starting mass) of potato, from the same potato with no skin, the concentration and volume of the solution (e.g. 0.8 M sucrose, or distilled water), temperature, time and blotting method.
  • Preparing the pieces, with their surface areas: cut 2 cm × 2 cm × 2 cm cubes, each of volume 8 cm³. Leave one cube whole, with surface area 6 × 2 × 2 = 24 cm². Cut one cube in half to give two 2 × 2 × 1 cm slabs, with surface area 2 × 16 = 32 cm². Cut one cube into four 2 × 1 × 1 cm bars, with surface area 4 × 10 = 40 cm². Cut one cube into eight 1 cm cubes, with surface area 8 × 6 = 48 cm². The total volume is 8 cm³ in every case, so only the surface area differs.
  • Method: 1. Cut the pieces from one potato on a tile, as above, with three sets of each.
  • 2. Blot each set and weigh all its pieces together.
  • 3. Place each set in its own beaker containing the same volume of the same solution, at the same temperature.
  • 4. After a fixed time, e.g. 30 minutes, remove them, blot each piece in the same way, and reweigh the set.
  • 5. Calculate the percentage change in mass for each set, and the mean for each surface area.
  • No separate control is needed, because the treatments are compared with each other; the whole 2 cm cube is the reference. The surface area to volume idea links to Levels of organisation.

Calculations and worked examples

  • Change in mass = final mass minus initial mass. Rate = change in mass divided by time. Percentage change in mass = (final mass minus initial mass) divided by initial mass, multiplied by 100. A gain is positive; a loss is negative.
  • Worked example, Visking bag: initial mass 12.40 g, mass after 30 minutes 13.30 g. Change = +0.90 g. Rate = 0.90 ÷ 30 = 0.03 g per minute. Percentage change = 0.90 ÷ 12.40 × 100 = 7.3%.
  • Worked example, potato: a cylinder of 2.50 g has a mass of 2.20 g after 30 minutes in 1.0 M sucrose. Change = −0.30 g, and percentage change = −0.30 ÷ 2.50 × 100 = −12%. A cylinder of 2.00 g that becomes 2.30 g in distilled water has a change of +0.30 g, so percentage change = +0.30 ÷ 2.00 × 100 = +15%.
  • Graphs: plot the factor (independent variable) on the x-axis and the rate or percentage change in mass (dependent variable) on the y-axis, using the mean of the replicates. Alternatively plot mass against time for each value, where a steeper line shows a faster rate.

Investigation: safety

  • Cut potato on a white tile, cutting away from the body and fingers; carry scalpels and cork borers safely.
  • Hot water baths: use a test-tube holder or tongs to handle beakers and bags in water above about 40 °C, and keep baths at or below 50 °C to avoid scalds.
  • Glass capillary tubes break easily and are sharp; handle and clamp them carefully, and wear safety glasses.
  • Mop up spilt sugar solution at once, as it makes floors slippery; keep water away from electrical sockets and water-bath controls.
  • Don't eat any food materials used. If using eggs, wash hands after handling raw egg.

Investigation: expected results and conclusions

  • Concentration gradient (Visking): the bags gain mass, faster with higher sucrose concentration, and the control bag stays the same. The mass gain slows over time in every bag, because the entering water dilutes the solution inside, reducing the gradient, and the bag becomes tight. Conclusion: the rate of osmosis increases as the concentration gradient increases.
  • Concentration (potato): cylinders gain mass and feel firm (turgid) in distilled water and dilute solutions, because water enters the cells. They lose mass and feel soft and bendy (flaccid) in concentrated solutions, because water leaves the cells. The larger the difference between the solution and the cell sap, the larger the change. Where the graph line crosses zero change, the solution has the same water concentration as the cell sap (isotonic), so there is no net osmosis.
  • Temperature: the rate of mass gain increases steadily as temperature increases. There is no optimum and no sharp fall at high temperature, because no enzyme is involved and Visking tubing isn't denatured. Conclusion: the rate of osmosis increases with temperature.
  • Surface area: the percentage change in mass in the same time is greatest for the eight 1 cm cubes (48 cm²) and least for the whole cube (24 cm²). Conclusion: the rate of osmosis increases with surface area.
  • Each conclusion should quote the primary data, e.g. the mean rates, and say whether they support the hypothesis.

Investigation: sources of error and limitations

  • Blotting: leaving more or less surface water on bags or potato pieces changes the mass, so blot in exactly the same way each time. Potato cut into more pieces holds more surface liquid, which can exaggerate the surface-area effect.
  • Leaks: badly tied bags leak; check the knots and discard any leaking bag. Some sucrose diffuses slowly out of Visking tubing, reducing the gradient over time.
  • Bags or pieces that differ in size, volume of solution or trapped air are not fair comparisons; measure volumes with a syringe and cut potato accurately.
  • Water-bath temperatures drift, and bags cool while being weighed; check the temperature at each reading and weigh quickly.
  • A balance reading only to 0.1 g or 1 g can't detect small changes; use one reading to 0.01 g.
  • Potato tissue varies, between different potatoes or between the centre and edge of one potato; cut all pieces from one potato and use replicates. Cut surfaces are damaged cells that don't behave normally.
  • Results from Visking tubing model a membrane, but they don't show what living, selectively permeable membranes do, such as moving substances actively.

Key terms

Visking (dialysis) tubing
A non-living, semi-permeable tubing that lets water through easily but holds back larger molecules such as sucrose and starch; used to model a membrane in osmosis experiments.
Osmometer
A device made of a bag tied to a capillary tube, in which the rise of liquid up the tube measures the rate of osmosis.
Sucrose
A sugar, with relative molecular mass 342, used to make the solutions in osmosis investigations.
Molar concentration (M)
The number of moles of solute per litre of solution; 1.0 M sucrose contains 342 g per litre.
Concentration gradient
The difference in concentration across a membrane, which here depends on the concentration of the solution inside the bag because the outside is distilled water.
Control (osmosis)
A bag of distilled water in distilled water, which should show no mass change and so confirms that any change in the other bags is due to osmosis.
Percentage change in mass
The change in mass divided by the initial mass, multiplied by 100; used when starting masses differ.
Turgid
Describes potato tissue that is firm because its cells have taken in water by osmosis.
Flaccid
Describes potato tissue that is soft and bendy because its cells have lost water by osmosis.
