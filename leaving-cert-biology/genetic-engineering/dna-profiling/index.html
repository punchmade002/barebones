What a DNA profile is

  • DNA profiling (also called DNA fingerprinting) is a method of making a pattern of bands (or peaks) from a sample of a person’s DNA, so that the DNA of different samples can be compared.
  • Except for identical twins, the pattern is unique to each person.
  • It is based on non-coding DNA (see “Chromosome structure and genes”): about 98% of human DNA does not code for protein.
  • Parts of non-coding DNA contain short base sequences repeated many times one after another. In short tandem repeats (STRs), a short sequence, often four bases (e.g. GATA), is repeated end to end, e.g. GATAGATAGATA…
  • The number of repeats at a particular place (locus) varies greatly between people, so the length of that piece of DNA varies.
  • Each person has two copies of every locus, one on the chromosome inherited from the mother and one on the chromosome inherited from the father.
  • Each copy can have a different number of repeats (two alleles, giving two bands), or the same number (giving one band, twice as strong).
  • So a child’s profile at each locus has one band from the mother and one from the father.
  • A profile tests many loci at once; modern forensic profiles use more than ten.
  • The chance that two unrelated people match at every locus is extremely small, which is why a full match is strong evidence of identity.
  • Identical twins come from one zygote, so they have the same DNA and the same profile.
  • How a profile is made: DNA is extracted from cells; many copies of the repeat regions are made by PCR (in older methods the DNA was cut with restriction enzymes); the fragments are separated by size by gel electrophoresis; and the pattern is analysed and compared. Each step is covered in detail in “Generating a DNA profile” (Higher Level).

Reading a profile

  • On a gel, each band is a group of DNA fragments of the same length.
  • DNA is negatively charged (its phosphate groups), so in an electric field it moves through the gel towards the positive electrode, away from the wells where samples are loaded.
  • Shorter fragments (fewer repeats) move faster and travel further from the wells; longer fragments (more repeats) stay nearer the wells. So band position shows fragment length.
  • Two samples match only if every band is in the same position. A single band that differs, at any locus, shows that they did not come from the same person; this is an exclusion.
  • Comparing band positions with a ladder of fragments of known length (a size marker) lets lengths be read.
  • In modern laboratories the fragments are labelled with fluorescent dyes and separated by machine. The result is printed as a graph of peaks, each peak being one allele labelled with its number of repeats, rather than as bands. The rules for matching are the same.
  • Diagram a gel with wells at the top (negative electrode) and the positive electrode at the bottom; lanes for a ladder, a crime-scene sample and three suspects; one suspect’s bands match the crime-scene lane exactly. Label wells, lanes, bands, “shorter fragments travel further”, + and − electrodes.

History

  • DNA profiling was invented by Alec Jeffreys at the University of Leicester, England, in 1984.
  • It was first used to convict a murderer, Colin Pitchfork, in 1988.
  • The same investigation first cleared an innocent suspect, who had confessed to one of the killings but whose DNA did not match.

Uses of DNA profiling

  • Forensic science (crime): DNA from blood, semen, saliva, skin cells or hair roots left at a crime scene is compared with a suspect’s DNA (taken from a mouth swab).
  • A match links the suspect to the scene; a mismatch excludes them.
  • Profiles are stored in national DNA databases and compared with new crime-scene samples.
  • Old crime-scene samples can be retested, solving “cold cases” and freeing people wrongly convicted.
  • Paternity and other family relationships: every band in a child’s profile must come from the mother or the father, so a man whose profile lacks a band the child must have inherited from its father is excluded as the father.
  • DNA profiling is also used to establish maternity, to reunite separated families, and in immigration cases where family relationships must be proven.
  • Identifying human remains, e.g. victims of disasters, wars and accidents, or missing persons, by comparing their DNA with that of relatives or with DNA from their personal items (e.g. a toothbrush).
  • Mitochondrial DNA and Y-chromosome profiles trace family lines when nuclear DNA is too damaged or no close relative is available: mitochondrial DNA is passed down the female line and the Y chromosome down the male line (see “Nuclear and non-nuclear inheritance” and “Sex determination”).
  • Example: the skeleton found under a car park in Leicester in 2012 was confirmed in 2013 to be King Richard III, partly by matching its mitochondrial DNA to living descendants of his sister’s female line.
  • Animals, plants and conservation: identifying illegally traded wildlife products (e.g. ivory, meat from protected species), checking the pedigree (parentage) of animals used for breeding, and keeping breeding programmes for endangered species from inbreeding.
  • Food and medicine: DNA tests identify the species in food, showing fraud. Example: in January 2013 the Food Safety Authority of Ireland found horse DNA in beef burgers on sale in Ireland, which revealed a Europe-wide horsemeat scandal.
  • DNA-based tests also help match donors and patients for bone-marrow transplants, and identify the strain of a disease-causing microorganism in an outbreak. Species tests check for a species’ DNA, not an individual’s profile.

Profiling is not genetic screening

  • DNA profiling compares band patterns of mostly non-coding DNA to identify a person or a relationship; it tells nothing about health.
  • Genetic screening (DNA testing) looks at particular genes to find whether a person has, or carries, an allele linked to a disease (see “Bioinformatics: genome databases” and, for screening of newborns, “Prenatal and postnatal care”).

Limitations of DNA evidence

  • Contamination of a sample with another person’s DNA (e.g. by investigators) can give a false link; samples must be collected with gloves, masks and sealed bags.
  • DNA can be degraded by heat, moisture, bacteria or time, giving incomplete profiles that are harder to interpret.
  • A match shows that the DNA came from that person (or an identical twin), not when or how it got there; DNA can be transferred innocently, e.g. by touch or on shared objects.
  • So DNA evidence must be weighed with other evidence; it does not prove guilt alone.
  • Mistakes in labelling or interpretation, and the human judgement needed for mixed samples from several people, can lead to errors.

Key terms

DNA profiling
A method of making a pattern of bands or peaks from a person’s DNA so that the DNA of different samples can be compared.
Non-coding DNA
DNA that does not code for protein, including short base sequences repeated many times in a row, which are used in DNA profiling.
Short tandem repeat (STR)
A short base sequence, often four bases long, that is repeated end to end, with a number of repeats that varies between people.
Locus
The particular place on a chromosome where a gene or repeat sequence is found.
Allele
One of the alternative forms of a gene or repeat region at a locus, which gives one band in a profile.
Size marker (ladder)
A mixture of DNA fragments of known lengths run on a gel so that the lengths of other fragments can be read.
Exclusion
A difference in one band at any locus, which shows that two samples did not come from the same person.
Genetic screening
DNA testing that looks at particular genes to find whether a person has, or carries, an allele linked to a disease.
Mitochondrial DNA
DNA in the mitochondria, passed down the female line, which can be used to trace family lines.
Contamination
The addition of another person’s DNA to a sample, which can give a false link between people.
