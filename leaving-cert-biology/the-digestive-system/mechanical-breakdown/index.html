What mechanical breakdown does

  • Mechanical (physical) digestion breaks food into smaller pieces, or fat into smaller droplets, without changing the molecules: no chemical bonds are broken and no new substances are formed. Chemical digestion by enzymes breaks bonds (see Chemical breakdown).
  • Smaller pieces have a much larger total surface area, so enzymes can act on more of the food at once and digestion is faster. Mechanical breakdown also makes food soft and moist enough to swallow, and mixes it with digestive juices.
  • The four mechanisms are chewing by the teeth (mouth), peristalsis (the whole gut), churning (stomach), and emulsification of fats by bile salts (duodenum).

Teeth and chewing

  • Chewing (mastication) breaks food into small pieces and mixes it with saliva; the tongue rolls the moistened food into a bolus for swallowing. Mucus in saliva lubricates the bolus.
  • Incisors are at the front of the mouth; they are chisel-shaped with sharp, flat edges, for biting and cutting food.
  • Canines, one on each side of the incisors in each jaw, are pointed, for gripping and tearing food. Human canines are small compared with those of carnivores.
  • Premolars and molars at the back are broad with ridged surfaces (cusps), for crushing and grinding food. Premolars have two cusps; molars are larger with more cusps. The jaw moves sideways as well as up and down, so molars grind.

Dental formula and tooth structure

  • The dental formula gives the number of each type of tooth in one half of the upper jaw over one half of the lower jaw, in the order incisors, canines, premolars, molars.
  • The adult human dental formula is 2.1.2.3 over 2.1.2.3, so there are 16 teeth on each side of the mouth (8 in each quarter) and 32 teeth in total.
  • Child's milk (deciduous) teeth have the formula 2.1.0.2 over 2.1.0.2, giving 20 teeth in total; they have no premolars.
  • The third molars are the wisdom teeth, which appear last, often in the late teens or later, or not at all.
  • A tooth has a crown above the gum and a root fixed in the jawbone. Enamel, the hardest substance in the body, covers the crown; dentine, a bone-like layer, lies beneath it; the pulp cavity in the centre contains nerves and blood vessels; cement holds the root in its socket.

Tooth decay and its prevention

  • Bacteria in plaque on the teeth feed on sugars and release acid, which dissolves enamel and then dentine, forming a cavity.
  • Prevention: eating and drinking sugary food less often, brushing with fluoride toothpaste, drinking fluoridated water (fluoride hardens enamel; see Minerals), and regular dental check-ups.

Peristalsis

  • Peristalsis is a wave of muscular contraction that passes along the wall of the gut, pushing food along. It happens from the oesophagus to the rectum, and also in the ureters.
  • The gut wall has an inner layer of circular muscle and an outer layer of longitudinal muscle; both are smooth, involuntary muscle.
  • The circular muscle just behind the bolus contracts, narrowing the gut and squeezing the food forwards; the circular muscle just in front relaxes, widening the gut to receive it. The wave then moves along.
  • Peristalsis is automatic (involuntary) once food is swallowed and works against gravity: a person can swallow while lying down or upside down.
  • Peristalsis also mixes food with digestive juices and breaks it up, so it is part of mechanical breakdown; in the small intestine it keeps moving the contents past the absorbing surface.
  • Dietary fibre adds bulk that the muscles can grip and push against, so a high-fibre diet keeps peristalsis effective and prevents constipation (see Diet).
  • Diagram a section of the gut showing a bolus with contracted circular muscle (narrow) behind it and relaxed muscle (wide) in front, with an arrow for the direction of movement; label circular muscle, longitudinal muscle, bolus, contraction and relaxation.

The stomach

  • The stomach wall has three layers of smooth muscle: longitudinal, circular and an oblique (diagonal) layer. Their contractions churn the food, breaking it up and mixing it thoroughly with gastric juice.
  • After a few hours of churning, food becomes chyme, a thick, creamy liquid. The sphincters at the entrance and exit stay closed during churning, so food is held in the stomach.
  • The pyloric sphincter relaxes now and then to let small amounts of chyme into the duodenum, so the small intestine is never overloaded and has time to digest and neutralise each portion.
  • The stomach's chemical role, with acid and pepsin, is covered in Chemical breakdown.

Bile salts and emulsification

  • Fats and oils do not mix with the watery contents of the gut; they form large droplets, so lipase, which works in water, can reach only the droplet surfaces.
  • Bile salts are made by the liver and enter the duodenum in bile. Each bile-salt molecule has one end that attracts water (hydrophilic) and one end that attracts fat (hydrophobic). They gather on the surface of fat droplets with the fat-loving end inwards, and with the churning of the gut they break the large droplets into many tiny droplets and stop them joining together again. This is emulsification; the mixture of tiny droplets in water is an emulsion.
  • Emulsification is mechanical: the fat molecules are unchanged and no bonds are broken. It greatly increases the surface area of fat exposed to lipase, so fat is digested much faster.
  • Bile contains no enzymes. Its other roles, neutralising stomach acid and excreting bile pigments, are covered in Chemical breakdown.
  • Washing-up liquid emulsifies grease in the same way, which is why bile salts are often compared to a detergent.

Key terms

Mechanical digestion
The breakdown of food into smaller pieces, or fat into smaller droplets, without breaking chemical bonds.
Mastication
Chewing, by which the teeth break food into small pieces and mix it with saliva.
Incisor
A chisel-shaped front tooth used for biting and cutting food.
Canine
A pointed tooth, one on each side of the incisors in each jaw, used for gripping and tearing food.
Premolar
A broad back tooth with two cusps, used for crushing and grinding food.
Molar
A large back tooth with several cusps, used for grinding food; the third molars are the wisdom teeth.
Dental formula
The number of each type of tooth in one half of the upper jaw over one half of the lower jaw, in the order incisors, canines, premolars, molars.
Enamel
The hardest substance in the body, which covers the crown of a tooth.
Dentine
The bone-like layer beneath the enamel of a tooth.
Peristalsis
A wave of muscular contraction that passes along the wall of the gut, pushing food along.
Circular muscle
The inner layer of smooth muscle in the gut wall, which narrows the gut when it contracts.
Longitudinal muscle
The outer layer of smooth muscle in the gut wall, which runs along the length of the gut.
Chyme
The thick, creamy liquid formed when food is churned with gastric juice in the stomach.
Emulsification
The breaking of large fat droplets into many tiny droplets by bile salts, which increases the surface area for lipase.
Bile salts
Molecules made by the liver that have water-attracting and fat-attracting ends and emulsify fats in the duodenum.
Emulsion
A mixture of tiny fat droplets dispersed in water.
