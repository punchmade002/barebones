What a balanced diet is

  • A balanced diet contains all the nutrients (carbohydrate, protein, lipid, vitamins and minerals), together with water and dietary fibre, in the right amounts and proportions to meet a person's needs, with enough energy but not too much. Balanced means right proportions, not equal amounts from each food group.
  • Malnutrition is any diet that does not meet the body's needs. Undernutrition is too little energy or too little of a nutrient, e.g. iron-deficiency anaemia or rickets. Overnutrition is too much energy or too much of a nutrient, e.g. obesity or too much salt.
  • Energy balance: if the energy taken in from food equals the energy used in metabolism and activity, body mass stays the same. Excess energy is stored as fat, causing weight gain. Too little energy leads to the body using its glycogen, then fat and eventually muscle protein, and weight loss.

Ireland's Food Pyramid

  • Ireland's Food Pyramid (Department of Health, 2016) is a guide to a balanced diet, in which most servings come from the lower shelves.
  • The lower shelves are vegetables, salad and fruit (5 to 7 servings a day), and wholemeal cereals, breads, potatoes, pasta and rice.
  • Milk, yoghurt and cheese (3 servings a day, or 5 for ages 9 to 18) and meat, poultry, fish, eggs, beans and nuts (2 servings) are in the middle.
  • Fats, spreads and oils are for very small amounts, and the top shelf, foods and drinks high in fat, sugar and salt, is not for every day.

Body mass index

  • Body mass index (BMI) is mass in kilograms divided by the square of height in metres.
  • For adults, the World Health Organization classes 18.5 to 24.9 as a healthy weight, 25 to 29.9 as overweight and 30 or more as obese; below 18.5 is underweight.
  • BMI does not distinguish fat from muscle, so a muscular athlete can have a high BMI, and it does not show where fat is stored. Children and teenagers are assessed with growth charts for their age and sex instead.
  • Worked example: 70 kg ÷ (1.75 m × 1.75 m) = 70 ÷ 3.06 = 22.9, a healthy weight.

How dietary needs vary

  • Health: illnesses change needs. People with coeliac disease must avoid gluten (see Emerging diseases and society); people with type 1 diabetes match carbohydrate intake to their insulin; people with high blood pressure are advised to eat less salt (see Minerals); people with anaemia need more iron; people recovering from injury or surgery need more protein for repair; people with lactose intolerance avoid or limit lactose and need other calcium sources.
  • Age, children and teenagers: growing children need more protein, calcium and vitamin D for bones, and more energy per kilogram of body mass than adults. The teenage growth spurt raises energy, protein, calcium and iron needs, which is why the Food Pyramid gives 5 dairy servings for ages 9 to 18. Babies' needs are met by breast milk or formula.
  • Age, older people: metabolic rate and activity usually fall, so less energy is needed, but protein (to limit muscle loss), calcium and vitamin D (to limit bone loss and osteoporosis), fibre (constipation is more common) and water (the sense of thirst weakens with age) remain important.
  • Gender: on average males have more muscle and a larger body, so they need more energy and protein. Females who menstruate lose iron in blood each month, so they need more iron. Pregnancy and breastfeeding raise the need for energy, protein, calcium and iron; folic acid is advised before and in early pregnancy (see Vitamins), and pregnant women should avoid alcohol and liver, which is high in vitamin A (see Vitamins).
  • Activity: the more active a person, the more energy they use. Active people need more energy, mainly from starchy carbohydrates, which build glycogen stores; more protein for muscle repair; and more water and salt to replace sweat. Inactive people need less energy, and eating as though active leads to weight gain.

Variety of foods

  • No single food contains every nutrient in the right proportions, so a varied diet from all the food groups is the most reliable way to get all the vitamins, minerals, essential amino acids and essential fatty acids.
  • Variety within groups also matters: different fruits and vegetables supply different vitamins, minerals and fibre types, e.g. citrus fruit for vitamin C, green leafy vegetables for iron, calcium and vitamin K, and carrots for beta-carotene. Combining plant proteins, e.g. beans with cereal, supplies all the essential amino acids (see Metabolic roles and nutritional sources).

Sufficient water

  • Water is lost every day in urine, sweat, breath and faeces, and must be replaced from drinks and food (roles of water are in Water). Needs rise in hot weather, during exercise, with fever, vomiting or diarrhoea, and when breastfeeding.
  • Signs of dehydration are thirst, dark-yellow urine in small volumes (ADH; see ADH and osmoregulation at Higher Level), headache, tiredness and poor concentration. Severe dehydration is dangerous, especially in babies and older people.
  • Water is the best drink. Sugar-sweetened drinks supply water but also free sugars (see the high-sugar diets below).

The role of dietary fibre

  • Dietary fibre, or roughage, is plant material that human enzymes cannot digest, mainly cellulose from plant cell walls (see Structures of biomolecules).
  • Sources of fibre are wholegrain cereals and wholemeal bread, brown rice, vegetables, fruit (especially with skins), beans, peas and lentils, and nuts and seeds. There is no fibre in meat, fish, eggs or dairy produce.
  • Fibre adds bulk and absorbs water, so faeces are soft and large; this gives the gut muscles something to push against, keeps peristalsis effective (see Mechanical breakdown) and prevents constipation.
  • Fibre makes food more filling and slows the absorption of sugar, which helps control body mass and blood glucose. High-fibre diets are linked to a lower risk of heart disease, type 2 diabetes and bowel cancer (bowel cancer detail is in Causes, prevention and treatment of cancer).
  • Bacteria in the colon ferment some fibre, which supports a healthy gut microbiome (detail in The microbiome).
  • Raising fibre intake suddenly can cause wind and bloating, and fibre needs extra water to work. Very large amounts of bran can reduce the absorption of some minerals such as iron and calcium.

How to research a dietary claim (Skills)

  • Step 1: pose a specific, testable question, e.g. 'Does a vegan diet supply enough iron for teenage girls?'
  • Step 2: find several reliable sources, such as public health bodies, peer-reviewed reviews and textbooks (see Recognising bias and evaluating sources).
  • Step 3: check who funded and wrote each source, and whether it sells a product.
  • Step 4: judge the type of evidence. Most diet evidence comes from observational studies of large populations, which show links (correlations) but not cause, because people who choose a diet often differ in other ways; vegetarians, for example, on average smoke less and exercise more. Controlled trials give stronger evidence of cause but are usually short and small.
  • Step 5: explain the biological mechanism behind each claim, naming the nutrient and the body process.
  • Step 6: reach a balanced, justified conclusion for a particular group of people.
  • Example of funding bias: in 1967 a review of diet and heart disease, published by Harvard scientists in the New England Journal of Medicine, played down the role of sugar and blamed dietary fat. In 2016 researchers revealed that the review had been paid for by the sugar industry's trade group, the Sugar Research Foundation, without this being disclosed.

Vegetarian and vegan diets: what they are and their benefits

  • A vegetarian diet excludes meat and fish; most vegetarians eat eggs and dairy produce (lacto-ovo vegetarians). A vegan diet excludes all animal products, including eggs, dairy produce and honey. Pescatarians eat fish but not meat.
  • Benefits: vegetarian and vegan diets are usually higher in fibre, fruit and vegetables and lower in saturated fat, so on average blood cholesterol and BMI are lower, and observational studies link them to a lower risk of heart disease and type 2 diabetes.
  • They avoid processed and red meat, which are linked to bowel cancer (see Causes, prevention and treatment of cancer).
  • Producing plant food generally uses less land and water and releases less greenhouse gas than producing meat (the sustainability point is in Metabolic roles and nutritional sources).
  • Ethical reasons include animal welfare.

Vegetarian and vegan diets: risks that need planning

  • Vitamin B12 occurs naturally only in animal foods, so vegans need fortified foods or supplements to prevent anaemia and nerve damage (see Vitamins).
  • Iron from plants is less easily absorbed than iron from meat, so vegans should eat iron-rich plants (beans, lentils, green leafy vegetables and fortified cereals) with vitamin C (see Minerals).
  • Vegans must get calcium from fortified plant milks, tofu and green vegetables instead of dairy produce.
  • Iodine comes mainly from milk and seafood, so vegans may be short of it.
  • The long-chain omega-3 fatty acids are found mainly in oily fish.
  • Plant proteins must be combined to supply all the essential amino acids.
  • Teenagers, pregnant women and young children have the highest needs, so a vegan diet for them needs careful planning.

Vegetarian and vegan diets: the evidence

  • A large UK study of people's diets and health over many years, EPIC-Oxford, found that vegetarians had a lower rate of heart disease but a slightly higher rate of stroke than meat eaters, and that vegans had more bone fractures. Observational findings like these show links that may have other causes.
  • Judgement: a well-planned vegetarian or vegan diet can meet all needs, but it must replace the nutrients that meat, fish and dairy produce supply.

High-sugar diets: what counts and the recommendation

  • Free sugars are sugars added to food and drink, plus the sugars in honey, syrups and fruit juice. The sugar inside whole fruit, vegetables and milk is not counted as free sugar.
  • The World Health Organization (2015) recommends that free sugars make up less than 10% of daily energy intake, and ideally less than 5%.

High-sugar diets: risks and counter-arguments

  • Tooth decay: plaque bacteria ferment sugar to acid (see Mechanical breakdown).
  • Excess energy from sugar is stored as fat, causing weight gain and obesity, which raises the risk of type 2 diabetes, heart disease, high blood pressure and some cancers.
  • Sugary foods often supply energy with few other nutrients ('empty' energy) and replace more nutritious foods, and sugary drinks add energy without making a person feel full.
  • Sugar triggers dopamine release, which can drive overeating (see Dopamine and endorphins).
  • Counter-arguments: glucose is the brain's main fuel, and sugar is a quick energy source, useful for example during long endurance exercise or for a person with diabetes whose blood glucose is too low. Sugar in whole fruit comes with fibre and vitamins and is not the problem; the risk comes from the amount and how often it is eaten.
  • Policy link: Ireland introduced a tax on sugar-sweetened drinks in 2018.
  • Judgement: small amounts of free sugar are harmless; frequent, large amounts harm teeth and raise the risk of obesity and type 2 diabetes.

High-fat diets

  • Saturated fats, found in butter, lard, fatty meat and cheese, raise the type of blood cholesterol (LDL) that is deposited in artery walls, narrowing them (atherosclerosis) and raising the risk of heart attack and stroke.
  • Artificial trans fats, once common in processed foods, are worse than saturated fats.
  • Replacing saturated fats with unsaturated oils (olive oil, nuts and oily fish) lowers this risk.
  • Counter-argument: fat is needed for energy, insulation, cell membranes, essential fatty acids and the absorption of vitamins A, D, E and K, so a very low-fat diet can cause deficiencies (see Vitamins).
  • Fat has about twice the energy per gram of carbohydrate (see Metabolic roles and nutritional sources), so fatty food makes weight gain easy.

High-protein diets

  • Extra protein beyond needs is not stored as muscle. Excess amino acids are deaminated and the urea is excreted (see Absorption, transport and storage), and the rest is used for energy or stored as fat.
  • Muscle growth depends on training, with enough protein and energy.
  • High-protein diets based on processed and red meat bring more saturated fat, and they are often low in fibre.

Low-fibre and highly processed diets

  • Many processed and fast foods are high in fat, sugar and salt and low in fibre, vitamins and minerals.
  • Implications include constipation, a higher risk of bowel cancer, heart disease, type 2 diabetes and obesity, and high salt intake linked to high blood pressure (see Minerals).
  • Counter-point: some processed foods are useful and nutritious, e.g. frozen vegetables, tinned beans and fortified cereals, so the label, not the word processed, is what matters.

Dairy-free and gluten-free diets

  • Dairy-free and gluten-free diets are necessary for people with milk allergy, lactose intolerance (where lactose-free milk is another option) or coeliac disease.
  • For other people they bring no proven benefit and need care, since dairy produce is the main calcium and iodine source in many Irish diets, and gluten-free products are often lower in fibre and more expensive.

Skipping meals and very low-energy diets

  • Skipping meals and very low-energy (crash) diets run down glycogen stores, leading to tiredness and poor concentration; the body breaks down muscle protein as well as fat; nutrients are likely to be missed; and weight lost quickly is often regained.

Key terms

Balanced diet
A diet containing all the nutrients, water and dietary fibre in the right amounts and proportions to meet a person's needs.
Malnutrition
Any diet that does not meet the body's needs, either undernutrition or overnutrition.
Undernutrition
A diet with too little energy or too little of a nutrient, such as iron-deficiency anaemia or rickets.
Overnutrition
A diet with too much energy or too much of a nutrient, such as the diets that lead to obesity or too much salt.
Energy balance
The state in which the energy taken in from food equals the energy used, so body mass stays the same.
Body mass index (BMI)
Mass in kilograms divided by the square of height in metres, used to classify weight for adults.
Dietary fibre
Plant material, mainly cellulose, that human enzymes cannot digest and that adds bulk to faeces.
Free sugars
Sugars added to food and drink, and the sugars in honey, syrups and fruit juice, but not the sugar inside whole fruit, vegetables and milk.
Saturated fat
A fat, found in butter, lard, fatty meat and cheese, that raises LDL cholesterol in the blood.
Trans fats
Artificial fats, once common in processed foods, that raise the risk of heart disease more than saturated fats.
Atherosclerosis
The narrowing of artery walls by deposits of cholesterol, which raises the risk of heart attack and stroke.
Observational study
A study that records people's diets and health without controlling them, which can show links but not cause.
Controlled trial
A study in which conditions are controlled, which gives stronger evidence of cause but is usually short and small.
Vegan diet
A diet that excludes all animal products, including eggs, dairy produce and honey.
Lacto-ovo vegetarian
A vegetarian who eats eggs and dairy produce but not meat or fish.
