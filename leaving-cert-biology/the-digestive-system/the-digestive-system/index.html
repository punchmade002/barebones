Human nutrition and the four stages

  • Humans are heterotrophs, obtaining food from other organisms, and omnivores: they eat both plant and animal material.
  • Ingestion is taking food into the body through the mouth.
  • Digestion is the breakdown of large, insoluble food molecules into small, soluble molecules that can be absorbed. It is both mechanical (physical breakdown into smaller pieces) and chemical (hydrolysis of large molecules by enzymes).
  • Digestion is necessary because large molecules such as starch, proteins and fats cannot pass through the wall of the gut or through cell membranes. Digestion converts them to small soluble molecules (glucose, amino acids, fatty acids and glycerol) that can be absorbed into the blood.
  • Cells use the absorbed molecules to build the body's own molecules or for respiration; this use is assimilation, detailed in Absorption, transport and storage.
  • Absorption is the passage of the small soluble products of digestion, with water, vitamins and minerals, through the wall of the gut into the blood or lymph, mainly in the small intestine.
  • Egestion is the removal of undigested and unabsorbed material, mainly dietary fibre, as faeces through the anus. Egestion is not excretion: faeces were never part of the body's metabolism (except bile pigments, see Chemical breakdown), whereas excretion removes metabolic waste (see Characteristics of living things).

The alimentary canal

  • The alimentary canal, or gut, is a continuous muscular tube from the mouth to the anus. Food passes in order through the mouth, pharynx (throat), oesophagus, stomach, small intestine, large intestine, rectum and anus.
  • The gut wall contains two layers of smooth muscle, circular and longitudinal, which move food along by peristalsis (detail in Mechanical breakdown). Its inner lining makes mucus, which lubricates food and protects the lining from digestive enzymes and acid.
  • Sphincters are rings of muscle that control movement between parts, for example at the entrance and exit of the stomach (the exit one is the pyloric sphincter) and at the anus.

Mouth, oesophagus and stomach

  • The mouth is where food is ingested and chewed by the teeth and mixed with saliva from three pairs of salivary glands; the tongue shapes it into a ball, the bolus, and pushes it back for swallowing. Starch digestion begins here.
  • The oesophagus, or gullet, is a muscular tube that carries food by peristalsis from the pharynx, down through the chest behind the trachea and through the diaphragm, to the stomach. No digestion or absorption happens in it.
  • During swallowing the epiglottis covers the entrance to the trachea so that food goes down the oesophagus (breathing detail in Gas exchange and the breathing mechanism).
  • The stomach is a J-shaped muscular bag in the upper left of the abdomen, just below the diaphragm. It stores food for a few hours, churns it with gastric juice (acid and a protease) into a thick liquid called chyme, kills most bacteria in food, and begins protein digestion.
  • The pyloric sphincter releases chyme a little at a time into the small intestine.

Small intestine, large intestine, rectum and anus

  • The small intestine is the longest part of the gut, coiled in the abdomen. Its first part, the duodenum, receives bile from the liver and pancreatic juice from the pancreas, and most chemical digestion happens there.
  • The later parts, the jejunum and the ileum, complete digestion and absorb most digested food and water through millions of villi.
  • The large intestine is wider but shorter than the small intestine. Its parts are the caecum, with the appendix attached, the colon and the rectum.
  • The appendix is a small dead-end tube with no digestive role in humans.
  • The colon absorbs water and some minerals from the remaining material, and is home to many bacteria (detail in Absorption, transport and storage and The microbiome).
  • The rectum is the last part of the large intestine, which stores faeces until egestion.
  • The anus is the opening at the end of the gut, closed by sphincter muscles; the outer sphincter is under voluntary control, so egestion can be delayed.

Associated glands and organs

  • The salivary glands are three pairs, around the mouth and below the jaw, releasing saliva into the mouth through ducts. Saliva contains water, mucus and the enzyme salivary amylase.
  • The liver is the largest internal organ, in the upper right of the abdomen just below the diaphragm. Its digestive role is to make bile. It also receives the absorbed products of digestion through the hepatic portal vein, stores glycogen and deaminates amino acids (detail in Absorption, transport and storage).
  • The gallbladder is a small sac under the liver that stores and concentrates bile, and squeezes it through the bile duct into the duodenum when fatty food arrives there. It makes no bile itself.
  • The pancreas is a soft, pinkish gland behind the stomach, lying in the curve of the duodenum. It makes pancreatic juice, which contains enzymes for starch, protein and fat and sodium hydrogencarbonate, and this flows through the pancreatic duct into the duodenum.
  • The pancreas is also an endocrine gland making insulin (see Glands, locations and hormones), so it has both ducted (exocrine) and ductless (endocrine) roles.
  • The liver, gallbladder and pancreas are associated organs: food never passes through them, and their secretions reach the gut through ducts. Food does pass through the mouth, stomach and intestines.

Summary of the journey through the gut

  • Mouth: ingestion; mechanical and chemical digestion begin.
  • Oesophagus: transport of food to the stomach.
  • Stomach: storage and churning; protein digestion begins.
  • Duodenum: most chemical digestion, with bile and pancreatic juice.
  • Ileum: digestion is completed and absorption takes place.
  • Colon: absorption of water.
  • Rectum: storage of faeces.
  • Anus: egestion.

Key terms

Heterotroph
An organism that obtains its food by eating other organisms.
Omnivore
An organism that eats both plant and animal material.
Ingestion
Taking food into the body through the mouth.
Digestion
The breakdown of large, insoluble food molecules into small, soluble molecules that can be absorbed, by mechanical and chemical processes.
Absorption
The passage of the small soluble products of digestion, with water, vitamins and minerals, through the gut wall into the blood or lymph.
Assimilation
The use of absorbed molecules by body cells, such as building amino acids into proteins or respiring glucose.
Egestion
The removal of undigested and unabsorbed material from the body as faeces.
Alimentary canal
The continuous muscular tube from the mouth to the anus through which food passes.
Bolus
The ball of chewed food, shaped by the tongue, that is pushed back for swallowing.
Chyme
The thick liquid formed from food churned with gastric juice in the stomach.
Sphincter
A ring of muscle that controls movement between parts of the gut.
Pyloric sphincter
The sphincter at the exit of the stomach that releases chyme a little at a time into the duodenum.
Duodenum
The first part of the small intestine, which receives bile and pancreatic juice.
Ileum
The final part of the small intestine, where digestion is completed and most absorption takes place.
Caecum
The pouch at the start of the large intestine, to which the appendix is attached.
