The concept of the niche

  • The niche of a species is its role, or functional position, in its ecosystem. It includes what it feeds on and what feeds on it, where in the habitat it lives and feeds, when it is active, the range of abiotic conditions (temperature, moisture, light, pH) it can tolerate, its interactions with other species, and its effects on its environment.
  • Habitat and niche differ. The habitat is where an organism lives, its address; the niche is how it lives there, its profession. Many species share a habitat, but each has its own niche. In an oak woodland, for example, caterpillars feed on the leaves, woodlice and earthworms feed on dead leaves on the floor, blue tits eat caterpillars in the canopy, and fungi decompose fallen wood.
  • A species' niche is the result of its adaptations. Its structural, behavioural and physiological features suit it to a particular way of life, and they also limit it to that way of life.
  • Competitive exclusion: two species cannot occupy exactly the same niche in the same place for long. If they try, they compete for the same resources, and the better-adapted species wins while the other declines or is excluded locally.
  • Example of competitive exclusion: the grey squirrel and the red squirrel have similar niches in Irish woodland, as tree-living seed and nut eaters. In broadleaf woodland the grey squirrel is larger and can feed efficiently on acorns that red squirrels digest poorly, so it outcompetes the red squirrel. The red squirrel survives better in conifer woodland.
  • Niche differentiation: similar species can live together if their niches differ, e.g. by feeding on different foods, at different heights or at different times of day. In the same grassland, the kestrel hunts small mammals by day and the barn owl hunts them at night. Both occur in Ireland.
  • Specialist niches: a specialist has a narrow niche, one food or one habitat, which makes it efficient but vulnerable to change. The caterpillars of the marsh fritillary butterfly feed mainly on one plant, devil's-bit scabious, so the butterfly depends on the wet grasslands and bogs where that plant grows.
  • Generalist niches: a generalist has a broad niche. The red fox eats rabbits, mice, birds, insects, earthworms, fruit and scraps, and lives in countryside and towns, so it copes well with change.
  • An invasive species is often one that finds a niche unfilled, or fills an existing niche better than the native species, in its new home (e.g. the grey squirrel; see “Biodiversity loss and conservation”).

Describing a niche in an exam

  • To describe an organism's niche, state its ecosystem and habitat; its trophic level and what it eats; what eats it; when and where it is active; the conditions it tolerates; and two or three adaptations, each matched to the part of its niche it suits.

Sundew: niche and adaptations (raised and blanket bogs)

  • Niche: a producer that photosynthesises in waterlogged, acidic bog where the peat is very poor in nitrogen, and that supplements its nitrogen by catching and digesting insects.
  • Adaptation: leaves covered in red hairs (tentacles), each tipped with a drop of sticky fluid that glistens, attracts small insects and traps them.
  • Adaptation: the tentacles bend over the trapped insect.
  • Adaptation: glands secrete enzymes that digest the insect, and the leaf absorbs the products, including nitrogen compounds.
  • Adaptation: a small rosette of leaves close to the ground, in an open, sunny habitat.

Limpet: niche and adaptations (rocky shore)

  • Niche: a primary consumer (grazer) that scrapes the thin film of algae off rocks between the tides. It is eaten by birds such as oystercatchers and by crabs.
  • Adaptation: a low, conical shell that waves flow over without dislodging it.
  • Adaptation: a strong muscular foot that clamps the limpet to the rock against waves and predators.
  • Adaptation (behavioural): at low tide it clamps down onto its own home scar, a groove ground into the rock to match its shell. This seals in water and prevents drying out.
  • Adaptation: a rough, tongue-like radula that rasps algae from the rock.
  • Adaptation (behavioural): it feeds when covered by the tide and returns to its home scar.

Barn owl: niche and adaptations (farmland and grassland)

  • Niche: a nocturnal predator (secondary or tertiary consumer) of small mammals such as mice, rats and shrews, nesting in old buildings and hollow trees.
  • Adaptation: soft feathers with fringed, comb-like edges on the flight feathers make its flight almost silent.
  • Adaptation: a heart-shaped facial disc of feathers funnels sound to the ears.
  • Adaptation: ears at slightly different heights on each side of the head, so it can locate prey by sound alone in darkness.
  • Adaptation: large, forward-facing eyes give binocular vision in dim light.
  • Adaptation: sharp, curved talons and a hooked beak grip and tear prey.

Bumblebee or honeybee: niche and adaptations (grassland, meadows, hedgerows and gardens)

  • Niche: a primary consumer that feeds on nectar (energy) and pollen (protein), and a pollinator of flowering plants, transferring pollen from flower to flower. The plant side of pollination is in “Flower structure and pollination”.
  • Adaptation: a long, tube-like tongue (proboscis) to reach nectar inside flowers.
  • Adaptation: a hairy body to which pollen sticks.
  • Adaptation: pollen baskets, stiff hairs on the hind legs, carry pollen back to the nest.
  • Adaptation: eyes that see the ultraviolet patterns on petals, which guide the bee to nectar.
  • Adaptation (behavioural): honeybees communicate the location of flowers to others by a dance.

Earthworm: niche and adaptations (soil of grassland and woodland)

  • Niche: a detritivore that eats dead leaves and soil organic matter, recycling nutrients. It aerates and drains the soil with its burrows and mixes the soil layers, and it is food for blackbirds, thrushes, badgers and foxes.
  • Adaptation: a long, thin, segmented body without legs for burrowing.
  • Adaptation: tiny bristles (chaetae) on each segment grip the burrow wall.
  • Adaptation: mucus lubricates movement and binds the burrow walls.
  • Adaptation: a thin, moist skin exchanges gases, so the earthworm lives in damp soil and comes to the surface mainly at night or in wet weather.
  • Adaptation (behavioural): it pulls leaves into its burrow, so it feeds below ground, away from predators.

Marram grass: niche and adaptations (sand dunes)

  • Niche: a pioneer producer that colonises loose, dry, salty sand and binds it into dunes, allowing other plants to settle later. Plant water-loss detail is in “Transport in Plants”.
  • Adaptation: leaves roll into a tube in dry conditions, with stomata on the inner surface in grooves lined with hairs. This traps moist air and reduces water loss by transpiration.
  • Adaptation: long roots reach water deep in the sand.
  • Adaptation: underground stems (rhizomes) spread and bind the sand.
  • Adaptation: it grows upward when it is buried by blowing sand.

Key terms

Niche
The role of a species in its ecosystem, including what it feeds on, where and when it is active, the conditions it tolerates and its interactions with other species.
Competitive exclusion
The principle that two species cannot occupy exactly the same niche in the same place for long, so the better-adapted species wins and the other declines or is excluded locally.
Niche differentiation
The way similar species live together because their niches differ, e.g. in their food, height or time of activity.
Specialist
A species with a narrow niche, such as one food or one habitat, which makes it efficient but vulnerable to change.
Generalist
A species with a broad niche, which allows it to feed on many foods and live in many habitats, so it copes well with change.
Adaptation
A structural, behavioural or physiological feature that suits an organism to its way of life and niche.
Pioneer species
A species that colonises bare or loose ground first, such as marram grass on sand dunes.
Invasive species
A non-native species introduced by people that spreads and harms native species and ecosystems.
Habitat
The place where an organism lives, which provides the food, shelter and conditions it needs.
