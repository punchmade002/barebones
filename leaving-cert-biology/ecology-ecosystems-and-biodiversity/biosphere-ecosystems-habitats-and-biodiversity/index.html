Ecology and the levels of organisation

  • Ecology is the study of the interactions between living organisms, and between organisms and their non-living environment. The word comes from the Greek oikos, meaning house or home.
  • A population is all the members of one species living in a particular area at the same time, e.g. all the daisies in a field, or all the red squirrels in a woodland. Members of a population can interbreed (species is defined in “Classification”).
  • A community is all the populations of all the different species living and interacting in an area at the same time, e.g. all the plants, animals, fungi and microorganisms of a woodland. A community is the living (biotic) part of an ecosystem only.
  • The ecological levels, smallest to largest, are: organism, population, community, ecosystem, biosphere. Each level includes the one before it; from the ecosystem upward the non-living environment is included.
  • These levels continue the levels of organisation in “Levels of organisation” (cell, tissue, organ, organ system, organism).
  • Diagram nested boxes or a stepped sequence showing one rabbit (organism), a group of rabbits (population), rabbits with grass, foxes and other species (community), the community with soil, water, air and light (ecosystem), and the Earth's living layer (biosphere). Label each level.

The biosphere

  • The biosphere is the part of the Earth where life exists: the lower atmosphere, the land surface and soil, and the water (fresh water and oceans). It is the sum of all the Earth's ecosystems.
  • The biosphere is a thin layer compared with the size of the Earth. Life is found from the deep ocean floor to a few kilometres up in the air, in birds, spores and bacteria carried on the wind. Most life is concentrated in a narrow zone near the surface, where light, liquid water and suitable temperatures are present.
  • The biosphere is a single, closed system for matter, because atoms are recycled and not added, but an open system for energy: energy enters as sunlight and leaves as heat. Energy transfer is detailed in “Energy and nutrient transfer”; the nutrient cycles are in “The carbon cycle” and “The nitrogen cycle”.

Ecosystems

  • An ecosystem is a community of organisms interacting with each other and with their non-living (abiotic) environment in a particular area. It has living (biotic) and non-living (abiotic) parts that affect each other.
  • Biotic factors are the living parts of the environment and the effects organisms have on each other: food supply, predation, competition, disease, parasites, pollinators and decomposers.
  • Abiotic factors are the non-living physical and chemical conditions of the environment: light, temperature, water, oxygen, soil type and pH, minerals, wind, slope and aspect.
  • Measuring biotic and abiotic factors is covered in “Abiotic factors” within “Investigating an Ecosystem”; how the factors limit populations is in “Carrying capacity and limiting factors”.
  • Ecosystems have no fixed size: a rock pool, a hedgerow, a lake, a raised bog or an ocean can each be treated as an ecosystem. Their boundaries are often gradual, e.g. a woodland edge merging into grassland.
  • Every ecosystem needs a continual input of energy, almost always sunlight captured by producers in photosynthesis, while its matter is recycled by decomposers. The roles of producers, consumers and decomposers are explained in “Energy and nutrient transfer”.
  • Irish ecosystems include: grassland, the most widespread land cover in Ireland, mostly farmed pasture and meadow; woodland, e.g. the native oak woodlands of Killarney National Park; peatlands, with raised bogs in the midlands and blanket bogs in the uplands and the west; freshwater, such as lakes like Lough Derg, a lake on the River Shannon, and rivers; and coastal and marine ecosystems, including rocky shores, sandy shores and sand dunes, salt marshes and the open sea.

Habitats and microhabitats

  • A habitat is the place where an organism lives, which provides the food, shelter and conditions it needs. A woodlouse's habitat is damp leaf litter, a limpet's is the rocky shore, and a frog's is a pond and the damp vegetation around it.
  • A microhabitat is a small area within a habitat with its own conditions, e.g. the underside of a stone on a rocky shore, a rock pool, a rotting log, or the bark of a tree. Shade, moisture and temperature there can differ greatly from the surroundings.
  • One ecosystem contains many habitats: a woodland contains the canopy, the trunk, the leaf litter and the soil, each with different species. A habitat is the organism's “address”; its role there is its niche, taught in “The niche”.

Biodiversity and its three levels

  • Biodiversity (biological diversity) is the variety of life in an area, or on Earth, at all its levels. It is usually described at three levels: genetic diversity, species diversity and ecosystem (habitat) diversity.
  • Genetic diversity is the variety of alleles (genes) within a species or population, e.g. the different breeds of cattle, or the variation in disease resistance among wild ash trees. It allows a population to adapt by natural selection when conditions change.
  • Low genetic diversity left the Irish potato crop of the 1840s vulnerable: it was largely one variety, and blight destroyed it (detail in “Variation”).
  • Species diversity is the variety of species in an area. It has two parts: species richness, the number of different species present, and species evenness, how evenly the individuals are spread among those species.
  • Two areas with the same species can differ in diversity if one is dominated by a single species. The Simpson's Diversity Index, which combines richness and evenness into one number, is taught in “Simpson's Diversity Index” and is Higher Level only.
  • Ecosystem (habitat) diversity is the variety of different ecosystems and habitats in a region, e.g. Ireland's bogs, woodlands, grasslands, lakes, rivers and coasts. A region with many habitats supports more species.

Where biodiversity is highest

  • Biodiversity is not spread evenly. It is generally higher in warm, wet, stable environments and lower in cold or extreme ones. Tropical rainforests and coral reefs are the most species-rich ecosystems.
  • Ireland has fewer species than Britain or mainland Europe, partly because it became an island after the last ice age, before many species had recolonised it from the south.
  • Why biodiversity matters, its loss, and conservation are taught in “Biodiversity loss and conservation”. In brief, biodiversity provides food, medicines and materials, supports processes such as pollination and nutrient cycling, and makes ecosystems more stable.

Key terms

Ecology
The study of the interactions between living organisms, and between organisms and their non-living environment.
Population
All the members of one species living in a particular area at the same time.
Community
All the populations of all the different species living and interacting in an area at the same time.
Ecosystem
A community of organisms interacting with each other and with their non-living environment in a particular area.
Biosphere
The part of the Earth where life exists, consisting of the lower atmosphere, the land surface and soil, and the water; the sum of all the Earth's ecosystems.
Habitat
The place where an organism lives, which provides the food, shelter and conditions it needs.
Microhabitat
A small area within a habitat with its own conditions, such as the underside of a stone or a rotting log.
Biotic factor
A living part of the environment, or an effect organisms have on each other, such as food supply, predation, competition or disease.
Abiotic factor
A non-living physical or chemical condition of the environment, such as light, temperature, water, oxygen or soil pH.
Biodiversity
The variety of life in an area, or on Earth, at all its levels.
Genetic diversity
The variety of alleles (genes) within a species or population.
Species diversity
The variety of species in an area, made up of species richness and species evenness.
Species richness
The number of different species present in an area.
Species evenness
How evenly the individuals are spread among the species present in an area.
Ecosystem (habitat) diversity
The variety of different ecosystems and habitats in a region.
