Why biodiversity matters

  • Ecosystem services are the benefits people get from ecosystems. Provisioning services include food (crops, fish and game), timber, fibre, clean water and medicines. Many medicines come from organisms: penicillin from the mould Penicillium, and aspirin's forerunner from willow bark.
  • Regulating services include pollination of crops by insects, purification of water by wetlands and soil, and flood control, as bogs and wetlands soak up rain and release it slowly. They also include carbon storage in bogs, forests and the sea, and pest control by predators.
  • Supporting services include soil formation and nutrient cycling by decomposers. Cultural services include recreation, tourism, education, heritage and wellbeing.
  • Resilience: an ecosystem with many species and much genetic diversity is more stable. If one species declines, others with similar roles can take its place, and some individuals are likely to survive a new disease or a change in climate. Low-diversity systems, such as a single crop variety, can collapse from one disease.
  • Ethical argument: many people argue that species have a right to exist regardless of their usefulness, and that this generation has a duty to pass on a living world to future generations (see “The cross-cutting themes”). Extinction is permanent.

Causes of biodiversity loss

  • Habitat loss and fragmentation is the biggest cause: land is drained, built on, cut, or converted to intensive farming or plantation forestry. Fragmentation leaves small, isolated patches that support only small populations, which are more easily wiped out. Irish examples: drainage and cutting of raised bogs, drainage of wet grassland, and removal of hedgerows.
  • Agricultural intensification: earlier and more frequent mowing for silage, reseeding with a few grass species, and more fertiliser reduce the variety of wild plants and destroy the nests of ground-nesting birds such as the curlew and the corncrake.
  • Pollution: excess nitrogen and phosphorus from fertiliser, slurry and sewage cause eutrophication of rivers and lakes (the sequence of events is in “Ethics and sustainability of nutrient cycling”). Silt from drainage, forestry and land works smothers river beds, and pesticides harm insects and their predators.
  • Invasive species are non-native species introduced by people that spread and harm native species and ecosystems, often because they have no natural predators or diseases in their new home. Irish examples: grey squirrel, zebra mussel and Rhododendron ponticum.
  • Overexploitation is harvesting faster than a population can replace itself, e.g. overfishing, peat extraction and over-collection.
  • Climate change: species must shift their range or the timing of their life cycles. Cold-adapted species, and those in fragmented habitats, cannot always move far enough (detail in “Climate change and biological strategies”).
  • Disease: new pathogens spread by trade. Ash dieback, a fungal disease recorded in Ireland in 2012, is killing ash trees in hedgerows and woodland (detail in “Emerging diseases and society”).

Case study: raised bogs

  • Raised bogs are dome-shaped peatlands, mainly in the Irish midlands, formed over thousands of years as Sphagnum moss grew over waterlogged ground. They are wet, acidic and low in nutrients.
  • Raised bogs support specialised species: Sphagnum mosses, sundews (insect-eating plants), bog cotton and heather, and animals such as the curlew, the red grouse and the Irish hare.
  • Loss came from centuries of hand turf cutting, then large-scale industrial peat extraction for fuel and horticulture in the twentieth century, plus drainage for farming and forestry. Less than about 1% of the original raised bog area is still active raised bog that is forming peat.
  • Environmental impacts: loss of a rare habitat and its specialised species; drained peat releases stored carbon (detail in “Climate change and biological strategies”); and loss of flood control and water purification.
  • Economic impacts: peat fuelled power stations and supported jobs in the midlands, so ending extraction cost jobs, while restoration can bring tourism and “green” jobs.
  • Social and cultural impacts: turf cutting for home fuel is a centuries-old rural tradition, and many families hold turbary (turf-cutting) rights. Bogs preserve archaeology and are part of Irish heritage and literature.
  • Conservation: many raised bogs are protected as Special Areas of Conservation under the EU Habitats Directive. Turf cutting on them is banned, and compensation is offered to turf cutters, which has been controversial.
  • Bord na Móna ended industrial peat harvesting in 2021. Drained bogs are being rewetted, by blocking drains to raise the water table, so that Sphagnum can regrow.
  • Arguments: restoration protects species and stores carbon, but it removes a traditional right and a cheap fuel, so it needs compensation and local support to succeed.

Case study: the curlew

  • The curlew is a large wading bird with a long, down-curved bill that breeds on bogs and wet grassland. A national survey in 2015–2017 found only 138 breeding pairs in the Republic of Ireland, a decline of at least 96% since the 1980s. A 2021 survey found about 105 pairs. The curlew is now one of Ireland's most threatened breeding birds.
  • Causes of the decline: loss and drainage of bogs and wet grassland; plantation forestry on breeding sites; intensive farming with early mowing; and predation of eggs and chicks by foxes and crows. Predation is worse because small breeding areas are surrounded by forest and farmland that support many predators.
  • Conservation: the Curlew Conservation Programme, run by the National Parks and Wildlife Service (NPWS) since 2017, employs local nest-finders and works with farmers to protect nests (e.g. with electric fencing against foxes), manage habitat and control predators.

Case study: the corncrake

  • The corncrake is a secretive grassland bird with a rasping call that was once heard across rural Ireland in summer. Its population has fallen by more than 96% since the 1970s. It now breeds mainly in Co. Donegal (including offshore islands) and on the western coast of Mayo and Galway.
  • Main cause: mechanised mowing of hay and silage during the nesting season destroys nests. Mowing from the edge of a field inwards traps the chicks in the centre.
  • Conservation: farmers are paid to delay mowing until the chicks can fly, to mow from the centre of the field outwards so chicks can escape to the edges, and to leave tall vegetation as cover. The Corncrake LIFE project, EU-funded and launched in 2022, uses payments based on results.
  • Cultural impact of both birds: the sounds of the curlew and the corncrake are part of rural Irish life, folklore, music and place names, so their loss is felt as a cultural loss as well as an ecological one.

Invasive species: grey squirrel and pine marten

  • The grey squirrel was introduced from North America. The Irish population began with a few animals released in 1911 at Castle Forbes, Co. Longford. It spread across the eastern half of the island, and red squirrels declined where it arrived.
  • The grey squirrel is larger and competes with the red squirrel for food, because their niches overlap (detail in “The niche”). It carries squirrelpox virus, which does not harm grey squirrels but is usually fatal to red squirrels. It also damages trees by stripping their bark.
  • The native pine marten, protected under the Wildlife Act 1976, has recovered. Where pine martens are common, e.g. in the Irish midlands, grey squirrels have declined or disappeared and red squirrels have returned. The heavier grey squirrel is more easily caught.
  • This shows how restoring a native predator can help a native species.

Invasive species: zebra mussel and quagga mussel

  • The zebra mussel is a small freshwater mussel native to the Ponto-Caspian region, around the Black and Caspian Seas. It was first recorded in Ireland in Lough Derg on the Shannon in 1997, probably brought on boats. It spreads attached to boat hulls and in bilge water.
  • The zebra mussel filters huge volumes of water, removing phytoplankton, the base of the food chain. The water becomes clearer, but food for zooplankton and young fish falls.
  • The zebra mussel smothers native mussels and blocks water-intake pipes, an economic cost.
  • The zebra mussel has itself been displaced in Lough Derg by a related invader, the quagga mussel, first recognised there in 2021. The quagga mussel is a separate species.

Invasive species: rhododendron in Killarney

  • Rhododendron ponticum is a shrub introduced to Killarney for ornamental planting in the nineteenth century. It forms dense thickets in the native oak woodlands of Killarney National Park.
  • Its evergreen leaves let very little light reach the woodland floor, so oak seedlings and ground plants cannot grow and the wood cannot regenerate.
  • Clearing it by cutting and treating regrowth is slow and costly, and the work must be repeated.

Other invasive species and prevention

  • Elodea canadensis (Canadian pondweed) is an invasive plant in fresh water.
  • Japanese knotweed spreads from small root fragments and damages buildings and riverbanks.
  • Prevention: biosecurity, e.g. the “check, clean, dry” routine for boats and angling gear moved between waters, and never releasing aquarium plants or pets into the wild.

Water pollution and freshwater species

  • The freshwater pearl mussel is a long-lived mussel of clean, fast-flowing rivers. Its larvae (glochidia) must attach to the gills of young native salmon or trout to develop, and the juveniles need clean, well-oxygenated gravel.
  • Silt and nutrient enrichment from drainage, forestry and farming clog the gravel. In many Irish rivers few or no young mussels survive, and populations consist only of old adults. Its decline is also linked to the decline of salmon and trout, its hosts.
  • Lough Neagh, in Northern Ireland and the largest lake on the island of Ireland, had a massive bloom of blue-green algae (cyanobacteria) in summer 2023. It was linked mainly to excess phosphorus from agriculture and wastewater built up over decades, along with warm, still weather and zebra mussels.
  • Impacts of the Lough Neagh bloom: toxic water unsafe for people, pets and livestock; harm to fish and birds; damage to the eel fishery and to tourism; and anger in local communities.

Impacts of biodiversity loss

  • Environmental: food webs are disrupted when a species is lost, affecting its predators and prey. Ecosystem services such as pollination, water purification and flood control are lost, ecosystems become less resilient, and extinction is irreversible.
  • Environmental example: about one third of Ireland's wild bee species are threatened with extinction, mainly through loss of flowers and nesting sites. This threatens the pollination of wild plants and crops.
  • Economic: farming depends on pollinators and healthy soil; fisheries and angling depend on clean rivers and lakes; tourism, e.g. at Killarney National Park, the Burren and the bogs, depends on landscapes and wildlife.
  • Economic costs: invasive species cost money to control, e.g. clearing rhododendron or unblocking pipes fouled by zebra mussels, and polluted water costs more to treat. Conservation also has costs, including compensation to farmers and turf cutters and lost income from land use.
  • Social: health and wellbeing benefit from access to nature. Polluted water threatens drinking supplies and recreation, e.g. on Lough Neagh.
  • Social conflict: communities that depend on fishing, farming or peat can lose their livelihoods either from biodiversity loss or from conservation rules, which causes conflict.
  • Cultural: species and landscapes are part of identity, heritage, folklore, music, literature and place names, e.g. the curlew, the corncrake and the bogs. Traditional practices such as turf cutting are themselves cultural heritage, which is why some conservation measures are resisted.

Approaches to conservation

  • Conservation is the protection, management and restoration of the natural environment to maintain biodiversity and prevent extinction, while allowing sustainable use.
  • In situ conservation protects species in their natural habitat, through protected areas such as national parks. Killarney National Park was Ireland's first, in 1932.
  • Natura 2000 sites across the EU include Special Areas of Conservation (SACs) under the EU Habitats Directive (1992) and Special Protection Areas (SPAs) for birds under the EU Birds Directive (1979).
  • Species protection under the Wildlife Act 1976 made it illegal to kill or disturb protected species.
  • Habitat management and restoration: rewetting bogs; planting native trees and hedgerows; farming for nature through agri-environment schemes that pay farmers for results such as species-rich meadows (e.g. in the Burren); and controlling invasive species.
  • Species recovery programmes include the Curlew Conservation Programme and the Corncrake LIFE project.
  • The All-Ireland Pollinator Plan, launched in 2015, asks councils, farmers, schools and gardeners to provide flowers and nesting sites for bees, e.g. by mowing road verges less often.
  • Ex situ conservation protects species outside their natural habitat, through seed banks, captive breeding and reintroduction, e.g. captive rearing of freshwater pearl mussels to release young mussels into rivers.
  • Policy: in May 2019 the Dáil declared a climate and biodiversity emergency, making Ireland the second country to do so. The Citizens' Assembly on Biodiversity Loss reported in April 2023 with 159 recommendations. Ireland's fourth National Biodiversity Action Plan covers 2023–2030, and the EU Nature Restoration Law, adopted in 2024, requires member states to restore damaged ecosystems.
  • Individuals can record species for citizen-science surveys, e.g. through the National Biodiversity Data Centre, garden for pollinators, and avoid spreading invasive species.

Evaluating conservation

  • Arguments for its effectiveness: protection has brought recoveries, e.g. the pine marten after 1976, with the return of red squirrels that followed. Rewetting can restore bog habitat. Targeted payments have raised the breeding success of curlew and corncrake in some areas. Protected areas keep the last strongholds of rare species.
  • Limits: declines continue, as curlew numbers fell further between 2015–17 and 2021. Designating a site is not enough without enforcement and management. Schemes cost money and can conflict with the incomes and traditions of farmers, turf cutters and landowners.
  • Small, isolated populations may still be lost to predators, disease or chance. Restoration is slow: bogs take centuries to re-form.
  • Conservation limits biodiversity loss when it has funding, enforcement and the support of the local people who manage the land.

Exploring local ecosystems through news and local media

  • 1. Find a current local story about a local ecosystem or species. Sources include local newspapers and radio, national news, the websites of the NPWS and the EPA (water quality), BirdWatch Ireland, the National Biodiversity Data Centre, and the county council's heritage or biodiversity officer.
  • 2. Identify the ecosystem, the species affected, the cause, and the evidence, such as survey data, photographs or expert opinion.
  • 3. Evaluate the source: who wrote it, whether it is based on data, whether it shows bias (e.g. from a campaign group or an industry body), and whether other sources agree (see “Recognising bias and evaluating sources” and “Evaluating evidence”).
  • 4. Describe the impacts under the four headings: environmental, economic, social and cultural.
  • 5. Assess what conservation is being done and whether it is likely to work.
  • 6. Where possible, visit or survey the ecosystem to gather primary data (methods are in “Investigating an Ecosystem”).

Key terms

Conservation
The protection, management and restoration of the natural environment to maintain biodiversity and prevent extinction, while allowing sustainable use.
Ecosystem services
The benefits people get from ecosystems, grouped as provisioning, regulating, supporting and cultural services.
Resilience
The ability of an ecosystem to remain stable or recover after change, which is higher where there are many species and much genetic diversity.
Habitat fragmentation
The breaking up of a habitat into small, isolated patches that support only small populations.
Agricultural intensification
Farming practices such as earlier mowing, reseeding and more fertiliser that reduce wild plant variety and destroy wildlife habitat.
Invasive species
A non-native species introduced by people that spreads and harms native species and ecosystems.
Overexploitation
Harvesting a population faster than it can replace itself.
Eutrophication
Enrichment of a lake or river with nutrients, such as nitrogen and phosphorus, which can lead to algal blooms.
Raised bog
A dome-shaped, wet, acidic and nutrient-poor peatland formed as Sphagnum moss grows over waterlogged ground.
Turbary
The traditional right to cut turf for fuel on a bog.
Rewetting
Blocking drains on a drained bog to raise the water table so that Sphagnum can regrow.
Special Area of Conservation (SAC)
A site protected under the EU Habitats Directive, forming part of the Natura 2000 network.
Special Protection Area (SPA)
A site protected under the EU Birds Directive for birds, forming part of the Natura 2000 network.
In situ conservation
Conservation of species in their natural habitat, e.g. through protected areas.
Ex situ conservation
Conservation of species outside their natural habitat, e.g. through seed banks, captive breeding and reintroduction.
Glochidia
The larvae of the freshwater pearl mussel, which must attach to the gills of young salmon or trout to develop.
Citizen science
Scientific surveys in which members of the public record species and report their observations.
Biosecurity
Measures, such as cleaning boats and gear between waters, that stop invasive species from spreading.
