What the index measures (Higher Level only)

  • A diversity index is a single number that measures the species diversity of a sample, taking into account both the number of species (richness) and how evenly the individuals are spread among them (evenness). Simpson's Diversity Index is the one in the specification.
  • The equation is Ds = 1 − Σn(n − 1) / N(N − 1). Here n is the total number of organisms of a particular species, and N is the total number of organisms of all species in the sample. Σ is the Greek capital letter sigma and means “the sum of”, so n(n − 1) is worked out for each species and the results are added. In words, the sum Σn(n − 1) is divided by N(N − 1), and the answer is subtracted from 1.
  • What it means: Ds is the probability that two individuals picked at random from the sample (the first not put back) belong to different species. Σn(n − 1) / N(N − 1) is the probability that both belong to the same species, and Ds is 1 minus this.
  • It is better than species richness alone because it gives less weight to rare species and shows when a habitat is dominated by one or two species. Two samples with the same species list can have very different Ds values.

Range and interpretation (Higher Level only)

  • Ds lies between 0 and 1. Ds = 0 when only one species is present, meaning there is no diversity. The closer Ds is to 1, the higher the diversity.
  • For a given number of species, Ds is highest when all the species have equal numbers. A species represented by one individual contributes 1 × 0 = 0 to the sum.
  • A high Ds generally indicates a stable, healthy ecosystem that is more resilient to change. A low Ds indicates domination by a few species, often after disturbance or pollution.

Method (Higher Level only)

  • 1. Draw a table with columns for species, n, n − 1 and n(n − 1).
  • 2. Fill in n for each species from the sample.
  • 3. Calculate n − 1, then n(n − 1), for each species.
  • 4. Add the n column to get N.
  • 5. Add the n(n − 1) column to get Σn(n − 1).
  • 6. Calculate N(N − 1).
  • 7. Divide Σn(n − 1) by N(N − 1).
  • 8. Subtract the answer from 1 to get Ds.
  • 9. Give Ds to two decimal places and interpret it, by comparing it with another sample or by saying whether it is near 0 or near 1.
  • Common errors: using n² instead of n(n − 1); forgetting to subtract from 1; working out N(N − 1) for each species instead of once for the whole sample; and rounding too early. Keep four decimal places until the last step.

Worked example 1: an untrampled meadow (sample data) (Higher Level only)

  • Sample data: daisy 10, dandelion 5, white clover 3, buttercup 2. N = 10 + 5 + 3 + 2 = 20.
  • n(n − 1): daisy 10 × 9 = 90; dandelion 5 × 4 = 20; clover 3 × 2 = 6; buttercup 2 × 1 = 2. Σn(n − 1) = 90 + 20 + 6 + 2 = 118.
  • N(N − 1) = 20 × 19 = 380. Σn(n − 1) / N(N − 1) = 118 / 380 = 0.31 (0.3105). Ds = 1 − 0.3105 = 0.69.

Worked example 2: a trampled path in the same field (sample data) (Higher Level only)

  • Sample data: daisy 16, dandelion 2, white clover 1, buttercup 1. N = 20. These are the same four species (the same richness) as example 1.
  • n(n − 1): daisy 16 × 15 = 240; dandelion 2 × 1 = 2; clover 1 × 0 = 0; buttercup 1 × 0 = 0. Σn(n − 1) = 242.
  • N(N − 1) = 380. 242 / 380 = 0.64 (0.6368). Ds = 1 − 0.6368 = 0.36.
  • Interpretation: both samples have four species and 20 plants, but the path is dominated by one trampling-tolerant species (daisy), so its evenness and its diversity are lower (0.36 against 0.69). Species richness alone would have shown no difference. This links to “Investigation: human activity and species diversity”.

Worked example 3: comparing evenness and richness (sample data) (Higher Level only)

  • Perfectly even sample: four species with 5 individuals each. N = 20. n(n − 1) = 5 × 4 = 20 for each, so Σn(n − 1) = 80. 80 / 380 = 0.2105. Ds = 0.79, the highest possible for four species and 20 individuals.
  • One species only: 20 individuals of one species. Σn(n − 1) = 20 × 19 = 380; 380 / 380 = 1; Ds = 1 − 1 = 0.
  • A richer sample, e.g. a rocky-shore quadrat: barnacles 12, limpets 8, dog whelks 6, periwinkles 3, beadlet anemone 1. N = 30. n(n − 1): 132, 56, 30, 6, 0; Σn(n − 1) = 224. N(N − 1) = 30 × 29 = 870. 224 / 870 = 0.2575. Ds = 0.74.
  • A six-species sample: 9, 6, 4, 3, 2, 1. N = 25. n(n − 1): 72, 30, 12, 6, 2, 0; Σ = 122. N(N − 1) = 25 × 24 = 600. 122 / 600 = 0.2033. Ds = 0.80.

Using the index (Higher Level only)

  • Uses: comparing the diversity of two habitats or sites, e.g. trampled and untrampled grassland, upstream and downstream of a pollution source, or a native woodland and a conifer plantation.
  • Uses: monitoring the same site over time to see whether diversity is falling or recovering, e.g. after bog rewetting or a change in farming, and judging the effect of conservation or human activity.
  • Limitations: the index depends on accurate identification and counting. Samples must be large, taken at random and taken by the same method for a fair comparison.
  • Limitations: it is hard to count individual plants that grow in clumps, e.g. grasses, so percentage cover is sometimes used instead, which this equation does not take into account.
  • Limitations: it treats all species as equally important, so it does not show whether the species are native, rare or invasive, and a sample is a snapshot at one time of year.
  • Other versions of Simpson's index exist in textbooks and websites, e.g. D = Σn(n − 1) / N(N − 1) without subtracting from 1 (where a low value means high diversity), or its reciprocal. The specification uses Ds = 1 − Σn(n − 1) / N(N − 1), and this form should always be used.

Key terms

Diversity index
A single number that measures the species diversity of a sample, taking into account both richness and evenness.
Simpson's Diversity Index
A diversity index, Ds = 1 − Σn(n − 1) / N(N − 1), giving the probability that two individuals picked at random belong to different species.
Ds
The value given by Simpson's Diversity Index, from 0 (one species only) to 1 (high diversity).
Sigma (Σ)
The Greek capital letter used in the Simpson's Diversity Index to mean “the sum of”.
Species richness
The number of different species present in a sample.
Species evenness
How evenly the individuals are spread among the species present in a sample.
