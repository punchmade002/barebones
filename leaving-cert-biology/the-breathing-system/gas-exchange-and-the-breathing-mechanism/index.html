Why gas exchange is needed

Gas exchange supplies every cell with oxygen and removes the carbon dioxide it produces.

  • Every cell respires aerobically, using oxygen and producing carbon dioxide (see ‘Respiration as a catabolic process’).
  • The breathing system exchanges gases between the external environment (the air) and the internal environment (the blood and tissue fluid): oxygen is taken into the blood, and carbon dioxide and some water vapour are removed.
  • Breathing, or ventilation, is the movement of air into and out of the lungs. It is not respiration; the distinction is made in ‘Characteristics of living things’.
  • A large animal such as a human cannot get enough oxygen by diffusion through its skin: its surface area is too small for its volume, and the distances to its inner cells are too great. It needs a specialised exchange surface, the alveoli, and a transport system, the blood, to carry gases to and from every cell.

The breathing tract: structure and function

  • Nose (nasal cavity): hairs and sticky mucus trap dust and microorganisms. The lining has a rich blood supply that warms the air, and the mucus moistens it, so the delicate alveoli do not dry out or chill. Breathing through the mouth skips this conditioning.
  • Pharynx (throat): the passage shared by air and food, behind the nose and mouth.
  • Epiglottis: a flap of cartilage that closes over the entrance to the larynx and trachea during swallowing, so food and drink pass into the oesophagus. Choking happens when food enters the trachea.
  • Larynx (voice box): at the top of the trachea. Its vocal cords vibrate as air passes over them, producing sound.
  • Trachea (windpipe): a tube held open by C-shaped rings of cartilage, so it does not collapse when the pressure inside falls during inhalation. The rings are incomplete at the back, where the trachea lies against the oesophagus, so the oesophagus can expand as food is swallowed.
  • Lining of the trachea and bronchi: ciliated epithelium with mucus-secreting goblet cells. Mucus traps dust and microorganisms, and the cilia beat in waves that sweep the mucus up to the throat, where it is swallowed or coughed out. This is part of the first line of defence (see ‘Innate and adaptive immunity’).
  • Bronchi: the trachea divides into two bronchi, one to each lung. They are also supported by cartilage.
  • Bronchioles: each bronchus branches repeatedly into thousands of narrower bronchioles. They have no cartilage; their walls contain smooth muscle, which can narrow or widen them and so change air flow. The finest bronchioles end in clusters of alveoli.
  • Alveoli (air sacs): tiny thin-walled sacs, each surrounded by a network of capillaries. They are the site of gas exchange, described below.
  • Lungs, pleural membranes and thorax: the two lungs are spongy, elastic organs in the thorax (chest cavity), protected by the rib cage (see ‘The axial skeleton’) and separated from the abdomen by the diaphragm. Each lung is enclosed by two pleural membranes with a thin layer of pleural fluid between them. The fluid reduces friction as the lungs move and makes them cling to the chest wall, so they expand when the thorax expands.
  • Diaphragm: a dome-shaped sheet of muscle and tendon forming the floor of the thorax. Intercostal muscles: two sets, external and internal, lying between the ribs, which move the rib cage.

The breathing mechanism

  • Air always moves from a region of higher pressure to one of lower pressure. Breathing changes the volume of the thorax: when the volume increases, the pressure inside the lungs falls below atmospheric pressure and air flows in; when the volume decreases, the pressure rises above atmospheric pressure and air flows out. The lungs have no muscle of their own; they follow the chest wall.
  • Inhalation (inspiration) is an active process: 1. The brain sends nerve impulses to the breathing muscles.
  • 2. The diaphragm contracts and flattens, moving downwards.
  • 3. The external intercostal muscles contract, pulling the ribs and sternum upwards and outwards.
  • 4. The volume of the thorax increases.
  • 5. The lungs expand with it, so the pressure inside them falls below atmospheric pressure.
  • 6. Air rushes in through the nose and trachea until the pressures are equal. Muscle contraction uses ATP, so inhalation is active.
  • Exhalation (expiration) at rest is a passive process: 1. The impulses stop and the diaphragm and external intercostal muscles relax.
  • 2. The diaphragm returns to its dome shape, pushed up by the abdominal organs, and the ribs move down and in under their own weight.
  • 3. The stretched, elastic lung tissue recoils.
  • 4. The volume of the thorax decreases and the pressure in the lungs rises above atmospheric pressure.
  • 5. Air is forced out. No muscle contracts, so no energy is used.
  • Forced exhalation, as in exercise, coughing, blowing or speaking loudly: the internal intercostal muscles contract, pulling the ribs down and in, and the abdominal muscles contract, pushing the diaphragm up. Air is expelled faster and more completely, so forced exhalation is active.
  • Inhalation compared with exhalation at rest: the diaphragm contracts and flattens, versus relaxes and domes upwards; the external intercostals contract, versus relax; the ribs move up and out, versus down and in; the thorax volume increases, versus decreases; the lung pressure falls below atmospheric pressure, versus rises above it; air moves in, versus out; inhalation is active, versus exhalation at rest being passive.
  • The bell-jar model shows the volume and pressure principle: pulling the rubber sheet (diaphragm) down inflates the balloons (lungs). Its parts and limitations are described in ‘Models’.

The brain and CO₂ levels

  • Breathing is controlled automatically by the breathing (respiratory) centre in the medulla oblongata, at the base of the brain (see ‘CNS and PNS’). It sends rhythmic nerve impulses to the diaphragm and intercostal muscles, so breathing continues without thought, even during sleep.
  • We can change breathing voluntarily for a short time, using the cerebrum, for example by holding our breath, speaking or singing. The automatic control takes over again afterwards.
  • The main factor that sets the rate and depth of breathing is the level of carbon dioxide in the blood. When CO₂ rises, as during exercise when muscles respire faster, the breathing centre increases the rate and depth of breathing, so more CO₂ is removed and more oxygen is taken in. When CO₂ falls, breathing slows. How the rise is detected is described in ‘CO₂ as a controlling factor in breathing’.
  • An adult at rest breathes about 12 to 20 times per minute, moving about 500 cm³ (half a litre) of air in each breath, the tidal volume. During hard exercise both the rate and the depth of breathing increase greatly.

Gas exchange at the alveoli and capillaries

  • Gases cross between the air in an alveolus and the blood in its capillaries by diffusion, from where they are more concentrated to where they are less concentrated. No energy is used.
  • Oxygen: the air in the alveoli has a higher oxygen concentration than the deoxygenated blood arriving from the body in the pulmonary artery. Oxygen dissolves in the moisture lining the alveolus, diffuses through the alveolus wall and the capillary wall into the plasma, and enters the red blood cells, where it combines with haemoglobin to form oxyhaemoglobin (see ‘Blood’).
  • Carbon dioxide: the blood arriving at the lungs has a higher CO₂ concentration than the air in the alveoli. CO₂, carried mostly in the plasma, diffuses out of the blood into the alveolus and is breathed out.
  • The oxygenated blood leaves the lungs in the pulmonary veins and returns to the heart (the full route is in ‘Circulation’).
  • The concentration gradients are kept steep in two ways: breathing constantly brings in fresh air with a high oxygen and low CO₂ content and removes stale air; and blood flow constantly carries oxygenated blood away and brings new deoxygenated blood.

Adaptations of the alveoli

  • Very large total surface area: about 300 million alveoli in the two lungs give an exchange surface of roughly 70 m², so a large amount of gas can diffuse at once.
  • Very thin walls: the alveolus wall and the capillary wall are each one cell thick and pressed close together, so the diffusion distance is very short.
  • Moist lining: gases dissolve in the moisture before they diffuse across the wall.
  • Dense capillary network: every alveolus is surrounded by capillaries with a continuous blood flow, which keeps the concentration gradient steep.
  • Narrow capillaries: they are just wide enough for red blood cells to pass in single file, pressed against the wall. This shortens the distance further and slows the blood, giving time for exchange.
  • Elastic fibres around the alveoli let them stretch on inhalation and recoil on exhalation.

Composition of inhaled and exhaled air

  • Inhaled air contains about 21% oxygen, about 0.04% carbon dioxide and 78% nitrogen, with a variable amount of water vapour.
  • Exhaled air contains about 16% oxygen, about 4% carbon dioxide and 78% nitrogen. It is saturated with water vapour and is warmer than inhaled air.
  • Nitrogen is not used by the body, so its proportion is the same in both.
  • Exhaled air still contains much oxygen, which is why rescue breaths work in CPR.
  • Lime water turns milky faster with exhaled air, showing that exhaled air contains more CO₂.

When the structures are damaged

  • Smoking: tar and other chemicals in cigarette smoke paralyse and destroy the cilia and increase mucus production. Mucus and trapped microorganisms build up in the airways, causing coughing (‘smoker’s cough’) and frequent chest infections.
  • Emphysema, mainly caused by smoking: if alveolus walls break down and merge into larger air spaces, the surface area for gas exchange falls and the lungs lose their elasticity, so the person becomes breathless. This shows why the large surface area of the alveoli matters.
  • Carbon monoxide in cigarette smoke combines with haemoglobin, reducing the amount of oxygen the blood can carry (see ‘Blood’).
  • Asthma: the smooth muscle in the bronchiole walls contracts, and the lining swells and produces extra mucus, narrowing the airways so that less air reaches the alveoli. Inhalers deliver drugs that relax the smooth muscle.

Key terms

Ventilation
The movement of air into and out of the lungs; it is not the same as respiration.
Alveoli
Tiny thin-walled air sacs, each surrounded by capillaries, where gases are exchanged with the blood.
Epiglottis
A flap of cartilage that closes over the entrance to the trachea during swallowing, so food goes into the oesophagus.
Pleural fluid
A thin layer of fluid between the two pleural membranes around each lung, which reduces friction and keeps the lung against the chest wall.
Diaphragm
A dome-shaped sheet of muscle and tendon forming the floor of the thorax; it contracts and flattens to inhale.
Intercostal muscles
The external and internal muscles between the ribs that move the rib cage during breathing.
Breathing centre
The part of the medulla oblongata that sends rhythmic nerve impulses to the diaphragm and intercostal muscles.
Tidal volume
The volume of air moved in and out in each breath at rest, about 500 cm³.
Goblet cell
A mucus-secreting cell in the lining of the trachea and bronchi.
