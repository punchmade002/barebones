Overview: eggs, hormones and the routes of egg and sperm

  • The female reproductive system produces eggs (ova; singular ovum), the female gametes, and the hormones oestrogen and progesterone.
  • It receives sperm during intercourse, is the site of fertilisation, and holds, nourishes and protects the developing baby until birth.
  • Its organs lie in the lower abdomen (pelvis), protected by the pelvic bones, except the external parts (the vulva).
  • Route of an egg: ovary → fallopian tube (where it may be fertilised) → uterus.
  • Route of sperm after intercourse: vagina → cervix → uterus → fallopian tube.

External parts: the vulva, labia and clitoris

The vulva is the external female genitalia. Its parts protect the openings and provide sexual sensation.

  • Vulva: the collective name for the external female genitals, including the labia, the clitoris and the openings of the urethra and the vagina.
  • Labia: two pairs of folds of skin around the openings.
  • The outer labia (labia majora) are fleshier and covered with pubic hair after puberty; the inner labia (labia minora) are thinner.
  • The labia protect the openings of the vagina and urethra from infection and damage.
  • Clitoris: a small, very sensitive organ at the front of the vulva where the inner labia meet, with many nerve endings.
  • The clitoris contains erectile tissue that fills with blood during arousal; its function is sexual pleasure.
  • The clitoris develops in the embryo from the same tissue as the penis.
  • In females the urethra (from the bladder) opens separately, in front of the vaginal opening, and carries urine only; it is not part of the reproductive system.
  • This is the main contrast with the male urethra, which carries semen as well as urine (see “The male reproductive system”).

Vagina and cervix

  • Vagina: a muscular, elastic tube about 7–10 cm long, leading from the vulva to the cervix.
  • The vagina receives the penis during intercourse; semen is deposited at its top, near the cervix.
  • The vagina is the birth canal, stretching greatly during birth, and menstrual blood leaves the body through it.
  • The vaginal lining is moist and kept acidic by harmless bacteria (lactobacilli), which helps prevent infection by pathogens; this is an innate defence (see “Innate and adaptive immunity”).
  • Cervix: the narrow neck at the lower end of the uterus, opening into the top of the vagina through a small canal.
  • Glands in the cervix make mucus. For most of the cycle the mucus is thick and blocks sperm and pathogens; around ovulation it becomes thin, clear and slippery, letting sperm pass (detail in “The menstrual cycle”).
  • During pregnancy a thick mucus plug seals the cervix; in labour the cervix widens (dilates) to about 10 cm to let the baby out (see “Pregnancy”).
  • Cells from the cervix are sampled in cervical screening (see “Causes, prevention and treatment of cancer”).

Uterus and endometrium

  • Uterus (womb): a hollow, pear-shaped organ, about the size of a fist when not pregnant, with a very thick wall of smooth muscle.
  • The embryo implants in the uterus and the foetus develops there.
  • The muscular wall stretches enormously during pregnancy and contracts powerfully to push the baby out in labour.
  • Endometrium: the inner lining of the uterus, rich in blood vessels and glands.
  • Each month the endometrium thickens under the influence of ovarian hormones, ready to receive an embryo.
  • If no embryo implants, the outer layer of the endometrium breaks down and is shed with blood in menstruation (see “The menstrual cycle”).
  • If an embryo implants, the endometrium stays and helps form the mother's part of the placenta (see “Pregnancy”).
  • Only the endometrium is shed in menstruation, not the muscular wall.

Fallopian tubes and ovaries

  • Fallopian tubes (oviducts): two narrow tubes, about 10 cm long, one from each side of the top of the uterus towards an ovary.
  • The open end near the ovary is funnel-shaped with finger-like projections that sweep the released egg into the tube.
  • Cilia lining the tube beat, and waves of muscle contraction (peristalsis) move the egg, or the early embryo, towards the uterus, because the egg cannot swim.
  • Fertilisation normally happens in the fallopian tube.
  • Ovaries: two almond-sized organs, one on each side of the uterus, close to but not joined to the open end of each fallopian tube.
  • The ovaries contain follicles, each holding an immature egg. Usually one follicle matures and releases its egg each month, from puberty to menopause; this is ovulation.
  • The ovaries also secrete oestrogen and progesterone (see “Glands, locations and hormones”).
  • Because the ovary is not attached to the tube, the egg must be swept in by the funnel.
  • Ectopic pregnancy: if an embryo implants in the fallopian tube instead of the uterus, the tube cannot stretch to hold it and may rupture, which is a medical emergency.
  • A damaged tube, for example one scarred by infection, raises the risk of an ectopic pregnancy (see “Fertility control and infertility”).

Summary: each part, its function and how its structure suits it

Listed from outside to inside.

  • Vulva – external genitals – collective name for the external parts that protect the openings and give sexual sensation.
  • Labia – around the openings of the vagina and urethra – protect them from infection and damage – folds of skin, the outer pair fleshier.
  • Clitoris – front of the vulva where the inner labia meet – sexual pleasure – many nerve endings and erectile tissue.
  • Vagina – from the vulva to the cervix – receives the penis, is the birth canal, and carries menstrual blood – muscular, elastic and stretchy, with an acidic lining.
  • Cervix – neck of the uterus – controls entry of sperm and pathogens, and dilates in labour – mucus changes through the cycle and the canal is narrow.
  • Uterus – between the bladder and rectum – holds and nourishes the foetus – thick smooth muscle that stretches and contracts.
  • Endometrium – inner lining of the uterus – receives the embryo and is shed if there is no pregnancy – rich in blood vessels and glands.
  • Fallopian tube – from the uterus towards each ovary – carries the egg to the uterus and is the site of fertilisation – funnel with finger-like projections, cilia and muscle.
  • Ovary – one on each side of the uterus, near the fallopian tube – produces eggs and oestrogen and progesterone – contains follicles, each holding an immature egg.

Comparing the male and female systems

The names of the gamete and hormone details are given in the notes named here; this comparison names them only.

  • Gonads: testes in the male; ovaries in the female.
  • Gamete produced: sperm, made continuously from puberty in the male; one egg a month from puberty to menopause in the female (see “Gametes and fertilisation”).
  • Main hormones: testosterone in the male; oestrogen and progesterone in the female (see “Hormones in reproduction”).
  • Gonad position: the testes lie outside the body in the scrotum; the ovaries lie inside the pelvis.
  • Urethra: shared with the reproductive system in the male, carrying urine and semen; separate in the female, carrying urine only.

Key terms

Ovum
The female gamete, or egg; plural ova.
Vulva
The collective name for the external female genitals, including the labia, clitoris and the openings of the urethra and vagina.
Labia majora
The outer, fleshier pair of skin folds of the vulva, covered with pubic hair after puberty.
Labia minora
The inner, thinner pair of skin folds of the vulva, which protect the openings of the vagina and urethra.
Clitoris
A small, very sensitive organ at the front of the vulva, with erectile tissue, whose function is sexual pleasure.
Vagina
A muscular, elastic tube about 7–10 cm long from the vulva to the cervix; it receives the penis and is the birth canal.
Cervix
The narrow neck at the lower end of the uterus, which opens into the vagina and produces mucus.
Uterus
The hollow, muscular womb in which the embryo implants and the foetus develops.
Endometrium
The inner, blood-rich lining of the uterus, which thickens each month and is shed in menstruation if no embryo implants.
Fallopian tube
One of two narrow tubes, about 10 cm long, that carry the egg from the ovary to the uterus and are the normal site of fertilisation; also called the oviduct.
Cilia
Hair-like structures on the lining of the fallopian tube that beat to move the egg towards the uterus.
Ovary
One of two almond-sized organs that contain follicles, release eggs and secrete oestrogen and progesterone.
Follicle
A structure in the ovary that holds an immature egg and develops until the egg is released.
Ovulation
The release of an egg from the ovary, usually one each month from puberty to menopause.
Ectopic pregnancy
A pregnancy in which the embryo implants outside the uterus, most often in a fallopian tube, which may rupture.
