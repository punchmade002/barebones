What prenatal and postnatal care is for

  • Prenatal (antenatal) care is the health care of a pregnant woman and her developing baby before birth.
  • Postnatal care is the care of the mother and baby after birth, usually for the first six weeks.
  • Its aims are to keep mother and baby healthy, to find problems early, when they can be treated or managed, and to give the parents information to make decisions.
  • A screening test is offered to everyone in a group, such as all pregnant women or all newborns, to find those at higher risk of a condition.
  • A screening test is quick, cheap and safe, but it is not certain.
  • A diagnostic test confirms whether a person actually has the condition; it is more accurate and may carry some risk.
  • A positive screening result leads to a diagnostic test.
  • The same distinction is used for cancer in “Causes, prevention and treatment of cancer”.

Monitoring the mother's health

  • At the first (booking) visit early in pregnancy, the mother's blood is tested for her blood group and Rhesus factor.
  • A Rhesus-negative mother carrying a Rhesus-positive baby may be given anti-D injections to protect this and later babies (see “Blood”).
  • The booking bloods also test haemoglobin, to detect anaemia, which is common in pregnancy and is treated with iron.
  • Immunity to rubella is checked at booking.
  • Infections that can pass to the baby are tested for, including HIV, hepatitis B and syphilis, so that treatment can greatly reduce the chance of passing them on.
  • At every visit her blood pressure is measured and her urine tested.
  • High blood pressure with protein in the urine is a sign of pre-eclampsia, a condition in pregnancy that can be dangerous to mother and baby; she is monitored closely and the baby may be delivered early.
  • Glucose in the urine, or a raised blood glucose in a glucose tolerance test, may show gestational diabetes, diabetes that develops in pregnancy.
  • Gestational diabetes can make the baby grow too large; it is managed by diet and, if needed, insulin.
  • Advice and prevention: take folic acid, a B vitamin, daily from before conception until the 12th week, which reduces the risk of neural tube defects such as spina bifida (see “Vitamins”).
  • Advice and prevention: do not smoke or drink alcohol, eat a balanced diet, and take medicines only on medical advice (see “Pregnancy” for substances that cross the placenta).
  • Advice and prevention: avoid foods that may carry harmful microorganisms, for example unpasteurised cheese and pâté, which can carry Listeria bacteria, and undercooked meat, which can carry Toxoplasma.
  • Vaccines in pregnancy: the flu vaccine and the whooping cough (pertussis) vaccine are recommended.
  • The mother's antibodies cross the placenta, so the baby is protected from whooping cough in the first weeks of life, before it can be vaccinated itself (passive immunity; see “Acquired immunity, prevention and treatment”).

Monitoring the foetus

  • Ultrasound scanning sends high-frequency sound waves into the body; their echoes from tissues of different density form a moving image on a screen.
  • Ultrasound uses no ionising radiation, unlike X-rays, which are avoided in pregnancy, so it is regarded as safe for mother and foetus.
  • A dating scan early in pregnancy, around 11–14 weeks, confirms the pregnancy, the number of babies and the expected date of delivery, and checks for an ectopic pregnancy.
  • An anomaly scan at about 20 weeks checks the baby's organs, for example the heart, spine, brain and kidneys, and its growth, and the position of the placenta.
  • Later scans check growth and the baby's position before birth.
  • Finding a problem such as a heart defect before birth allows the birth to be planned in a hospital ready to treat the baby at once.
  • During labour the baby's heart rate and the mother's contractions are monitored, for example by a cardiotocograph (CTG).
  • This means that signs that the baby is short of oxygen are noticed, and the birth can be speeded up, for example by Caesarean section (see “Pregnancy”).

Genetic screening and diagnosis before birth

  • Genetic screening tests for genetic conditions, or for carriers of them.
  • Before or during pregnancy, couples with a family history of an inherited condition, for example cystic fibrosis, can have carrier testing to find out their chance of having an affected child (Punnett squares in “Genetic Inheritance”; the cystic fibrosis mutation in “Mutations”).
  • Chromosome conditions such as Down syndrome (trisomy 21) are screened for (see “Mutations”).
  • The combined test uses an ultrasound measurement of the fluid at the back of the baby's neck (nuchal translucency) together with a blood test of the mother, at about 11–14 weeks.
  • The combined test gives the chance, a probability, of Down syndrome, taking the mother's age into account.
  • Non-invasive prenatal testing (NIPT), from about 10 weeks, tests a sample of the mother's blood for small fragments of DNA from the placenta that circulate in it.
  • NIPT is a much more accurate screening test for trisomy 21, 18 and 13, but a positive result still needs a diagnostic test.
  • Neither the combined test nor NIPT harms the pregnancy.
  • Diagnostic tests take cells from the baby's side of the pregnancy so its chromosomes or DNA can be examined, for example as a karyotype (methods in “Generating a DNA profile” and “DNA sequencing”).
  • Chorionic villus sampling (CVS), at about 11–14 weeks, takes a sample of placental tissue with a fine needle through the abdomen or a thin tube through the cervix.
  • Amniocentesis, from about 15 weeks, uses a fine needle guided by ultrasound to take a sample of amniotic fluid, which contains cells shed by the foetus.
  • CVS and amniocentesis both give a definite result, but both carry a small risk of miscarriage, well under 1 in 100, so they are offered only when the chance of a condition is raised.
  • Preimplantation genetic testing: in IVF, one or a few cells are taken from each early embryo and tested for a known serious genetic condition, and only unaffected embryos are placed in the uterus.
  • IVF is in “Fertility control and infertility”, and the ethical questions in “Ethics of genetic technologies”.

Postnatal checks and screening

  • Apgar score: at one minute and five minutes after birth, the baby is scored 0, 1 or 2 on each of five signs: heart rate, breathing, muscle tone, reflex response and skin colour, for a maximum of 10.
  • A low Apgar score shows that the baby needs help, for example with breathing.
  • Vitamin K is offered to every newborn, usually by injection, to prevent a rare but serious bleeding disorder (why newborns need it is in “Vitamins”).
  • A full physical examination of the newborn checks, for example, the heart, the hips for hip dysplasia (a loose or dislocated hip joint, treatable if found early), the eyes and, in boys, whether the testes have descended (see “The male reproductive system”).
  • Newborn hearing screening is offered to all babies in Ireland, so deafness is found early and language development can be supported.
  • Later postnatal care records weight, length and head size on growth charts.
  • A public health nurse visits the family at home in the first weeks.
  • The mother's recovery and her mental health are checked, because postnatal depression is common and treatable.
  • Breastfeeding is supported (benefits in “Hormones in reproduction” and “Acquired immunity, prevention and treatment”).
  • The baby's routine vaccinations begin at 2 months of age.

The heel-prick test: how and when it is done

  • Every baby born in Ireland is offered newborn bloodspot screening, the heel-prick test, with the parents' consent.
  • When the baby is 3 to 5 days old (72–120 hours after birth), a midwife or public health nurse pricks the baby's heel and places drops of blood on a special card.
  • The card is sent to the national screening laboratory at Children's Health Ireland at Temple Street, Dublin.
  • The conditions screened for are rare and serious, and show no signs at birth, but early treatment prevents or greatly reduces lasting harm.
  • A positive screen leads to diagnostic tests.
  • The test is done at 3–5 days because by then the baby has been feeding on milk for a few days, so a substance that the baby cannot break down, such as phenylalanine in PKU, has had time to build up in the blood to a detectable level.
  • Testing too early can miss it.

Conditions found by the heel-prick test

  • Phenylketonuria (PKU) has been screened for in Ireland since the 1960s. It is the condition for which the blood-spot test was first developed, by Robert Guthrie in the USA; the card is often called a Guthrie card.
  • The test measures phenylalanine in the blood spot; a high level shows the baby cannot convert phenylalanine (the cause is in “Enzymes as catalysts”).
  • PKU is inherited as a recessive condition.
  • Treatment for PKU is a special diet low in phenylalanine, which means restricting high-protein foods and using special protein substitutes, from the first weeks of life and continued for life.
  • The sweetener aspartame contains phenylalanine, which is why food labels warn people with PKU.
  • Treated early, children with PKU develop normally; untreated, phenylalanine builds up and causes severe intellectual disability.
  • Women with PKU need very strict diet control before and during pregnancy, because high phenylalanine harms the developing foetus.
  • Cystic fibrosis is also screened for by the heel-prick test (cause and mutation in “Mutations”).
  • A raised level of a protein made by the pancreas in the blood spot is followed by DNA testing of the same spot for common CFTR mutations.
  • Early diagnosis allows early treatment: physiotherapy to clear mucus, antibiotics, pancreatic enzymes with food, and newer drugs, which improve growth and lung health.
  • Congenital hypothyroidism: the baby's thyroid gland makes too little thyroxine (see “Glands, locations and hormones”).
  • Untreated, congenital hypothyroidism causes poor growth and intellectual disability.
  • Treatment for congenital hypothyroidism is a daily dose of thyroxine by mouth, which allows normal development.
  • Classical galactosaemia: the baby cannot break down galactose, one of the two sugars in lactose (milk sugar; see “Structures of biomolecules”), because an enzyme in the pathway is missing.
  • Galactose builds up as soon as the baby drinks milk and can quickly cause serious illness.
  • Treatment is a diet free of lactose and galactose, for example a soya-based formula instead of breast or cow's milk.
  • The heel-prick programme also screens for a small number of other rare inherited conditions.

Benefits of modern technology before birth and for newborns

  • Problems in the mother, such as anaemia, pre-eclampsia, gestational diabetes and infections, are found early and managed.
  • Problems in the baby, such as growth problems, heart and other defects, and the baby's position, are also found early and managed.
  • Pregnancy and birth are now far safer for mothers and babies than in the past.
  • Newborn screening prevents intellectual disability and early death in PKU, galactosaemia and congenital hypothyroidism.
  • Newborn screening gives children with cystic fibrosis early treatment.
  • Parents and doctors can plan the birth and treatment in advance.

Technology after birth

  • Neonatal intensive care allows many premature babies to survive.
  • Incubators keep the baby warm and protected from infection.
  • Ventilators help the baby breathe.
  • Surfactant, a substance that stops the air sacs in the lungs collapsing, can be given into the lungs; premature lungs lack it.
  • Phototherapy, using blue light, breaks down bilirubin in babies with jaundice.
  • These treatments link to the late maturing of the lungs (see “Pregnancy”).

Limits and costs

  • No screening test is perfect: a false positive causes anxiety and further tests, and a false negative gives false reassurance.
  • Screening gives a chance, not a certainty, which can be hard to understand.
  • Invasive diagnostic tests, CVS and amniocentesis, carry a small risk of miscarriage.
  • Finding a condition that cannot be treated can leave parents with difficult decisions.
  • Equipment, staff and intensive care are expensive, so access is unequal between and within countries.
  • Some very premature babies who survive have long-term health problems.

Ethical points

These are arguments to weigh; no side is taken.

  • Parents have the right to information about their pregnancy and to decide whether to have tests (informed consent; see the principles in “Science, scientists and society”).
  • Genetic tests raise questions about who can see the results and how they are stored (detail in “Ethics of genetic technologies”).
  • Some people with disabilities and their families argue that screening for conditions such as Down syndrome can send a negative message about people living with them.
  • Others argue that information from screening lets families prepare.
  • Newborn blood-spot cards contain the baby's DNA, which raises questions about how long they are stored and who may use them.

Key terms

Prenatal care
The health care of a pregnant woman and her developing baby before birth; also called antenatal care.
Postnatal care
The care of the mother and baby after birth, usually for the first six weeks.
Screening test
A test offered to everyone in a group to find those at higher risk of a condition; quick, cheap and safe but not certain.
Diagnostic test
A test that confirms whether a person has a condition; more accurate than screening and may carry some risk.
Pre-eclampsia
A condition in pregnancy, shown by high blood pressure with protein in the urine, which can be dangerous to mother and baby.
Gestational diabetes
Diabetes that develops during pregnancy, detected by glucose in the urine or a glucose tolerance test.
Folic acid
A B vitamin taken daily from before conception to the 12th week, which reduces the risk of neural tube defects such as spina bifida.
Ultrasound scan
An imaging method that uses high-frequency sound waves and their echoes, with no ionising radiation, to form a moving image of the foetus.
Cardiotocograph (CTG)
A monitor that records the baby's heart rate and the mother's contractions during labour.
Nuchal translucency
The fluid at the back of the baby's neck, measured by ultrasound as part of the combined test for Down syndrome at about 11–14 weeks.
Non-invasive prenatal testing (NIPT)
A screening test from about 10 weeks that examines fragments of placental DNA circulating in the mother's blood for chromosome conditions.
Chorionic villus sampling (CVS)
A diagnostic test at about 11–14 weeks that takes a sample of placental tissue; it carries a small risk of miscarriage.
Amniocentesis
A diagnostic test from about 15 weeks that takes a sample of amniotic fluid, which contains foetal cells; it carries a small risk of miscarriage.
Karyotype
An image of a person's chromosomes, used to examine them for chromosome conditions.
Preimplantation genetic testing
Testing one or a few cells from an early embryo made by IVF for a known serious genetic condition, before it is placed in the uterus.
Apgar score
A score from 0 to 10, given at one and five minutes after birth, based on heart rate, breathing, muscle tone, reflex response and skin colour.
Heel-prick test
The newborn bloodspot screen, done at 3–5 days, in which drops of blood from the heel are tested for rare serious conditions.
Guthrie card
The special card on which the heel-prick blood spots are collected, named after Robert Guthrie, who developed the test for PKU.
Phenylketonuria (PKU)
A recessive inherited condition in which phenylalanine cannot be converted, treated for life with a low-phenylalanine diet.
Galactosaemia
An inherited condition in which galactose cannot be broken down, treated with a diet free of lactose and galactose.
Congenital hypothyroidism
A condition present from birth in which the thyroid makes too little thyroxine, treated with a daily dose of thyroxine.
Neonatal intensive care
Specialist care for premature or sick newborns, including incubators, ventilators, surfactant and phototherapy.
Surfactant
A substance that stops the air sacs of the lungs collapsing, which premature lungs lack and which can be given into the lungs.
