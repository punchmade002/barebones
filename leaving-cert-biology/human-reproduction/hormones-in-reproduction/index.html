Overview: where the reproductive hormones come from

  • Reproduction is controlled by hormones from three places: the pituitary gland at the base of the brain, the testes and the ovaries.
  • The pituitary gland at the base of the brain makes oxytocin and prolactin, and also the hormones that stimulate the gonads.
  • The testes make testosterone, and the ovaries make oestrogen and progesterone; in pregnancy the placenta also makes oestrogen and progesterone.
  • The locations of these glands and hormones are given in “Glands, locations and hormones”.
  • Prolactin is made by the pituitary gland, and its main role is to stimulate milk production in the breasts.

Puberty

  • Puberty is the stage, usually in the early teens, when the reproductive organs mature and become able to produce gametes, and the secondary sexual characteristics develop.
  • Puberty typically begins between about 8 and 13 in girls, and between about 9 and 14 in boys.
  • It begins when the brain signals the pituitary to increase its release of the hormones that stimulate the gonads.
  • As a result the testes begin to make much more testosterone, and the ovaries much more oestrogen (Higher Level detail is in the Higher Level section on the pituitary's gonad-stimulating hormones).
  • Testosterone at puberty starts sperm production, causes growth of the penis and testes, and causes the male secondary sexual characteristics (see “Glands, locations and hormones”); it also increases sex drive.
  • Oestrogen at puberty causes growth of the uterus and vagina and the female secondary sexual characteristics (see “Glands, locations and hormones”).
  • Oestrogen, with progesterone, starts the menstrual cycles; the first period is called menarche.

Sperm and egg production

  • Testosterone is needed throughout adult life for sperm production in the seminiferous tubules, and to maintain the male reproductive organs and secondary sexual characteristics.
  • Its level stays fairly steady from day to day, so sperm are made continuously (see “Fertility control and infertility” for continuous fertility).
  • Control of this production by the pituitary is in the Higher Level section on the pituitary's gonad-stimulating hormones.
  • Egg production: each month hormones cause one follicle to mature and release its egg, and prepare the endometrium; the levels and feedback are described in “The menstrual cycle”.

Implantation and pregnancy

  • Progesterone prepares the endometrium for implantation: it keeps it thick, spongy and well supplied with blood vessels, and makes its glands secrete nutrients for the early embryo before the placenta forms.
  • Without high progesterone, implantation cannot succeed.
  • hCG (human chorionic gonadotrophin) is a hormone made by the outer cells of the embryo from implantation.
  • hCG keeps the corpus luteum alive and secreting progesterone, so the endometrium is not shed and menstruation stops.
  • Home pregnancy tests detect hCG in urine.
  • From about the end of the third month the placenta takes over from the corpus luteum, making large amounts of progesterone and oestrogen for the rest of pregnancy; the corpus luteum then breaks down.
  • Progesterone during pregnancy maintains the endometrium and so the placenta.
  • Progesterone keeps the muscle of the uterus relaxed, so it does not contract and expel the foetus early.
  • Progesterone keeps the cervical mucus plug thick, prepares the milk-producing glands of the breasts, and (with oestrogen) prevents further ovulation and menstruation.
  • Oestrogen during pregnancy drives the growth of the foetus: it stimulates growth of the muscle of the uterus so it enlarges with the growing foetus, and increases blood flow to the uterus and placenta.
  • Oestrogen also develops the milk ducts of the breasts.
  • Late in pregnancy oestrogen makes the uterus muscle more sensitive to oxytocin, preparing it for labour.
  • Progesterone and oestrogen rise steadily to very high levels by late pregnancy, far above their levels in the menstrual cycle, and fall sharply after the placenta is delivered.

Birth

  • Near the end of pregnancy the balance of hormones changes: oestrogen's effects increase relative to progesterone's, so the uterus muscle becomes more responsive to oxytocin and begins to contract.
  • Oxytocin is made in the hypothalamus and released by the pituitary into the blood.
  • Oxytocin causes the muscle of the uterus to contract strongly in labour.
  • Positive feedback in birth, step 1: the baby's head presses on and stretches the cervix.
  • Step 2: stretch receptors in the cervix send nerve impulses to the brain.
  • Step 3: the pituitary releases more oxytocin.
  • Step 4: stronger contractions push the head harder against the cervix, stretching it more.
  • Step 5: the cycle repeats, building up until the baby is born, which removes the stimulus.
  • A nervous and a hormonal pathway work together here (see “Nervous vs hormonal coordination”); positive feedback is defined in “Homeostasis as a stimulus-response model”.
  • After the birth, oxytocin keeps the uterus contracting, which expels the placenta and closes the blood vessels at its attachment site, reducing bleeding.
  • Oxytocin also helps the uterus shrink back towards its normal size.
  • Synthetic oxytocin is given by drip in hospital to start (induce) or speed up labour, and to reduce bleeding after birth.
  • Diagram the positive feedback loop of the birth steps as a cycle: baby's head stretches cervix → nerve impulses to brain → pituitary releases oxytocin → uterus contracts more strongly → back to the start; birth ends the loop.

Colostrum and milk

  • During pregnancy, oestrogen and progesterone develop the breasts' ducts and milk glands, and prolactin rises.
  • The high levels of oestrogen and progesterone stop milk being produced.
  • When the placenta is delivered, oestrogen and progesterone fall sharply, so prolactin can act and milk production begins.
  • Prolactin stimulates the milk glands to produce milk.
  • Suckling by the baby stimulates nerve endings in the nipple, causing the pituitary to release more prolactin, so the more the baby feeds, the more milk is made: supply matches demand.
  • Oxytocin causes the milk to be released (“let-down”, or milk ejection): suckling triggers oxytocin release, which makes muscle cells around the milk glands contract and squeeze milk into the ducts and out of the nipple.
  • Prolactin makes milk; oxytocin releases it.
  • Colostrum is the first milk, produced for the first few days after birth.
  • Colostrum is a small volume of thick, yellowish fluid, richer in protein and antibodies than later milk and lower in fat and sugar.
  • Colostrum gives the newborn passive immunity (see “Acquired immunity, prevention and treatment”).
  • Over the following days and weeks it changes to mature milk, with more fat and lactose for energy and growth.
  • While a mother breastfeeds frequently, high prolactin suppresses ovulation, so periods often do not return for some months; this is not reliable contraception (see “Fertility control and infertility”).

Menopause

  • Menopause is the end of a woman's menstrual cycles and of her fertility, confirmed when she has had no period for 12 months.
  • It usually happens between about 45 and 55 years of age.
  • Cause: the ovaries run out of follicles that can respond, so ovulation stops and the ovaries make much less oestrogen and progesterone.
  • Without these hormones the endometrium is no longer built up, and periods stop.
  • Effects of falling oestrogen in the short term: hot flushes and night sweats, mood changes and poorer sleep, and vaginal dryness.
  • Effects of falling oestrogen in the longer term: loss of bone density, raising the risk of osteoporosis (brittle bones), and a rise in the risk of heart disease.
  • Hormone replacement therapy (HRT) is in “Hormonal manipulation”.
  • Men have no equivalent of the menopause: testosterone falls only slowly with age, and many men can produce sperm into old age, although sperm numbers and quality decline.

Roles and levels of each hormone

One bullet per hormone: its gland, its roles and how its level changes through life.

  • Testosterone (testes; small amounts also in females): male development before birth (see “Sex determination”), puberty, sperm production and sex drive.
  • Testosterone level: low in childhood, rises sharply at puberty, high and fairly steady in adult men, and declines slowly in later life.
  • Oestrogen (ovaries; placenta in pregnancy): female puberty, repair and thickening of the endometrium each cycle, growth of the uterus and breast ducts in pregnancy, preparing the uterus for oxytocin, and keeping bones strong.
  • Oestrogen level: low in childhood, rises at puberty, rises and falls each cycle, very high in pregnancy, and falls at menopause.
  • Progesterone (corpus luteum; placenta in pregnancy): maintains the endometrium for implantation and pregnancy, relaxes the uterus muscle, prepares the milk glands and prevents ovulation in pregnancy.
  • Progesterone level: high only in the second half of each cycle; very high in pregnancy; falls sharply at birth; very low after menopause.
  • Oxytocin (made in the hypothalamus, released by the pituitary): contractions of the uterus in labour and after birth, and milk let-down.
  • Oxytocin level: rises sharply during labour and with each feed.
  • Prolactin (pituitary): milk production.
  • Prolactin level: rises during pregnancy, stays high while breastfeeding, rising further with each feed, and falls when breastfeeding stops.
  • The pituitary hormones that stimulate the gonads, with their roles and levels, are covered in the Higher Level section on the pituitary's gonad-stimulating hormones.

FSH and LH in males and females (Higher Level only)

  • At puberty the hypothalamus, the part of the brain above the pituitary, signals the pituitary to increase its release of FSH and LH.
  • In males these hormones stimulate the testes to make sperm and testosterone; in females they stimulate follicles to develop and the ovaries to make oestrogen, starting the cycles.
  • In males, FSH stimulates sperm production in the seminiferous tubules, working with testosterone.
  • In males, LH (sometimes called interstitial-cell-stimulating hormone in males) stimulates the interstitial cells of the testes to secrete testosterone.
  • Male negative feedback: when testosterone in the blood rises, it inhibits the release of LH and FSH from the pituitary, so less testosterone is made; when testosterone falls, LH rises again.
  • This keeps testosterone and sperm production steady and continuous, unlike the female cycle.
  • Example: men who take anabolic steroids (synthetic testosterone; see “Hormonal manipulation”) have high testosterone-like levels, which switch off their own LH and FSH, so their testes shrink and sperm production falls.
  • In females, FSH stimulates follicle development and oestrogen production, and the LH surge causes ovulation and forms the corpus luteum; the levels and feedback through the month are in “The menstrual cycle”.
  • In pregnancy, high progesterone and oestrogen keep FSH and LH low, so no follicles develop and no ovulation happens.
  • While breastfeeding, high prolactin also suppresses FSH and LH.
  • After the menopause, FSH and LH rise to high levels, because the ovaries no longer make enough oestrogen and progesterone to inhibit them; a high FSH level in the blood is a sign of the menopause.
  • Levels summary: FSH and LH are low in childhood and rise at puberty.
  • In men FSH and LH are fairly steady; in women they vary through each cycle, with an LH surge at ovulation.
  • In women FSH and LH are low in pregnancy and while breastfeeding, and high after the menopause.

Modelling the control system (Higher Level only)

  • A model of hormonal control shows, in order: the control centre (hypothalamus and pituitary), the hormone released, the target organ, the hormone or effect the target produces, and the feedback, with − for inhibition and + for stimulation.
  • Diagram two flow charts side by side. Male: hypothalamus → pituitary → FSH and LH → testes → sperm and testosterone, with a “−” arrow from testosterone back to the pituitary. Female: hypothalamus → pituitary → FSH and LH → ovary → follicle, oestrogen, corpus luteum, progesterone → endometrium, with “−” arrows from progesterone (and oestrogen) to the pituitary and a “+” arrow from high oestrogen to the LH surge.
  • Use the model to predict effects: blocking testosterone's feedback raises LH; giving steady oestrogen and progesterone, as in the pill, keeps FSH and LH low.

Key terms

Puberty
The stage, usually in the early teens, when the reproductive organs mature, gametes can be produced and the secondary sexual characteristics develop.
Secondary sexual characteristics
Features that develop at puberty and are not directly part of the reproductive organs, caused by testosterone in males and by oestrogen in females.
Menarche
The first menstrual period, which occurs during puberty.
Testosterone
The male sex hormone, made by the interstitial cells of the testes, which starts and maintains sperm production and sex drive.
Oestrogen
A female sex hormone, made by the ovaries and the placenta, which drives female puberty, repairs the endometrium and grows the uterus and breast ducts.
Progesterone
A hormone made by the corpus luteum and then the placenta, which maintains the endometrium, relaxes the uterus and prevents ovulation in pregnancy.
hCG
Human chorionic gonadotrophin, made by the embryo, which keeps the corpus luteum alive so that menstruation stops; detected by pregnancy tests.
Oxytocin
A hormone made in the hypothalamus and released by the pituitary, which makes the uterus contract in labour and causes milk let-down.
Prolactin
A hormone made by the pituitary gland, which stimulates the milk glands to produce milk; suckling increases its release.
Colostrum
The thick, yellowish first milk, produced for the first few days after birth, rich in protein and antibodies.
Menopause
The end of a woman's menstrual cycles and fertility, confirmed after 12 months without a period, usually at about 45–55 years.
Anabolic steroids
Synthetic testosterone-like drugs whose high levels switch off the body's own LH and FSH, shrinking the testes and reducing sperm production.
FSH
Follicle-stimulating hormone, made by the pituitary gland; in males it stimulates sperm production, and in females it stimulates follicle development (Higher Level).
LH
Luteinising hormone, made by the pituitary gland; in males it stimulates testosterone production by the interstitial cells, and in females its surge causes ovulation (Higher Level).
Negative feedback
A control in which a rise in a hormone inhibits the release of the hormone that caused it; in males, testosterone inhibits LH and FSH (Higher Level).
Positive feedback
A control in which the output strengthens the stimulus, as in labour, where oxytocin increases contractions and so increases the release of more oxytocin.
