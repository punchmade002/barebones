Overview: sperm, testosterone and the route of sperm

  • The male reproductive system produces sperm, the male gametes, and the hormone testosterone, and delivers sperm into the female's vagina during sexual intercourse.
  • Sperm pass in order: testis → epididymis → vas deferens → urethra (where fluids from the seminal vesicles and prostate are added) → out through the penis.
  • Semen is sperm suspended in seminal fluid. Seminal fluid, made by the seminal vesicles and the prostate, gives the sperm a liquid to swim in and supplies an energy source.
  • Seminal fluid is slightly alkaline overall, which neutralises the acidity of the vagina and of any urine left in the urethra, because acid damages sperm.
  • Sperm make up only a small part of the volume of semen.

The testes and scrotum

The testes make sperm and testosterone. The scrotum keeps them at the temperature sperm production needs.

  • Scrotum: a sac of skin and muscle hanging outside the abdomen that holds the two testes.
  • The scrotum keeps the testes about 2–3 °C below core body temperature (37 °C), which sperm production needs; this is why the testes are outside the body. Its function is temperature control, not protection.
  • Muscles in the scrotum pull the testes closer to the body when it is cold and relax to let them hang lower when it is warm.
  • Testes (singular testis): the two oval male gonads, which are the primary sex organs.
  • Each testis is packed with many long, tightly coiled seminiferous tubules, whose walls produce sperm continuously from puberty (detail in “Gametes and fertilisation”).
  • The coiling packs a very long length of tubule, and so a large sperm-producing surface, into a small organ.
  • Between the seminiferous tubules are interstitial cells, which secrete testosterone into the blood; the testes are therefore also endocrine glands (see “Glands, locations and hormones”).
  • Testosterone's roles in puberty, sperm production and secondary sexual characteristics are covered in “Hormones in reproduction”.
  • The testes form inside the abdomen of the foetus and normally descend into the scrotum before birth.
  • A testis that has not descended cannot produce sperm normally, which is a known cause of male infertility (see “Fertility control and infertility”).

The ducts: epididymis, vas deferens and urethra

  • Epididymis: a single, very long, tightly coiled tube lying against the back of each testis.
  • Sperm from the seminiferous tubules pass into the epididymis, where they are stored and mature, gaining the ability to swim.
  • The coiling gives a long tube for storage in a small space.
  • Vas deferens (sperm duct; plural vasa deferentia): a muscular tube carrying sperm from the epididymis up into the abdomen and around the bladder, to join the urethra.
  • During ejaculation the smooth muscle of the vas deferens contracts in waves (peristalsis) to push sperm along.
  • The vas deferens is the tube that is cut and tied in a vasectomy (see “Fertility control and infertility”).
  • Urethra: the tube running from the bladder through the length of the penis to the outside.
  • In males the urethra carries both urine and semen, but never at the same time: during ejaculation a ring of muscle (sphincter) at the base of the bladder closes, so urine cannot mix with semen.
  • In females the urethra carries only urine and opens separately from the vagina (see “The female reproductive system”).

The glands: seminal vesicles and prostate

  • Seminal vesicles: two glands behind the bladder whose ducts join the vasa deferentia.
  • The seminal vesicles secrete a thick, alkaline fluid rich in the sugar fructose; the sperm's mitochondria respire fructose to release energy for swimming.
  • This fluid makes up most of the volume of semen.
  • Prostate gland: a single gland, about the size of a walnut, just below the bladder, surrounding the start of the urethra.
  • The prostate secretes a thin, milky fluid containing enzymes that keep the semen liquid after ejaculation, so the sperm can swim free.
  • The secretions of the seminal vesicles and the prostate together form seminal fluid.
  • Because the prostate surrounds the urethra, an enlarged prostate, common in older men, can press on the urethra and make urination difficult.

The penis, glans and foreskin

  • Penis: the male organ that places semen in the vagina during intercourse, and through which urine and semen leave the body via the urethra.
  • The penis contains spongy erectile tissue. During sexual arousal its blood vessels widen and the spaces fill with blood under pressure, so the penis becomes larger, firm and erect, which allows it to enter the vagina.
  • Glans: the rounded, sensitive tip of the penis, with many nerve endings; stimulation of these leads to ejaculation.
  • The urethra opens at the tip of the glans.
  • Foreskin: a fold of loose skin that covers and protects the glans when the penis is not erect, and pulls back (retracts) during an erection.
  • The foreskin is removed in circumcision.
  • Ejaculation: at the peak of sexual stimulation, waves of muscle contraction in the epididymis, vasa deferentia, glands and urethra expel semen from the penis.
  • The semen from one ejaculation (a few millilitres) contains many millions of sperm.

Summary: each part, its function and how its structure suits it

Listed in the order of the sperm pathway.

  • Scrotum – holds the testes outside the abdomen – keeps them 2–3 °C below body temperature – muscles pull the testes closer to the body in cold conditions.
  • Testis – the sperm-producing organ, inside the scrotum – makes sperm in the coiled seminiferous tubules and testosterone in the interstitial cells – very long coiled tubules give a large surface in a small organ.
  • Epididymis – on the back of each testis – stores and matures sperm so they can swim – a single very long, coiled tube that stores a lot in a small space.
  • Vas deferens – from the epididymis, up into the abdomen and around the bladder to the urethra – carries sperm along the route – a muscular tube whose walls contract in peristalsis.
  • Seminal vesicle – behind the bladder – adds a thick, alkaline, fructose-rich fluid that gives energy and makes up most of the semen – a gland whose ducts join the vasa deferentia.
  • Prostate – below the bladder, around the urethra – adds an enzyme-containing fluid that keeps semen liquid – a gland that surrounds the start of the urethra.
  • Urethra – from the bladder through the penis – carries semen and urine, never together – a sphincter closes the bladder during ejaculation.
  • Penis – outside the body – places semen in the vagina – erectile tissue fills with blood so it becomes firm enough to enter the vagina.
  • Glans – tip of the penis – detects stimulation and leads to ejaculation – many nerve endings in its sensitive tip, and the urethra opens there.
  • Foreskin – fold of skin over the glans – protects the glans and pulls back during an erection – loose skin that retracts.

Key terms

Gamete
A sex cell, such as a sperm or an egg, which carries one set of chromosomes and fuses with another gamete at fertilisation.
Semen
The fluid ejaculated from the penis, made of sperm suspended in seminal fluid.
Seminal fluid
The fluid from the seminal vesicles and the prostate that feeds the sperm, supplies energy and is slightly alkaline overall.
Scrotum
The sac of skin and muscle outside the abdomen that holds the testes at a temperature below body temperature.
Testis
One of the two oval male gonads, which produce sperm and testosterone.
Seminiferous tubules
The long, tightly coiled tubules in the testes whose walls produce sperm.
Interstitial cells
Cells between the seminiferous tubules of the testes that secrete testosterone.
Epididymis
The long, coiled tube on the back of each testis where sperm are stored and mature and gain the ability to swim.
Vas deferens
The muscular sperm duct that carries sperm from the epididymis to the urethra; also called the sperm duct.
Seminal vesicle
A gland behind the bladder that secretes a thick, alkaline, fructose-rich fluid making up most of the volume of semen.
Prostate gland
A walnut-sized gland below the bladder, surrounding the urethra, that secretes a milky fluid with enzymes which keep semen liquid.
Urethra
The tube from the bladder through the penis that carries urine and, in males, semen, never at the same time.
Sphincter
A ring of muscle that closes an opening, such as the one at the base of the bladder that stops urine mixing with semen.
Peristalsis
Waves of muscle contraction that push material along a tube, such as sperm along the vas deferens.
Erectile tissue
Spongy tissue in the penis that fills with blood under pressure during arousal, making the penis erect.
Glans
The rounded, sensitive tip of the penis, where the urethra opens.
Foreskin
A fold of loose skin that covers the glans and pulls back during an erection.
Ejaculation
The expulsion of semen from the penis by waves of muscle contraction at the peak of sexual stimulation.
