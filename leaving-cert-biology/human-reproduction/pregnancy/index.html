From zygote to implantation

  • Fertilisation in the fallopian tube gives a diploid zygote, the first cell of the new individual (see “Gametes and fertilisation”).
  • The zygote divides repeatedly by mitosis as cilia and muscle contractions move it down the fallopian tube.
  • The cells do not grow between divisions, so they become smaller while the ball stays about the size of the egg.
  • After about five days it reaches the uterus as a small ball of cells.
  • Implantation: about 6–7 days after fertilisation the ball of cells sinks into the thick, spongy endometrium and embeds itself there, with enzymes from its outer cells digesting a way in.
  • From implantation the woman is pregnant.
  • Implantation needs an endometrium kept thick by progesterone (see “Hormones in reproduction”).
  • After implantation, a hormone from the embryo keeps the corpus luteum alive, so the endometrium is kept and menstruation stops.
  • A missed period is often the first sign of pregnancy, and pregnancy tests detect this hormone, hCG, in urine (detail in “Hormones in reproduction”).
  • If the ball of cells implants in the fallopian tube instead, it is an ectopic pregnancy (see “The female reproductive system”).

Placenta, umbilical cord and amnion

  • Placenta formation: after implantation, the outer cells of the embryo grow many finger-like projections (villi) into the endometrium.
  • The villi, with the mother's endometrium and the blood spaces around them, form the placenta, a disc-shaped organ attached to the wall of the uterus.
  • The placenta is fully formed by about the end of the third month (12 weeks) and keeps growing until birth.
  • The umbilical cord connects the foetus to the placenta.
  • The cord contains two umbilical arteries, which carry deoxygenated blood with wastes from the foetus to the placenta.
  • The cord contains one umbilical vein, which carries oxygenated blood with nutrients from the placenta to the foetus.
  • The vein carries oxygenated blood, the reverse of the adult pattern for most arteries and veins (see “Circulation”).
  • The mother's and the foetus's blood do not mix. They are separated by a thin barrier of the villi, only a few cells thick.
  • This barrier protects the foetus from the mother's high blood pressure and from her immune cells, and matters because their blood groups may differ (see “Blood”).
  • The placenta's structure suits exchange: the villi give a very large surface area; the barrier is very thin; the foetus's capillaries in the villi lie close to pools of the mother's blood; and blood flow on both sides keeps the concentration gradients high.
  • Substances cross by diffusion, and some, such as glucose and amino acids, by facilitated diffusion or active transport (see “Diffusion, osmosis and active transport”).
  • Functions of the placenta, part 1: it supplies oxygen, glucose, amino acids, fatty acids, vitamins, minerals and water to the foetus, acting as the foetus's lungs and gut.
  • Functions of the placenta, part 2: it removes carbon dioxide and urea, which pass into the mother's blood to be excreted by her lungs and kidneys, acting as the foetus's kidneys.
  • Functions of the placenta, part 3: it passes antibodies from mother to foetus, giving passive natural immunity for the first months after birth (see “Acquired immunity, prevention and treatment”).
  • Functions of the placenta, part 4: it secretes hormones, taking over the production of progesterone and oestrogen from the corpus luteum (see “Hormones in reproduction”).
  • Functions of the placenta, part 5: it forms a barrier to many pathogens and to the mother's blood cells.
  • Harmful substances can cross the placenta: alcohol, which can cause foetal alcohol spectrum disorder, with damage to the brain and growth.
  • Nicotine and carbon monoxide from smoking reduce the oxygen supply, and are linked to low birth weight and premature birth.
  • Many drugs, legal and illegal, cross the placenta, for example the thalidomide case (see “Science, scientists and society”).
  • Some viruses cross the placenta: rubella (German measles) can cause deafness, blindness and heart defects if caught in early pregnancy, and HIV can also cross.
  • This is why antenatal advice matters (see “Prenatal and postnatal care”).
  • Amnion: a thin membrane that forms a fluid-filled sac, the amniotic sac, around the embryo.
  • Amniotic fluid cushions the foetus against shocks, keeps its temperature steady, lets it move and develop its muscles and bones, and prevents it sticking to the membranes.
  • Foetal cells in the fluid can be sampled for testing by amniocentesis (in “Prenatal and postnatal care”).

Growth and development of the baby

  • The baby develops by repeated mitosis (increasing cell number), cell growth, and cell differentiation (cells becoming specialised for different jobs), so that one zygote gives rise to all the tissues and organs.
  • Length of pregnancy (gestation): about 38 weeks from fertilisation, which in practice is counted as about 40 weeks from the first day of the woman's last period.
  • The pregnancy is divided into three trimesters of about three months each.
  • In the first two months all the main organs form, and the heart begins beating by the end of the first month.
  • By about 12 weeks the baby is recognisably human with all organs present, though tiny.
  • During the rest of pregnancy the organs grow and mature, the baby moves (felt by the mother from about 16–20 weeks), and fat is laid down under the skin in the last weeks.
  • The lungs are among the last organs to mature, which is why babies born very early have difficulty breathing (see “Prenatal and postnatal care”).
  • The first two months are when the baby is most at risk from harmful substances, because that is when the organs are forming; damage then can cause structural birth defects.

Early development: morula and blastocyst (Higher Level only)

  • Zygote: the single diploid cell formed at fertilisation.
  • Morula: after about 3–4 days of division the zygote has become a solid ball of about 16 or more cells, still inside the zona pellucida, looking like a mulberry, which is the meaning of “morula”.
  • The morula is still in the fallopian tube or just entering the uterus.
  • Blastocyst: by about day 5 the cells have rearranged into a hollow ball with a fluid-filled cavity.
  • The blastocyst has two parts: an outer layer of cells, the trophoblast, which will form the embryo's part of the placenta and its membranes; and a small cluster inside, the inner cell mass, which will form the embryo itself.
  • The blastocyst hatches out of the zona pellucida and is the stage that implants, trophoblast first.
  • The cells of the inner cell mass can develop into any type of body cell: they are embryonic stem cells.
  • Their use and the ethical questions they raise belong to “Ethics of genetic technologies”.
  • Diagram the sequence zygote → 2 cells → 4 cells → morula → blastocyst, with the blastocyst in section showing the trophoblast, inner cell mass and fluid-filled cavity; and a side view of the fallopian tube and uterus showing where each stage is: fertilisation in the tube, the morula in the tube, and the blastocyst implanting in the endometrium.

Germ layers, embryo and foetus (Higher Level only)

  • Germ layers: during the third week after fertilisation the cells of the inner cell mass move and arrange themselves into three layers, the germ layers, each of which gives rise to particular tissues and organs.
  • Ectoderm (outer layer) forms the epidermis of the skin, hair and nails, the whole nervous system (brain, spinal cord and nerves), and parts of the eye.
  • Mesoderm (middle layer) forms muscles, bones and cartilage, the heart, blood and blood vessels, the kidneys and the reproductive organs.
  • Endoderm (inner layer) forms the lining of the digestive system and of the breathing system (trachea, bronchi and lungs), and the liver and pancreas.
  • Memory aid: ecto = outer skin and nerves; meso = movement, blood and support; endo = inner linings.
  • Embryo: the developing individual from implantation, about the second week after fertilisation, until the end of the eighth week, during which all the main organs form (organ formation).
  • By the end of week 8 the embryo is about 3 cm long and has all its major organs.
  • Foetus: from the ninth week until birth. No new organs form, but the existing ones grow, mature and begin to work.
  • The distinction: embryo means organs forming; foetus means organs present and growing.
  • Summary sequence: zygote (day 0, fallopian tube) → morula (days 3–4) → blastocyst (about day 5; implants about days 6–7) → germ layers form (week 3) → embryo (to the end of week 8; organs form) → foetus (week 9 to birth; growth and maturation).

Labour and birth

  • Labour is the process by which the baby, then the placenta, is pushed out of the uterus.
  • Labour usually begins at about 40 weeks of pregnancy, when the baby is normally head down.
  • Hormonal changes start it, and the hormone oxytocin drives the contractions by positive feedback (detail in “Hormones in reproduction”; positive feedback is defined in “Homeostasis as a stimulus-response model”).
  • Stage 1 (dilation): the muscle of the uterus wall begins to contract regularly, and the contractions become stronger and more frequent.
  • The contractions pull the cervix open until it is fully dilated, about 10 cm wide.
  • The mucus plug comes away, and the amnion usually bursts, releasing the amniotic fluid (“the waters break”).
  • Stage 1 is the longest stage, often many hours in a first birth.
  • Stage 2 (delivery, or expulsion): strong contractions of the uterus, helped by the mother pushing with her abdominal muscles, push the baby, usually head first, out through the cervix and the vagina, the birth canal.
  • The umbilical cord is then clamped and cut; the stump later dries and falls off, leaving the navel.
  • At birth the baby must start breathing for itself: the first breaths inflate the lungs, and its circulation changes so that blood goes to the lungs for oxygen instead of relying on the placenta.
  • Stage 3 (afterbirth): shortly after the birth, further contractions separate the placenta and membranes from the uterus wall and push them out.
  • Contraction of the uterus also closes the blood vessels where the placenta was attached, limiting bleeding.
  • A Caesarean section is the delivery of the baby through a surgical cut in the abdomen and uterus, used when a vaginal birth would be unsafe for the mother or baby, for example a baby lying the wrong way, or a placenta blocking the cervix.

Key terms

Zygote
The single diploid cell formed when a sperm fertilises an egg; the first cell of the new individual.
Implantation
The embedding of the blastocyst in the thick endometrium of the uterus, about 6–7 days after fertilisation.
Ectopic pregnancy
A pregnancy in which the embryo implants outside the uterus, most often in a fallopian tube.
Villi
Finger-like projections from the outer cells of the embryo that grow into the endometrium and form the placenta.
Placenta
A disc-shaped organ attached to the uterus wall, formed from the embryo's villi and the mother's endometrium, which exchanges substances between mother and foetus without their blood mixing.
Umbilical cord
The cord that joins the foetus to the placenta, containing two umbilical arteries and one umbilical vein.
Amnion
The thin membrane that forms the fluid-filled amniotic sac around the embryo and foetus.
Amniotic fluid
The fluid in the amniotic sac that cushions the foetus, keeps its temperature steady and lets it move.
Foetus
The developing baby from the ninth week after fertilisation until birth, when its organs are present and growing.
Embryo
The developing individual from implantation until the end of the eighth week, during which all the main organs form.
hCG
Human chorionic gonadotrophin, a hormone made by the embryo after implantation that keeps the corpus luteum alive; pregnancy tests detect it in urine.
Labour
The process by which the baby and then the placenta are pushed out of the uterus, in three stages.
Dilation
The widening of the cervix to about 10 cm during the first stage of labour.
Morula
The solid ball of about 16 or more cells formed by the zygote after about 3–4 days of division (Higher Level).
Blastocyst
The hollow ball of cells formed by about day 5, with an outer trophoblast and an inner cell mass, which implants (Higher Level).
Trophoblast
The outer layer of the blastocyst, which forms the embryo's part of the placenta and its membranes (Higher Level).
Inner cell mass
The small cluster of cells inside the blastocyst that forms the embryo itself (Higher Level).
Embryonic stem cells
Cells of the inner cell mass that can develop into any type of body cell (Higher Level).
Germ layers
The three layers, ectoderm, mesoderm and endoderm, formed in the third week, each giving rise to particular tissues and organs (Higher Level).
Ectoderm
The outer germ layer, which forms the skin's epidermis, hair, nails, the nervous system and parts of the eye (Higher Level).
Mesoderm
The middle germ layer, which forms muscles, bones, cartilage, the heart, blood vessels, kidneys and reproductive organs (Higher Level).
Endoderm
The inner germ layer, which forms the linings of the digestive and breathing systems, the liver and the pancreas (Higher Level).
