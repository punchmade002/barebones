Fertile periods

  • Fertility is the ability to produce offspring. A fertile period is the time during which intercourse can lead to pregnancy.
  • Males are continuously fertile: from puberty, sperm are made every day for the rest of life, declining slowly in old age, and every ejaculation contains millions of sperm (see “Gametes and fertilisation”).
  • Females are cyclically fertile: from puberty to menopause, usually one egg is released per cycle, and it survives only about 12–24 hours, while sperm can survive up to about five days.
  • So the fertile window in each cycle is about six days: the five days before ovulation and the day of ovulation.
  • In a regular 28-day cycle this is roughly days 9 to 14, but because ovulation day varies (see “The menstrual cycle”), the window cannot be predicted exactly from the calendar alone.
  • Female fertility also changes over a lifetime: it is highest in the twenties and falls with age, more steeply after about 35, because the number and quality of the remaining eggs decline (see “Gametes and fertilisation”).
  • Female fertility ends at the menopause (see “Hormones in reproduction”).

Contraception: what it is

  • Contraception is the deliberate prevention of pregnancy.
  • Methods work by preventing ovulation, by preventing sperm from reaching the egg (preventing fertilisation), or by preventing implantation.
  • Methods are grouped as natural, mechanical (barrier), surgical and chemical (hormonal and spermicidal).
  • Effectiveness with perfect use: the male condom is about 98% effective; the pill, the implant, the coils and sterilisation are each more than 99% effective.
  • With typical, real-life use, methods that depend on the user remembering or using them correctly every time, namely natural methods, condoms and the pill, fail more often.
  • Long-acting methods, namely the implant, coils and sterilisation, stay above 99% effective with typical use.
  • Only condoms, male and female, protect against sexually transmitted infections (STIs).
  • People using another method need condoms as well to reduce the risk of STIs.

Natural methods

  • Abstinence, not having intercourse, is the only method that is completely effective against pregnancy and STIs.
  • Fertility awareness (natural family planning): the couple avoid intercourse during the fertile window, found by combining signs.
  • Sign 1, the calendar (rhythm) method: recording cycle lengths to estimate when ovulation happens.
  • Sign 2, body temperature: measured each morning before getting up, it rises by a small amount, a few tenths of a degree Celsius, just after ovulation and stays up for the rest of the cycle, because of progesterone.
  • Sign 3, cervical mucus: it becomes thin, clear, stretchy and slippery around ovulation.
  • Home kits that detect the hormone surge before ovulation in urine can also be used (see “The menstrual cycle”).
  • Advantages of natural methods: no drugs, devices or side effects, and they are acceptable to people whose religious beliefs rule out artificial contraception, for example the teaching of the Catholic Church.
  • Limits of natural methods: they need training, daily records and regular cycles, and are much less reliable with typical use.
  • Natural methods are hard to use after childbirth, when breastfeeding or near the menopause, and give no protection against STIs.
  • Withdrawal of the penis before ejaculation is unreliable, because some sperm may be released before ejaculation.
  • Breastfeeding delays ovulation but is not reliable contraception (see “Hormones in reproduction”).

Mechanical (barrier and device) methods

  • Male condom: a thin latex, or non-latex, sheath placed over the erect penis that collects the semen, so sperm cannot enter the vagina.
  • The male condom also blocks the exchange of body fluids, so it reduces the spread of STIs, including HIV.
  • The male condom is cheap, widely available and has no hormones; it can tear or slip, and must be put on before any contact.
  • Female condom: a soft pouch placed in the vagina that lines it and collects semen; it also protects against STIs.
  • Diaphragm or cap: a dome of soft silicone placed over the cervix before intercourse, used with a spermicide and left in for several hours afterwards; it does not protect well against STIs.
  • Copper coil (copper intrauterine device, IUD): a small T-shaped device with copper, placed in the uterus by a doctor or nurse.
  • Copper is toxic to sperm and eggs, preventing fertilisation, and it changes the endometrium so an embryo is unlikely to implant.
  • The copper coil lasts for 5–10 years, contains no hormones, and can be removed at any time with a quick return to fertility.
  • The copper coil can also be used as emergency contraception if fitted within five days.
  • The copper coil can make periods heavier, and gives no protection against STIs.
  • It is grouped as mechanical because it is a device; the hormonal coil is in the chemical block.

Surgical methods

  • Vasectomy (male sterilisation): under local anaesthetic, each vas deferens is cut and tied or sealed, so sperm cannot leave the testes (see “The male reproductive system”).
  • After a vasectomy the man still ejaculates semen, because the fluids come from glands that are untouched; his testosterone and sex drive are unaffected.
  • The sperm that are still made are broken down and reabsorbed.
  • A vasectomy is not effective at once, because sperm remain in the ducts beyond the cut; semen tests are needed after some weeks to confirm there are no sperm.
  • Tubal ligation (female sterilisation): each fallopian tube is cut, tied or blocked with a clip, usually by keyhole surgery under general anaesthetic, so the egg and sperm cannot meet.
  • After tubal ligation ovulation and the menstrual cycle continue as before, because the egg is reabsorbed.
  • Tubal ligation is effective at once.
  • Sterilisation is very effective and permanent, with nothing to remember, but it should be treated as irreversible: reversal is difficult and often fails.
  • Sterilisation carries the small risks of any surgery, and gives no protection against STIs.
  • It suits people who are sure they want no more children.

Chemical methods

  • The combined pill contains synthetic oestrogen and progesterone and prevents ovulation (how, in “Hormonal manipulation”); it also thickens the cervical mucus so sperm cannot pass.
  • The combined pill is taken daily, usually with a break or dummy pills each month, during which a withdrawal bleed happens.
  • The progestogen-only pill (synthetic progesterone only) works mainly by thickening the cervical mucus, and in some types also stops ovulation; it suits women who cannot take oestrogen.
  • Long-acting hormonal methods release a synthetic progesterone continuously.
  • The implant is a small flexible rod placed under the skin of the upper arm, lasting about 3 years.
  • The injection is given every few months.
  • The hormonal coil (intrauterine system, IUS) is placed in the uterus and lasts several years; it thickens the cervical mucus and thins the endometrium.
  • The contraceptive patch and vaginal ring release oestrogen and progesterone, like the combined pill.
  • Emergency hormonal pills, taken as soon as possible after unprotected intercourse, work mainly by delaying ovulation; one type works within 3 days and another within 5 days.
  • The copper coil fitted within 5 days is the most effective emergency method (see the mechanical methods). Emergency pills are not for regular use.
  • Spermicides are chemicals in a gel or cream that kill sperm; they are not reliable alone and are used with a diaphragm or cap.
  • Hormonal methods are very effective; periods often become lighter, more regular and less painful.
  • The combined pill lowers the risk of cancers of the ovary and of the endometrium (womb).
  • Hormonal methods can cause side effects, for example headaches, mood changes and irregular bleeding with progesterone-only methods.
  • The combined pill slightly raises the risk of blood clots (deep vein thrombosis) and stroke, especially in smokers and women over 35, so it is not prescribed to everyone.
  • Hormonal methods give no protection against STIs.
  • The pill must be taken correctly every day; vomiting or some medicines can stop it working.

Preventing sexually transmitted infections

  • An STI (sexually transmitted infection) is an infection passed on mainly by sexual contact.
  • Bacterial STIs are chlamydia, gonorrhoea and syphilis, which are treated with antibiotics.
  • Viral STIs are HIV, genital herpes, human papillomavirus (HPV, which causes genital warts and most cervical cancers) and hepatitis B, which can be controlled but is not usually cured.
  • Pathogens are described in “Pathogens”.
  • Chlamydia and gonorrhoea often cause no symptoms, especially in women, but can spread to the uterus and fallopian tubes, causing pelvic inflammatory disease, which scars and blocks the tubes.
  • Blocked tubes from pelvic inflammatory disease are a major preventable cause of female infertility, and they raise the risk of ectopic pregnancy (see “The female reproductive system”).
  • In men chlamydia and gonorrhoea can inflame the epididymis.
  • Prevention: correct use of condoms every time, and fewer sexual partners.
  • Prevention: regular testing and treatment of infected people and their partners breaks the chain of transmission, because many infected people have no symptoms.
  • Prevention: vaccination against HPV (see “Causes, prevention and treatment of cancer”) and hepatitis B.
  • For HIV, anti-HIV drugs taken before possible exposure (pre-exposure prophylaxis, PrEP), and treatment of people with HIV, which reduces their virus to levels at which it is not passed on sexually.
  • Gonorrhoea is becoming resistant to antibiotics, which makes prevention more important (resistance in “Emergence and spread of infectious disease”).

Freezing eggs

  • Egg freezing (oocyte cryopreservation) preserves fertility for later.
  • Step 1: hormone injections stimulate the ovaries to mature several eggs at once.
  • Step 2: the eggs are collected with a fine needle through the vaginal wall, guided by ultrasound, under sedation.
  • Step 3: the eggs are frozen very rapidly (vitrification), so that ice crystals, which would damage the cells, do not form, and they are stored in liquid nitrogen at −196 °C.
  • Step 4: later the eggs are thawed and fertilised by IVF or ICSI (below).
  • Uses: before cancer treatment, because chemotherapy or radiotherapy can destroy the eggs in the ovaries, or by women who wish to delay having children.
  • Sperm, and embryos made in IVF, can also be frozen; sperm freezing is offered to men before cancer treatment.
  • Limits: success depends strongly on the woman's age when the eggs are frozen, since younger eggs have fewer chromosome errors and survive freezing better.
  • Freezing gives no guarantee of a baby, and not every egg survives thawing.
  • The process involves hormone treatment and a procedure, and it is expensive.

Infertility and its causes

  • Infertility is the failure to become pregnant after 12 months of regular unprotected intercourse.
  • The World Health Organization estimated in 2023 that about 1 in 6 people worldwide experience infertility at some point in their lives.
  • Causes are found in the man, the woman, both, or neither, which is called unexplained infertility.
  • Male causes: low sperm count; sperm that swim poorly or are abnormally shaped, for example with a faulty tail.
  • Male causes: a blockage of the epididymis or vas deferens, for example after infection or vasectomy; undescended testes; and low testosterone or low pituitary hormones.
  • Male causes: lifestyle and environmental factors that reduce sperm production, such as smoking, heavy drinking, obesity, anabolic steroids (see “Hormonal manipulation”) and overheating of the testes.
  • Male infertility is diagnosed by semen analysis, which tests sperm count, movement and shape under the microscope.
  • Female cause: failure to ovulate, caused by a hormone imbalance, for example polycystic ovary syndrome (PCOS), or by being very underweight or overweight.
  • Female cause: blocked or damaged fallopian tubes, for example after pelvic inflammatory disease from an STI.
  • Female cause: endometriosis, in which tissue like the endometrium grows outside the uterus and can cause scarring around the tubes and ovaries.
  • Female causes also include problems of the uterus or cervical mucus, and age.
  • Female infertility is diagnosed by blood hormone tests, ultrasound of the ovaries and uterus, and tests that show whether the tubes are open.

Stimulating ovulation

  • Stimulating ovulation is for women who do not ovulate regularly, for example because of PCOS.
  • Fertility drugs, as tablets or injections, cause the ovaries to mature and release eggs.
  • The woman is monitored by ultrasound to see how many follicles develop, and intercourse or insemination is timed to ovulation.
  • Risk: more than one egg may be released, so twins or triplets are more likely.
  • Risk: the ovaries can over-respond (ovarian hyperstimulation syndrome), becoming swollen and painful, which is occasionally serious.
  • Lifestyle change, for example weight loss, can restore ovulation in some women.

How ovulation-stimulating drugs work (Higher Level only)

  • Some fertility tablets block oestrogen's negative feedback on the brain and pituitary, so the pituitary releases more FSH and LH, which stimulate follicle development and ovulation (feedback in “The menstrual cycle”).
  • Injected fertility drugs contain FSH, often with LH, and act directly on the ovaries to make follicles develop.
  • A final injection of a hormone that acts like LH triggers the eggs' final maturation and ovulation, imitating the LH surge.
  • The same injections are used to stimulate the ovaries in IVF and egg freezing.

Artificial insemination

  • Artificial insemination, or intrauterine insemination (IUI): semen from the partner or a donor is collected, the sperm are washed and concentrated, and they are placed directly into the uterus through the cervix with a thin tube, at the time of ovulation.
  • This places many active sperm close to the fallopian tubes.
  • It is used for a mildly low sperm count or poor motility, cervical mucus that blocks sperm, difficulty with intercourse, and single women or female couples using donor sperm.
  • The woman's fallopian tubes must be open.
  • The same technique is widely used in farming, for example to breed cattle from selected bulls.

In vitro fertilisation (IVF)

  • IVF means “in glass”, that is, fertilisation outside the body.
  • Step 1: hormone injections stimulate the ovaries to mature several eggs.
  • Step 2: the eggs are collected with a needle guided by ultrasound.
  • Step 3: the eggs are mixed with sperm in a dish in the laboratory, where fertilisation takes place.
  • Step 4: the embryos are grown in an incubator for 2–5 days, up to the blastocyst stage (see “Pregnancy”).
  • Step 5: usually one, sometimes two, embryos are placed in the uterus through the cervix with a thin tube.
  • Step 6: a pregnancy test about two weeks later shows whether an embryo has implanted.
  • Spare good-quality embryos can be frozen for later attempts.
  • IVF is used for blocked or damaged fallopian tubes, unexplained infertility, endometriosis, failure of other treatments, and couples needing preimplantation genetic testing (see “Prenatal and postnatal care”).
  • History: the first baby conceived by IVF, Louise Brown, was born in England on 25 July 1978, after work by the scientist Robert Edwards and the gynaecologist Patrick Steptoe.
  • Edwards was awarded the Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine in 2010.
  • Millions of babies have since been born by IVF worldwide.

Intracytoplasmic sperm injection (ICSI)

  • ICSI is the same as IVF except that in step 3 a single sperm is chosen and injected directly into the cytoplasm of each egg with a very fine glass needle, under a microscope.
  • Because the sperm does not have to swim to or enter the egg itself, ICSI overcomes severe male infertility.
  • ICSI is used for very low sperm count, sperm that cannot swim or cannot penetrate the egg, and previous failure of fertilisation in IVF.
  • ICSI is also used when sperm are taken directly from the testis or epididymis by a needle, for example after a vasectomy or a blockage.
  • ICSI is also used with frozen eggs (see the block on freezing eggs above).

Contraception: arguments for and against

  • For: contraception lets people choose whether and when to have children and how many, and to space pregnancies, which improves the health of mothers and babies.
  • For: it prevents unintended pregnancy, including in teenagers.
  • For: condoms reduce the spread of STIs, including HIV, a major public-health benefit.
  • For: hormonal methods can treat heavy or painful periods, and the combined pill lowers the risk of ovarian and endometrial cancer.
  • For: at a global scale, access to contraception slows population growth, reducing pressure on food, water and habitats (a sustainability link; see “The cross-cutting themes”).
  • Against: hormonal methods have side effects and, for some women, a small increased risk of blood clots.
  • Against: no method except condoms prevents STIs, so a person relying on the pill may still catch one.
  • Against: user-dependent methods often fail in real life.
  • Against: sterilisation is effectively irreversible and may be regretted.
  • Against: some people object to artificial contraception on religious or moral grounds.
  • Against: synthetic oestrogen from the pill, along with natural oestrogens, passes through sewage treatment into rivers, where it has been linked to the feminisation of male fish, an environmental effect.

Infertility treatment: arguments for and against

  • For: it allows people with infertility to have a child who is genetically their own, relieving much distress.
  • For: ICSI lets many men with very few or poorly swimming sperm become fathers.
  • For: egg and sperm freezing preserve fertility for people having cancer treatment.
  • For: preimplantation testing in IVF lets couples who carry a serious genetic condition avoid passing it on.
  • For: donor eggs or sperm allow people without their own gametes to have children.
  • For: research in IVF has increased understanding of fertilisation and early development.
  • Against: stimulating ovulation and transferring more than one embryo raise the chance of twins and triplets, which are more often born premature and underweight and carry more risk for the mother; this is why single-embryo transfer is now favoured.
  • Against: ovarian hyperstimulation can be dangerous.
  • Against: success rates per cycle are limited and fall sharply with the woman's age, so treatment can mean repeated cycles with much stress and cost, and unequal access for those who cannot pay.
  • Against: ICSI bypasses the natural selection of sperm, so a genetic cause of the father's infertility, for example some changes on the Y chromosome, can be passed to a son.
  • Against: spare frozen embryos raise questions about how long they are stored and whether they may be used in research or discarded (see “Ethics of genetic technologies”).
  • Against: children conceived with donor gametes may want to know their genetic origins, which raises questions about donor anonymity.
  • Against: treatment allows parenthood at older ages, when pregnancy carries more risk, for example a higher chance of chromosome conditions (see “Mutations”).

Egg freezing: arguments for and against

  • For: it preserves a woman's own eggs before cancer treatment.
  • For: it gives women more choice over when to have children.
  • Against: success is uncertain, and lower when eggs are frozen at older ages.
  • Against: it involves hormone treatment, egg collection and high costs.
  • Against: it may give a false sense of security that leads to delayed attempts at pregnancy.

Key terms

Fertility
The ability to produce offspring.
Fertile window
The fertile period of a cycle, about six days, from five days before ovulation to the day of ovulation.
Contraception
The deliberate prevention of pregnancy, by stopping ovulation, stopping fertilisation or stopping implantation.
Abstinence
Not having intercourse; the only method completely effective against pregnancy and STIs.
Fertility awareness
A natural method of avoiding intercourse in the fertile window, using the calendar, body temperature and cervical mucus.
Condom
A sheath, male or female, that collects semen so sperm cannot enter the vagina; the only method that protects against STIs.
Copper coil
A T-shaped copper intrauterine device that is toxic to sperm and eggs and makes implantation unlikely; it contains no hormones.
Vasectomy
Male sterilisation in which each vas deferens is cut and tied or sealed, so sperm cannot leave the testes.
Tubal ligation
Female sterilisation in which each fallopian tube is cut, tied or clipped, so egg and sperm cannot meet.
Combined pill
A pill containing synthetic oestrogen and progesterone, which prevents ovulation and thickens the cervical mucus.
Progestogen-only pill
A pill containing synthetic progesterone only, which mainly thickens the cervical mucus.
Emergency contraception
Methods used after unprotected intercourse, mainly emergency pills that delay ovulation or a copper coil fitted within 5 days.
Spermicide
A chemical in a gel or cream that kills sperm, used with a diaphragm or cap.
Sexually transmitted infection (STI)
An infection passed on mainly by sexual contact, such as chlamydia, gonorrhoea, syphilis, HIV, herpes, HPV or hepatitis B.
Pelvic inflammatory disease
Infection that spreads from the cervix to the uterus and fallopian tubes, scarring and blocking them, often caused by chlamydia or gonorrhoea.
PrEP
Pre-exposure prophylaxis: anti-HIV drugs taken before possible exposure to HIV to prevent infection.
Egg freezing
Preserving a woman's eggs by stimulating and collecting them, then freezing them by vitrification in liquid nitrogen at −196 °C.
Vitrification
Very rapid freezing that prevents ice crystals from forming and damaging cells, used to freeze eggs.
Infertility
The failure to become pregnant after 12 months of regular unprotected intercourse.
Semen analysis
A test of sperm count, movement and shape under the microscope, used to diagnose male infertility.
Polycystic ovary syndrome (PCOS)
A hormone imbalance that can stop regular ovulation and is a cause of female infertility.
Endometriosis
A condition in which tissue like the endometrium grows outside the uterus and can scar the tubes and ovaries.
Ovarian hyperstimulation syndrome
An over-response of the ovaries to fertility drugs, making them swollen and painful and occasionally serious.
Artificial insemination (IUI)
Placing washed, concentrated sperm directly into the uterus at ovulation through a thin tube through the cervix.
In vitro fertilisation (IVF)
Fertilisation of eggs with sperm in a laboratory dish, followed by transfer of an embryo to the uterus.
Intracytoplasmic sperm injection (ICSI)
A form of IVF in which a single sperm is injected directly into the cytoplasm of each egg.
Preimplantation genetic testing
Testing one or a few cells of an early IVF embryo for a serious genetic condition before it is placed in the uterus.
FSH
Follicle-stimulating hormone, made by the pituitary, which stimulates follicles to develop; injected FSH is used to stimulate ovaries (Higher Level).
LH
Luteinising hormone, made by the pituitary, whose surge triggers ovulation; a hormone that acts like LH is injected to trigger egg maturation (Higher Level).
