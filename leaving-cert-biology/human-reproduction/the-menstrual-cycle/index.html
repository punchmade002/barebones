What the menstrual cycle is

  • The menstrual cycle is the series of changes in the ovary and the uterus that happens about once a month in a woman from puberty to menopause, except during pregnancy.
  • Its purpose is to release an egg and to prepare the endometrium to receive an embryo.
  • The cycle lasts about 28 days on average, but a cycle of about 21 to 35 days is normal, and its length varies between women and from month to month.
  • Day 1 is the first day of bleeding.
  • Two sets of changes run side by side: the ovarian cycle, in which a follicle develops, releases its egg and becomes the corpus luteum, and the uterine cycle, in which the endometrium is shed, rebuilt and thickened.
  • Hormones coordinate the two sets of changes.
  • The first period (menarche) occurs during puberty, and the cycles end at the menopause (see “Hormones in reproduction”).

The four phases

The typical days are for a 28-day cycle.

  • Menstruation (days 1–5, about): if no egg was fertilised in the previous cycle, the thickened outer layer of the endometrium breaks down and is shed with a small amount of blood through the cervix and vagina; this is a period.
  • Shedding lasts about 3–7 days.
  • Follicular phase (day 1 to about day 13, overlapping menstruation): in the ovary, several follicles start to develop, and usually one grows into a large, mature, fluid-filled follicle containing the egg, called a Graafian follicle.
  • In the uterus, after menstruation ends, the endometrium is repaired and grows thicker from about day 6.
  • Near the end of this phase the cervical mucus becomes thin, clear and slippery, letting sperm through.
  • Ovulation (about day 14): the Graafian follicle bursts at the surface of the ovary and releases its egg, which is swept into the fallopian tube.
  • This is the most fertile time; the egg survives about 12–24 hours (see “Gametes and fertilisation”).
  • Luteal phase (about days 15–28): the empty follicle turns into a yellow mass of cells, the corpus luteum (“yellow body”).
  • The endometrium thickens further, becoming spongy, with more blood vessels and glands, ready for an embryo to implant.
  • If no embryo implants, the corpus luteum breaks down after about 10–14 days and the endometrium is shed; the next menstruation begins on day 1.
  • If an embryo implants, the corpus luteum stays active (see “Hormones in reproduction”) and there is no period.
  • Ovulation happens about 14 days before the next period starts. The luteal phase is fairly constant, at about 14 days; differences in cycle length come mostly from the follicular phase.
  • So in a 35-day cycle ovulation is about day 21, not day 14.
  • Summary, one bullet per phase – days – what happens in the ovary – what happens in the endometrium. Menstruation: days 1–5 – the corpus luteum of the last cycle has broken down – the endometrium is shed.
  • Summary: follicular phase – days 1–13 – a follicle develops into the Graafian follicle – the endometrium is repaired and grows thicker from about day 6.
  • Summary: ovulation – about day 14 – the Graafian follicle bursts and releases the egg – the endometrium is at the thickness built up in the follicular phase.
  • Summary: luteal phase – days 15–28 – the corpus luteum forms and then breaks down if there is no pregnancy – the endometrium thickens further, becoming spongy, and is shed if no embryo implants.

Hormone changes in each phase (Higher Level only)

  • The four hormones and their sources: FSH and LH are made by the pituitary gland; oestrogen is made mainly by the developing follicle, and in smaller amounts by the corpus luteum; progesterone is made by the corpus luteum.
  • Menstruation: oestrogen and progesterone are both low, because the corpus luteum of the last cycle has broken down.
  • The fall in progesterone is what causes the endometrium to break down.
  • Low progesterone no longer holds back the pituitary, so FSH begins to rise.
  • Follicular phase: FSH stimulates follicles to develop, and one becomes the Graafian follicle; the growing follicle secretes oestrogen, so oestrogen rises steadily.
  • Oestrogen repairs and thickens the endometrium and makes the cervical mucus thin.
  • Rising oestrogen at first inhibits FSH, so FSH falls back and usually only one follicle completes development.
  • Ovulation: when oestrogen reaches a high peak (around day 12–13), it stimulates the pituitary to release a large surge of LH, with a smaller rise in FSH.
  • The LH surge causes the Graafian follicle to burst and release its egg about a day later (ovulation, about day 14).
  • Oestrogen falls just after ovulation.
  • Luteal phase: LH causes the empty Graafian follicle to develop into the corpus luteum.
  • The corpus luteum secretes progesterone (and some oestrogen), so progesterone rises to a peak around day 21.
  • Progesterone maintains and further thickens the endometrium, making it ready for implantation, and makes the cervical mucus thick again.
  • High progesterone, with oestrogen, inhibits FSH and LH, so no new follicle develops and no further egg is released while the corpus luteum is active; FSH and LH stay low.
  • End of the cycle: without pregnancy, low LH means the corpus luteum is no longer maintained; it breaks down after about 10–14 days, so progesterone and oestrogen fall.
  • The endometrium can no longer be maintained and is shed (menstruation); the fall removes the inhibition of FSH, which rises and starts a new cycle.
  • If an embryo implants, a hormone from the embryo keeps the corpus luteum alive, so progesterone stays high and menstruation does not happen (detail in “Hormones in reproduction”).
  • Summary table, one bullet per phase – FSH, LH, oestrogen, progesterone, and the state of the ovary: menstruation – FSH rising, LH low, oestrogen low, progesterone low – a new follicle begins to grow.
  • Summary: follicular phase – FSH high then falling, LH low, oestrogen rising, progesterone low – a follicle grows to the Graafian follicle.
  • Summary: ovulation – LH surge, a small FSH peak, oestrogen at its peak then falling, progesterone low – the Graafian follicle bursts.
  • Summary: luteal phase – FSH and LH low, progesterone high with a peak about day 21, oestrogen at a moderate second, smaller peak, both falling at the end – the corpus luteum active, then breaking down.

Feedback between the ovary and the pituitary (Higher Level only)

  • The cycle is controlled by feedback.
  • Negative feedback: progesterone, and moderate oestrogen, inhibits the pituitary's release of FSH and LH; this keeps FSH and LH low in the luteal phase, and their fall lets the corpus luteum break down (see “Homeostasis as a stimulus-response model” for negative feedback).
  • Positive feedback: high oestrogen just before ovulation causes more LH to be released, giving the LH surge.
  • Each hormone controls the next: FSH → follicle grows → oestrogen → LH surge → ovulation and corpus luteum → progesterone → FSH and LH inhibited → corpus luteum breaks down → progesterone falls → menstruation and FSH rises.
  • Diagram this sequence as a loop (a flow chart) linking the pituitary, the ovary and the endometrium, with “+” on the oestrogen-to-LH arrow and “−” on the progesterone-to-FSH/LH arrow.
  • Technology link: home ovulation test kits detect the LH surge in urine, showing that ovulation is likely within about a day or two.
  • Technology link: the combined contraceptive pill gives steady oestrogen and progesterone, which by negative feedback keep FSH and LH low, so no follicle matures and no LH surge occurs (the pill is in “Hormonal manipulation” and “Fertility control and infertility”).

The hormone graphs (Higher Level only)

  • How to read the standard graphs of a 28-day cycle: FSH has a rise early in the cycle and a small peak at day 14.
  • LH is low except for a sharp, tall peak just before day 14.
  • Oestrogen rises through the follicular phase to a peak just before the LH surge, drops after ovulation, then has a lower second rise in the luteal phase.
  • Progesterone is low until ovulation, rises to a peak around day 21, then falls to day 28.
  • Exam answers explain each change by its cause, for example: progesterone rises after day 14 because the corpus luteum secretes it.

Label the LH surge, the oestrogen peak, the progesterone peak and ovulation.

Key terms

Menstrual cycle
The monthly series of changes in the ovary and uterus, from puberty to menopause, which releases an egg and prepares the endometrium for an embryo.
Menstruation
The shedding of the endometrium with a small amount of blood when no egg has been fertilised; a period, lasting about 3–7 days.
Follicular phase
The phase in which follicles develop in the ovary, one becomes a Graafian follicle, and the endometrium is repaired and thickened.
Ovulation
The bursting of the Graafian follicle at the surface of the ovary, which releases the egg into the fallopian tube, about day 14 of a 28-day cycle.
Luteal phase
The phase after ovulation in which the corpus luteum forms and the endometrium thickens further, lasting about 14 days.
Graafian follicle
A mature, fluid-filled follicle in the ovary that contains the egg and bursts at ovulation.
Corpus luteum
The yellow mass of cells that forms from the empty follicle after ovulation and secretes progesterone; it breaks down about 10–14 days later if there is no pregnancy.
Endometrium
The inner lining of the uterus, which thickens each cycle to receive an embryo and is shed in menstruation if no embryo implants.
FSH
Follicle-stimulating hormone, made by the pituitary gland; it stimulates follicles to develop and falls back when oestrogen rises (Higher Level).
LH
Luteinising hormone, made by the pituitary gland; its surge causes ovulation and it turns the empty follicle into the corpus luteum (Higher Level).
LH surge
A sudden large rise in LH, triggered by high oestrogen, which causes the Graafian follicle to release its egg about a day later (Higher Level).
Oestrogen
A hormone made mainly by the developing follicle, which repairs and thickens the endometrium and triggers the LH surge when it peaks (Higher Level).
Progesterone
A hormone made by the corpus luteum that maintains and thickens the endometrium and inhibits FSH and LH; it falls when the corpus luteum breaks down (Higher Level).
Negative feedback
A control in which a rise in a hormone inhibits the release of the hormone that caused it, keeping levels steady (Higher Level).
Positive feedback
A control in which a change is amplified, such as high oestrogen causing more LH to be released, giving the LH surge (Higher Level).
