Gametes and meiosis

  • A gamete is a haploid sex cell. In humans the haploid number is 23 (n = 23).
  • Sperm and eggs are made by meiosis, so each carries one chromosome of each pair, and each gamete is genetically different (see “Mitosis and meiosis”).
  • At fertilisation two haploid gametes fuse to give a diploid zygote with 46 chromosomes (2n = 46).
  • Meiosis happens only in the gonads: in the seminiferous tubules of the testes (sperm) and in the ovaries (eggs).
  • The cells that undergo meiosis are diploid; every other cell in the body divides by mitosis.

Sperm production

  • Sperm production begins at puberty and then continues every day throughout a man's life, though it declines in old age.
  • Many millions of sperm are made each day in the walls of the seminiferous tubules, from diploid cells that first multiply by mitosis and then divide by meiosis.
  • Each cell that completes meiosis gives four sperm, all of equal size.
  • Half the sperm carry an X chromosome and half carry a Y (see “Sex determination”).
  • The new sperm move into the epididymis, where they are stored and mature, becoming able to swim (see “The male reproductive system”).
  • It takes about two to three months for a sperm to be produced and matured, so a change in lifestyle or health affects sperm only after some weeks.
  • Production needs the lower temperature of the scrotum and hormones, including testosterone (control in “Hormones in reproduction”).

Egg production

  • Egg production starts before birth: while a girl is a foetus, her ovaries form all the immature eggs she will ever have, each inside a follicle.
  • Each of these eggs begins meiosis but stops part-way.
  • A baby girl is born with about one to two million immature eggs; most degenerate, and only about 400–500 are ever released in her life.
  • No new eggs are made after birth.
  • From puberty, usually one follicle each month develops fully and releases its egg at ovulation (the monthly cycle is in “The menstrual cycle”).
  • Meiosis continues around ovulation but is completed only if a sperm enters the egg.
  • Division in egg production is unequal: almost all the cytoplasm goes to one cell, the egg, and the other products are tiny cells called polar bodies, which degenerate.
  • So one cell undergoing meiosis gives one egg, not four. The egg keeps a large store of cytoplasm, food reserves and organelles for the early embryo.
  • Because the eggs are as old as the woman, they age with her: in older women more eggs have errors in chromosome separation, which is why the risk of conditions such as Down syndrome rises with the mother's age (see “Mutations”).
  • Fertility also falls with age because of these egg changes (see “Fertility control and infertility”).

Sperm and egg structure

  • Sperm: a very small, mobile cell, about 0.05 mm long.
  • The sperm head contains the haploid nucleus (23 chromosomes) and, at its tip, the acrosome, a cap containing enzymes that digest a path through the layers around the egg.
  • The sperm middle piece is packed with mitochondria that release energy (ATP) by aerobic respiration to power the tail.
  • The sperm tail is a flagellum that beats to make it swim. The sperm has very little cytoplasm, which keeps it small and streamlined.
  • Egg (ovum): one of the largest cells in the body, about 0.1 mm across, just visible to the naked eye; it cannot move by itself.
  • The egg contains a haploid nucleus (23 chromosomes, always including an X), a large volume of cytoplasm with food reserves and many mitochondria, and a cell membrane.
  • Around the egg is a thick, clear, jelly-like coat called the zona pellucida, and an outer layer of follicle cells.

Comparing sperm and egg

  • Size: sperm are tiny, about 0.05 mm long; the egg is far larger, about 0.1 mm across, with a far greater volume.
  • Movement: the sperm swims with its tail; the egg is moved by cilia in the fallopian tube.
  • Numbers: millions of sperm per ejaculation; usually one egg a month.
  • Cytoplasm and food: sperm have almost none; the egg is a large store.
  • Sex chromosome: a sperm carries an X or a Y; the egg always carries an X.
  • Production: sperm are made continuously from puberty, four per meiosis; eggs begin to form before birth, one per meiosis.

From intercourse to the egg

  • During intercourse semen is ejaculated into the top of the vagina, near the cervix.
  • Sperm swim through the cervical mucus, which is easiest to pass around ovulation when it is thin, then through the uterus and into the fallopian tubes, helped by contractions of the uterus.
  • Only a few hundred of the millions of sperm reach the egg; most die in the acidic vagina, are blocked by mucus, or go into the tube with no egg.
  • Sperm can survive up to about five days in the female tract.
  • An egg survives only about 12–24 hours after ovulation.
  • So intercourse in the five days before ovulation, or on the day of ovulation, can lead to fertilisation (used in “Fertility control and infertility”).

Fertilisation

  • Fertilisation is the fusion of the haploid nucleus of a sperm with the haploid nucleus of an egg to form a diploid zygote.
  • In humans it normally takes place in the upper part of a fallopian tube, within a day of ovulation.
  • Step 1: sperm reach the egg and push between the follicle cells.
  • Step 2: the acrosome of a sperm releases its enzymes, which digest a path through the zona pellucida (the acrosome reaction).
  • Step 3: the membrane of one sperm fuses with the egg's membrane, and the sperm's nucleus enters the egg; the tail is left outside.
  • Step 4: at once the egg's outer layer changes and the zona pellucida hardens, so no other sperm can enter; this ensures the zygote receives only one set of chromosomes from the father.
  • Step 5: the egg completes its meiosis.
  • Step 6: the sperm nucleus and egg nucleus fuse, giving a zygote with 46 chromosomes in 23 pairs, one of each pair from each parent.
  • At fertilisation the sex of the child is decided by whether the sperm carries an X or a Y (see “Sex determination”).
  • The zygote receives its mitochondria from the egg only (see “Nuclear and non-nuclear inheritance”).
  • Fertilisation restores the diploid number and combines genetic material from two parents, so the offspring is genetically unique; with meiosis, this is a source of variation.
  • Fertilisation starts the development of the embryo. The zygote begins to divide by mitosis as it moves down the tube (continued in “Pregnancy”).
  • Diagram a sequence showing a sperm entering an egg (the acrosome digesting the zona pellucida), the sperm nucleus inside the egg, and the two nuclei fusing to form the zygote. Label the sperm nucleus, egg nucleus, zona pellucida, zygote, n and 2n.

Twins

  • Identical twins come from one zygote that splits into two embryos early in development, so they have the same genes.
  • Non-identical (fraternal) twins come from two eggs released at the same time and fertilised by two different sperm, so they are no more alike genetically than other brothers and sisters.

Key terms

Gamete
A haploid sex cell, a sperm or an egg, which fuses with another gamete at fertilisation.
Haploid
Having one set of chromosomes, as in gametes; in humans n = 23.
Diploid
Having two sets of chromosomes, as in the zygote and most body cells; in humans 2n = 46.
Meiosis
The cell division that makes haploid gametes from diploid cells in the gonads.
Fertilisation
The fusion of a haploid sperm nucleus with a haploid egg nucleus to form a diploid zygote, normally in the fallopian tube.
Zygote
The diploid cell formed when a sperm fertilises an egg; the first cell of a new individual.
Acrosome
A cap at the tip of the sperm head containing enzymes that digest a path through the layers around the egg.
Flagellum
The whip-like tail of a sperm that beats to make it swim.
Zona pellucida
The thick, clear, jelly-like coat around the egg, which the acrosome enzymes digest and which hardens after fertilisation to block other sperm.
Polar body
A tiny cell produced in egg formation that receives little cytoplasm and degenerates, leaving one large egg.
Identical twins
Twins that develop from one zygote splitting into two embryos, so they share the same genes.
Non-identical twins
Twins that develop from two eggs fertilised by two different sperm, so they are genetically no more alike than siblings.
Acrosome reaction
The release of acrosome enzymes by a sperm, which digests a path through the zona pellucida.
Follicle cells
The outer layer of cells around the egg, which the sperm must push between to reach it.
