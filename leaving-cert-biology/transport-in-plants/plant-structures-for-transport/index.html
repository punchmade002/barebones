Why plants need transport, and the root and shoot systems

  • A large plant has a small surface area to volume ratio (see Levels of organisation), so diffusion alone cannot supply its cells.
  • Roots take in water and minerals from the soil, but these are needed in the leaves; leaves make sugar, but this is needed in the roots, growing tips, flowers, fruits and storage organs.
  • Xylem and phloem, the two transport tissues, link the roots and the leaves.
  • The root system, everything below ground, anchors the plant, absorbs water and minerals through root hairs, and often stores food, e.g. the swollen root of a carrot.
  • The shoot system (stem, leaves, buds and flowers) holds the leaves up to the light, carries out photosynthesis and gas exchange, transports materials between roots and leaves, and reproduces.
  • The two systems are joined by continuous vascular tissue: the xylem and phloem of the root run up through the stem and branch out into every leaf as the veins, giving an unbroken pipeline from root hair to leaf cell.

The three tissue types

  • Dermal tissue is the outer protective layer. In young roots, stems and leaves it is the epidermis, usually one cell thick.
  • In leaves and stems the epidermis is covered by a waxy, waterproof cuticle that cuts water loss and keeps out pathogens. It contains stomata, pores that each lie between two guard cells, for gas exchange.
  • In roots the epidermis has no cuticle, and some epidermal cells grow out as root hairs for absorbing water and minerals.
  • In woody stems the epidermis is replaced by bark, which is pierced by lenticels.
  • Ground tissue makes up the bulk of the plant between the dermal and vascular tissue. It is mostly made of living, thin-walled cells with large vacuoles.
  • The job of ground tissue depends on its position: photosynthesis in the mesophyll of the leaf and in green stems; storage of starch and water in the root cortex, stem pith and potato tubers; and support, because its cells are kept firm (turgid) by water pressure.
  • Vascular tissue is the transport tissue: xylem carries water and dissolved minerals, and phloem carries dissolved food, mainly sucrose.
  • Xylem and phloem lie together in groups called vascular bundles in the stem and leaf, and in a central core in the root. Xylem also supports the plant.

Xylem: structure and function

  • Xylem carries water and dissolved minerals from the roots, up the stem, to the leaves, in one direction only, which is upwards.
  • Xylem is made of two kinds of cell, both dead and hollow when mature, with no cytoplasm, nucleus or cell membrane, so water flows through them without obstruction.
  • Xylem vessels are long continuous tubes formed from a column of cells joined end to end, whose end walls have broken down.
  • Tracheids are single long, narrow cells with tapering ends. Water passes from one tracheid to the next through thin areas in the walls called pits.
  • Pits in the side walls let water move sideways between neighbouring vessels and tracheids, and out to the surrounding cells.
  • The cell walls are thickened with lignin, a hard, waterproof substance, laid down in rings, spirals or as a solid layer with pits.
  • Lignin stops the vessels collapsing inwards when the water in them is under tension, pulled from above (see Transport of water and minerals). It makes xylem the main supporting tissue of woody plants: wood is xylem.

Phloem: structure and function

  • Phloem carries dissolved food, mainly sucrose and also amino acids, from the leaves, where it is made, to the parts that use or store it. It can carry food upwards or downwards.
  • Sieve tubes are columns of living sieve tube elements joined end to end. Their end walls are perforated with many small holes, forming sieve plates, through which the sap flows from one cell to the next.
  • A mature sieve tube element has a thin layer of cytoplasm but no nucleus and very few organelles, which leaves room for the sap to flow.
  • Each sieve tube element has a companion cell beside it, joined to it by many fine connections through the walls.
  • The companion cell has a nucleus, dense cytoplasm and many mitochondria. It keeps the sieve tube element alive and supplies the ATP needed to load sucrose into the sieve tube (see Transport of CO₂ and photosynthetic products).
  • Phloem cell walls are cellulose only, not lignified, so phloem gives little support. In a woody stem the phloem lies just inside the bark.
  • Diagram a longitudinal view of two sieve tube elements end to end, with a sieve plate between them and a companion cell alongside; label sieve tube element, sieve plate, cytoplasm, companion cell and nucleus (in the companion cell only).

Xylem and phloem compared

  • Cells: xylem vessels and tracheids are dead and hollow; sieve tube elements and companion cells are living.
  • Walls: xylem walls are thickened with lignin; phloem walls are cellulose only.
  • End walls: xylem vessels have none; sieve tubes have sieve plates.
  • Contents: xylem carries water and minerals; phloem carries sucrose and amino acids in solution.
  • Direction: xylem moves water upwards only, from roots to leaves; phloem moves food up or down, from where it is made or stored to where it is used.
  • Support: xylem supports the plant; phloem gives little support.

The root

  • The root anchors the plant in the soil, absorbs water and minerals, transports them to the stem, and may store food.
  • A root cap protects the delicate growing tip as it pushes through the soil. Just behind it is meristematic tissue whose cells divide, so the root grows in length. Root hairs develop a little further back, on the region that has stopped growing, so they are not torn off as the root moves forward.
  • Each root hair is a long, thin outgrowth of a single epidermal cell. There are thousands of them, giving a huge surface area for absorption, and they grow between soil particles in close contact with the film of soil water.
  • The root hair's wall is thin and has no cuticle, so water passes in easily. Its large vacuole holds cell sap more concentrated than the soil water, so water enters by osmosis. Its many mitochondria supply ATP for the active transport of mineral ions.
  • The root cortex is a wide zone of ground tissue made of large, thin-walled, loosely packed living cells with air spaces between them. Water and minerals pass across it from the root hairs to the xylem, it stores food, often as starch, and the air spaces let oxygen from the soil air reach the cells for respiration.
  • The innermost single layer of the cortex, the endodermis, surrounds the vascular tissue and controls what passes into the xylem.
  • In the centre of the root the xylem forms a star or X shape, with the phloem in groups between its arms. This central core resists the pulling forces on a root as the shoot is blown about, as the core of a rope does.
  • Diagram a transverse section of a young root of a broad-leaved (dicotyledonous) plant, labelled epidermis (dermal tissue), root hair, cortex (ground tissue), endodermis, xylem and phloem (vascular tissue); label the three tissue types by name as well as the structures.

The stem

  • The stem supports the leaves and flowers, holding the leaves up to the light and the flowers up for pollination. It carries water and minerals up in the xylem and food up or down in the phloem, between the roots and the leaves.
  • The stem may store food, as the potato tuber does, since it is a swollen underground stem, and if it is green it photosynthesises.
  • The epidermis on the outside of a stem is dermal tissue with a waxy cuticle, which protects the stem and reduces water loss. In a woody stem it is replaced by bark.
  • The cortex, just inside the epidermis, and the pith in the centre are both ground tissue. Their living cells store food and water and help support the stem when they are turgid.
  • The vascular bundles are arranged in a ring near the outside of the stem. In each bundle the phloem is on the outside and the xylem on the inside.
  • The ring of strong tissue near the edge resists bending as the stem is blown sideways, as a hollow tube does.
  • Diagram a transverse section of a young, non-woody stem, labelled epidermis and cuticle (dermal tissue), cortex and pith (ground tissue), and a vascular bundle with phloem outside and xylem inside (vascular tissue).

Lenticels

  • A lenticel is a small raised pore in the bark of a woody stem. Lenticels are also found on some roots, on potato tubers, and as the speckles on apple skins.
  • A lenticel is a patch of loosely packed cells with air spaces between them.
  • Bark is waterproof and airtight, so the living cells beneath it, including the phloem, would be cut off from air. Lenticels allow gas exchange by diffusion: oxygen in for respiration and carbon dioxide out.
  • A small amount of water vapour is also lost through lenticels.
  • Lenticels stay open all the time. Unlike stomata, they have no guard cells and cannot close.
  • Examples: the horizontal lines on the bark of birch and cherry trees are lenticels.

The leaf

  • The leaf carries out photosynthesis, gas exchange (CO₂ in and oxygen out, in the light) and transpiration, which pulls water up the plant (see Transport of water and minerals).
  • The leaf is a thin, flat, broad blade. This gives a large surface area to absorb light and CO₂, and a short distance for gases to diffuse.
  • The leaf stalk (petiole) holds the blade out towards the light and carries the vascular tissue into it. The midrib and veins are vascular bundles that bring water and minerals in and take sucrose away, and their network supports the blade.
  • The waxy cuticle on the upper, and usually the lower, epidermis is waterproof and reduces water loss by evaporation. It is transparent, so light passes through, and it is usually thicker on the upper surface, which faces the sun.
  • The upper epidermis is one layer of transparent cells with no chloroplasts, letting light through to the cells below, and it has few or no stomata.
  • The palisade mesophyll is one or two layers of tall, column-shaped cells packed with chloroplasts. It lies just below the upper epidermis, where light is strongest, and most photosynthesis takes place here.
  • The spongy mesophyll is made of irregular, loosely packed cells with fewer chloroplasts and large air spaces between them. The air spaces connect to the stomata and let CO₂, oxygen and water vapour diffuse through the leaf.
  • The moist cell surfaces of the spongy mesophyll are where CO₂ dissolves before entering the cells and where water evaporates.
  • The vascular bundle, or vein, has xylem on the upper side, bringing water and minerals, and phloem on the lower side, carrying sucrose away.
  • The lower epidermis contains most of the stomata. Each stoma (plural stomata) is a pore between two bean-shaped guard cells, which, unlike other epidermal cells, contain chloroplasts.
  • The guard cells open and close the pore (the mechanism is in Transport of water and minerals). Stomata on the shaded lower surface lose less water than they would on the sunny upper surface.
  • Diagram a transverse section of a leaf, labelled cuticle, upper epidermis (dermal tissue), palisade and spongy mesophyll with air spaces (ground tissue), vascular bundle with xylem above and phloem below (vascular tissue), lower epidermis, stoma and guard cells.
  • Diagram a surface view of the lower epidermis showing a stoma between two guard cells (with chloroplasts) surrounded by irregular epidermal cells; label stoma, guard cell, chloroplast and epidermal cell.

Key terms

Dermal tissue
The outer protective tissue of a plant, the epidermis in young roots, stems and leaves, and bark in woody stems.
Ground tissue
The bulk of a plant's tissue between the dermal and vascular tissue, made of living thin-walled cells that photosynthesise, store food and water, or support the plant.
Vascular tissue
The transport tissue of a plant, made of xylem and phloem, grouped into vascular bundles or, in the root, a central core.
Cuticle
A waxy, waterproof layer on the outside of the epidermis that reduces water loss and keeps out pathogens.
Stoma (stomata)
A pore in the epidermis, bounded by two guard cells, through which gases diffuse in and out of the leaf.
Guard cell
One of two bean-shaped cells that surround a stoma and open or close it by changing their turgor.
Lenticel
A small raised pore in the bark of a woody stem, with loosely packed cells, that allows gas exchange because bark is airtight.
Root hair
A long, thin outgrowth of a single epidermal cell of a root that absorbs water and minerals over a large surface area.
Root cortex
The wide zone of ground tissue in a root, between the root hairs and the endodermis, that transports water and minerals, stores food and has air spaces.
Endodermis
The innermost single layer of the root cortex, which surrounds the vascular tissue and controls what passes into the xylem.
Xylem
Vascular tissue made of dead, hollow vessels and tracheids, lignified, that carries water and dissolved minerals upwards.
Xylem vessel
A long continuous tube of dead, hollow cells, formed from a column of cells whose end walls have broken down, with lignified walls.
Tracheid
A single long, narrow, dead xylem cell with tapering ends and pits, through which water passes to the next tracheid.
Lignin
A hard, waterproof substance that thickens the walls of xylem and stops the vessels collapsing.
Pit
A thin area in the wall of a xylem vessel or tracheid through which water passes sideways to neighbouring cells.
Phloem
Vascular tissue of living sieve tube elements and companion cells that carries sucrose and amino acids up or down the plant.
Sieve tube element
A living phloem cell with no nucleus, joined end to end to others to form a sieve tube.
Sieve plate
A perforated end wall between sieve tube elements through which phloem sap flows.
Companion cell
A living cell beside a sieve tube element, with a nucleus and many mitochondria, that keeps it alive and loads sucrose into it.
Palisade mesophyll
Tall, column-shaped leaf cells packed with chloroplasts, just below the upper epidermis, where most photosynthesis takes place.
Spongy mesophyll
Loosely packed leaf cells with large air spaces that connect to the stomata and allow gases to diffuse through the leaf.
