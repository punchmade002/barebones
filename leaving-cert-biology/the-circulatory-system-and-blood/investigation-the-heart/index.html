Structure and function of the heart

  • Position and size: the heart is a hollow, muscular organ about the size of a clenched fist, in the thorax between the two lungs and behind the sternum, with its pointed lower end (the apex) tilted to the left. It is enclosed in the pericardium, a double membrane with fluid between its layers that reduces friction as the heart beats and prevents it from over-stretching.
  • Atria (singular atrium): the two upper chambers receive blood returning to the heart from the veins and pump it the short distance into the ventricles below, so their walls are thin. Each has an ear-like flap on the outside, the auricle.
  • Ventricles: the two lower chambers pump blood out of the heart into the arteries, so their walls are thick and muscular. The left ventricle wall is much thicker than the right, because the left pumps blood all round the body (the systemic circuit) against high resistance, while the right pumps blood only to the nearby lungs (the pulmonary circuit) at lower pressure, which protects the delicate lung capillaries.
  • Septum: the thick wall of muscle dividing the heart into left and right sides. It keeps oxygenated blood (left side) separate from deoxygenated blood (right side), so the blood pumped to the body carries as much oxygen as possible. The right side pumps deoxygenated blood to the lungs; the left side pumps oxygenated blood to the body.
  • Veins entering the heart are thin-walled and bring blood to the atria. The venae cavae, the superior (anterior) vena cava from the head and arms and the inferior (posterior) vena cava from the rest of the body, bring deoxygenated blood into the right atrium. The pulmonary veins, four in humans, bring oxygenated blood from the lungs into the left atrium.
  • Arteries leaving the heart are thick-walled and carry blood away from the ventricles. The pulmonary artery leaves the right ventricle and divides to take deoxygenated blood to the two lungs. The aorta, the largest artery, leaves the left ventricle, arches over the heart and takes oxygenated blood to the body. The coronary arteries branch from the base of the aorta to supply the heart muscle itself (see ‘Heartbeat’).
  • Atrioventricular (AV) valves lie between each atrium and its ventricle: the tricuspid valve, with three flaps, on the right, and the bicuspid (mitral) valve, with two flaps, on the left. They let blood pass from atrium to ventricle and close when the ventricle contracts, so blood is not pushed back into the atrium.
  • The flaps of the AV valves are attached by tough cords, the chordae tendineae (‘heart strings’), to small papillary muscles on the ventricle wall. These hold the flaps and stop them turning inside out into the atria under the high pressure when the ventricles contract.
  • Semilunar valves lie at the base of the pulmonary artery and of the aorta. Each has three half-moon-shaped pockets. Blood pushed out by the ventricle flattens the pockets against the artery wall. When the ventricle relaxes, blood falling back fills the pockets, which meet and close the opening, so blood cannot flow back into the ventricle.
  • Cardiac muscle tissue is found only in the heart wall. Its cells are striated (striped), branched and joined end to end by intercalated discs, which let electrical impulses pass quickly from cell to cell so that the muscle contracts as a coordinated unit. Each cell has one nucleus and very many mitochondria, which supply ATP by aerobic respiration, so the heart beats throughout life without tiring. It is myogenic: it contracts by itself without needing a nerve impulse. Cardiac muscle is one of three muscle types (see ‘The musculoskeletal system as a system for response’).

Planning the investigation

  • Aim: to identify the external and internal structures of a mammalian heart by dissection, and to relate each structure to its function, supporting conclusions with primary data (observations and measurements) and secondary data (diagrams, models, micrographs and images of the human heart).
  • Hypothesis to test: the wall of the left ventricle is thicker than the wall of the right ventricle, because it pumps blood around the whole body.
  • Hypothesis to test: arteries leaving the heart have thicker walls than veins entering it.
  • Hypothesis to test: the valves allow blood to flow in one direction only.
  • Variables for the measured part (wall thickness): the independent variable is the chamber (left ventricle, right ventricle or atrium); the dependent variable is the wall thickness in mm. Controlled variables: measure at the same level, about halfway between the base and the apex, with the same ruler or calipers, on the same heart, with the cut made at right angles to the wall. Repeat each measurement three times at different points and calculate the mean. Pool class results from several hearts to check repeatability.
  • Secondary data: a labelled diagram or model of the human heart to compare with the dissected heart; micrographs or a prepared slide of cardiac muscle; published images or measurements of human hearts. The dissected heart is a model for the human heart, because mammalian hearts have the same four chambers, valves and vessels.
  • Equipment: a fresh or thawed sheep’s, pig’s or ox’s heart from a butcher, ideally with the vessels left long; dissecting board or tray; scalpel; sharp dissecting scissors; forceps; a blunt seeker or probe; a glass rod or drinking straw; ruler or calipers; wash bottle or beaker of water; disposable gloves; lab coat or apron; safety glasses; paper towels; disinfectant; a labelled diagram of the heart; a camera or phone to record observations.

Method: dissecting the heart

  • 1. Put on gloves, lab coat and safety glasses. Rinse the heart under cold running water to remove blood clots, and dry it with paper towel.
  • 2. Identify the front (ventral) surface: it has a coronary artery running diagonally across it, and the pulmonary artery leaves from the front. Place the heart with the front facing you, so the heart’s left side is on your right. Squeeze both sides: the left ventricle feels firmer and thicker.
  • 3. Examine the outside. Identify the atria (soft, ear-like flaps at the top), the ventricles (firm, at the bottom), the apex, the coronary arteries and fat on the surface, and the vessels at the top. Arteries (aorta and pulmonary artery) have thick, rubbery walls and stay open; veins (venae cavae and pulmonary veins) have thin, floppy walls and collapse. Draw and label what you see, and photograph it.
  • 4. Find which vessel joins which chamber: push a blunt probe or glass rod gently into each vessel to see which chamber it enters. The vena cava leads to the right atrium; the pulmonary artery leaves the right ventricle; the pulmonary veins lead to the left atrium; the aorta leaves the left ventricle. Butchers often trim the vessels short, so use the diagram (secondary data) to identify any that are missing.
  • 5. Open the right side. Using scissors, cut from the right atrium down the outside wall of the right ventricle towards the apex, and open the chambers. Observe the tricuspid valve (three flaps), the chordae tendineae and the papillary muscles. Pull gently on a cord to see how it holds a flap.
  • 6. Push the probe from the right ventricle up into the pulmonary artery, then cut along the artery to open it, and observe the three pockets of the semilunar valve at its base.
  • 7. Open the left side in the same way: cut through the left atrium and down the left ventricle. Observe the bicuspid valve (two flaps) and its chordae tendineae. Follow the probe from the left ventricle into the aorta, cut it open and observe its semilunar valve. Look just above the valve pockets for the small openings of the coronary arteries.
  • 8. Valve test (optional): before opening the arteries, hold the heart upright and pour water from a wash bottle into the aorta or pulmonary artery from above. The semilunar pockets fill and close, so the water does not pass down into the ventricle, showing that the valves allow one-way flow. Similarly, water poured into a ventricle through the atrium lifts the AV valve flaps up towards a closed position.
  • 9. Cut a transverse section across both ventricles about halfway between the base and the apex. Observe the septum between them, and measure the wall thickness of the left ventricle, the right ventricle and the septum with a ruler, following the variables and repeats above. Compare with the thickness of an atrium wall.
  • 10. Examine a prepared slide of cardiac muscle under the light microscope, or a micrograph, and draw it.
  • 11. Record observations in a table and on labelled drawings. Dispose of the heart and clean up as described under ‘Safety and ethics’.

Safety and ethics

  • Scalpels and scissors are very sharp: cut on the dissecting board, away from your body and fingers, and never towards another person. Hold the tissue with forceps, not fingers, near the blade, carry sharp instruments pointing down, and report any cut at once.
  • Raw meat can carry bacteria, such as Salmonella, that cause food poisoning. Wear gloves, do not touch your face, never eat or drink in the laboratory, keep the heart on the tray, and wash your hands thoroughly with soap afterwards.
  • Disinfect the board, instruments and bench after the dissection. Wrap the heart and remove it as directed by the teacher; it is not to be left in an open bin.
  • Wear safety glasses, as fluid can spray when cutting or when pouring water through the vessels.
  • Ethics: use a heart from an animal already killed for food, a by-product of the food industry, so that no animal is killed for the dissection, and treat the tissue with respect. A student who objects can use a model, a virtual dissection or videos as secondary data, in line with the 3Rs of animal use (see ‘Science, scientists and society’).

Results and secondary data

  • Expected observations: four chambers; atria thin-walled and small, ventricles thick-walled; the left ventricle wall clearly thicker than the right, typically several times thicker in measurements; the septum thick and muscular; the tricuspid valve with three flaps on the right and the bicuspid valve with two on the left, both held by chordae tendineae to papillary muscles; three-pocket semilunar valves at the base of the aorta and pulmonary artery; the aorta with a much thicker, more elastic wall than the venae cavae; the coronary arteries on the surface, with their openings just above the aortic valve.
  • Results table to record: the structure; where it is found; its description (colour, texture, thickness, number of flaps); the measured wall thickness in mm, with three readings and the mean; and its function.
  • Secondary data: the dissected heart matches the human heart diagram in chambers, valves and vessels. Differences seen are due to the butcher’s trimming, which may remove the atria or vessels, or to species size. Micrographs show the branched, striated cardiac muscle cells and intercalated discs, which cannot be seen with the naked eye.

Conclusion

  • The left ventricle has the thickest wall because it must generate the highest pressure to pump blood all round the body; the right ventricle pumps only to the lungs.
  • Atria have thin walls because they pump blood only into the ventricles below them.
  • The valves, the AV valves with their chordae tendineae and the semilunar valves, allow blood to flow only from atria to ventricles to arteries, never backwards. The water test supports this.
  • The septum separates oxygenated and deoxygenated blood. Arteries have thick walls to withstand high pressure, and veins have thin walls (see ‘Blood vessels’).
  • For each hypothesis in the planning section, state whether it is supported, citing the measurements and observations.

Sources of error and limitations

  • Butchers often remove the atria or cut the vessels very short, so some structures may be missing or hard to identify. Vessels and valves may be damaged by cutting.
  • Wall-thickness measurements vary with where and at what angle the cut is made, and soft tissue compresses under the ruler. Measure at the same level at right angles, repeat three times and use the mean.
  • Heart muscle relaxes and blood clots after death, and freezing and thawing change the texture, so the chambers are not in their living shape.
  • A sheep’s or pig’s heart is a model: its size and some features differ from a human heart, for example the number of pulmonary vein openings, so conclusions about humans need secondary data.
  • One heart per group is a small sample; pooling class data improves repeatability.

Key terms

Septum
The thick muscular wall dividing the left and right sides of the heart, keeping oxygenated and deoxygenated blood apart.
Atrium
One of the two thin-walled upper chambers of the heart that receive blood from the veins and pump it into the ventricles.
Ventricle
One of the two thick-walled lower chambers of the heart that pump blood into the arteries.
Pericardium
The double membrane enclosing the heart, with fluid between its layers that reduces friction.
Atrioventricular valve
A valve between an atrium and a ventricle that lets blood flow from the atrium to the ventricle and closes when the ventricle contracts.
Semilunar valve
A valve with three half-moon pockets at the base of the aorta and pulmonary artery that prevents blood flowing back into the ventricles.
Chordae tendineae
Tough cords that attach the flaps of the AV valves to the papillary muscles, stopping the flaps turning inside out.
Papillary muscle
A small muscle on the wall of a ventricle to which the chordae tendineae are attached.
Intercalated disc
A junction between cardiac muscle cells that lets electrical impulses pass quickly from cell to cell.
Myogenic
Describes muscle, such as cardiac muscle, that contracts by itself without needing a nerve impulse.
Auricle
The ear-like flap on the outside of each atrium.
