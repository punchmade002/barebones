The heartbeat and the pacemaker

  • A heartbeat is one contraction and relaxation of the heart muscle, pumping blood out of the ventricles into the arteries. An adult heart beats about 70 times a minute at rest, a normal resting range being about 60 to 100, and it does so every minute of life.
  • Cardiac muscle is myogenic: the beat starts within the heart muscle itself, without a nerve impulse. A heart removed for transplant keeps beating for a time if it is supplied with oxygenated fluid (cardiac muscle features are in ‘Investigation: the heart’).
  • The pacemaker is the sinoatrial node (SA node), a small patch of specialised cardiac muscle in the wall of the right atrium, near where the superior vena cava enters. It produces electrical impulses at regular intervals by itself. Each impulse starts one heartbeat, so the SA node sets the rate of the heartbeat.
  • How the impulse spreads: 1. The impulse from the SA node spreads across the walls of both atria, so the atria contract together, pushing blood into the ventricles.
  • 2. A layer of non-conducting tissue between the atria and the ventricles stops the impulse passing straight to the ventricles.
  • 3. The impulse reaches the atrioventricular node (AV node), at the base of the right atrium near the septum, which delays it for a fraction of a second, giving the atria time to empty into the ventricles.
  • 4. The AV node passes the impulse down conducting fibres in the septum (the bundle of His) to the apex, and then up through the ventricle walls (Purkinje fibres).
  • 5. The ventricles contract from the apex upwards, squeezing blood up into the aorta and pulmonary artery. The atria therefore contract before the ventricles, and the two sides beat together.

Control of heart rate

  • The SA node sets the basic rhythm, but the rate is changed to match the body’s needs. It rises during exercise, when muscles need more oxygen and glucose and produce more CO₂.
  • Nervous control: the cardiac centre in the medulla oblongata sends impulses along two sets of nerves to the SA node. One set, the sympathetic nerves, speeds up the heart rate; the other, the vagus nerve, a parasympathetic nerve, slows it down. The medulla responds to information from receptors: a rise in CO₂ in the blood increases heart rate (see ‘CO₂ as a controlling factor in breathing’), and a rise in blood pressure slows it (the medulla is described in ‘CNS and PNS’).
  • Hormonal control: adrenaline, released by the adrenal glands in stress, danger or excitement, acts on the SA node and increases the heart rate and the force of contraction (see ‘Glands, locations and hormones’).
  • Other factors that change heart rate include exercise and fitness, age (higher in babies and children), body temperature (a fever raises it), emotion, caffeine and nicotine, which raise it, and some medicines.
  • Artificial pacemaker: if the SA node fails, or the impulse does not reach the ventricles properly (heart block), the heart beats too slowly or irregularly. A small battery-powered device is implanted under the skin below the collarbone, with a wire, or lead, running through a vein into the heart. It detects the heart’s own rhythm and sends an electrical impulse to start a beat when one is missed or the rate is too slow. The batteries last several years and are replaced in a minor operation.

The pulse

  • The pulse is a wave of expansion of an artery wall, caused by the surge of blood forced into the arteries each time the ventricles contract. The stretched elastic wall then recoils. The pulse rate therefore equals the heart rate.
  • The pulse can be felt where an artery runs close to the skin over a bone or firm tissue. The usual sites are the radial artery at the wrist, on the thumb side, and the carotid artery in the neck, beside the windpipe. Veins have no pulse, because the pressure wave has been lost in the capillaries.
  • Method for measuring pulse: 1. Sit quietly for a few minutes.
  • 2. Press lightly with the index and middle fingers, not the thumb, which has its own pulse, on the radial artery.
  • 3. Count the beats for 30 seconds and double the number to give beats per minute, or count for a full 60 seconds.
  • 4. Repeat three times and take the mean.
  • Exercise and pulse, a standard class investigation with primary data: measure the resting pulse; exercise, for example with step-ups for two minutes; then measure the pulse at once and every minute until it returns to the resting value. The pulse rises during exercise and falls gradually afterwards. The time taken to return to the resting rate, the recovery time, is shorter in fitter people, who also tend to have a lower resting pulse because each beat pumps more blood.
  • Variables for the exercise investigation: the independent variable is activity level (rest, or after exercise); the dependent variable is pulse rate in beats per minute. Controlled variables are the type, duration and intensity of the exercise, the same person, the same counting method and the same posture.

Blood pressure

  • Blood pressure is the force that the blood exerts on the walls of the blood vessels, produced by the contraction of the ventricles. It is highest in the aorta and large arteries, falls as blood passes through the arterioles and capillaries, and is lowest in the veins and venae cavae (details of the vessels are in ‘Blood vessels’).
  • Measurement: a sphygmomanometer, or blood pressure monitor, uses an inflatable cuff on the upper arm over the brachial artery. Pressure is given in millimetres of mercury (mmHg) as two numbers: the higher pressure, when the ventricles contract, and the lower pressure, when the heart relaxes between beats. A healthy young adult at rest has a value of about 120/80 mmHg.
  • Blood pressure rises naturally during exercise or stress and falls during sleep. High blood pressure, or hypertension, means a resting value consistently at or above about 140/90 mmHg. It usually has no symptoms, but it strains the heart, damages artery walls and raises the risk of heart attack, stroke and kidney damage.
  • Factors that raise blood pressure include too much salt in the diet (see ‘Minerals’), being overweight, lack of exercise, smoking, too much alcohol, stress, older age and family history. It is controlled by lifestyle changes, such as less salt, exercise, weight loss and not smoking, and by medication.

The heart’s own blood supply

  • The heart muscle cannot take oxygen and nutrients from the blood passing through its chambers: the wall is too thick for diffusion to reach most cells, and the blood on the left side passes too quickly. So the heart has its own circulation, the coronary circulation.
  • The two coronary arteries, left and right, are the first branches of the aorta, arising from its base just above the aortic semilunar valve. They run over the surface of the heart, branching into smaller arteries that enter the muscle and supply a dense network of capillaries. This gives the cardiac muscle the oxygen and glucose for the aerobic respiration that keeps it working continuously.
  • Deoxygenated blood from the heart muscle is collected by the cardiac (coronary) veins, which run over the surface and join a large vein at the back of the heart, the coronary sinus, which empties into the right atrium.

Coronary heart disease (health link)

  • Fatty deposits, called atheroma or plaque and containing cholesterol, can build up in the walls of the coronary arteries and narrow them (atherosclerosis), so less blood reaches the heart muscle. This can cause chest pain during exercise, called angina.
  • If a blood clot forms on a damaged plaque and blocks a coronary artery, the part of the heart muscle that it supplies is starved of oxygen and dies. This is a heart attack, or myocardial infarction. Clot-dissolving enzymes given soon after are described in ‘Industrial enzymes’; clotting is described in ‘Blood’.
  • Risk factors: smoking, a diet high in saturated fat, which raises blood cholesterol (see ‘Metabolic roles and nutritional sources’), high blood pressure, obesity, lack of exercise, diabetes, older age and family history. Prevention: not smoking, a balanced diet, regular exercise and a healthy weight.
  • Technology: a narrowed coronary artery can be widened by angioplasty, in which a balloon on a catheter is inflated inside the artery and a small mesh tube, a stent, is left in place to hold it open. Alternatively it can be bypassed by a coronary artery bypass graft, using a piece of vein, often from the leg, or an artery to carry blood around the blockage.

Key terms

Pacemaker (SA node)
The sinoatrial node, a patch of specialised cardiac muscle in the wall of the right atrium that produces regular impulses and sets the heart rate.
Atrioventricular (AV) node
The node at the base of the right atrium that delays the impulse before it passes to the ventricles.
Bundle of His
Conducting fibres in the septum that carry the impulse from the AV node down to the apex of the heart.
Purkinje fibres
Conducting fibres in the ventricle walls that carry the impulse up from the apex, so the ventricles contract from the bottom upwards.
Myogenic
Describes heart muscle that starts each beat within itself, without a nerve impulse.
Pulse
The wave of expansion of an artery wall, caused by the surge of blood when the ventricles contract.
Sphygmomanometer
A device with an inflatable cuff that measures blood pressure in mmHg.
Hypertension
High blood pressure, meaning a resting value consistently at or above about 140/90 mmHg.
Coronary arteries
The arteries that branch from the base of the aorta and supply the heart muscle with blood.
Coronary sinus
The large vein at the back of the heart that collects blood from the cardiac veins and empties it into the right atrium.
Atheroma
A fatty deposit, containing cholesterol, that builds up in the wall of an artery and narrows it.
Angina
Chest pain during exercise caused by reduced blood supply to the heart muscle.
Myocardial infarction
A heart attack, in which part of the heart muscle dies because a coronary artery is blocked.
Angioplasty
A procedure in which a balloon on a catheter is inflated inside a narrowed artery, and a stent is left in place to hold it open.
