Composition of blood

  • Blood is a liquid tissue made of cells and cell fragments suspended in a liquid, the plasma. An adult has about 5 litres of blood.
  • If blood, with a chemical added to stop it clotting, is spun in a centrifuge, it separates into about 55% plasma, a straw-coloured liquid on top, and about 45% cells. The cells form a thin pale layer of white blood cells and platelets above a dark red layer of red blood cells at the bottom. Red blood cells make up nearly all of the cell volume.
  • Numbers in a healthy adult: red blood cells about 5 million per mm³ (cubic millimetre); white blood cells about 4,000 to 11,000 per mm³; platelets about 150,000 to 400,000 per mm³. Red cells therefore greatly outnumber white cells, by several hundred to one.
  • All blood cells and platelets are made in the red bone marrow, in spongy bone such as the sternum, ribs, vertebrae, pelvis and the ends of long bones, from stem cells that divide by mitosis (see ‘The musculoskeletal system as a system for response’).
  • Functions of blood: transport (oxygen, carbon dioxide, nutrients, wastes, hormones and heat), defence against infection, clotting to seal wounds, and keeping the internal environment steady (pH, temperature and water balance). Each is taught in the blocks below.

Plasma

  • Plasma is about 90% water, with substances dissolved or suspended in it. Water is a good solvent and carries heat (see ‘Water’).
  • Dissolved substances transported in plasma: nutrients (glucose, amino acids, fatty acids and glycerol, vitamins and minerals); wastes (urea, from the liver to the kidneys, and most of the carbon dioxide); hormones; and salts, such as sodium, chloride, calcium and hydrogencarbonate ions.
  • Carbon dioxide is carried mostly in the plasma as hydrogencarbonate ions (HCO₃⁻), formed in red blood cells by the enzyme carbonic anhydrase (CO₂ + H₂O → carbonic acid → H⁺ + HCO₃⁻). A small amount is dissolved, and some is carried by haemoglobin. Its role in controlling breathing is in ‘CO₂ as a controlling factor in breathing’.
  • Plasma proteins: fibrinogen and other clotting factors, for clotting; antibodies, for defence (see ‘Innate and adaptive immunity’); and albumin, which holds water in the blood by osmosis and carries some substances. Serum is plasma with the clotting proteins removed.
  • Plasma distributes heat from active organs around the body, and its hydrogencarbonate and protein buffers keep the blood pH near 7.4.

Red blood cells

  • Red blood cells, or erythrocytes, are biconcave discs, thinner in the centre than at the edge, about 7 to 8 µm across. They carry oxygen from the lungs to all body cells.
  • Structure related to function: (a) the biconcave shape gives a large surface area for their volume, so oxygen diffuses in and out quickly, and every part of the cell is close to the surface; (b) there is no nucleus, and no mitochondria, in the mature cell, which leaves more room for haemoglobin; (c) the cell is packed with haemoglobin; (d) the cell is small and flexible, so it squeezes through capillaries in single file, close to the capillary wall. Because they have no mitochondria, red blood cells respire anaerobically and do not use up the oxygen they carry.
  • Haemoglobin is a red protein made of four polypeptide chains, each with a haem group containing iron, so each haemoglobin molecule can carry four oxygen molecules. In the lungs, where oxygen is plentiful, haemoglobin combines with oxygen to form oxyhaemoglobin, which is bright red. In respiring tissues, where oxygen is low, oxyhaemoglobin releases its oxygen. Haemoglobin + oxygen ⇌ oxyhaemoglobin.
  • Life cycle: red blood cells are made in the red bone marrow and live about 120 days; with no nucleus they cannot repair themselves. Old cells are broken down in the liver and spleen. The iron is recycled to make new haemoglobin, and the rest of the haem is converted into bile pigments, which are excreted in bile. The kidney hormone EPO stimulates their production (see ‘Hormonal manipulation’).
  • Health links: anaemia is a shortage of red blood cells or haemoglobin, so less oxygen is carried, causing tiredness and pale skin. A common cause is iron deficiency (see ‘Minerals’). Carbon monoxide, in cigarette smoke and from faulty gas appliances, combines with haemoglobin far more tightly than oxygen does, so less oxygen is carried, and it can kill. Sickle-cell anaemia is described in ‘Mutations’.

White blood cells

  • White blood cells, or leucocytes, defend the body against infection. Unlike red blood cells, they have a nucleus, are larger and colourless (they have no haemoglobin), and are far fewer in number (see the composition block). They are made in the red bone marrow, and many can squeeze out through capillary walls into the tissues.
  • There are two broad groups: phagocytes, which engulf and digest pathogens (phagocytosis), and lymphocytes, which produce antibodies or kill infected cells. How they act is described in ‘Innate and adaptive immunity’, and the named types are described in ‘White blood cells’.
  • The number of white cells rises during an infection, so a white cell count is used in diagnosing infections. Leukaemia is a cancer of the white-cell-forming cells (see ‘Cancer’).

Platelets and clotting

  • Platelets, or thrombocytes, are small fragments of cytoplasm enclosed in membrane, with no nucleus, broken off from large cells in the red bone marrow. They live about 7 to 10 days and start the clotting of blood.
  • Clotting matters because it stops blood loss from a damaged vessel and seals the wound so that pathogens cannot enter. It is part of the first line of defence (see ‘Innate and adaptive immunity’).
  • Clotting, step by step: 1. A blood vessel is damaged.
  • 2. Platelets stick to the exposed damaged wall and to each other, forming a temporary plug.
  • 3. The platelets and damaged tissue release clotting factors.
  • 4. A series of reactions, needing calcium ions and clotting proteins made in the liver with the help of vitamin K, converts the inactive plasma protein prothrombin into the active enzyme thrombin.
  • 5. Thrombin converts the soluble plasma protein fibrinogen into insoluble threads of fibrin.
  • 6. The fibrin threads form a mesh that traps red blood cells and platelets, forming a clot.
  • 7. At the surface the clot dries and hardens into a scab, under which the tissue repairs.
  • Links: calcium and vitamin K are needed for clotting (see ‘Minerals’ and ‘Vitamins’). Haemophilia is an inherited disorder in which a clotting factor is missing (see ‘Sex-linked traits’). An unwanted clot inside a vessel, a thrombosis, can block an artery and cause a heart attack or stroke (see ‘Heartbeat’).

ABO blood groups

  • A blood group is set by the antigens on the surface membrane of a person’s red blood cells. An antigen is a molecule that can trigger an immune response (see ‘Innate and adaptive immunity’). In the ABO system there are two antigens, A and B.
  • The plasma naturally contains antibodies against the ABO antigen or antigens that a person’s own red cells lack. Group A has antigen A on the red cells and anti-B antibodies in the plasma. Group B has antigen B and anti-A antibodies. Group AB has both antigens and no antibodies. Group O has no antigens and both anti-A and anti-B antibodies.
  • Agglutination: if red cells carrying an antigen meet the matching antibody, for example A cells meeting anti-A, the antibodies bind to the antigens and stick the cells together in clumps. In a transfusion the clumps can block small blood vessels, and the donor cells are destroyed, or burst. This can cause kidney failure and death.
  • Transfusion rule: what matters is the donor’s red-cell antigens and the recipient’s antibodies. A recipient can safely receive red cells that carry no antigen against which their own plasma has antibodies. Group O red cells carry no antigens, so they can be given to anyone, and group O is called the universal donor. Group AB people have no antibodies, so they can receive red cells of any ABO group, and group AB is the universal recipient. In practice, hospitals give the same group whenever possible.
  • Compatibility: group O can receive O only; group A can receive A or O; group B can receive B or O; group AB can receive A, B, AB or O.
  • Blood-group testing: a drop of blood is mixed separately with anti-A serum and with anti-B serum. Clumping with anti-A only means group A; clumping with anti-B only means group B; clumping with both means AB; clumping with neither means O. A third test, with anti-D, tests the Rhesus group.

Rhesus factor

  • The Rhesus (Rh) factor is another red-cell antigen, the D antigen. People whose red cells carry it are Rhesus positive (Rh+), and those without it are Rhesus negative (Rh−). A person’s full blood group combines both systems, for example O negative or A positive.
  • Unlike anti-A and anti-B, anti-D antibodies are not naturally present. A Rh− person makes them only after Rh+ red cells enter their blood, for example through a transfusion of Rh+ blood or during pregnancy. A first exposure therefore does little harm, but a second exposure triggers a rapid, strong attack on Rh+ cells. So Rh− patients must receive Rh− blood.
  • Haemolytic disease of the newborn, step by step: 1. A Rh− mother carries a Rh+ baby, inherited from a Rh+ father.
  • 2. Normally the blood of mother and fetus does not mix, but at birth, or after a miscarriage, abortion or injury, some fetal red cells enter the mother’s blood.
  • 3. Her immune system makes anti-D antibodies and memory cells, and she is now sensitised; the first baby is usually unaffected, because it is born before many antibodies are made.
  • 4. In a later pregnancy with a Rh+ baby, her anti-D antibodies cross the placenta and destroy the fetus’s red blood cells.
  • 5. The baby can suffer severe anaemia and jaundice, brain damage or death.
  • Prevention: a Rh− mother is given an injection of anti-D antibodies, anti-D immunoglobulin, during pregnancy and within 72 hours after the birth of a Rh+ baby, or after a miscarriage or injury. These antibodies destroy any fetal Rh+ cells in her blood before her own immune system can respond, so she does not make her own anti-D or memory cells. The injected antibodies are broken down over a few weeks. This is artificial passive immunity (see ‘Acquired immunity, prevention and treatment’). Every pregnant woman’s blood group is tested early in pregnancy for this reason (see ‘Prenatal and postnatal care’).

The value of knowing blood groups for health

  • Safe blood transfusion: before a transfusion, the patient’s ABO and Rhesus groups are tested, and the donor blood is cross-matched, meaning it is mixed with the patient’s plasma to check that no clumping occurs. This prevents fatal transfusion reactions and allows transfusions in surgery, after accidents, in childbirth, for anaemia and in cancer treatment.
  • Emergencies: group O Rh− red cells are given when there is no time to test the patient, because they carry no A, B or D antigens. Only a minority of people are O negative, so their donations are especially valuable.
  • Pregnancy: identifying Rh− mothers and giving anti-D has made haemolytic disease of the newborn rare.
  • Blood donation: knowing which groups are needed lets the blood service plan donor sessions and stocks. Donors of all groups are needed, because blood can be stored only for a limited time. In Ireland, donations are collected and tested by the Irish Blood Transfusion Service, and donated blood is screened for infections (see ‘Acquired immunity, prevention and treatment’).
  • Organ and bone-marrow transplants: donors and recipients are matched for ABO group and tissue type to reduce the chance of rejection.
  • Nature of Science: before blood groups were known, transfusions often killed patients. Karl Landsteiner discovered the ABO blood groups in 1901, for which he received the Nobel Prize in 1930, which made safe transfusion possible. In 1940 Landsteiner and Alexander Wiener described the Rhesus factor, named after the rhesus monkeys whose blood was used in the experiments.

Key terms

Erythrocyte
A red blood cell: a biconcave, anucleate cell packed with haemoglobin that carries oxygen.
Haemoglobin
A red iron-containing protein in red blood cells that combines reversibly with oxygen.
Oxyhaemoglobin
Haemoglobin combined with oxygen, formed in the lungs, which releases its oxygen in respiring tissues.
Plasma
The straw-coloured liquid part of blood, about 90% water, which carries cells, nutrients, wastes, hormones and heat.
Serum
Plasma with the clotting proteins removed.
Platelet
A small fragment of cytoplasm, with no nucleus, that starts the clotting of blood.
Prothrombin
An inactive plasma protein that is converted into the active enzyme thrombin during clotting.
Fibrin
The insoluble protein threads, formed from fibrinogen, that make up the mesh of a blood clot.
Antigen
A molecule on a cell surface that can trigger an immune response, such as A and B on red blood cells.
Antibody
A protein in the plasma that binds to a specific antigen.
Agglutination
The clumping of red cells caused by antibodies binding to antigens on their surface.
Universal donor
Group O, whose red cells carry no A, B or D antigens and can be given to anyone.
Universal recipient
Group AB, whose plasma has no antibodies against A or B, so it can receive red cells of any ABO group.
Rhesus factor
The D antigen on red blood cells; people who carry it are Rhesus positive.
Anti-D
An antibody against the D antigen, given by injection to Rh− mothers to prevent sensitisation.
Haemolytic disease of the newborn
The destruction of a Rh+ fetus’s red blood cells by anti-D antibodies from a sensitised Rh− mother.
Passive immunity
Immunity given by injected antibodies, rather than made by the person’s own immune system.
