Giving information: recall and description

  • Identify: “recognise patterns, facts, or details; provide an answer from a number of possibilities; recognize and state briefly a distinguishing fact or feature”. Example: identify the location of the major glands in the endocrine system. Taught in “Glands, locations and hormones”.
  • Outline: “give the main points; restrict to essentials”. Example: outline the concept of a microbiome. Taught in “The microbiome”.
  • Describe: “develop a detailed picture or image of, for example a structure or a process, using words or diagrams where appropriate; produce a plan, simulation or model”. Example: describe the basic structure and function of a DNA and RNA nucleotide. Taught in “Nucleotides, DNA and RNA”.
  • Illustrate: “use examples to describe something”. Example: illustrate and state Mendel's Laws. Taught in “Mendel's Laws”.
  • Classify: “group things based on common characteristics”. Example: identify and classify living things using classification principles, e.g. with a key. Taught in “Classification”.
  • Organise: “arrange; to systematise or methodise”. Example: organise and communicate research and investigative findings. Taught in “Communicating results”.
  • Recognise: “identify facts, characteristics or concepts that are critical (relevant/appropriate) to the understanding of a situation, event, process or phenomenon”. Example: recognise questions appropriate for scientific investigation. Taught in “Questioning and predicting”.
  • Appreciate: “recognise the meaning of, have a practical understanding of”. Example: appreciate the impact of modern technology on monitoring mother and foetus. Taught in “Prenatal and postnatal care”.

Giving reasons: explanation and application

  • Explain: “give a detailed account including reasons or causes”. Example: explain how reliability, accuracy, precision, error, fairness and safety have been considered in an investigation. Taught in “Designing and conducting investigations”.
  • Relate: “associate, giving reasons”. Example: relate the structures of the male and female reproductive systems to their functions. Taught in “The male reproductive system”.
  • Justify: “give valid reasons or evidence to support an answer or conclusion”. Example: draw and justify conclusions. Taught in “Producing and analysing data”.
  • Apply: “select and use information and/or knowledge and understanding to explain a given situation or real circumstances”. Example: apply learning and skills to solving problems in unfamiliar contexts. Taught in “Reflecting on investigations”.
  • Use: “apply knowledge or rules to put theory into practice”. Example: use a genome database to search for alleles that cause genetic diseases. Taught in “Bioinformatics: genome databases”.
  • Predict: “give an expected result of an event; explain a new event based on observations or information using logical connections between pieces of information”. Example: use models to predict the behaviour of a system. Taught in “Models”.
  • Model: “represent an idea, structure, process or system through a variety of means such as words, diagrams, equations, physical models or simulations; use models to describe, explain, make predictions and solve problems, recognising that all models have limitations”. Example: model the carbon cycle. Taught in “The carbon cycle”.

Breaking down: analysis and comparison

  • Analyse: “study or examine something in detail, break down in order to bring out the essential elements or structure; identify parts and relationships, and to interpret information to reach conclusions”. Example: critically analyse data to identify patterns and relationships. Taught in “Producing and analysing data”.
  • Interpret: “use knowledge and understanding to recognise trends and draw conclusions from given information”. It is used in questions that give graphs or tables of data and ask for the trends or conclusions they show.
  • Consider: “describe patterns in data; use knowledge and understanding to interpret patterns; make predictions and check reliability”. Example: consider the evidence for evolution. Taught in “Evidence for evolution”.
  • Compare: “give an account of the similarities and (or) differences between two (or more) items or situations, referring to both (all) of them throughout”. Example: compare the use of pyramids of numbers and pyramids of biomass (Higher Level outcome). Taught in “Pyramids of biomass”.
  • Distinguish: “make the differences between two or more concepts or items clear”. Example: distinguish between bacteria and fungi in terms of structure, nutrition and cellular nature. Taught in “Bacteria and fungi”.
  • Examine: “consider an argument or concept in a way that uncovers the assumptions and relationships of the issue”. Example: examine the role of infectious agents, environmental factors and genetic susceptibility in cancers. Taught in “Causes, prevention and treatment of cancer”.

Making judgements

  • Discuss: “offer a considered, balanced review that includes a range of arguments, factors or hypotheses; opinions or conclusions should be presented clearly and supported by appropriate evidence”. Example: discuss the importance of microorganisms in industries. Taught in “Microorganisms in industry”.
  • Evaluate (data): “collect and examine data to make judgments and appraisals; describe how evidence supports or does not support a conclusion in an inquiry or investigation; identify the limitations of data in conclusions; make judgments about the ideas, solutions or methods”. Example: evaluate primary or secondary data on the effects of human activity on species diversity. Taught in “Investigation: human activity and species diversity”.
  • Evaluate (ethical judgement): “collect and examine evidence to make judgments and appraisals; describe how evidence supports or does not support a judgement; identify the limitations of evidence in conclusions; make judgments about the ideas, solutions or methods”. Example: evaluate ethical and sustainability issues associated with the cycling of nutrients. Taught in “Ethics and sustainability of nutrient cycling”.
  • Review: “re-examine deliberately or critically, usually with a view to approval or dissent; to analyse results for the purpose of giving an opinion”. Example: review the skills and thinking used in an investigation. Taught in “Reflecting on investigations”.
  • Reflect: “consider in order to correct or improve”. Example: reflect on the skills and thinking used in an investigation. Taught in “Reflecting on investigations”.

Finding out: investigating and practical work

  • Investigate: “observe, study, or make a detailed and systematic examination, in order to establish facts and reach new conclusions”. Example: investigate factors affecting the growth of microorganisms. Taught in “Investigation: growth of microorganisms”.
  • Explore: “observe, study, in order to establish facts”. Example: explore the role of microbiomes in human health and in nutrient cycling in soils. Taught in “The microbiome”.
  • Research: “inquire specifically, using involved and critical investigation”. Example: research the use of enzymes in industries. Taught in “Industrial enzymes”.
  • Pose: “put forward for consideration”. Example: pose testable hypotheses. Taught in “Questioning and predicting”.
  • Plan: “devise or project a method or a course of action”. Example: design, plan and conduct investigations. Taught in “Designing and conducting investigations”.
  • Design: “conceive, create and execute according to plan”. Example: design, plan and conduct investigations. Taught in “Designing and conducting investigations”.
  • Conduct: “perform an activity”. Example: design, plan and conduct investigations. Taught in “Designing and conducting investigations”.
  • Measure: “quantify changes in systems by reading a measuring tool”. Example: measure abiotic factors such as temperature, aspect, wind speed and slope in a habitat. Taught in “Abiotic factors”.
  • Produce: “bring into existence by intellectual or creative ability”. Example: produce and select qualitative and quantitative data. Taught in “Producing and analysing data”.

Confusable verbs compared

Each pair is told apart by the quoted meanings above.

  • Outline or describe: outline gives the main points only, restricted to essentials; describe gives a detailed picture, in words or diagrams.
  • Describe or explain: describe says what something is or what happens; explain adds the reasons or causes.
  • Compare or distinguish: compare refers to both items throughout and may give similarities as well as differences; distinguish makes only the differences clear.
  • Discuss or evaluate: discuss gives a balanced review of a range of arguments, supported by evidence; evaluate goes further, judging how far the evidence supports a conclusion, stating its limitations, and making a judgement.
  • Evaluate (data) or evaluate (ethical judgement): the first judges data from an inquiry or investigation and its limitations; the second judges the evidence for an ethical judgement and its limitations.
  • Explore or investigate: both observe and study to establish facts; investigate is a detailed, systematic examination that reaches new conclusions.
  • Review or reflect: review re-examines critically to give an opinion of approval or dissent; reflect considers in order to correct or improve.
