## 1. Must cover

Nutrients (assumed knowledge: each with function, food sources and relevance to performance)
- [ ] N1 Carbohydrate: the body's main energy source for moderate and high-intensity exercise; stored as glycogen in muscles and liver; sources bread, pasta, rice, potatoes, cereals, fruit.
- [ ] N2 Protein: growth and repair of muscle and other tissue; sources meat, fish, eggs, dairy, pulses.
- [ ] N3 Fat: concentrated energy for low-intensity, long-duration exercise; insulation and fat-soluble vitamins; sources oils, nuts, dairy, oily fish.
- [ ] N4 Vitamins and minerals: e.g. iron (oxygen transport in red blood cells; deficiency causes fatigue), calcium (bones), B vitamins (energy release).
- [ ] N5 Fibre and water (water covered fully under hydration).
- [ ] N6 A balanced diet for a performer: mostly carbohydrate-based, adequate protein, limited saturated fat; energy intake matched to training load.

Before, during and after performance
- [ ] B1 Before: a high-carbohydrate, moderate-protein, low-fat and low-fibre meal about 3–4 hours before; a small carbohydrate snack about 1–2 hours before if needed; familiar foods only; fluid.
- [ ] B2 Carbohydrate loading for endurance events lasting more than about 90 minutes: increased carbohydrate intake in the days before while training tapers, to maximise glycogen stores.
- [ ] D1 During: water for short events; for events longer than about an hour, carbohydrate (sports drink, gel, banana) and fluid to maintain blood glucose and delay fatigue.
- [ ] A1 After: carbohydrate to restore glycogen and protein to repair muscle, ideally within about 30–60 minutes; rehydration with fluid and electrolytes (e.g. chocolate milk, a chicken and rice meal).
- [ ] A2 Applied to one named activity (e.g. a Gaelic football match day: what to eat and why at each stage).

Hydration
- [ ] H1 Why water matters: regulates body temperature through sweating, transports nutrients, maintains blood volume.
- [ ] H2 Dehydration effects: raised heart rate and body temperature, fatigue, headache, cramp, reduced concentration and skill; a fluid loss of about 2% of body mass can impair performance.
- [ ] H3 Different activities and settings: long-duration and high-intensity activities, hot or humid conditions, indoor halls, wearing heavy kit, and altitude increase fluid needs; swimmers still sweat and need fluid.
- [ ] H4 Monitoring hydration: urine colour (pale straw is good), thirst (a late sign), weighing before and after exercise; replace about 1.5 times the fluid lost.
- [ ] H5 Over-drinking plain water in very long events can dilute blood sodium (hyponatraemia); include electrolytes.

Sports drinks and supplements
- [ ] S1 Sports drinks: hypotonic (lower concentration than body fluid; rapid rehydration), isotonic (about 6–8% carbohydrate; fluid and energy together, the most common during exercise), hypertonic (higher carbohydrate; energy, often after exercise).
- [ ] S2 Benefits of sports drinks: replace fluid, electrolytes (sodium, potassium) and carbohydrate; convenient. Disadvantages: sugar and calories (dental decay, weight gain for inactive users), cost, unnecessary for short or low-intensity activity.
- [ ] S3 Common supplements and their role: protein powders (muscle repair, convenience), creatine (raises phosphocreatine stores for repeated short maximal efforts), caffeine (stimulant, can reduce perceived effort and improve alertness), energy gels (rapid carbohydrate), vitamin and mineral supplements (correct a diagnosed deficiency).
- [ ] S4 Challenges of supplements: risk of contamination with prohibited substances (strict liability means the athlete is responsible), side effects, cost, unregulated claims, usually unnecessary with a balanced diet ("food first"), possible dependence. Batch-tested products reduce but do not remove risk.

Designing a dietary plan
- [ ] P1 Plan for one selected activity covering a training day and a competition day: meals and snacks before, during and after, fluid plan, with a rationale for each choice linked to the activity's duration, intensity and energy system.
- [ ] P2 Outline three aspects of a dietary plan with rationale (the 2024 Higher format).

## 3. Traps

- Supplements here are about nutrition and performance; the ethics of supplements and anti-doping are in `pe-ethics-fair-play` ("Best practice for the use of supplements", "Anti-doping rules"). Mention strict liability once only.
- Do not name commercial supplement brands or sponsors from past papers.
- The 2026 specification lists specific supplements (nitrate, beta-alanine, sodium bicarbonate); they are not required here.
- Energy systems belong to the next subsection; refer to them only to justify food choices.

## 4. What exams ask

- Nutritional considerations before, during and after an event; nutrients to eat on event day with foods; one nutritional requirement before and one after.
- Two nutritional considerations after performance to enhance recovery (excluding hydration).
- Nutrients and food sources; side effects of dehydration; true/false on nutrition myths.
- Advantages and disadvantages of sports drinks; benefits of sports drinks; challenges of taking supplements; name a supplement and justify its use.
- Three aspects of a dietary plan with rationale.

## 5. Practice questions to write

1. 12 marks, Higher: "Examine three nutritional considerations for designing a dietary plan for an athlete in a named physical activity." Model: three distinct considerations (e.g. pre-event carbohydrate, post-event recovery, hydration), each with rationale tied to the activity.
2. 8 marks, Higher: "Discuss the challenges for performers who use sports supplements." Model: contamination and strict liability, side effects, cost, unnecessary with a balanced diet.
3. 6 marks, Ordinary: "List two side effects of dehydration and explain how a performer can stay hydrated."
