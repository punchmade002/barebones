## 1. Must cover

Scalars and vectors
- [ ] V1 Scalar quantity: has size (magnitude) only, e.g. distance, speed, mass, time.
- [ ] V2 Vector quantity: has size and direction, e.g. displacement, velocity, acceleration, force, momentum, weight. Vectors can be drawn as arrows whose length shows size.
- [ ] V3 Distance (total path travelled, scalar) versus displacement (straight-line change in position from start to finish, in a given direction, vector). Example: a 400 m runner on a standard track runs a distance of 400 m but finishes where they started, so displacement is zero.
- [ ] V4 Speed = distance ÷ time (scalar); velocity = displacement ÷ time (vector).
- [ ] V5 Acceleration = change in velocity ÷ time taken (vector); deceleration is negative acceleration.
- [ ] V6 Momentum = mass × velocity (vector). Classifying speed, velocity and momentum is a past question.
- [ ] V7 Why the distinction matters in sport: direction decides outcomes (e.g. a pass's velocity, not just its speed, decides whether it reaches a team-mate).

External forces (assumed)
- [ ] F1 Forces that change motion: muscular force, gravity (weight), friction, air resistance and water resistance (drag), ground reaction force.

Newton's laws of motion
- [ ] N1 First law (law of inertia): a body stays at rest or keeps moving at a constant velocity in a straight line unless an external (unbalanced) force acts on it. Examples: a ball on the penalty spot stays still until kicked; a sprinter in the blocks stays still until they drive against the blocks; a sliding performer slows only because friction acts.
- [ ] N2 Second law (law of acceleration): the acceleration of a body is proportional to the force applied, takes place in the direction of the force, and is inversely proportional to its mass (F = m × a). Examples: a harder kick gives the ball greater acceleration; the same force accelerates a lighter implement more than a heavier one. This is the law used to calculate the acceleration of a kicked ball.
- [ ] N3 Third law (law of action and reaction): for every action there is an equal and opposite reaction. Examples: a sprinter pushes back and down on the blocks and the ground reaction force drives them forward; a swimmer's hand pushes water backwards and the water pushes the swimmer forwards; a jumper drives down into the take-off board and is pushed up.
- [ ] N4 Applying all three laws to one named activity (e.g. swimming: start on the blocks, propulsion from the arm pull, streamlining off the wall to keep speed against water resistance).
- [ ] N5 Why use Newton's laws to analyse movement: they explain what causes and changes motion, show where and in what direction force must be applied, help correct technique (e.g. direct force in the intended direction of travel) and inform choice of equipment.

Quality/effectiveness: economy of movement
- [ ] E1 Economy of movement: performing a skill with no unnecessary movement and the least energy needed for the result.
- [ ] E2 Effects on quality/effectiveness: delays fatigue, improves consistency and accuracy, keeps technique intact late in a performance, looks more fluent (aesthetic).
- [ ] E3 Applied to a named skill, e.g. front crawl: a flat, streamlined body position reduces drag so more of the pull produces forward motion; a distance runner's relaxed arm swing without side-to-side rotation.
- [ ] E4 How economy is improved: practice to the autonomous stage, technique correction using video, flexibility and strength to hold positions.

Quality/effectiveness: creative application of skill
- [ ] C1 Creative application: using a well-learned skill in a new, original or unexpected way to solve the problem a situation presents, rather than reproducing the standard version.
- [ ] C2 It depends on the skill being automatic, so attention is free for the situation.
- [ ] C3 Benefits: deceives and unbalances opponents, opens options when the expected one is closed off, makes the performer less predictable, adds aesthetic or artistic merit (dance, gymnastics).
- [ ] C4 Examples: a no-look pass in basketball; a disguised drop shot in badminton; a dummy solo in Gaelic football; an original linking movement in a gymnastics floor routine.

## 3. Traps

- Speed is a scalar; velocity is a vector. Mass is a scalar; weight is a vector (a force).
- Newton's second law is the acceleration law, not the "action–reaction" law.
- Do not use past-paper figures or named athletes (e.g. a named rugby kicker or sprinter from a case study).
- Planes, axes and levers belong to the previous subsection. Do not repeat them.
- Economy of movement is not the same as moving slowly: it is achieving the result with the least wasted energy.

## 4. What exams ask

- Which of Newton's laws can be used to calculate the acceleration of a kicked ball? Name and define another law. Explain why you would use the laws when analysing human movement.
- Define Newton's second law; explain the third law using a named activity; apply the first law to two skills.
- Examine how each of Newton's three laws applies in a named activity (Higher, 40-mark part).
- Discuss the difference between vectors and scalars; identify scalar quantities in a case study; classify speed, velocity and momentum and justify velocity.
- Examine how economy of movement affects the quality/effectiveness of a named skill (12 marks, Higher case study).
- Explain creative application of a skill; describe two ways it benefits performance; give an example in a named skill.
