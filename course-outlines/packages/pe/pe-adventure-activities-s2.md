# Package: pe-adventure-activities-s2 — Orienteering

  **syllabusRef:** S6
- **Syllabus (skills and techniques):** map-reading; pacing skills; navigation skills;
  route-planning; adhering to safe-practice procedures; demonstrating respect for the
  environment.
- **Prepared by:** a higher model, 2026-10-30. Write in this order: what orienteering is and
  its formats; the map (scale, symbols and colours, contours); each listed skill as its own
  block with key techniques; safety; environment; rules and conventions; fitness and
  psychological demands; analysing an orienteering performance.

## 1. Must cover

- [ ] O1 Orienteering: navigating between a series of control points marked on a map, in the right order (or any order in score events), as quickly as possible; controls are marked on the ground by orange-and-white flags and checked with a punch or electronic chip.
- [ ] O2 Formats: cross-country (fixed order), score (visit as many controls as possible in a time limit, with time penalties for lateness), line, relays; school and park courses.

Map-reading
- [ ] M1 Scale: the ratio between map distance and ground distance (e.g. 1:10,000 means 1 cm on the map is 100 m on the ground).
- [ ] M2 Map colours and symbols: white (runnable forest), yellow (open land), green (thick vegetation, harder to pass), blue (water features), brown (landforms and contours), black (rock and man-made features such as paths, walls and buildings).
- [ ] M3 Contours: lines joining points of equal height; close together means steep, far apart means gentle.
- [ ] M4 The control description sheet: tells the orienteer what feature the control is on and where.
- [ ] M5 Setting (orienting) the map: turning it so it matches the ground, using the compass or features; thumbing the map (keeping the thumb on the current position).

Pacing
- [ ] P1 Pacing: measuring distance by counting double paces; each performer knows how many double paces they take per 100 m on different terrain and adjusts for slope, vegetation and fatigue.

Navigation
- [ ] N1 Compass skills: taking and following a bearing; using the compass to set the map.
- [ ] N2 Handrails: following linear features such as paths, walls, streams and fences.
- [ ] N3 Catching features: features beyond the control that tell the orienteer they have gone too far.
- [ ] N4 Attack points: an easy-to-find feature near the control from which to navigate precisely to it.
- [ ] N5 Aiming off: deliberately aiming to one side of a target on a linear feature so you know which way to turn.
- [ ] N6 Relocation when lost: stop, set the map, identify nearby features, return to the last known point.

Route-planning
- [ ] R1 Choosing between routes: the direct route (shorter, harder navigation or terrain) versus a longer route along handrails (easier, faster running); weigh distance, climb, terrain and the risk of error.
- [ ] R2 Splitting each leg into stages: attack point, handrail, catching feature; planning the next leg while moving.

Safety, environment, rules
- [ ] S1 Safe practice: suitable footwear and clothing for terrain and weather, a whistle, knowing the safety bearing and finish closing time, always reporting to the finish even when retiring, staying within boundaries, out-of-bounds areas, first aid.
- [ ] S2 Respect for the environment: staying out of out-of-bounds areas, closing gates, avoiding crops and wildlife, taking all litter home.
- [ ] S3 Rules and conventions: punch controls in order on a cross-country course; do not follow others or receive help; fair play; check in at the start and finish.

Demands and analysis
- [ ] D1 Fitness: cardio-respiratory endurance, muscular endurance, agility and balance on rough ground.
- [ ] D2 Psychological: concentration and decision-making under fatigue; staying calm when lost.
- [ ] D3 Analysing performance: split times per leg, route comparison after the event (drawing your route), error analysis (time lost per control).

## 3. Traps

- Contour lines close together mean steep ground, not flat.
- A bearing is taken from the map with the compass, then followed on the ground; keep the order clear.

## 4. What exams ask

- Orienteering is used in written answers as a named activity (e.g. it is the specification's own example adventure activity). Typical applications: decision-making, concentration, confidence, safety regulations, fitness demands.
