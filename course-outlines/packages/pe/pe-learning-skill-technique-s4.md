## 1. Must cover

Stages of learning (Fitts and Posner; each stage: what the performer is doing, what the movement looks like, errors, attention, what the coach should do)
- [ ] L1 Cognitive (beginner) stage: forming a mental picture of the skill, usually from a demonstration; movements slow, jerky and inconsistent; frequent, large errors the performer often cannot detect; full conscious attention on the movement; needs simple, unopposed practice, demonstrations and frequent, specific external feedback. Progress is rapid but fragile.
- [ ] L2 Associative (intermediate) stage: the basic pattern is in place; movements smoother and more consistent; fewer, smaller errors and the performer begins to detect some through "feel" (kinaesthetic sense); practice can add pressure, speed and simple opposition; feedback becomes more detailed. Most learners spend the longest time here.
- [ ] L3 Autonomous (expert) stage: the skill is performed automatically with little conscious attention, consistently and fluently; attention is free for tactics, opponents and decision-making; the performer detects and corrects own errors; practice keeps the skill grooved under competitive conditions; feedback is fine-tuning.
- [ ] L4 Describing the performance of one named skill at each of the three stages (a 2023 Higher case-study task), e.g. the basketball jump shot.
- [ ] L5 Identifying a performer's stage from evidence (consistency, errors, attention, level of competition) and suggesting how they could improve at that stage.

How skills are learned effectively
- [ ] G1 Demonstration and visual guidance: accurate demonstrations, video, a model performer, especially at the cognitive stage.
- [ ] G2 Verbal guidance: short, clear key teaching points; too much information overloads a beginner.
- [ ] G3 Manual guidance (physically moving the learner, e.g. a coach guiding the arm through a swing) and mechanical guidance (equipment that supports the learner, e.g. a float or a trampoline harness); useful for safety and confidence, but over-use creates dependence.
- [ ] G4 Feedback after attempts so the learner knows what to change (one bullet, refer to "Psychological preparation" for types).
- [ ] G5 Mental rehearsal (imagery) of the skill between physical attempts.
- [ ] G6 Motivation, clear goals and practice matched to the learner's stage.

Practice methods (each: what it is, when to use it, example)
- [ ] M1 Whole practice: the skill practised in its entirety; for skills that cannot easily be broken down or are fast and fluid (e.g. a golf swing, a javelin throw).
- [ ] M2 Part practice: one sub-routine practised in isolation (e.g. the toss in a tennis serve); for complex serial skills or dangerous elements.
- [ ] M3 Whole–part–whole: try the whole, isolate a weak part, return to the whole.
- [ ] M4 Progressive part (chaining): learn part one, add part two, and so on (e.g. the phases of a triple jump).
- [ ] M5 Massed practice: continuous practice with little or no rest; suits simple, closed, discrete skills and motivated, fit, experienced performers (e.g. repeated free throws).
- [ ] M6 Distributed practice: practice broken by rest or other activities; suits beginners, complex or tiring skills, and lowers fatigue and injury risk.
- [ ] M7 Fixed practice: the same skill repeated in unchanging conditions to groove the movement; suits closed skills (e.g. a free throw, a penalty).
- [ ] M8 Variable practice: the skill practised in changing situations; suits open skills because it builds adaptability (e.g. passing under different levels of defensive pressure).

Principles of effective practice (the specification does not list them; these are the ones exam answers use)
- [ ] P1 Specificity: practice should reproduce the demands of the competitive situation (same skills, speed, conditions, fatigue).
- [ ] P2 Progression: simple to complex, unopposed to opposed, slow to game speed, closed to open.
- [ ] P3 Appropriate distribution of practice and rest (massed or distributed according to the learner and skill).
- [ ] P4 Variety: different drills and conditions to keep motivation and build adaptability.
- [ ] P5 Quality of repetitions: correct technique repeated, not just volume; stop before fatigue degrades technique.
- [ ] P6 Clear goals and regular feedback.
- [ ] P7 Matched to the individual's stage of learning and ability; safe, with a warm-up.

Designing a practice schedule
- [ ] D1 A practice session structure: warm-up; introduction and demonstration of the skill with key teaching points; progressive drills from simple to complex; a conditioned game or competitive scenario; cool-down; review and reflection.
- [ ] D2 A schedule across several weeks: sessions per week, duration, progression of difficulty, the practice methods chosen and why, how progress is recorded (e.g. a success rate out of ten attempts).
- [ ] D3 Justify each choice by the principles and by the type of skill (e.g. fixed, massed whole practice for a closed free throw; variable practice for an open pass).

## 3. Traps

- Do not reverse the stages: cognitive comes first, then associative, then autonomous.
- "Variable practice best suits open skills" is correct; do not recommend fixed practice for open skills except as an early-stage step.
- Massed practice is not the same as whole practice; one concerns rest, the other how the skill is broken down. Keep the three pairs (whole/part, massed/distributed, fixed/variable) distinct.
- Do not list feedback types (intrinsic, extrinsic, knowledge of results, knowledge of performance) here; they belong in "Psychological preparation".
- The 2026 specification's list of practice principles ("variety, progressive, specific, measurable, achievable, realistic, time, exciting, challenging, recorded") is not this course. Use the principles in section 1.
- Do not use case-study names from past papers.

## 4. What exams ask

- Name and outline the three stages of learning; describe one skill at each stage; identify a performer's stage from case-study evidence (often autonomous for elite athletes, cognitive for a beginner).
- Describe two ways a performer at a given stage could improve.
- Outline or identify two or three principles of effective practice and justify them; show how to use one in a practice session.
- Explain one type of practice from a list (massed, part, variable) to improve a named skill; explain three practice methods; name a practice method for a javelin thrower and explain how to apply it.
- Describe an activity to improve a named skill and explain why it is effective; discuss how learning skills effectively benefits performance.

## 5. Practice questions to write

1. 9 marks, Higher: "Name and outline the stages of skill learning." Model: all three stages, each with movement quality, errors, attention and coaching implication.
2. 15 marks, Higher: "Identify three principles of effective practice that could be used in designing skill practice sessions, and justify each." Model: three distinct principles from P1–P7, each justified with a specific example from one named activity.
3. 8 marks, Ordinary: "Explain how variable practice could be used to improve a skill of your choice." Model: definition, an open skill, a described drill with changing conditions, why it builds adaptability.
