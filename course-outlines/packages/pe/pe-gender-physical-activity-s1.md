# Package: pe-gender-physical-activity-s1 — Gender, sport and physical activity

- **loId:** `pe-gender-physical-activity-s1` · **chapterId:** `pe-gender-physical-activity` ·
  **syllabusRef:** 9.1
- **Prescribed for June 2027** (SEC circular S51/25). Write in full Higher Level depth.
- **Syllabus:** "examine the main influences that impact on the participation patterns of boys
  and girls in physical activity and sport"; "suggest ways in which the imbalance between boys'
  and girls' levels of participation in physical activity might be addressed in school and in
  the community". The topic introduction: young women have lower levels of participation, and
  young men and women are more likely to take part in some activities than others. Ordinary and
  Higher level; no Higher Level only material.
- **Prepared by:** a higher model, 2026-10-30. Write in this order: sex and gender (assumed);
  participation patterns (how much, which activities, when drop-out happens); influences, one
  block per group of influences, each showing how it affects boys and girls differently;
  addressing the imbalance in school; in the community. Body image and media are the next
  subsection; socialisation, social regulation and hegemonic ideals the one after. Name those
  influences here in one bullet each and point to the notes by title.

## 1. Must cover

Patterns
- [ ] T1 Sex (biological) versus gender (the socially constructed roles, behaviours and expectations a society attaches to being male or female) (assumed).
- [ ] T2 Participation patterns: girls are, on average, less active than boys and drop out of sport in greater numbers during adolescence, especially in the senior cycle years.
- [ ] T3 Activity choices differ: boys more often play competitive team and contact sports; girls more often choose dance, gymnastics, individual fitness and non-contact activities; many activities are labelled "for boys" or "for girls".
- [ ] T4 Participation patterns change across the life course and are shaped by many interacting influences, not by biology alone.

Influences (each: how it works, effect on boys, effect on girls, an example)
- [ ] I1 Family: parents' own activity, encouragement, willingness to pay and drive; parents may encourage sons into contact sports and daughters into "suitable" activities.
- [ ] I2 Peers: friends' choices and fear of being judged; for teenage girls, being sporty may be seen as uncool; for boys, sport can be central to status among peers.
- [ ] I3 School: range of activities offered (often competitive team games), PE kit and changing facilities, teacher expectations, attention given to boys' teams, timetabling.
- [ ] I4 Provision and opportunity in the community: number of teams, coaches, facility time and fixtures; boys often have more teams and better facility slots.
- [ ] I5 Role models and leadership: fewer visible female athletes, coaches and administrators.
- [ ] I6 Clothing and kit: tight, short or revealing kit (swimwear, leotards, athletics briefs, short skirts) can make self-conscious teenagers, especially girls, drop out; comfortable, choice-based kit helps.
- [ ] I7 Body image and media representation (one bullet; taught in "Gender, media and body image").
- [ ] I8 Gender socialisation and hegemonic masculinity/femininity (one bullet; taught in "Gender socialisation and its impact on participation").
- [ ] I9 Influences on boys specifically: provision and pathways, pressure to be strong and competitive, ridicule if they choose activities labelled feminine (e.g. dance), which can narrow boys' choices too.
- [ ] I10 Practical influences: time, cost, safety and transport, which can weigh more heavily on girls (e.g. safety concerns about evening travel).

Addressing the imbalance in school
- [ ] S1 Broaden the activity offer (dance, fitness classes, aquatics, outdoor pursuits, non-contact versions of games) and give students choice.
- [ ] S2 Girls-only or friendship-group options and non-competitive sessions alongside competitive teams.
- [ ] S3 Flexible, comfortable PE kit and private, clean changing facilities.
- [ ] S4 Equal facility time, fixtures, coaching and recognition (assemblies, noticeboards, social media) for girls' teams.
- [ ] S5 Female role models and leaders: older students as leaders, visiting female athletes and coaches.
- [ ] S6 Student voice: survey students and plan activities from the results; track participation to see if the gap closes.

Addressing the imbalance in the community
- [ ] C1 Clubs offering girls' and women's teams at every age, with female coaches.
- [ ] C2 Social, beginner and "return to sport" programmes (e.g. women's recreational leagues, mothers-and-daughters sessions).
- [ ] C3 Fair allocation of pitches, halls and coaching between girls' and boys' teams.
- [ ] C4 Safe, accessible, well-lit facilities and transport.
- [ ] C5 Campaigns and visibility (20x20; Sport Ireland's Women in Sport policy) and media coverage of women's sport.
- [ ] C6 Local Sports Partnership programmes targeting teenage girls.

## 3. Traps

- Do not explain participation differences by biology alone; the specification frames them as social.
- Do not quote survey percentages, including those in past papers.
- Body image and media (9.2) and socialisation, social regulation and hegemonic ideals (9.3) are separate subsections; give one bullet each here.
- Boys' participation is also shaped by gender (the 2026 Higher question asks about boys); include I9.

## 4. What exams ask

- Examine the main influences on the participation patterns of young people (10 marks, Higher case study).
- Describe two influences on the participation patterns of boys (8 marks, Higher).
- Discuss two ways a school could tackle a gender imbalance in participation (8 marks, Higher).
- Explain how required clothing can affect participation in certain sports (Ordinary).
- Which group participates most in school sport, why, and how to increase participation (Ordinary, from a chart).

## 5. Practice questions to write

1. 10 marks, Higher: "Examine the main influences that impact on the participation patterns of boys and girls in physical activity and sport." Model: at least four influences, each showing a different effect on boys and girls.
2. 8 marks, Higher: "Discuss two ways in which a school could address an imbalance between boys' and girls' levels of participation." Model: two specific strategies, each linked to the barrier it removes.
3. 6 marks, Ordinary: "Explain how the clothing required in some sports can affect participation." Model: self-consciousness, examples of kit, a solution.
