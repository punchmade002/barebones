## 1. Must cover

- [ ] E1 ATP (adenosine triphosphate) is the only direct source of energy for muscle contraction; muscles store only enough for about 2–3 seconds, so it must be constantly resynthesised by the energy systems.

ATP-PC (alactic) system
- [ ] P1 Fuel: phosphocreatine (PC) stored in the muscle; no oxygen needed (anaerobic).
- [ ] P2 Intensity and duration: maximal intensity, about 8–10 seconds.
- [ ] P3 By-products: none that cause fatigue; fatigue comes from PC running out.
- [ ] P4 Recovery: PC stores are about half restored in 30 seconds and almost fully in about 3 minutes of rest.
- [ ] P5 Examples: 100 m sprint, long jump, shot-put, a tackle, a jump to catch a ball.

Lactic acid (anaerobic glycolytic) system
- [ ] L1 Fuel: carbohydrate (glycogen/glucose) broken down without oxygen.
- [ ] L2 Intensity and duration: high intensity, from about 10 seconds to about 2 minutes (predominant roughly 10–90 seconds).
- [ ] L3 By-product: lactic acid (lactate and hydrogen ions), which lowers muscle pH and causes fatigue and a burning feeling.
- [ ] L4 Recovery: removal of lactic acid takes longer (about an hour; faster with an active cool-down).
- [ ] L5 Examples: 400 m race, 100 m swim, a long sustained attack in a team game.

Aerobic system
- [ ] O1 Fuel: carbohydrate and fat (protein only in extreme cases) broken down with oxygen.
- [ ] O2 Intensity and duration: low to moderate intensity, from about 2 minutes to hours.
- [ ] O3 By-products: carbon dioxide and water, which are easily removed; no fatigue-causing by-products.
- [ ] O4 Produces far more ATP from each molecule of fuel than the anaerobic systems, but more slowly.
- [ ] O5 Examples: marathon, 10 km open-water swim, the background work of a Gaelic football match; it also powers recovery between high-intensity efforts.

The energy continuum and relative contribution
- [ ] C1 All three systems work at all times; the one that predominates depends on duration and intensity of the activity.
- [ ] C2 Intermittent team games use all three: ATP-PC for sprints and jumps, lactic acid for repeated efforts with short recovery, aerobic for the overall duration and recovery between efforts.
- [ ] C3 Identifying the main energy system for a given event, duration and intensity (e.g. marathon: aerobic; 400 m: lactic acid; long jump: ATP-PC).
- [ ] C4 Reading an energy-system graph: which line or letter represents each system against time.

Applying the systems
- [ ] A1 Preparation for practice: train the predominant system (specificity), e.g. short maximal sprints with full recovery for ATP-PC; repeated high-intensity intervals with short recovery for lactate tolerance; continuous or long intervals for aerobic fitness.
- [ ] A2 Work-to-rest ratios match the system: ATP-PC about 1:3 or longer rest to restore PC; lactic acid shorter recovery; aerobic near-continuous.
- [ ] A3 Performance: pacing strategy (going too hard early in a 400 m or 1500 m race floods the muscles with lactic acid); substitutions and tactical rests in games.
- [ ] A4 Recovery: active cool-down removes lactic acid faster; carbohydrate refuels glycogen for the lactic acid and aerobic systems; full rest between maximal efforts restores PC.
- [ ] A5 Diet link: carbohydrate fuels the lactic acid and aerobic systems; creatine supplementation targets PC stores (one bullet; details in "Diet, nutrition and hydration").

## 3. Traps

- The ATP-PC system does not produce lactic acid.
- Do not say a system "switches on" only after another runs out: all three contribute continuously.
- The aerobic system uses fat as well as carbohydrate; the anaerobic systems use carbohydrate only (and PC for ATP-PC).
- Do not copy times or swimmers from the 2026 Higher case study.

## 4. What exams ask

- Name three energy systems; discuss the role of one in a named activity.
- Describe how two energy systems contribute to performance in a named activity (e.g. ice hockey, a team game).
- Identify the main energy system for activities given duration and intensity (marathon, 400 m, long jump); identify the aerobic line on a graph; explain why an activity uses the aerobic system.
- Explain how understanding the ATP-PC and aerobic systems can inform preparation for two contrasting swimming events (Higher case study).
