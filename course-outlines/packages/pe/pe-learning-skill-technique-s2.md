## 1. Must cover

Biomechanics
- [ ] B1 Biomechanics: the study of the forces acting on and produced by the body and their effects on movement. Uses in analysis: improve technique, reduce injury risk, inform equipment design.
- [ ] B2 Anatomical position (standing upright, facing forward, arms by the sides, palms facing forward) is the reference for describing planes and movements (assumed).

Planes and axes (a plane is a flat surface through the body in which movement occurs; an axis is the imaginary line about which the body or a limb rotates; each plane pairs with the axis at right angles to it)
- [ ] P1 Sagittal plane: divides the body into left and right sides. Movements: flexion and extension (e.g. running leg action, biceps curl, squat, kicking a ball straight ahead, forward roll, front somersault). Axis: transverse (also called frontal or medial-lateral) axis, running side to side.
- [ ] P2 Frontal (coronal) plane: divides the body into front and back. Movements: abduction, adduction, lateral flexion (e.g. cartwheel, star jump, side-step lateral movement, a side bend). Axis: sagittal (also called antero-posterior) axis, running front to back.
- [ ] P3 Transverse (horizontal) plane: divides the body into upper and lower halves. Movements: rotation (e.g. discus or hammer turn, golf swing, a pirouette, full twist in trampolining). Axis: longitudinal (vertical) axis, running head to toe.
- [ ] P4 Applying planes: identify the plane and axis of a named movement and explain why (e.g. a hammer thrower's turns in the circle are rotation in the transverse plane about the longitudinal axis).
- [ ] P5 Joint actions needed to name movements (assumed, vocabulary only): flexion, extension, abduction, adduction, rotation, plantar flexion, dorsiflexion.

Levers
- [ ] L1 A lever is a rigid bar that turns about a fixed point. In the body: bone = lever (bar), joint = fulcrum (pivot), muscle contraction = effort, the weight of the body part plus anything held or struck = load (resistance).
- [ ] L2 Identifying the class: which component is in the middle. 1st class: fulcrum in the middle. 2nd class: load in the middle. 3rd class: effort in the middle. Memory aid "1, 2, 3 — F, L, E".
- [ ] L3 First-class lever examples: nodding the head (neck joint fulcrum, neck muscles effort, weight of the head load); extension of the elbow by the triceps (elbow fulcrum, triceps effort behind it, forearm and hand or ball load), e.g. the arm action in a soccer throw-in or a basketball set shot follow-through.
- [ ] L4 Second-class lever examples: plantar flexion of the ankle when rising onto the toes (ball of the foot fulcrum, body weight load through the ankle, calf muscle (gastrocnemius) effort via the Achilles tendon at the heel), e.g. the push-off in a sprint start or a jump take-off.
- [ ] L5 Third-class lever examples: elbow flexion by the biceps (biceps curl; the arm in a basketball chest-pass recovery or a hurling lift); knee extension by the quadriceps when kicking a ball. Most levers in the body are third class.
- [ ] L6 Labelling a lever diagram: mark fulcrum, effort, load and direction of movement for a named action.

Mechanical advantage and application
- [ ] M1 Effort arm (distance from fulcrum to effort) and load arm (distance from fulcrum to load).
- [ ] M2 Mechanical advantage (effort arm longer than load arm, typical of 2nd-class levers): a large load can be moved with a small effort, but with a small range and speed of movement.
- [ ] M3 Mechanical disadvantage (load arm longer, typical of 3rd-class levers): more effort is needed, but the end of the lever moves through a large range at high speed, which suits throwing and striking.
- [ ] M4 Lengthening the lever (a straight arm, or adding a racket, hurley, bat or club) increases the speed at the end of the lever and so the speed of release or impact, but needs more effort to move. Apply to a named skill.
- [ ] M5 Comparing 2nd- and 3rd-class levers with sporting examples (the 2024 Higher question): position of components, mechanical advantage versus disadvantage, force versus speed and range.
- [ ] M6 Using levers to analyse and improve a skill: e.g. a fuller arm extension in a tennis serve or javelin throw to lengthen the lever at release.

## 3. Traps

- Plane and axis pairs are fixed: sagittal plane with transverse axis; frontal plane with sagittal axis; transverse plane with longitudinal axis. Do not pair a plane with the axis of the same name (e.g. never "sagittal plane, sagittal axis").
- The transverse axis is also called the frontal axis in some textbooks, and the sagittal axis is also called the antero-posterior axis. Give both names once, then use one name consistently.
- A discus or hammer turn is the transverse plane (rotation), not the frontal plane.
- Keep muscle naming to the single muscle providing the effort for each example. Do not teach muscle or skeletal anatomy (that is the 2026 specification, not this course).
- Vectors, scalars and Newton's laws belong to the next subsection. Do not include them.
- Do not use past-paper figures or case-study names (e.g. the wheelbarrow diagram, the 2022 discus article).

## 4. What exams ask

- Name two planes of human movement and describe each with a sporting example.
- Name the plane and axis of a described movement (discus spin, hammer turn) and give another movement from a different activity in the same, or a different, plane and axis, explaining why.
- Label load, effort and fulcrum on a diagram and identify the class of lever.
- Name a skill that uses a third-class lever and justify it; elbow flexion is a common prompt.
- Complete a table: class of lever, skill/technique, joint action for 1st, 2nd and 3rd class.
- Identify the body parts acting as fulcrum, effort and load for a named lever.
- Examine the differences between second- and third-class levers using sporting examples (Higher, 40-mark question part).

## 5. Practice questions to write

1. 10 marks, Higher: "Using sporting examples, examine the differences between second-class and third-class levers." Model: arrangement of components for each, one example each with the parts named, mechanical advantage versus disadvantage and the force/speed trade-off.
2. 8 marks, Higher: "Name the plane and axis in which a discus thrower rotates, and give a movement from a different activity that occurs in the same plane and axis." Model: transverse plane, longitudinal axis; e.g. a golf swing or a pirouette, explained as rotation.
3. 6 marks, Ordinary: "Name the class of lever used in elbow flexion and state where the fulcrum, effort and load are." Model: third class; elbow joint, biceps, forearm and hand or object held.
