# Package: pe-gender-physical-activity-s3 — Gender socialisation and its impact on participation

- **loId:** `pe-gender-physical-activity-s3` · **chapterId:** `pe-gender-physical-activity` ·
  **syllabusRef:** 9.3
- **Prescribed for June 2027** (SEC circular S51/25). Write in full Higher Level depth.
- **Syllabus:** "examine how social regulation of the body has impacted and continues to
  impact on the participation of both men and women in physical activity and sport"; "explain
  why the characteristics associated with hegemonic masculinity and hegemonic femininity might
  impact on the participation of both boys and girls in sport and physical activity". Ordinary
  and Higher level; no Higher Level only material.
- **Prepared by:** a higher model, 2026-10-30. Write in this order: gender socialisation and

## 1. Must cover

Gender socialisation
- [ ] G1 Socialisation: the lifelong process of learning the norms, values and behaviour expected in a society. Gender socialisation: learning what is seen as appropriate for boys/men and girls/women.
- [ ] G2 Agents of socialisation: family (toys, activities encouraged, praise), peers, school (activities offered, teacher expectations), media, sports clubs and coaches, religion and culture.
- [ ] G3 Examples: boys given footballs and encouraged to be rough and competitive; girls given dance classes and praised for being neat and graceful; comments such as "throws like a girl".

Social regulation of the body
- [ ] R1 Definition: the way society, through its norms, expectations, rules and media images, defines how bodies should look, move and behave, and so influences what physical activity people feel is appropriate or available to them.
- [ ] R2 Past: women were historically excluded or restricted (barred from some events and clubs, limited competition distances, dress rules, beliefs that strenuous exercise harmed women's health); women's boxing was only added to the Olympics in 2012.
- [ ] R3 Present: dress codes and kit expectations, judgement of muscular women or of men in "feminine" activities, unequal provision and pay, expectations around appearance.
- [ ] R4 Impact on women: girls steered away from contact, power and strength sports and from visible muscle; self-surveillance of appearance; drop-out in adolescence.
- [ ] R5 Impact on men: pressure to be strong, tough and competitive; avoidance of dance, gymnastics or synchronised swimming; pressure to play through pain and injury; reluctance to show weakness or seek help.

Hegemonic masculinity
- [ ] M1 Definition: the dominant, socially accepted idea of what it means to be a man in a society, treated as the norm against which all men are measured (concept developed by R. W. Connell).
- [ ] M2 Characteristics: physical strength, toughness, aggression, competitiveness, dominance, emotional restraint, risk-taking, heterosexuality.
- [ ] M3 Impact on boys: rewards boys who fit (high status in contact team sports); excludes or ridicules boys who are less sporty or prefer aesthetic activities; encourages playing through injury and violence; may lead to drop-out of boys who don't fit.
- [ ] M4 Impact on girls: sport itself is defined as masculine, so girls who play are seen as less feminine; women's sport is treated as second-rate.

Hegemonic femininity
- [ ] F1 Definition: the dominant, socially accepted idea of what it means to be a woman in a society, treated as the norm for women.
- [ ] F2 Characteristics: slimness, grace, attractiveness and appearance focus, gentleness, passivity and non-aggression, caring, deference.
- [ ] F3 Impact on girls: activities that are aggressive, muscular, sweaty or contact-based seem unfeminine, so girls avoid them or drop out; emphasis on appearance leads to self-consciousness; girls channelled into aesthetic activities.
- [ ] F4 Impact on boys: activities linked to femininity (dance, gymnastics) are avoided by boys for fear of ridicule.

Challenging the norms
- [ ] C1 Visible role models who break stereotypes (women in rugby, boxing and Gaelic games; men in dance); equal provision and coverage; mixed and choice-based PE; coaches and teachers challenging stereotyped comments.

## 3. Traps

- Hegemonic means dominant and widely accepted, not "all men" or "all women".
- Both ideals affect both boys and girls; every block needs both.
- Body image and media representation are taught in "Gender, media and body image"; refer to them in one bullet.
- Do not use the past-paper equal-pay case as a socialisation example; it belongs to policy, not socialisation.

## 4. What exams ask

- What is social regulation of the body; discuss how it affects the participation of men and women (Higher short question).
- Explain hegemonic masculinity; explain hegemonic femininity (Ordinary 4 marks each; Higher 10 marks together).
- Identify two characteristics of hegemonic femininity and explain why they might affect girls' participation.

## 5. Practice questions to write

1. 10 marks, Higher: "Explain hegemonic femininity and hegemonic masculinity, and why each might impact on the participation of boys and girls." Model: both definitions, characteristics, effects on both sexes.
2. 8 marks, Higher: "Discuss how social regulation of the body impacts on the participation levels of men and women in physical activity."
3. 6 marks, Ordinary: "Explain hegemonic masculinity and give one way it can affect boys' participation in sport."
