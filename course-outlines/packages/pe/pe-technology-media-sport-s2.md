## 1. Must cover

- [ ] M1 Types of media: television (free-to-air and subscription/pay-per-view), radio, newspapers and magazines, internet and streaming, social media, podcasts.

Elite versus mass participation
- [ ] E1 Elite coverage: live broadcasts, highlights, analysis, celebrity athletes; concentrated on a few popular men's sports; drives sponsorship and funding.
- [ ] E2 Mass-participation coverage: limited, mostly local or charity-focused stories, social media and event livestreams; can promote participation (e.g. coverage of city marathons and parkrun).
- [ ] E3 Investigating coverage: count airtime, articles or social media posts by sport, gender and level over a set period; analyse who is shown and how.
- [ ] E4 Effects of coverage on participation: role models and inspiration, increased awareness; but unequal coverage can make some activities and groups invisible.

Gender stereotypes
- [ ] G1 Gender stereotyping: fixed beliefs about which sports and behaviours suit men and women (e.g. men as strong and aggressive; women as graceful and suited to "feminine" sports).
- [ ] G2 How media maintain stereotypes: much less coverage of women's sport; commentary on women's appearance, relationships or emotions rather than performance; sexualised images; "male as norm" language ("football" versus "women's football"); men in aesthetic sports portrayed as unusual.
- [ ] G3 Counter-examples and change: growing live coverage of women's sport; campaigns for visibility.
- [ ] G4 Free-to-air versus pay-per-view: free-to-air reaches large audiences and promotes the sport; pay-per-view raises money for the sport but limits access (a 2025 Ordinary pattern with named services).

Media and spectator appeal
- [ ] A1 Characteristics: fast pace and continuous action, high scoring or dramatic moments, clear and easily understood rules, star players and rivalries, national or local identity, short duration or fitting a broadcast slot, spectacular skills, close contests and unpredictability, suitability for cameras, good venues and atmosphere.
- [ ] A2 Identifying four characteristics for a named activity (e.g. hurling: speed, skill, physical contest, county identity).
- [ ] A3 How sports have changed to increase appeal (shorter formats such as Twenty20 cricket, rule changes to speed up play, scheduling for television).

Impact on spectator behaviour
- [ ] B1 Positive: inspires attendance and participation, builds knowledge and appreciation of skills, creates fan communities, promotes fair play through visible sanctions.
- [ ] B2 Negative: can sensationalise rivalries and controversy; replays of decisions can inflame crowds; online abuse of players and officials on social media; fans may stay at home rather than attend; copying poor behaviour seen on television.
- [ ] B3 Analysing the overall impact with examples.

## 3. Traps

- Body image and the media's effect on participation by gender belong mainly to `pe-gender-physical-activity`; keep G1–G3 to the media's role in stereotypes.
- Technology's impact on spectators (replays, apps) is in the previous subsection; here focus on media coverage.
- Do not invent statistics on coverage percentages.

## 4. What exams ask

- List two positive and two negative impacts the media has on spectators and describe one of each; analyse how spectator behaviour has been affected by media coverage; describe how coverage can positively affect spectator behaviour.
- Outline two characteristics that could give a named activity media or spectator appeal; identify four.
- Investigate media coverage of elite performance and explain findings.
- Explain gender stereotyping in sport and the media's role; give an example of a gender stereotype; outline how media contribute to gender stereotypes; describe the role of the media in maintaining stereotypes of women (Higher, with examples).
- Advantage and disadvantage of free-to-air versus pay-per-view coverage.
