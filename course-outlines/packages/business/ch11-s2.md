# Package: ch11-s2 — Strategies to adapt or expand, and the role of technology

- **loId:** `ch11-s2` · **chapterId:** `ch11`
- **Syllabus:** Strand 2, section 6 (Growth, development and expansion), outcomes [2.6.3] and [2.6.4]. Students learn about "how enterprise is not static and how a business may develop and may use technology to support growth." They should be able to outline the strategies employed by a business to adapt or expand, and appreciate the potential of technology to support adaptation and expansion. Outline: reasons to expand; organic (internal) strategies; inorganic (external) strategies with advantages and disadvantages; finance for expansion; technology enabling growth; risks of growing too fast.
- **Prepared by:** a higher model, 2026-11-23. The writer works only from this package and `course-outlines/NOTES_STANDARD.md`. Specification first examined June 2027. Nothing here is Higher Level only.

## 1. Must cover (one bullet or block for every item; tick each off)

Background (assumed)
- [ ] A1 (assumed) A business is not static: it must adapt to customers, competitors and technology to survive (enterprise is not static).
- [ ] A2 (assumed) Competition and competitive advantage (the notes on Porter's five forces): firms grow partly to defend or build advantage.
- [ ] A3 (assumed) Economies of scale: average cost per unit falls as output rises.
- [ ] A4 (assumed) Share capital, loans, retained earnings and overdrafts as sources of finance (taught in the finance chapter).
- [ ] A5 (assumed) Working capital: the cash needed to run day to day.

Why businesses expand
- [ ] R1 Economies of scale: lower average cost per unit through bulk buying, specialised equipment and spreading fixed overheads.
- [ ] R2 Growing market share: more bargaining power and brand recognition.
- [ ] R3 Diversification: spreading risk so a fall in one product or market does not sink the business.
- [ ] R4 Survival: a growing competitor may squeeze a small firm out.

Organic (internal) strategies
- [ ] O1 Definition of organic growth: uses the firm's own resources; slower than external growth but keeps control within the firm.
- [ ] O2 New product development (builds on existing brand and skills).
- [ ] O3 Entering new markets (existing or new products to new customer groups or places).
- [ ] O4 Increasing capacity (more premises, machinery or staff).
- [ ] O5 Franchising out: licensing the brand and system to franchisees who pay fees and royalties.
- [ ] O6 Exporting: selling abroad directly or through agents or distributors.
- [ ] O7 E-commerce as an organic route (selling online to new customers).
- [ ] O8 Advantages and disadvantages: no purchase premium and decisions stay in-house; slow, may lack skills or capital; franchising spreads capital across franchisees but the franchisor loses direct control of quality and service; exporting adds transport and distribution costs and reduces control of how the product is sold abroad; a new product can fail if customers do not accept it, so test demand first.

Inorganic (external) strategies
- [ ] I1 Definition of inorganic growth: combines with or buys other businesses; fast but brings integration risk.
- [ ] I2 Merger: two firms join into one new combined business, usually for scale and shared skills.
- [ ] I3 Takeover or acquisition: one firm buys control of another, agreed with the target's board or forced on it.
- [ ] I4 Strategic alliance: cooperation agreement between independent firms that share resources or markets without merging.
- [ ] I5 Joint venture: a separate new business owned jointly by two or more firms that share costs, risks and profits.
- [ ] I6 Advantages and disadvantages: mergers and takeovers give rapid access to customers, markets and skills; acquisitions are costly (often a premium over market value, financed by debt or share issues); culture clashes and integration problems can destroy value; alliances are flexible and cheap to set up but risk disputes over control and a partner learning the firm's methods; joint ventures share risk and bring expertise but conflicting goals and slow joint decisions are common.
- [ ] I7 Large mergers and takeovers may need clearance from the Competition and Consumer Protection Commission, which can delay or block them.

Before expanding abroad (links to the international trade chapter)
- [ ] X1 Market research to show real demand in the target country.
- [ ] X2 Legal and regulatory requirements such as product standards and tax rules.
- [ ] X3 Exchange rate movements change the euro value of sales and costs (currency risk).
- [ ] X4 Cultural and language differences affect the product, packaging and promotion message.

Financing expansion (refer to the finance chapter by subject)
- [ ] F1 The sources of finance covered earlier apply; match the source to how long the investment takes to pay back.
- [ ] F2 Long-term assets suit long-term finance; a temporary build-up of stock suits short-term finance such as an overdraft.
- [ ] F3 Retained earnings avoid interest and loss of control but limit growth to what profits allow.

Technology supporting growth [2.6.4]
- [ ] T1 E-commerce and online marketplaces: a small firm sells nationally or internationally quickly.
- [ ] T2 Automation (robotic production, automated stock management): more output without matching labour increases.
- [ ] T3 Cloud computing: rent computing power and software as needed, scale without buying servers.
- [ ] T4 Data and AI tools: predict demand, personalise offers, spot trends faster than manual analysis.
- [ ] T5 Remote working: wider pool of workers, less office space.
- [ ] T6 Digital angle of risk: cyber-security and data protection (GDPR compliance) when customer data is collected as the firm grows; dependence on a few platforms.

Risks of growing too fast
- [ ] G1 Overtrading: sales grow faster than the working capital to fund them, so cash runs short despite healthy sales.
- [ ] G2 Falling quality as production or service is stretched across more customers and new staff.
- [ ] G3 Overstretched management and slow decisions.
- [ ] G4 Heavy borrowing raises fixed repayments and the risk of default.
- [ ] G5 Brand and culture diluted.

Cross-cutting themes
- [ ] E1 Ethical angle: takeovers can cost jobs and close local sites; franchisors and joint-venture partners owe fair treatment; stakeholders (staff, suppliers, communities) should be weighed before a takeover.
- [ ] E2 Sustainability angle: growth raises resource use, transport emissions (exporting) and waste; expansion plans can include energy-efficient premises and sustainable supply chains; cloud and remote working can reduce travel and office use.
- [ ] E3 Digital angle: see T1 to T6.

## 3. Traps

- Do not use: any threshold figure for CCPC notification (such as €60 million or €100 million), because the figures changed twice in 2026 and the official position is easy to misstate.
- Do not use: any named exporter, alliance, joint venture, marketplace or software product, because none is checked.
- Textbooks differ on franchising: some treat it as inorganic. The specification outline here treats "franchising out" as organic; keep it in organic growth and state it grows the brand with other people's capital.
- Confusable: merger (two firms become one new firm, by agreement) versus takeover (one firm buys control, possibly hostile). Strategic alliance (no new company, firms stay independent) versus joint venture (a new separate company jointly owned). Organic means the firm's own resources; inorganic means buying or combining with another firm.
- Confusable: overtrading (growing sales beyond working capital) is not the same as overcapacity; it leads to a cash-flow problem, not a loss.
- Belongs to a neighbouring subsection: the detailed method for weighing costs and benefits of expansion (the notes on cost-benefit analysis); competitive advantage and the five forces (the first subsection); how the marketing mix and business model adapt to external drivers (the last subsection). Do not teach the sources of finance themselves here; refer to them by subject.
- The notes' "Before expanding abroad" is the same topic as the international trade chapter; give only the four checks here.
- Do not use GDPR as one of the student's "one EU regulation" choices anywhere in this chapter; the specification reserves that choice for another strand. Mention data protection here only as a risk.

## 4. What exams ask

- Outline factors before expanding into an international market (2025 Higher Level Sample 1, Question 2(c)): "outline" means give the main points, restricted to essential information. A full answer gives three distinct factors (market research and demand, legal and regulatory requirements, exchange rate risk, cultural and language differences, cost and logistics, finance), each with a one-line reason tied to the business in the stimulus. Common mistakes: naming a factor with no reason; giving three versions of cost; ignoring the stimulus firm.
- Likely tested forms (from the specification verbs, not from a quoted paper): outline strategies a business uses to adapt or expand; compare organic and inorganic growth (compare means refer to both throughout, similarities and differences); appreciate the potential of technology (state its value with an example of how it supports growth).
- A full answer names the strategy, says what it involves, gives an advantage and a disadvantage, and ties it to the firm. Common mistakes: confusing merger with takeover, alliance with joint venture; calling every growth "expansion into new markets"; forgetting the risks.
- Discuss or evaluate: give three or four developed points, each with its effect, then a justified view (for example, organic growth suits a firm that wants control and has time).
- Never invent mark allocations or question wording.
