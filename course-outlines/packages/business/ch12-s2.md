# Package: ch12-s2 — Risk management strategies and insurance

- **loId:** `ch12-s2` · **chapterId:** `ch12`

## 1. Must cover (one bullet or block for every item; tick each off)

Assumed knowledge
- [ ] A1 (assumed) Risk, likelihood and impact, and the risk matrix (from the earlier notes on the risks of enterprise).
- [ ] A2 (assumed) Transfer: shifting the financial consequences of a risk to another party. Retention: accepting a risk and paying any loss from the business's own funds.
- [ ] A3 (assumed) A contract is a legally binding agreement; contracts can shift responsibility between parties.

Strategies (each: what it is, when it suits, cost, limit)
- [ ] S1 Avoidance: not taking on the risk at all (not entering a market, launching a product or doing an activity); removes the risk and the cost of controlling it; cost is lost opportunity and profit; suits severe risks the firm cannot afford; limits growth if overused.
- [ ] S2 Spread (diversification): product, market and supplier diversification; a failure in one is offset by others; works only if the parts are not hit by the same event; cost is extra management time and expense.
- [ ] S3 Prevention (reduction): staff training, health and safety procedures, planned maintenance, cyber security (firewalls, updates, strong passwords, backups), quality control; lowers likelihood or impact; costs money and needs monitoring, so weigh cost against loss prevented.
- [ ] S4 Transfer: insurance (the insurer bears the cost for a premium); contracts (supplier responsible for defective goods, subcontractor liable for own work); outsourcing (some operational risk, but reputational exposure stays); it does not remove the risk and depends on the other party's ability to pay.
- [ ] S5 Retention: accept small, predictable losses where expected loss is less than the cost of insuring; contingency fund; dangerous if losses could be large.
- [ ] S6 Comparing the strategies: each suits different risks; a firm combines several (for example insure premises, train staff and hold a cash reserve).
- [ ] S7 Analysis of strategies for each category of risk (financial, market, operational, legal, reputational, cyber, environmental, personal), linking the response to the risk.

Insurance basics
- [ ] I1 Insurance is a contract in which the insurer pays specified losses in return for a payment.
- [ ] I2 Premium: payment made to the insurer, usually monthly or yearly.
- [ ] I3 Policy: the written contract setting out cover, limits, exclusions and excess.
- [ ] I4 Claim: request to the insurer to pay for an insured loss.
- [ ] I5 Excess: the amount the policyholder pays towards each claim before the insurer pays the rest; a higher excess usually means a lower premium.
- [ ] I6 Insurable risk: accidental, measurable, not certain to happen (fire damage). Uninsurable risk: a loss insurers will not cover (a poor business decision, a fall in demand).
- [ ] I7 Insurance reduces the financial impact but does not prevent the event, and a claim can be refused if a policy condition is breached.

Business insurance products (each with what it covers)
- [ ] P1 Property (buildings, stock, equipment: fire, theft, flood).
- [ ] P2 Public liability (claims by members of the public injured or whose property is damaged).
- [ ] P3 Employer's liability (claims by employees injured or made ill at work); not compulsory in Ireland but widely held (see Traps).
- [ ] P4 Product liability (claims that a product sold caused injury or damage).
- [ ] P5 Professional indemnity (claims that advice or a professional service was negligent).
- [ ] P6 Motor (company vehicles; third-party cover compulsory for vehicles on public roads).
- [ ] P7 Business interruption (lost income and some fixed costs while an insured event halts trading).
- [ ] P8 Cyber (data-breach response, legal costs, restoring systems).
- [ ] P9 Goods in transit (stock lost or damaged moving by road, sea or air).
- [ ] P10 Key-person (pays if a crucial employee dies or cannot work).

Choosing insurance
- [ ] H1 Risk exposure: which risks are most likely or serious for this business.
- [ ] H2 Cost against cover: compare premium with potential loss.
- [ ] H3 Legal requirements: compulsory cover (motor third-party) must be bought.
- [ ] H4 Policy details: limits, exclusions and excess.
- [ ] H5 Review as the business grows, moves or adds products.
- [ ] H6 An insurance broker compares insurers and explains terms.

Ethical, sustainability and digital angle
- [ ] X1 Sustainability: climate-related risk (flood, storm) affects insurance cover and cost; prevention can include flood defences, and a firm may be refused cover or pay more in high-risk locations.
- [ ] X2 Ethics: honest disclosure to the insurer; ethical sourcing as prevention of reputational risk; fair treatment of staff reduces compliance and reputational risk.
- [ ] X3 Digital: cyber security measures and cyber insurance respond to digital risk; backups and cloud services support continuity.

Applying my learning [2.8]

## 3. Traps

- Do not use: "employer's liability insurance ... is compulsory in Ireland" (current notes, fact card "Compulsory insurance in Ireland" and a model answer), because it is wrong: it is not compulsory in Ireland. Compulsory cover to name is motor third-party.
- Do not use: any premium amounts, percentage discounts or fines, because they change and are unchecked.
- Do not use: the six principles of insurance (utmost good faith, insurable interest, indemnity, contribution, subrogation, proximate cause) or averaging/average-clause calculations, because they are old-syllabus only (Appendix A4).
- Do not use: "insurance prevents risk", because insurance only transfers the financial cost.
- Confusable: prevention (lowers likelihood or impact) versus transfer (moves the cost); spread (diversify) versus avoidance (do not do it); retention (accept and pay) versus doing nothing (retention is a considered choice, often with a contingency fund).
- Confusable: premium (what you pay regularly) versus excess (what you pay per claim); public liability (public) versus employer's liability (employees) versus product liability (goods sold) versus professional indemnity (advice).
- Confusable: insurable risk (accidental, measurable) versus a speculative business risk such as falling demand, which is uninsurable.
- Boundary: identifying risks, the five steps of assessment, the risk matrix and the reasons for managing risk belong to the notes on the risks of enterprise; here use them only as assumed knowledge.
- Boundary: consumer insurance rights, consumer protection law and financial-services regulation belong to other chapters; do not teach them here.

## 4. What exams ask

- The 2025 Sample Paper 1 asks students to discuss four risk management strategies. A full answer covers four named strategies (the specification names avoidance, spread and prevention; add transfer or retention), each developed with what the firm does, the effect on the risk, and a limit or cost, applied to the business in the stimulus (Appendix 1: "discuss" is a considered, balanced review with a range of arguments supported by evidence). Common mistakes: naming strategies with no application; describing insurance as the only strategy; no limit or cost for any strategy; four strategies that are really one repeated.
- "Analyse" asks the student to break a strategy down: what it is, why it suits this risk, what it costs, what it cannot do.
- "Explain" or "distinguish" questions on terms (premium and excess; public liability and employer's liability) need both terms defined and the difference made explicit.
- A "choose insurance for this business" question needs risks matched to named products and the factors for choosing (exposure, cost against cover, legal requirements, policy details), justified for the business given. A common mistake is listing products without linking them to the business's risks.
- Wrong claim to avoid in answers: that employer's liability insurance is compulsory in Ireland.
- Include the digital and sustainability angle when the stimulus gives one (a storm claim, a cyber attack): name prevention and transfer together.
