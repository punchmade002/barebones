# Package: ch1-s2 — Stakeholder interaction, conflict and its resolution

- **loId:** `ch1-s2` · **chapterId:** `ch1`
- **Syllabus:** NCCA Leaving Certificate Business specification (first examined June 2027), Strand 1, section 1.1, learning outcomes [1.1.2] and [1.1.3]. Students learn to demonstrate how stakeholders interact and identify potential conflict between stakeholders, and to suggest appropriate ways of avoiding and resolving conflict between them. No Higher Level only content.
- **Prepared by:** a higher model, 2026-11-23. The writer works only from this package and `course-outlines/NOTES_STANDARD.md`.

## 1. Must cover (one bullet or block for every item; tick each off)

Assumed knowledge
- [ ] A1 (assumed) The seven stakeholders (owners, investors, employees, consumers, suppliers, local community, government) and what each needs; managers and interest groups. Do not re-teach in full: one line recalling them.
- [ ] A2 (assumed) Interdependence: stakeholders rely on each other, so a conflict with one has effects on others.

How stakeholders interact
- [ ] R1 Co-operative relationships: interests align (owners and employees both gain when profit and the business grow).
- [ ] R2 Competitive relationships: one group's gain is another's loss (owners want low wages to maximise profit, employees want high pay).
- [ ] R3 Most relationships mix both: the same pair can co-operate on product quality while competing over pay.
- [ ] R4 Recognising the balance shows which relationships to protect and which to manage carefully.

Common conflicts: each with cause, effect and how to avoid it (a three-part block for each)
- [ ] C1 Owners and employees: pay and working conditions. Cause: cost pressure leads to proposed pay cuts or longer hours while staff expect improvements as profits rise. Effect: low morale, absenteeism, industrial action, loss of skilled staff, lower output and service quality. Avoid: fair pay structures, clear communication about performance and profit, regular consultation.
- [ ] C2 Business and community: planning and pollution. Cause: factory expansion increases traffic and noise or raises concern about waste discharged into a local river. Effect: objections to planning applications, protests, reputational damage, possible legal action by residents or authorities. Avoid: consulting residents before applying, publishing environmental plans, investing in local facilities.
- [ ] C3 Corporate social responsibility (CSR): voluntary action beyond legal requirements, such as local sponsorship, reduces friction with the community, but works only when conduct matches stated commitments (keyTerm).
- [ ] C4 Business and consumers: price and quality. Cause: a faulty, misdescribed or overpriced product produces complaints and demands for refunds. Effect: lost customers, negative online reviews, possible action by consumer protection authorities. Avoid: quality control, clear labelling, honest pricing, a complaints process that resolves problems quickly.
- [ ] C5 Transparency about sourcing and pricing protects trust in the brand: an ethical as well as a commercial asset.
- [ ] C6 Business and suppliers: price and payment terms. Cause: late payment or sharp price demands strain the supplier's cash flow. Effect: the supplier refuses future orders, raises prices or withholds stock, disrupting production. Avoid: payment terms agreed in writing (keyTerm Payment terms), regular price reviews, long-term supply partnerships.
- [ ] C7 Business and suppliers: a partnership agreement can set volumes, quality standards and pricing rules that both parties accept in advance.
- [ ] C8 Investors and management: risk and dividends. Cause: managers want to keep profit for expansion, investors want dividends now and may distrust risky projects. Effect: shareholder pressure, calls for changes in management, loss of investor confidence that can lower the share price. Avoid: clear reporting of performance, an agreed dividend policy, open communication about strategy and risk.
- [ ] C9 Business and government: tax and regulation. Cause: a new environmental or employment regulation adds costs the business sees as excessive. Effect: lobbying, delayed investment, penalties or enforcement where rules are broken. Avoid: keeping up to date with law, taking part in public consultations, building compliance into daily operations.
- [ ] C10 The formal legal routes for resolving these disputes are taught later in the course; mention only that they exist (see section 3).

Ways of resolving conflict
- [ ] X1 Negotiation: both sides discuss their positions and aim for an outcome each can accept.
- [ ] X2 Compromise: each side gives up part of its demand, so neither gets everything.
- [ ] X3 Mediation: a neutral third party helps the parties reach their own agreement without deciding the outcome.
- [ ] X4 Partnership agreement: a written arrangement setting shared goals, responsibilities and procedures for settling future disputes.
- [ ] X5 Choosing a method depends on how serious the conflict is and whether the parties still need each other (the notes' own point; keep it).
- [ ] X6 Avoiding conflict as the best option: communication and consultation, CSR, fair contracts, shared goals, transparency (the outline's list; ensure every one appears at least once).
- [ ] X7 Third parties: when direct talks fail, an independent body can help (a mediator, or a state body); the checked examples are in section 2.

Ethical, sustainability and digital angle
- [ ] E1 Ethical: fair pay, honest pricing and transparent sourcing are ethical commitments that prevent conflict; greenwashing (claiming sustainability without conduct to match) damages trust. This is why CSR works only when conduct matches commitments.
- [ ] E2 Sustainability: community and environmental conflicts (pollution, emissions, energy use) are increasingly decided on sustainability grounds.
- [ ] E3 Digital: social media and online reviews escalate conflicts with consumers and communities quickly; digital tools (e-mail updates, online consultation, customer service chat, an online complaints form) help avoid and resolve them.

## 3. Traps

Do not use:
- Do not use: a "partnership agreement" to mean the legal form of a business partnership, because here it is a written agreement between a business and another stakeholder setting shared goals and dispute procedures.
- Do not use: "mediation" and "arbitration" as the same, because the mediator does not decide. Do not teach arbitration: it is not in this subsection.
- Do not use: that the WRC mediator can force a settlement, or that every mediation outcome is legally binding; the checked row says both sides must agree to mediation and the service is voluntary.
- Do not use: the late payment interest rate, compensation amounts, or the euro figures, because they change.
- Do not use: the Labour Court as something that makes binding rulings in an industrial relations dispute; its recommendations are not binding.
- Do not use: the statement that consumer protection authorities take cases on behalf of individual consumers; the CCPC does not.
- Do not use: the court outcome of the Google or Killala cases beyond the checked wording.

Confusable terms
- Conflict (a real clash of interests) vs competition (two firms rivalling for customers): stakeholder conflict is between a business and a stakeholder group.
- Negotiation (the parties talk) vs compromise (the outcome, each gives something) vs mediation (a neutral third party helps).
- Co-operative vs competitive relationship: not good vs bad; both appear in the same relationship.
- CSR (voluntary, beyond legal requirements) vs compliance (what the law requires).

## 4. What exams ask

(Source: SEC 2025 sample papers, Higher Level; no marks or frequencies invented. The outline lists no exam question tied only to this subsection, so conflict and resolution is examined through applied case studies.)
- Identify a potential conflict between two named stakeholders in a case (identify = recognise and state briefly): name the pair and the issue in a sentence (for example owners and employees over pay).
- Explain a conflict: a full answer gives cause and effect (explain = detailed account with reasons or causes). Common mistake: naming the issue without the effect on the business.
- Suggest ways of avoiding and resolving it (suggest = propose solutions): state the method and why it suits this conflict. Distinguish avoiding (before it arises, such as consultation or CSR) from resolving (after, such as negotiation, compromise, mediation).
- Discuss or evaluate (considered, balanced review): weigh two methods against each other (for example negotiation is quick and cheap but may favour the stronger party; mediation helps when the parties still need each other, but needs both to agree). Give three or four developed points, each with its effect on the business or its stakeholders.
- Interaction in a stimulus: explain how stakeholders interact in a news-based case, showing both co-operative and competitive sides. A common mistake is treating all relationships as conflict.
- Applying ethics, sustainability and digital themes: a conflict may be set in an environmental or online-reputation context; link the resolution to transparency or sustainable conduct.
