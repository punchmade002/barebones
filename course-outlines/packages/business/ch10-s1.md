# Package: ch10-s1 — The operational model: key partners, activities and resources

- **loId:** `ch10-s1` · **chapterId:** `ch10`
- **Syllabus:** NCCA *Curriculum Specification for Leaving Certificate Business* (first examined June 2027), Strand 2, section 2.5 "Operations and finance", learning outcome [2.5.1]: students learn about the elements of the operational aspect of the business, including key partners, key activities and key resources, and how they may evolve over time, and should be able to outline the main elements of a business's operational model and explain why they may change.
- **Prepared by:** a higher model, 2026-11-23. The writer works only from this package and `course-outlines/NOTES_STANDARD.md`.

## 1. Must cover (one bullet or block for every item; tick each off)

Background (assumed)
- [ ] A1 (assumed) A business turns inputs (resources) into outputs (a product or service) that customers value; the operational side is how this is organised day to day.
- [ ] A2 (assumed) The business model canvas maps a business model on one page. Its nine blocks are key partners, key activities, key resources, value proposition, customer relationships, channels, customer segments, cost structure and revenue streams. This subsection covers the three operational blocks and their link to the cost structure; the rest of the canvas belongs to the notes on business models.
- [ ] A3 (assumed) A supplier is a business that provides the goods or services another business needs as inputs.
- [ ] A4 (assumed) Fixed and variable costs: an operational choice (buy or outsource, own or lease) changes the cost base. The costs themselves are taught in the notes on costs and revenue.

Core content
- [ ] O1 Definition of the operational model: the resources a business uses, the work it does and the external organisations it relies on to create and deliver its product or service. Link it to the canvas blocks key resources, key activities and key partners, which feed the cost structure.
- [ ] O2 The Appendix 2 definitions of "business model" and "business model canvas" (quoted in section 2), used to place the operational model within the whole business model.
- [ ] R1 Key resources: the assets a business must have to operate. Four types, each defined with at least one example: physical (buildings, vehicles, machinery, stock), human (skills, experience and effort of employees and owners), intellectual (patents, trademarks, brand names, software, know-how), financial (cash, credit, investment).
- [ ] R2 Why resources differ by business: a manufacturer leans on physical resources, a software firm on intellectual and human resources, a start-up on financial resources.
- [ ] K1 Key activities: the core work a business performs to deliver value to customers. Three kinds, each with an example: production (make or assemble), problem-solving (a bespoke solution for an individual customer or client), platform or network (maintaining a system others use, such as an online marketplace matching buyers and sellers).
- [ ] K2 A business can have more than one key activity (for example platform maintenance plus customer support).
- [ ] P1 Key partners: the external organisations a business relies on to operate; they give access to resources or capabilities not held in house.
- [ ] P2 Suppliers: provide raw materials, components or services; the business depends on them for timely, quality inputs.
- [ ] P3 Strategic alliances: cooperative agreements between businesses that remain independent (for example two airlines sharing routes).
- [ ] P4 Outsourcing: paying an external firm to perform an activity (payroll, cleaning, IT support); the type of partner relationship decides how much control the business keeps.
- [ ] M1 (assumed) Production methods shape the resources and activities needed. Job production: one-off items to a customer's specification (custom yacht, tailored suit). Batch production: a group of identical items in one run before switching product (a bakery's batch of loaves then a batch of rolls). Mass production: large volumes of standard items, usually on a continuous line (cars, tinned food).
- [ ] M2 Job production relies on skilled workers and flexible equipment; mass production on specialised machinery and steady, high demand.
- [ ] C1 Why the model changes over time, five drivers each with its effect: growth (job towards batch or mass production as orders become larger and more predictable); technology (automation, cloud software replace manual activity and change which resources matter); cost pressure (outsource activities or move to cheaper suppliers); sustainability goals (change suppliers or processes, for example certified materials or less packaging waste); supply-chain shocks (find a second supplier or hold more stock).
- [ ] E1 Evaluate a change to the model, with its effect on the business and its stakeholders: reliance on a single key partner is a risk (failure or price rise can halt production); outsourcing cuts cost and brings expertise but reduces control over quality and timing; investing in machinery adds capacity but adds fixed costs that sales must cover; the three blocks work together, so a new partner usually changes activities and costs.

Cross-cutting angle
- [ ] X1 Ethical: a business is responsible for how its suppliers and outsourced partners treat workers and the environment; ethical sourcing is a reason to change a supplier; weigh cost saved against reputational risk if a partner behaves badly.
- [ ] X2 Sustainability: the circular economy (definition in section 2) and sustainability goals as a driver of change to resources, suppliers and processes (reuse, waste reduction, certified materials); ESG expectations on reporting.
- [ ] X3 Digital: automation, cloud software, platforms and data as key resources and activities; digital platforms as the key activity of marketplace businesses; digital tools make outsourcing and remote partnerships easier but add dependence on technology partners and cyber risk.

## 3. Traps

- Do not use: "physical evidence" as part of the operational model or as a P, because the specification's 7 Ps are product/service, pricing, promotion, people, packaging, process and place.
- Do not use: ratios, production planning calculations or stock-control methods (just-in-time, economic order quantity) as topics, because the specification does not name them for this outcome. A one-line mention of holding more stock as a response to a supply-chain shock is fine.
- Do not use: total quality management or empowerment as named topics, because they are old-syllabus only.
- Do not use: a named Irish firm as the example of a key partner, alliance or outsourcing arrangement, because no example was checked.
- Confusable: key partners (organisations the business relies on) are not stakeholders in general (owners, employees, customers, community); a customer is on the canvas as a customer segment, not a key partner. A strategic alliance keeps both firms independent; a merger or takeover does not (growth strategies belong to the notes on growth and expansion).
- Confusable: outsourcing (paying another firm to do an activity) is not the same as buying inputs from a supplier (buying materials or components). Leasing (paying rentals to use an asset) is a finance choice (the notes on sources of finance), not an operational partnership.
- Confusable: key resources (what the business has) versus key activities (what it does).
- Boundary: the costs that result (fixed, variable, start-up) and revenue streams belong to the notes on costs and revenue streams. Sources of finance belong to the notes on sources of finance. Forecasting cash belongs to the notes on cash-flow analysis. The wider canvas (value proposition, segments, channels) belongs to the notes on the business model. Do not teach those here beyond the link to the cost structure.
- Boundary: growth strategies (franchising, takeover, joint venture) belong to the notes on adapting and expanding.

## 4. What exams ask

- Appendix 1 verbs for this outcome: **outline** ("give the main points; restrict to essential points of information") and **explain** ("give a detailed account including reasons or causes"). The syllabus wording "outline the main elements ... and explain why they may change" is the likely shape of questions.
- The outline records no 2025 sample-paper question on this subsection. Do not claim one. The SEC style is a short stimulus about a named business, so notes should train students to apply the elements to the firm in the stimulus.
- Outline-type answer: name each element asked for (key resource, activity, partner), give one clear point per element, and tie each to the business in the stimulus. Common mistake: listing all four resource types with no link to the firm, which earns little at this depth.
- Explain-type answer for why the model may change: a driver (growth, technology, cost pressure, sustainability goal, supply-chain shock), then its effect on a resource, activity or partner, then the result for cost or control. Common mistake: naming the driver without the effect.
- Evaluation-style answers: give a benefit and a risk (outsourcing saves cost but loses control) and then a justified judgement for the firm described. Common mistake: one-sided answers.
- Mix-ups to avoid: confusing job, batch and mass production, or confusing a strategic alliance with outsourcing.
- Applying the cross-cutting themes: a good answer may add an ethical sourcing, sustainability or digital point where the stimulus allows it.
