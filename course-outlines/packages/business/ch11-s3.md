# Package: ch11-s3 — Cost-benefit analysis of expansion

- **loId:** `ch11-s3` · **chapterId:** `ch11`
- **Syllabus:** Strand 2, section 6 (Growth, development and expansion), outcome [2.6.5]. Students learn about "the importance of considering both potential benefits and potential costs when making business decisions." They should be able to conduct a cost-benefit analysis to analyse the implications of business expansion. Applied tool: cost-benefit analysis (use the Skills block: purpose, numbered steps, worked example). ## 1. Must cover (one bullet or block for every item; tick each off)

Background (assumed)
- [ ] A1 (assumed) Expansion options (takeover, new site, new product) from the notes on strategies to expand; this subsection weighs one of them.
- [ ] A2 (assumed) Revenue, fixed and variable costs, and net result (revenue minus costs).
- [ ] A3 (assumed) Stakeholders: staff, customers, suppliers, owners, the local community.
- [ ] A4 (assumed) Cash flow: money in and out in a period; costs often come before benefits.

What CBA is
- [ ] W1 Cost-benefit analysis compares the expected costs of a decision with its expected benefits, so the business can judge whether the decision is worth making.
- [ ] W2 It makes the business weigh the downside as well as the upside, guarding against decisions based only on optimism.
- [ ] W3 It can be applied to any major decision (new site, buying a rival, launching a product).
- [ ] W4 Its result depends on forecasts, so it is only as good as the figures entered.

Steps (Skills block, numbered)
- [ ] S1 Identify the decision and the alternatives compared.
- [ ] S2 List all costs and benefits, financial and non-financial, for the firm and for stakeholders (staff, customers, local community).
- [ ] S3 Quantify each item where possible by giving it a money value.
- [ ] S4 Compare total benefits with total costs over the chosen period.
- [ ] S5 Decide, and justify by reference to the comparison and the non-financial factors.

Tangible and intangible items
- [ ] T1 Tangible items are measured in money (sales revenue, equipment cost, rent).
- [ ] T2 Intangible items are real but hard to measure in money (brand reputation, staff morale, customer goodwill).
- [ ] T3 Intangibles should still be listed because leaving them out distorts the decision.
- [ ] T4 Approach: estimate a money value, or record them as qualitative factors in the recommendation.

Opportunity cost and timing
- [ ] O1 Opportunity cost: the value of the next best alternative given up when a choice is made (for example, using the cash to fund marketing instead).
- [ ] O2 Start-up costs such as a fit-out arise before benefits, so timing affects the comparison.
- [ ] O3 Short term (first year's cash position) versus longer term (benefits over several years).

Worked example (sample bakery, invented figures; label every figure "sample")
- [ ] E1 Setting: a bakery with a Limerick shop considers a second shop in Galway; sales held at the same level each year after opening.
- [ ] E2 One-off costs in year 1: fit-out and equipment €180,000; initial marketing €15,000.
- [ ] E3 Annual running costs: rent €36,000; wages €60,000; ingredients and packaging €90,000 (30 per cent of sales).
- [ ] E4 Annual sales €300,000 in each year.
- [ ] E5 Year 1: benefits €300,000; costs €381,000; net minus €81,000. Year 2: benefits €300,000; costs €186,000; net €114,000. Year 3: same as year 2, net €114,000. Three-year total net €147,000 (minus 81,000 + 114,000 + 114,000).
- [ ] E6 Payback: cumulative minus €81,000 at the end of year 1 and plus €33,000 at the end of year 2, so the outlay is recovered in year 2 (at base-case sales).
- [ ] E7 Sensitivity: sales fall 10 per cent to €270,000 a year and ingredients fall to €81,000; the three-year total is €84,000, still positive. (Year 1 net minus €102,000; years 2 and 3 net €93,000 each.) At these lower sales the cumulative position at the end of year 2 is minus €9,000, so payback moves to year 3.
- [ ] E8 Non-financial benefits: new jobs in Galway, a stronger brand presence in the west, management experience for staff.
- [ ] E9 Non-financial costs: owners' management time, which may slow the development of the existing Limerick shop.
- [ ] E10 Recommendation: proceed, because the three-year result stays positive even if sales fall 10 per cent and the outlay is recovered within three years (year 2 at base sales), provided a staffing plan limits disruption to the Limerick shop.

Limitations
- [ ] L1 Depends on forecasts that can be wrong, especially sales at a new location.
- [ ] L2 Intangible effects are hard to value, so the figure may omit what matters most to stakeholders.
- [ ] L3 The time period changes the answer: a short period hides later benefits; a long one relies on distant forecasts.
- [ ] L4 The net gain does not show the risk of failure or the cash needed before benefits arrive.
- [ ] L5 It takes time and money to prepare, which may not be worthwhile for small, low-risk decisions.

Cross-cutting themes
- [ ] X1 Ethical angle: include costs and benefits to wider stakeholders (jobs created or lost, effects on local suppliers and competitors), not only the owners; do not hide a costly effect on others.
- [ ] X2 Sustainability angle: list environmental costs (energy use, transport, waste) and benefits (energy savings, lower carbon) as items, often intangible; a plan that is profitable but damages the environment may carry a reputation cost.
- [ ] X3 Digital angle: spreadsheets and forecasting tools make scenarios and sensitivity tests quick; data-based forecasts still depend on good inputs.

## 3. Traps

- Do not use: "the outlay is recovered in year 2" as a claim about the sensitivity case, because at 10 per cent lower sales the cumulative position is still minus €9,000 at the end of year 2 and payback is in year 3. State year 2 only for the base case.
- Do not use: net present value, discounting, interest rates or ratios, because the specification asks for a cost-benefit analysis, not investment appraisal (and the old syllabus's ratio work is not in this course).
- Do not use: any real firm, share price, takeover premium or regulatory outcome as a worked figure, because none is checked.
- Confusable: opportunity cost is the next best alternative forgone, not a payment made; it is not the same as a cost in the list. Net benefit is benefits minus costs, not profit as taught in the finance chapters (profit uses the firm's accounts, CBA includes stakeholder and intangible items).
- Confusable: payback period (how long to recover the outlay) is part of the worked example, not a separate technique; do not teach it as a stand-alone finance method.
- Confusable: tangible/intangible (can it be measured in money) is not the same as financial/non-financial (in the firm's accounts); an intangible item may still be given a money estimate.
- Belongs to a neighbouring subsection: the strategies themselves (organic and inorganic growth), the sources of finance, Porter's five forces, and adapting the marketing mix. A cost-benefit analysis of a "takeover" only needs the main items here; describe takeover in one line.
- Marketing-mix reminder if used: the 7 Ps are product/service, pricing, promotion, people, packaging, process and place, never "physical evidence".

## 4. What exams ask

- Conduct a cost-benefit analysis of a proposed takeover (2025 Higher Level Sample 2, Question 5(b)), with data given in the stimulus: share price, a premium, profits and regulatory risk. "Conduct" means perform an activity, so a table of costs and benefits is expected. A full answer lists the costs and benefits using the given data (including the premium paid and the regulatory risk), separates tangible from intangible items, totals what can be totalled, compares, and ends with a justified recommendation (proceed, do not proceed, or proceed with conditions). Common mistakes: using data not in the stimulus; listing without a comparison; leaving out non-financial items or wider stakeholders; no recommendation; a recommendation that contradicts the figures.
- Short items: define cost-benefit analysis; distinguish tangible and intangible; explain opportunity cost; outline limitations. Give a one-line example each.
- Discuss or evaluate: give three or four developed points on usefulness against limitations, and a judgement.
- Never invent mark allocations or question wording.
