# Package: ch7-s3 — The feasibility study

- **loId:** `ch7-s3` · **chapterId:** `ch7`
- **Syllabus:** NCCA *Curriculum Specification for Leaving Certificate Business*, Strand 2, section 2.2 "Idea development": the importance of evaluating a business idea to determine its potential future viability [2.2.2], and the outcome "appreciate the importance of conducting a feasibility study to evaluate a business idea" [2.2.3]. Outline scope: what a feasibility study is and when it is done; its parts (market; financial, covering start-up and running costs, projected revenue, break-even as assumed knowledge and cash needs; technical or production; organisational and staffing; legal and regulatory; environmental and ethical); outcomes (proceed, modify, abandon); benefits (reduces risk, supports funding, identifies problems early); limitations (cost, time, forecast uncertainty). This is the last subsection of the chapter, so it also carries the "Applying my learning" rule: one or two current Irish news examples linked to the chapter's topic. Examined first in June 2027. Nothing here is Higher Level only. "Appreciate" (Appendix 1) means recognise the meaning, value or importance of, or have a practical understanding of.
- **Prepared by:** a higher model, 2026-11-23. The writer works only from this package and `course-outlines/NOTES_STANDARD.md`.

## 1. Must cover (one bullet or block for every item; tick each off)

Background (assumed)
- [ ] A1 (assumed) Ideas are generated and screened first (notes on how business ideas are developed), and may be developed with design thinking (notes on design thinking); the feasibility study tests one chosen idea in detail.
- [ ] A2 (assumed) Fixed costs stay the same whatever the output (rent); variable costs change with output (materials). Start-up costs are one-off before trading; running costs recur. Classification is taught in the notes on costs and revenue.
- [ ] A3 (assumed) Break-even (assumed knowledge): the sales level at which total revenue equals total costs; contribution per unit is selling price minus variable cost per unit; break-even in units is fixed costs divided by contribution per unit.
- [ ] A4 (assumed) A cash flow forecast estimates cash coming in and going out over time; profit and cash are not the same thing.

What it is
- [ ] F1 Definition: a detailed assessment of whether a business idea is likely to be viable (survive and earn a sustainable profit over time) before significant money is committed.
- [ ] F2 When: after the idea has passed screening and before the firm invests in launch; also before a major expansion.
- [ ] F3 Output: usually a written report giving the evidence and a recommendation.
- [ ] F4 Feasibility versus screening: screening compares many ideas against criteria; a feasibility study gathers evidence on one idea across each part of the business.

Parts of a study
- [ ] M1 Market: size of the target market, customer needs, competition, likely sales volume and price from research and comparison with similar products; no clear customer group means redesign or abandon.
- [ ] M2 Financial, start-up costs: one-off costs before trading (equipment, fit-out, licences, initial stock).
- [ ] M3 Financial, running costs: recurring costs (wages, rent, utilities, marketing, raw materials).
- [ ] M4 Financial, projected revenue: expected sales volume multiplied by selling price.
- [ ] M5 Financial, break-even (assumed knowledge): used to test whether expected sales can reach the break-even point.
- [ ] M6 Financial, cash needs: a cash flow forecast shows whether bills can be paid in the early low-sales months; links to sources of finance (a later chapter).
- [ ] M7 Technical or production: equipment, technology, processes and capacity to make the product to the required standard.
- [ ] M8 Organisational and staffing: people and management skills the firm has or can hire.
- [ ] M9 Legal and regulatory: licences, permits, safety standards, planning, and data rules where relevant, checked before launch.
- [ ] M10 Environmental and ethical: environmental impact, waste, sourcing, and whether the idea is acceptable to customers and society. Include digital: a digital idea needs checks on data protection, cyber security and the cost of keeping the technology current.

Outcomes
- [ ] O1 Proceed: evidence shows the idea is viable, so move on to launch and the business plan.
- [ ] O2 Modify: potential exists but one part (price, design, scale) must change first.
- [ ] O3 Abandon: not viable; saves resources for better ideas.

Benefits and limitations
- [ ] B1 Reduces risk: finds problems before money is spent.
- [ ] B2 Supports funding: banks, investors and state agencies want evidence; name a checked support in section 2.
- [ ] B3 Identifies problems early (for example an unexpected legal requirement), when they are cheaper to fix.
- [ ] L1 Costs time and money, a burden for a very small firm.
- [ ] L2 Forecasts of sales and costs are uncertain, so a positive study can still prove wrong.
- [ ] L3 Evaluation: the value depends on the quality of the research; test key assumptions with real customers; a study is a guide, not a guarantee.

Applying my learning (end of chapter)
- [ ] N1 One or two checked current Irish news examples linked to idea development (see section 2), each with month, year and source, and a short prompt on what the story shows about idea sources, feasibility or start-up support, including an informed opinion line.
- [ ] N2 Link the news example to a factor from the chapter (technology, finance, competition, ethics and sustainability or timing), so students practise giving a justified view.

## 3. Traps

- Do not use: the Innovation Voucher at the old value of €5,000 or 12 months, because it is out of date.
- Do not use: "the Local Enterprise Office will fund the whole study" or a single national rate for all areas, because the rate depends on the region and covers only part of the cost.
- Do not use: "the Innovation Voucher pays for market research", because standard market research is excluded.
- Do not use: the current note "state agencies" as a vague funder with a named grant not listed above, because only the two schemes above are checked.
- Do not use: the old-syllabus ratio analysis (profitability or liquidity ratios) or balance sheets as parts of a feasibility study, because those are old-syllabus topics and not in this specification.
- Do not use: "a feasibility study guarantees success", because forecasts are estimates.
- Confusable: feasibility study (one idea, detailed, evidence based, ends in proceed, modify or abandon) versus screening (many ideas, criteria). The notes on how business ideas are developed own screening.
- Confusable: feasibility study versus business plan. The study asks whether to start; the plan sets out how the business will operate and be financed. The business plan belongs to the notes on business planning.
- Confusable: start-up costs (one-off, before trading) versus running costs (recurring); fixed and variable costs are a different classification, so wages can be a running cost and also fixed or variable depending on the case.
- Confusable: profit versus cash. A feasible idea can still fail if the early cash flow is negative.
- Belongs to a neighbouring subsection: sources of ideas, screening and the helping and hindering factors belong to the notes on how business ideas are developed; clarify, ideate, develop and implement, prototypes and iteration belong to the notes on design thinking; full cost classification, revenue streams and break-even calculations belong to the notes on costs and revenue; sources of finance and the cash flow forecast belong to the business finance chapter; the business plan and business model canvas belong to business planning.

## 4. What exams ask

- The SEC 2025 sample papers (Higher Level Samples 1 and 2) have no question recorded in the outline on this subsection. Do not claim one. The sample-paper style is a short real-world stimulus (a headline or a firm profile) followed by parts using the command words.
- "Identify" (recognise and state briefly): name parts of a study or the three outcomes; one word or phrase each.
- "Outline" (give the main points): each part with one point on what it checks, for a named business.
- "Explain" (a detailed account with reasons or causes): for each part say what the firm checks and why, and how the result changes the decision (for example the market part finds too few customers, so the firm modifies the price or abandons).
- "Appreciate the importance": state benefits (reduces risk, supports funding, identifies problems early) with the effect on the business, and say what could go wrong without one.
- "Discuss" or "evaluate" (Appendix 1: collect and examine evidence to make judgments; identify the limitations of evidence): three or four developed points mixing benefits and limitations (cost, time, uncertain forecasts), ending with a justified judgement for the business in the question, for example more valuable for a costly idea than for a cheap, easily reversed one.
- Applied: a break-even or projected revenue figure can be given in the stem, so students need the formulas from assumed knowledge; show working and interpret the result (can the firm reach it, what outcome follows).
- Common mistakes: listing the parts with no link to the business in the question; mixing up start-up and running costs; forgetting the environmental and ethical or legal parts; treating the study as a guarantee; stopping at "reduces risk" without saying how; naming a grant with the wrong amount.
- News-based questions: expect students to read a current Irish business headline and give an informed opinion using the chapter's factors; the answer should name the factor, apply it to the story, and give a justified view.
