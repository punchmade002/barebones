# Package: ch8-s2 — Business models and the business model canvas

- **loId:** `ch8-s2` · **chapterId:** `ch8`
- **Syllabus:** Strand 2, section 2.3 (Business planning), [2.3.3] and [2.3.4]. Students learn about the role of business models in generating income and in creating, delivering and capturing value for the enterprise and its customers, the importance of the business model, and the elements of the business model canvas. They should be able to explain what a business model is and its role within the business plan, identify the key elements of the canvas, and outline the role of business models in successful enterprises.
- **Prepared by:** a higher model, 2026-11-23. The writer works only from this package and `course-outlines/NOTES_STANDARD.md`. The NCCA specification is first examined in June 2027; nothing here is Higher Level only. The canvas is an applied tool, so teach it with a "Skills" block (what it is for, numbered steps, a worked example).

## 1. Must cover (one bullet or block for every item; tick each off)

Assumed knowledge
- [ ] A1 (assumed) A business plan is the written document setting out goals, methods and resources (taught in the notes on the business plan). The model sits inside it.
- [ ] A2 (assumed) Revenue is income earned from customers; costs are what the business spends; a business is viable when revenue exceeds costs over time.
- [ ] A3 (assumed) Stakeholders, customers and suppliers: who they are (from the unifying material on stakeholders).
- [ ] A4 (assumed) A value proposition answers "why would a customer choose this?"; customer segment means a distinct group the business serves.

Business model: definition and role
- [ ] G1 Definition (Appendix 2, quoted in Section 2): the model describes how a business operates and creates, delivers and captures value in economic, social and cultural contexts.
- [ ] G2 Value creation: transforming inputs such as materials, skills and knowledge into products or services customers want.
- [ ] G3 Value delivery: getting the product or service to customers through chosen channels and relationships.
- [ ] G4 Value capture: earning income from customers enough to cover costs and leave a profit.
- [ ] G5 The model generates the income that sustains the enterprise; a weak model cannot succeed even when the product is good.
- [ ] G6 Value is created for the enterprise and for its customers (the syllabus words), and also in social and cultural terms (the glossary words).

Role of the model within the business plan
- [ ] P1 The model is the core logic that the business plan turns into operational detail and financial forecasts.
- [ ] P2 The plan explains the model in full; the financial plan tests whether the revenue streams cover the cost structure.
- [ ] P3 The canvas sketches the model on one page, easier to discuss with investors, staff and advisers.
- [ ] P4 Changing one element (for example the sales channel) affects costs, customer relationships and revenue, so the plan must be updated.

The business model canvas
- [ ] C1 Definition (Appendix 2, quoted in Section 2): a tool used to map out a business model.
- [ ] C2 One page, nine linked blocks describing how an enterprise creates, delivers and captures value.
- [ ] C3 Built around the customer and the offer; grouped into the customer side, the infrastructure supporting the offer, and the financial side.
- [ ] C4 Blocks are linked, so a change in one (such as a new supplier) is traced through the others to see its full effect.
- [ ] C5 A starting point for analysis, not a finished plan; revised as the business learns from customers.
- [ ] C6 Skills block: steps to complete a canvas (start with customer segments and value proposition; then channels and relationships; then revenue streams; then key resources, activities and partners; then cost structure; check the financial test; revise).

Nine blocks, each defined with an example
- [ ] B1 Customer segments: the distinct groups of people or organisations served (students, families, small firms).
- [ ] B2 Value proposition: the bundle of products and services that solves a problem or meets a need, and why customers choose it (the glossary: why the customer would choose the product or service); example a fast, cheap commute.
- [ ] B3 Channels: how the business communicates with customers and delivers the offer (shops, website, app, sales team).
- [ ] B4 Customer relationships: the type of relationship kept with each segment (personal advice, self-service, loyalty scheme).
- [ ] B5 Key resources: assets needed to deliver the proposition (premises, staff, technology, patents, capital); a bakery needs its ovens and skilled bakers.
- [ ] B6 Key activities: the most important things the business must do (production, software development, distribution); a courier firm's key activity is route planning.
- [ ] B7 Key partners: suppliers and outside organisations relied on (outsourced providers, alliances); a food brand may rely on a co-packer.
- [ ] B8 Revenue streams: sources of income from each segment (sales, subscriptions, fees, advertising); a gym earns from memberships and personal training.
- [ ] B9 Cost structure: main costs (wages, rent, materials, marketing, finance costs); a shop's costs are dominated by rent and stock.
- [ ] B10 Financial test: a model is sound when revenue streams exceed the cost structure by enough to sustain the business over time.

Worked example (one completed canvas)
- [ ] W1 A completed canvas for one Irish business: Ryanair, in general terms (Section 2), with all nine blocks filled in and linked.

Ethics, sustainability and digital angle
- [ ] E1 A value proposition can include sustainable materials or fair trade sourcing, which some customers pay a premium for.
- [ ] E2 Responsible sourcing adds cost to the cost structure; the model must show customers' willingness to pay covers it.
- [ ] E3 The conduct of key partners affects the reputation of the whole model, so partners are chosen with care.
- [ ] E4 Digital angle: digital channels (website, app) can lower distribution costs and make customer relationships self-service; data about customers can sharpen the value proposition. The canvas can be built and shared in digital tools.

Why a sound model underpins success
- [ ] S1 A business with a good product can still fail if its model does not earn enough to cover costs.
- [ ] S2 A clear model shows where income comes from so managers focus on the revenue streams that matter.
- [ ] S3 A model reliant on one partner or one revenue source is fragile.
- [ ] S4 A model can be tested on a small scale before large sums are spent, reducing the risk of a failed launch.
- [ ] S5 Successful businesses review their models as customers, technology and costs change.

## 3. Traps

- Do not use: figures for Ryanair other than the FY2026 passengers and ancillary revenue above, because other figures change and are not verified.
- Confusable: business model versus business plan. The model explains how the firm makes money and delivers value (one element within the plan); the plan covers the whole business (market, marketing, operations, finance, risk).
- Confusable: value proposition (why a customer chooses the offer) versus product (what is sold). Keep the glossary meaning.
- Confusable: revenue streams (income) versus cost structure (spending); channels (how the offer reaches customers) versus customer relationships (the type of contact kept).
- Confusable: key resources (assets) versus key activities (things the firm does) versus key partners (outside organisations).
- Boundary: the five named business models (retail, manufacturing, subscription, franchise, affiliate) belong to the notes on common business models; marketplace, subscription, crowdfunding and advertising-supported digital models belong to the notes on the digital economy. Mention them here only as "revenue stream" examples (for instance subscriptions).
- Boundary: functions and sections of a business plan and ethics in planning belong to the notes on the business plan. Market research and segmentation techniques belong to the notes on the target market; here a customer segment is simply defined.
- Boundary: a SWOT analysis and the power-interest grid are separate tools; do not mix them into the canvas.

## 4. What exams ask

The outline records no 2025 sample question on this subsection. The points below follow the Appendix 1 verbs and the SEC's applied style, not a counted frequency.
- Explain what a business model is and its role in the business plan. A full answer gives the definition (create, deliver, capture value), says the model is one element within the plan, and gives a reason or effect (the plan turns the model into operations and finance). Common mistake: confusing the model with the whole plan.
- Identify canvas elements. Answer with the block name and a short fact or example for each; a short list is enough. Common mistake: mixing key resources with key activities.
- Outline the role of business models in successful enterprises. Give three or four distinct points (shows where income comes from; avoids reliance on one partner or source; can be tested on a small scale; reviewed as conditions change), each with an effect on the business. Common mistake: only stating "it helps the business succeed".
- Applied use: given a stimulus about a business, complete or fill in blocks of the canvas, or say which block changes when something happens (a new supplier, a move online). A full answer names the block and traces the effect through linked blocks. Common mistake: treating blocks as unrelated.
- Ethics, sustainability and digital angle: say how a sustainable value proposition or responsible partners affect cost structure and reputation, or how digital channels change cost and relationships.
- Tips to build: quote the "create, deliver, capture" idea; always pair a block with its example; check revenue exceeds cost.
