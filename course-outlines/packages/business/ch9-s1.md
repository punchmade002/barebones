# Package: ch9-s1 — Market research and identifying the target market

- **loId:** `ch9-s1` · **chapterId:** `ch9`
- **Syllabus:** NCCA Leaving Certificate Business specification (first examined June 2027), Strand 2, section 2.4 (The target market), learning outcome [2.4.1]. Students learn about the importance of identifying the target market and about conducting market research; they appreciate its importance and discuss how businesses conduct it. Outline scope: purpose of market research; desk (secondary) and field (primary) research with methods (surveys, interviews, focus groups, observation, test marketing, CSO and industry reports, online analytics) and the strengths and weaknesses of each; quantitative and qualitative data; sampling as assumed knowledge; market segmentation (demographic, geographic, psychographic, behavioural) as the way a target market is identified; niche and mass markets; consumer profiles. Glossary terms used: sources of information (Appendix 2). No Higher Level only content.

## 1. Must cover (one bullet or block for every item; tick each off)

Assumed knowledge
- [ ] A1 (assumed) A market is the set of actual and potential buyers of a product or service; customers differ in needs, income and habits, so a business cannot serve everyone equally well.
- [ ] A2 (assumed) A business exists to meet customer needs at a profit; decisions made without evidence carry the risk of a failed product.
- [ ] A3 (assumed) Sampling: a sample is a subset of a population; a representative sample reflects the population's key characteristics (age, location); a small or biased sample (such as only friends) misleads; random sampling gives every member an equal chance of selection.

Purpose and importance
- [ ] P1 Market research is gathering and analysing information about customers, competitors and the market so decisions rest on evidence.
- [ ] P2 It identifies the target market, the group of customers the business most wants to reach.
- [ ] P3 It tests demand for a product or service before large sums are spent developing it.
- [ ] P4 It shows what customers value, what they will pay and where gaps in the market exist.
- [ ] P5 It reduces the risk of launching something customers do not want.
- [ ] P6 Why identifying the target market matters: a narrower group lets the business tailor the product and marketing to customers it understands, and use limited money where it counts.
- [ ] P7 Defining the research question first: a clear, specific question keeps research focused (quote the Appendix 2 "SMART objectives (for research)" definition if the notes mention research objectives).

Sources and types of research
- [ ] D1 Desk (secondary) research: existing data collected by others for another purpose; quicker and cheaper. Quote the Appendix 2 "Sources of information" definition for primary and secondary.
- [ ] D2 Desk sources: Central Statistics Office (CSO) publications, industry reports, trade associations, competitors' published accounts.
- [ ] D3 Online analytics from the business's own website or social media pages: secondary data about existing customers.
- [ ] D4 Desk research strengths: fast, inexpensive, wide area or long period. Weaknesses: may be out of date, may not fit the exact question, rivals can see it too.
- [ ] F1 Field (primary) research: new data collected first-hand for the business's own question.
- [ ] F2 Surveys: questionnaires given to a sample (post, online, phone, in person); usually quantitative.
- [ ] F3 Interviews: one-to-one conversations; detailed insight into motives and attitudes.
- [ ] F4 Focus groups: a small group in a guided discussion; shared views and reactions to a product.
- [ ] F5 Observation: records what customers do, not what they say they do.
- [ ] F6 Test marketing: launch in a limited area to see the response before a full launch.
- [ ] F7 Field research strengths: specific to the question, up to date. Weaknesses: expensive and slower.
- [ ] F8 Why businesses combine desk and field research (desk first to frame the question and save cost, field to fill gaps).

Quantitative and qualitative data
- [ ] Q1 Quantitative data is numerical, counted or measured, and can be statistically analysed to compare groups and spot trends.
- [ ] Q2 Qualitative data is descriptive; it explores opinions, motives and feelings, usually from interviews and focus groups.
- [ ] Q3 Quantitative shows how many hold a view; qualitative explains why. Businesses often use both.
- [ ] Q4 Choosing the method: depends on the question, cost, time, accuracy needed and size of the business.

Identifying the target market
- [ ] T1 The target market is the specific group a business chooses to focus on, found through research and segmentation; narrower than the whole market.
- [ ] T2 Market segmentation divides a market into groups sharing similar characteristics or needs.
- [ ] T3 Demographic segmentation: age, gender, income, occupation, family size.
- [ ] T4 Geographic segmentation: region, town, country, urban or rural.
- [ ] T5 Psychographic segmentation: personality, values, attitudes, lifestyle.
- [ ] T6 Behavioural segmentation: purchase habits, loyalty, usage rate, benefits sought.
- [ ] T7 Niche market: a small, specialised group with distinctive needs. Mass market: a large group with broadly common needs. Advantages and risks of each.
- [ ] T8 A consumer profile sketches a typical customer in the target market (age, lifestyle, needs, buying habits).
- [ ] T9 Segment, then choose one or more segments, then build a profile: the sequence from research to a target market.

Evaluating research
- [ ] V1 Reliability issues: bias in questions, unrepresentative sample, out-of-date data, what people say versus what they do.
- [ ] V2 Cost and time against the value of the decision; a small business may rely on desk research and a short survey.

Ethical, sustainability and digital angle
- [ ] E1 Ethical: respondents must be told how data is used, taking part is voluntary, data is stored securely, and children and vulnerable people need extra care. Personal data is protected by law (see section 2).
- [ ] E2 Digital: online analytics and online surveys are now the cheapest sources of customer data; their full treatment (social listening, big data, AI) belongs to the notes on digital technology in marketing, so here give only the one-line link.
- [ ] E3 Sustainability: research can show whether customers value sustainable products; a biased or misleading survey wastes resources and trust.

## 3. Traps

Do not use:
- Do not use: "a sample must be at least a certain number of people" or any minimum sample size, because no figure is given in the specification and a fixed number is misleading.
- Do not use: any claim that surveys are "always" quantitative or interviews "always" qualitative. Surveys can include open questions and interviews can be structured; say "usually".
- Do not use: Census 2022 figures without the date, because the next census will change them.
- Do not use: any named company as an example of a research method, because none has been checked.

Confusable terms
- Desk (secondary) research vs field (primary) research: existing data from others versus new first-hand data. Online analytics from the firm's own site are classed here as secondary data, as the existing notes do; say so explicitly.
- Quantitative vs qualitative data: numbers (how many) versus words and opinions (why).
- Market segmentation (dividing the market) vs target market (the segment or segments chosen) vs consumer profile (a description of the typical customer in it).
- Niche market vs mass market.
- Sample vs population.
- Market research (about customers and the market) vs market analysis tools such as STEEPLE (external factors); the second belongs to the notes on analytical tools.

## 4. What exams ask

(Source: the outline records no 2025 sample-paper item for this subsection; the questions below follow the Appendix 1 verbs. No marks or frequencies are invented.)
- Identify (recognise and state briefly a distinguishing fact): a likely item asks a student to identify a research method or a type of segmentation from a short stimulus (a business surveying customers, a firm segmenting by age). A full answer names the method or base in one line. Common mistake: naming the wrong class, for example calling an interview secondary research.
- Outline (give the main points): outline how a business can conduct market research. A full answer gives two or three methods, each with a one-line description. Common mistake: listing methods with no description.
- Explain (give a detailed account including reasons or causes): explain why identifying the target market matters. A full answer gives reasons, each with its effect on the business (tailoring the mix, saving money, reducing risk). Common mistake: saying "it helps the business" without a reason.
- Discuss (a considered, balanced review): discuss primary and secondary research, or surveys against focus groups. A full answer gives strengths and weaknesses of each side, ties them to the business in the stimulus and ends with a judgement. Common mistakes: one-sided answers, ignoring the business's size or budget.
- Distinguish (make the differences clear): quantitative from qualitative data, or niche from mass market, with an example of each.
- Appreciate (recognise the importance of): importance of identifying the target market shown through a consequence, such as the cost of a failed launch.
- Common mistakes across the topic: confusing segmentation with the target market, giving definitions with no example from the stimulus, and forgetting that a sample must be representative.
