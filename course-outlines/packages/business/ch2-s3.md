# Package: ch2-s3 — Regulation, governance and ESG

- **loId:** `ch2-s3` · **chapterId:** `ch2`
- **Syllabus:** NCCA Leaving Certificate Business specification (first examined June 2027), Strand 1, section 1.2, learning outcomes [1.2.3] to [1.2.5]. Students learn how organisations are regulated internally and externally, including audit, risk management and compliance; outline the purpose of regulation; explain governance; and outline the three factors in an ESG report. Glossary term: Environmental, Social and Governance (ESG) factors (Appendix 2). Outline Cover: purpose of regulation (protect stakeholders, fair competition, safety, trust); internal regulation (board and directors' duties, internal audit, risk management, compliance function, codes of conduct); external regulation (statutory external audit and regulators); corporate governance (definition, accountability, transparency, board structure); ESG pillars with examples, why reporting matters, and the EU Corporate Sustainability Reporting Directive as transposed in Ireland. The chapter's last subsection also asks for current Irish news examples (the "Applying my learning" outcome [1.6]). No Higher Level only content.
- **Prepared by:** a higher model, 2026-11-23. The writer works only from this package and `course-outlines/NOTES_STANDARD.md`.

## 1. Must cover (one bullet or block for every item; tick each off)

Assumed knowledge
- [ ] A1 (assumed) Stakeholders (shareholders, employees, customers, creditors, the community) and that their interests can conflict with a firm's profit aim. One-line recall.
- [ ] A2 (assumed) Company directors, shareholders and the board; ownership separated from control in a company (taught in the notes on forms of ownership). One-line recall.
- [ ] A3 (assumed) A statute is a law passed by the Oireachtas; a regulation and a directive are EU laws (EU legal forms are taught in their own chapter).
- [ ] A4 (assumed) An audit is an independent check of accounts or controls.

Purpose of regulation [1.2.3]
- [ ] R1 Regulation: rules set by the State or an independent body that control how businesses operate.
- [ ] R2 Protects stakeholders (customers, employees, public) from harm, such as unsafe products or misleading advertising.
- [ ] R3 Promotes fair competition: no advantage from breaking rules, colluding with rivals or abusing a dominant position.
- [ ] R4 Protects safety at work and in the environment, by minimum standards regardless of profit motive.
- [ ] R5 Builds trust in markets, which encourages customers, employees and investors to take part (for example secure customer deposits in banks).

Internal regulation (controls a business sets itself)
- [ ] I1 Board of directors: duty to act in the best interests of the company, with care and skill, and to avoid conflicts of interest.
- [ ] I2 Internal audit: independent review of whether controls are working; reports to the audit committee or board.
- [ ] I3 Risk management: identify, assess and control events that could stop the business meeting its objectives (fraud, a cyber attack, a supply disruption); usually recorded in a risk register.
- [ ] I4 Compliance function: checks the business obeys laws, regulations and its own policies; reports breaches to senior management.
- [ ] I5 Code of conduct for ethical behaviour of employees and directors; protected reporting channels let staff raise concerns.

External regulation
- [ ] X1 Statutory external audit under the Companies Act 2014 for many companies: an independent auditor reports to shareholders whether the accounts give a true and fair view.
- [ ] X2 Central Bank of Ireland: supervises banks, insurers and investment firms; can investigate and penalise.
- [ ] X3 Competition and Consumer Protection Commission (CCPC): enforces competition and consumer law; reviews mergers that could reduce competition.
- [ ] X4 Corporate Enforcement Authority (CEA): enforces company law; investigates companies and directors; can prosecute.
- [ ] X5 Revenue (tax law), Health and Safety Authority (workplace safety), Environmental Protection Agency (environmental law).
- [ ] X6 Data Protection Commission: enforces the GDPR on how personal data is collected, stored and used.
- [ ] X7 Internal versus external regulation compared: who sets it, who enforces it, what a breach costs.

Corporate governance [1.2.4]
- [ ] C1 Definition: the system of rules, practices and processes by which a company is directed and controlled.
- [ ] C2 Accountability: directors answer to shareholders and other stakeholders for decisions and results. Transparency: disclosing accurate information on time.
- [ ] C3 Board structure: executive directors run the business; non-executive directors bring independent scrutiny.
- [ ] C4 Separate roles of chair and chief executive so no one person holds unchecked power.
- [ ] C5 Board committees: an audit committee (financial reporting and internal audit) and a remuneration committee (executive pay).
- [ ] C6 Shareholder rights: vote at general meetings, including on appointing and removing directors.
- [ ] C7 Governance as ethics in practice: it sets the culture that decides whether controls are respected.

ESG [1.2.5]
- [ ] E1 Define ESG using the Appendix 2 wording (section 2), then give the three pillars.
- [ ] E2 Environmental: carbon emissions, energy use, water use, waste, pollution (a food company cutting packaging waste sent to landfill).
- [ ] E3 Social: employees' health and safety, pay and diversity, fair treatment of customers and suppliers, effect on the local community.
- [ ] E4 Governance: board structure, executive pay, anti-bribery controls, tax transparency, shareholder rights.
- [ ] E5 Why ESG matters: environmental and social harm can cost customers, staff and finance, so managing ESG risk is practical sustainability.
- [ ] E6 ESG reporting: a disclosure of ESG performance, usually with the financial results; used by investors and lenders to judge long-term risk; builds stakeholder trust; can help win contracts from customers who set sustainability standards.
- [ ] E7 The EU Corporate Sustainability Reporting Directive (CSRD), as transposed in Ireland, with its independent assurance (use the checked rows and the Traps).

Evaluation
- [ ] V1 Benefits: protection, trust, a level playing field.
- [ ] V2 Costs: compliance expense and staff time, heavier on small and medium enterprises.
- [ ] V3 Over-regulation can slow innovation and deter entrepreneurs; under-regulation allows fraud, unsafe practices and environmental damage.
- [ ] V4 Works best when enforced consistently and when firms go beyond the legal minimum.

Ethical, sustainability and digital angle
- [ ] Y1 Ethics: codes of conduct and protected reporting channels; accountable directors; fair pay and treatment; honest disclosure (greenwashing is the ethical failure of ESG reporting: claiming better environmental performance than the evidence supports).
- [ ] Y2 Sustainability: the E pillar and CSRD reporting; managing ESG risk as business sustainability.
- [ ] Y3 Digital: cyber attack as a risk in the risk register; data protection law (GDPR) as external regulation of digital activity; electronic reporting and digital audit trails. State generally.

Current Irish news (Applying my learning)
- [ ] N1 One or two news examples from section 2, each linked to a topic above (regulation, internal controls, governance, ownership), with month and year and source. State what happened, which rule or control it shows, and one effect on a stakeholder.

## 3. Traps

- Do not use: "the CSRD requires larger companies to publish sustainability reports" as the current scope, because the EU narrowed it in 2026 (Omnibus I) and timings changed (stop-the-clock 2025); say the Directive requires reports from the companies it covers, and that scope and dates have changed since 2024.
- Do not use: any CSRD size threshold (employees, turnover) or reporting year, because they were being revised and the thresholds found were not from a primary source.
- Do not use: "Reports under the Directive must be checked by an independent assurance provider" without the qualifier that assurance is a requirement of the Directive's reporting regime (the Department says reports are subject to assurance); do not name the level of assurance.
- Do not use: GDPR or the employment directives as the specification's chosen EU regulation or directive; the CSRD is a candidate and belongs to the EU chapter too, so keep its description accurate and refer to other notes by title only.
- Do not use: Companies Act 2014 section numbers, audit-exemption thresholds or director-duty lists beyond the three duties in the current notes, because they were not checked.
- Do not use: any named company's ESG score or report, because none was checked.
- Do not use: the old 1996 syllabus content on final accounts, ratios and auditing as a topic (Appendix A4).
- Confusable: internal audit (inside the firm, reports to the board or audit committee) versus statutory external audit (independent auditor reports to shareholders on the accounts).
- Confusable: regulation (rules imposed by law or a regulator) versus governance (how a company directs and controls itself) versus compliance (obeying the rules). Governance is wider than compliance.
- Confusable: "G" in ESG (governance factors reported on) versus corporate governance (the system itself).
- Confusable: non-executive directors (independent, not in daily management) versus executive directors (run the business).
- Belongs to a neighbouring subsection: types of enterprise and who owns them (the notes on public, private, semi-state and not-for-profit enterprises); forms of ownership, flotation, privatisation and nationalisation (the notes on forms of ownership and how ownership changes); consumer protection law, employee law and the detail of GDPR (later chapters on consumers, employees and the EU).

## 4. What exams ask

Appendix 1 verbs for [1.2.3] to [1.2.5]: outline (main points only), explain (reasons or causes), identify. The 2025 sample papers test this subsection in Sample 1, Question 3(a): identify the three elements of an ESG report. No other question is recorded, so the rest is drawn from the verbs.

- Identify the three elements of an ESG report: environmental, social, governance, written as three short labels. A full answer may add one example of each. Common mistake: writing "economic" or "sustainable" as the third factor, or "governance" with no meaning.
- Outline the purpose of regulation: give the main points only: protect stakeholders, fair competition, safety, trust, each in a few words. Common mistake: a long explanation of one point.
- Explain how an organisation is regulated internally or externally: name the control (internal audit, risk management, compliance function, code of conduct; or statutory audit and a named regulator), say what it does, and give the reason it protects stakeholders. Common mistake: naming a regulator with no explanation, or mixing up internal and external.
- Explain governance: define it, then give accountability and transparency with an effect (for example, separate chair and chief executive prevent unchecked power). Common mistake: confusing governance with management.
- Explain why a business reports on ESG: link a reason (investors judge long-term risk, trust, winning contracts) to its effect on a named stakeholder. Common mistake: describing the pillars without saying why reporting matters.
- Applied or news-based question: use a short stimulus (a headline or fine) to name the rule or control involved and its effect on stakeholders. A full answer uses the facts given in the stimulus. Common mistake: general comments about regulation with no link to the case.
- Evaluate regulation: a benefit and a cost, for example the cost on small firms, and a justified conclusion. Common mistake: one-sided answers.
