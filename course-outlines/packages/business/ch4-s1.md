# Package: ch4-s1 — Irish government policy, legislation and how business influences policy

- **loId:** `ch4-s1` · **chapterId:** `ch4`
- **Syllabus:** NCCA Leaving Certificate Business specification (first examined June 2027), Strand 1, section 1.4, learning outcomes [1.4.1]–[1.4.3]. Students learn about how Irish government policy impacts across different sectors, how policy and legislation differ, and how business stakeholders inform and influence policy (interest groups and lobbying). They should be able to outline three Irish government policies that affect three different sectors, explain the difference between policy and legislation, and outline business's role in national policy. Glossary terms: Irish government policy; sector of the economy (in Ireland).
- **Prepared by:** a higher model, 2026-11-23. The writer works only from this package and `course-outlines/NOTES_STANDARD.md`.

## 1. Must cover (one bullet or block for every item; tick each off)

Definitions
- [ ] P1 Irish government policy, defined as in the specification glossary (see section 2): actions or statements of guidance adopted by the government and used as a basis for decision-making. Also the existing keyTerm: a plan of action that sets goals and the means of reaching them.
- [ ] P2 Legislation: law passed by the Oireachtas that binds individuals, businesses and public bodies (keyTerm). A Bill becomes an Act after passing both Houses (Dáil and Seanad) and being signed by the President (assumed knowledge, kept from the current notes).
- [ ] P3 The difference between policy and legislation: a statement of intent with no legal force of its own vs a law that must be obeyed and can be enforced; policy can change quickly with a government, legislation stays until amended or repealed.
- [ ] P4 Statutory instruments (keyTerm): secondary legislation a minister makes under an Act to fill in detail. Use a generic example only.
- [ ] P5 How policy becomes law: a goal can be written into an Act that sets binding targets (the Climate Action plan and its Act is the worked case).
- [ ] P6 (assumed) The Oireachtas is the national parliament: the Dáil, the Seanad and the President.
- [ ] P7 (assumed) The Department that leads on a policy, and the government as the body that adopts it.

Sectors of the economy (glossary term)
- [ ] T1 Sector of the economy (in Ireland), defined as in the glossary (section 2); the CSO publishes the list.
- [ ] T2 Sectors the notes can use as plain categories: agriculture and agri-food, industry/manufacturing, construction, energy, transport, retail and wholesale, tourism and hospitality.
- [ ] T3 One policy can reach several sectors, and a sector is affected by several policies.

Policies and their sectors (at least four; the student picks three for three different sectors)
- [ ] G1 Climate Action Plan: sectors energy/electricity, transport, buildings, industry, agriculture; aim and effect on business; the Act behind it.
- [ ] G2 Carbon tax: charge on fossil fuels set to rise on a schedule fixed in law; effect on transport firms and on investment in efficiency (no amounts).
- [ ] G3 Housing: the construction sector; the plan for housing supply and its effect on builders, developers and suppliers.
- [ ] G4 National Development Plan: public capital investment (roads, public transport, water, housing, energy); effect on engineering and construction firms.
- [ ] G5 Food Vision 2030: agri-food sector, the aims and effect on farmers and food processors.
- [ ] G6 National Minimum Wage: labour cost in low-paid sectors such as hospitality and retail; set under the National Minimum Wage Act 2000.
- [ ] G7 Policy effects are two-sided (cost and opportunity): worked example of a haulier facing higher fuel cost and a reason to buy electric vans.
- [ ] G8 Tourism or digital strategy: the outline suggests one. No checked example. Do not name one.
- [ ] G9 Ethical, sustainability and digital angle: climate policy and carbon tax push firms to cut emissions and report (sustainability); a living-wage-type floor such as the minimum wage is an ethical fairness measure for workers; digital policy has no checked example, so make the digital angle the use of online registers and public consultations (the Register of Lobbying is searchable online) and say a policy can require firms to adopt low-carbon technology.

How business influences policy [1.4.3]
- [ ] H1 Representative bodies speak for groups of firms: Ibec (employers), ISME (small and medium firms), Chambers Ireland (local business communities).
- [ ] H2 Sector bodies lobby on issues specific to an industry (no named body; see section 3).
- [ ] H3 Trade unions represent workers and take part in consultation on pay and employment law (no named body; see section 3).
- [ ] H4 Interest group (keyTerm): an organised body that represents the shared views of a group of people or businesses to government.
- [ ] H5 Public consultations and written submissions on draft policy.
- [ ] H6 Pre-Budget submissions to the Department of Finance and to Oireachtas committees.
- [ ] H7 Lobbying (keyTerm): communicating with politicians and public officials in order to influence a decision; a normal part of the policy process.
- [ ] H8 Social dialogue (keyTerm): continuing discussion between government, employers and trade unions on economic and social matters; less formal than the national partnership agreements of the past.
- [ ] H9 Business's role in national policy in one line: it informs policy with practical knowledge and influences it with organised pressure.

Regulation of lobbying
- [ ] L1 Regulation of Lobbying Act 2015: lobbyists must register and file returns on the Register of Lobbying.
- [ ] L2 The Register is kept by the Standards in Public Office Commission and is public.
- [ ] L3 The Act covers contact with designated public officials (ministers, senior officials); a public body publishes its list of them.
- [ ] L4 Transparency: citizens, journalists and rival interests can see who is trying to influence a decision.
- [ ] L5 Breaches are offences; a business that lobbies must know whether it has to register.
- [ ] L6 Register of Lobbying (keyTerm): public record of registered lobbyists and their lobbying activity.

Evaluating influence
- [ ] E1 Advantage: practical knowledge of markets, costs and jobs makes policy workable.
- [ ] E2 Advantage: consultation gives government evidence, improving legislation.
- [ ] E3 Disadvantage: large firms with lobbying staff gain more access than small firms.
- [ ] E4 Disadvantage: consumers, workers and the environment can be under-represented.
- [ ] E5 Interest groups compete, so a proposal can face pressure from employers, unions and environmental groups at once.

## 3. Traps

- Do not use: Housing for All as the current housing plan, because the Government replaced it in November 2025. The notes may say it was the plan from 2021 that the new plan succeeded.
- Do not use: any carbon tax rate, minimum wage rate beyond the dated one in G6, or National Development Plan sector amounts, because they change or sources disagree.
- Do not use: "a plan updated each year" as a description of the Climate Action Plan without saying it is the Act that requires the update, because the plan is a policy and the annual update is a legal duty.
- Do not use: the Register of Lobbying as "run by the Dáil" or as listing donations to parties, because it lists lobbying activity only and SIPO runs it.
- Confusable: policy vs legislation vs regulation. A regulation (EU) is law; "regulation" in the Act's title means control. Keep EU regulations for the notes on EU law.
- Confusable: lobbying vs bribery or corruption. Lobbying is legal and a recognised part of policy-making; the Act exists to make it visible.
- Confusable: interest group vs pressure on a single firm; an interest group represents many.
- Belongs to a neighbouring subsection: the EU institutions and EU law types (the notes on EU decision-makers), and evaluating a named EU regulation and directive (the notes on evaluating one regulation and one directive). Do not explain EU directives here. Consumer and employee legislation belongs to later chapters on consumer and employee protection.
- Belongs to a neighbouring chapter: trading blocs and trade (international business chapter).

## 4. What exams ask

- Fill-in-the-blank: the 2025 Sample 1 paper has a gap whose answer is that interest groups engage in lobbying. A tip: learn the keyTerms word for word (lobbying, interest group, policy, legislation).
- "Identify" (Appendix 1: recognise and state briefly a distinguishing fact): name a policy, its sector, a body that lobbies. Give the name and the sector in a few words.
- "Outline" (give the main points; restrict to essential points): outline three Irish government policies affecting three different sectors [1.4.1]. A full answer gives three different sectors, one named policy each and one sentence on the effect on business each. Common mistake: two policies that hit the same sector, or a policy named with no effect.
- "Distinguish" (make the differences clear) policy and legislation: a full answer gives at least two differences (legal force, who makes it, how easily changed) and an example of each. Common mistake: saying policy is "not important" or that legislation is a type of policy.
- "Outline" business's role in national policy: representative bodies, consultations and submissions, lobbying, social dialogue, the lobbying register, each with how it works.
- "Explain" how a policy affects a sector: give the policy, the sector, the cause (what changes for firms) and the consequence (cost or opportunity). Common mistake: effects on households only.
- "Discuss" or "evaluate" whether business should influence policy: balanced answer with advantages (evidence, workable policy) and disadvantages (unequal access, under-represented groups), then a judgement. Common mistake: a one-sided list or no conclusion.
- Applying to unseen stimulus: a news headline about a new policy; identify the policy, the sector, who gains and who loses, and one ethical or sustainability point.
