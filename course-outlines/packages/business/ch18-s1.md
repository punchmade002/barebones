# Package: ch18-s1 — Saving, investing and borrowing

- **loId:** `ch18-s1` · **chapterId:** `ch18`
- **Syllabus:** NCCA Leaving Certificate Business specification (first examined June 2027), Strand 4, section 4.2, learning outcome [4.2.1]. Students learn about informed decision-making about saving and investing, considering inflation, risk, interest rates, taxes and charges, the time frame (short, medium and long term) and return on investment; they should be able to examine the factors involved in saving, investing and borrowing. Nothing here is Higher Level only.
- **Prepared by:** a higher model, 2026-11-23. The writer works only from this package and `course-outlines/NOTES_STANDARD.md`.

## 1. Must cover (one bullet or block for every item; tick each off)

Assumed knowledge
- [ ] A1 (assumed) A consumer is an individual household making financial choices; a lender (bank, credit union, other regulated lender) and a saver or investor are the two sides of most financial products.
- [ ] A2 (assumed) Interest is the price of using money: earned by savers, paid by borrowers. Simple vs compound interest: compounding means interest earns interest, so growth speeds up over time.
- [ ] A3 (assumed) Inflation is a general and sustained rise in prices that reduces what each unit of money can buy.
- [ ] A4 (assumed) The AER and APR as the two standard annual rates for comparing (AER for savings, APR for borrowing). Explain what each includes, not how to calculate them.

Core ideas
- [ ] S1 Saving: setting money aside in a low-risk, accessible account to keep it safe and earn a modest return in interest.
- [ ] S2 Investing: putting money into assets such as shares or investment funds in the hope of growth, accepting that the value can fall.
- [ ] S3 Borrowing: receiving money now and agreeing to repay more later, because the lender charges interest and often fees.
- [ ] S4 The three compared (risk, purpose, direction the money flows): a saver lends money to a bank, an investor buys a stake in an asset, a borrower receives money and owes it back.

The factors a consumer weighs [4.2.1]
- [ ] F1 Inflation: a return that does not beat inflation leaves the saver with less buying power.
- [ ] F2 Risk: the chance money put aside falls in value or is lost; low for deposit accounts, higher for shares. Higher return usually means higher risk.
- [ ] F3 Interest rates: they change savings returns and borrowing costs; variable-rate products move with rates, fixed-rate products do not for the fixed period.
- [ ] F4 Taxes on returns: deposit interest is taxed (DIRT, deducted at source by the bank), many investment funds pay exit tax, profits on shares can attract capital gains tax. Explain how they reduce the net return; give no rates.
- [ ] F5 Charges: account fees, management fees, arrangement fees, early repayment or exit charges.
- [ ] F6 Time frame: short term (money needed soon, keep it accessible), medium term, long term (can ride out market falls and benefit from compounding). Match the product to when the money is needed.
- [ ] F7 Return on investment (ROI): the gain from an investment relative to its cost, shown as a percentage. Give the formula: (gain divided by cost) multiplied by 100.
- [ ] F8 Liquidity and access (how quickly money can be withdrawn without penalty) as the link between time frame and product choice.

Nominal and real return
- [ ] N1 Nominal return: the headline growth rate before inflation.
- [ ] N2 Real return: the growth rate after inflation is deducted; shows the change in purchasing power. Working rule: real return is roughly nominal return minus inflation.
- [ ] N3 When inflation is higher than the nominal return the real return is negative.
- [ ] N4 Comparing nominal returns alone can mislead; a higher headline rate can still give a lower real return.

AER and APR
- [ ] R1 AER: the yearly interest rate on a savings product including the effect of compounding, so products paying interest at different intervals can be compared.
- [ ] R2 APR: the yearly cost of borrowing as a percentage, including interest and compulsory charges.
- [ ] R3 A low headline rate with big fees can have a higher APR than a rival loan; lenders must show the APR before the agreement is signed so offers compare on the same basis.

Common products
- [ ] P1 Saving and investing: deposit accounts (instant access and notice accounts, low interest), State Savings (NTMA; backed by the State), shares (dividends and price rises; real risk of loss), investment funds (pool money, spread risk), pensions (tax relief on contributions, restricted access before retirement age).
- [ ] P2 Borrowing: overdraft (current account below zero up to an agreed limit; expensive over a long period), credit card (revolving limit; interest on uncleared balances, usually high), personal loan (fixed sum, regular instalments, agreed term), mortgage (long-term loan secured on a property; lender can repossess), buy-now-pay-later (instalments at checkout; may be interest-free if paid on time, missed payments and fees build up), credit union loan (member-owned lender, often competitively priced).
- [ ] P3 Fixed vs variable rate on a loan; the secured vs unsecured distinction (mortgage is secured on the property, personal loan usually unsecured).

Worked comparison
- [ ] W1 A step-by-step method: state the purpose and time frame; compare AER (savings) or APR (loans); check exit terms and charges; compare total cost over the full term, not the headline rate; test affordability if income falls or rates rise.
- [ ] W2 One worked example for two savings options (real return) and one for two loans (APR, fees and total cost), using the labelled sample figures in section 2.

Ethics, sustainability, digital
- [ ] E1 Ethical angle: responsible lending (lenders must assess whether the borrower can afford to repay) and responsible borrowing; the ethics of easy credit, high-cost credit and BNPL aimed at young people.
- [ ] E2 Sustainability angle: ethical and sustainable (green) investment funds let investors consider environmental and social impact as well as return; the trade-off is that "sustainable" claims need checking. Name no fund.
- [ ] E3 Digital angle: apps, online comparison sites and instant online credit make saving and borrowing quicker and more visible, but also make impulsive borrowing and BNPL use easier; products are increasingly bought without face-to-face advice.

## 3. Traps

- Do not use: any figure for the DIRT rate, the exit tax rate, the capital gains tax rate, any interest or inflation rate, State Savings rates or prize-bond prizes, because they change (sources I checked conflict on the exit tax rate) and are not verified for the exam year. Explain how each tax works instead.
- Do not use: the claim that credit unions or State Savings are "guaranteed up to a figure" in these notes, because the deposit guarantee scheme is dealt with in the notes on regulation and protection, and it covers deposits in authorised banks, building societies and credit unions, not shares or investment funds.
- Do not use: any statement that Ireland has fully transposed the second Consumer Credit Directive, because transposition status was not confirmed.
- Do not use: the word "yield", "gilt", "bond ladder" or any product jargon not in the notes, because it is outside the specification.
- Confusable: saving vs investing (capital is protected vs capital is at risk). Nominal vs real return. AER (savings, includes compounding) vs APR (borrowing, includes compulsory charges); the headline or "flat" rate is neither.
- Confusable: interest rate vs APR; fixed vs variable rate; secured vs unsecured loan; credit card (revolving) vs personal loan (fixed term).
- Boundary: risk tolerance, the types of risk (mis-selling, hidden charges, over-indebtedness), reliable information, switching and credit ratings belong to the neighbouring subsection on risk and credit ratings. Here, risk is only one factor in choosing a product.
- Boundary: how fintech, apps and digital currency change finance belongs to the subsection on fintech and digital currency; regulation, the Central Bank's role, the deposit guarantee scheme and fraud belong to the subsection on regulation and fraud. Mention BNPL here only as a borrowing product.
- Boundary: this is personal finance (the consumer). Sources of finance for a business (overdraft, term loan, equity) belong to the business finance chapters.
- Do not teach calculations of AER or APR; students need what they show and how to use them.

## 4. What exams ask

- Appendix 1 verbs: **Explain**: "give a detailed account including reasons or causes". **Outline**: "give the main points; restrict to essential points of information". **Discuss**: "offer a considered, balanced review that includes a range of arguments, factors or hypotheses and is supported by appropriate evidence". **Examine**: "look closely at arguments, data, information, concepts and/or stories in order to uncover origins, assumptions, perspectives, trends and/or relationships". **Compare**: "give an account of the similarities and (or) differences between two (or more) items or situations, referring to both (all) of them throughout".
- Seen on a 2025 sample paper (Sample 2): explain three factors a consumer should consider before borrowing. A full answer names three distinct factors (for example the APR including charges, whether the rate is fixed or variable, and affordability if income falls or rates rise), and for each gives the reason and the effect on the borrower. Common mistakes: listing factors with no explanation; giving three versions of "interest rate"; naming the product but not the factor; quoting a rate from memory.
- Likely related asks (stated by the specification, no past frequency known): examine factors in saving and investing (inflation, risk, interest rates, taxes and charges, time frame, ROI), with the consequence for the consumer. A full answer links each factor to a decision ("because inflation is higher than the nominal return, the real return is negative, so the saver should look for a higher return").
- Tip to build in: always say what a rate includes (AER includes compounding, APR includes compulsory charges) and compare on that basis, never the headline rate.
- Tip: when told to compare two options, refer to both throughout and end with a judgement tied to the person's purpose and time frame.
- Common mistakes: confusing nominal and real return; treating every investment as safe; ignoring tax and charges; forgetting that borrowing has to be affordable.
