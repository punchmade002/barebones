# Package: ch2-s1 — Public, private, semi-state and not-for-profit enterprises

- **loId:** `ch2-s1` · **chapterId:** `ch2`
- **Syllabus:** NCCA Leaving Certificate Business specification (first examined June 2027), Strand 1, section 1.2, learning outcome [1.2.1]. Students distinguish between public, private and not-for-profit enterprises (the "learn about" adds semi-state) and analyse the contribution of each locally and nationally. Glossary term: not for profit enterprises (Appendix 2). Outline Cover: definitions and aims of each type; examples; charities, social enterprises and NGOs; each type's contribution (jobs, services, infrastructure, community, social goals) locally and nationally; how each is funded and held to account. No Higher Level only content.
- **Prepared by:** a higher model, 2026-11-23. The writer works only from this package and `course-outlines/NOTES_STANDARD.md`.

## 1. Must cover (one bullet or block for every item; tick each off)

Assumed knowledge
- [ ] A1 (assumed) A business is an organisation that produces goods or services; what differs between types is who owns it, its main aim and where its money comes from. One-line recall.
- [ ] A2 (assumed) Stakeholders (owners, employees, customers, the community, government) and that different enterprise types answer to different stakeholders.
- [ ] A3 (assumed) Profit, revenue and surplus: profit is what is left after costs; a not-for-profit body calls it a surplus and reinvests it.
- [ ] A4 (assumed) The State: the Government, the Oireachtas and local authorities, funded mainly by taxation.

The four types: definitions and aims
- [ ] T1 Private enterprise: owned by individuals or shareholders; main aim is profit for owners.
- [ ] T2 Public enterprise: owned and run by the State or a local authority; aim is to deliver services for the whole community, not profit.
- [ ] T3 Semi-state enterprise: State-owned commercial body that sells goods or services in the market and operates at arm's length from government; must balance a public service role with covering costs.
- [ ] T4 Not-for-profit enterprise: pursues a social, charitable or community purpose; surplus reinvested, not paid to owners. Quote the Appendix 2 definition (section 2).
- [ ] T5 A short comparison of the four on owner, aim, funding and who they answer to (a table-style block is fine).

Examples and sub-types
- [ ] E1 Public: government departments, local authorities, the HSE (use the checked rows).
- [ ] E2 Semi-state: ESB, An Post, Coillte (use the checked rows).
- [ ] E3 Private: range from sole traders to listed multinational companies; funded by owners, banks, investors, retained profit.
- [ ] E4 Not-for-profit: charities (regulated by the Charities Regulator; must serve a charitable purpose of public benefit), social enterprises (trade to pursue a social aim and reinvest most surplus), NGOs (non-governmental organisations working on development, humanitarian or social problems).
- [ ] E5 Not-for-profit does not mean unpaid: these bodies employ staff and compete for funding, contracts and skilled people.
- [ ] E6 Semi-state bodies can compete with private firms, which pushes them to improve efficiency; a surplus can be reinvested or paid to the State as a dividend.

Contribution, locally and nationally (analyse)
- [ ] C1 Private: most private-sector employment, tax revenue (corporation tax, income tax, VAT), exports, innovation.
- [ ] C2 Public: essential services and infrastructure every business and community depends on; jobs spread across every county.
- [ ] C3 Semi-state: national infrastructure (electricity grid, postal network, forestry) that underpins business activity.
- [ ] C4 Not-for-profit: local community support (a community centre, a care service, a training project run by a social enterprise); social goals; entrepreneurial thinking with an ethical purpose.
- [ ] C5 Local effect: in a town the largest employer may be a public body, a private firm or a co-operative; that mix affects local spending and resilience to shocks.

Funding and accountability
- [ ] F1 Public: funded mainly from taxation and State grants; accountable to the Oireachtas and the public.
- [ ] F2 Public bodies' spending is audited by the Comptroller and Auditor General and examined by the Public Accounts Committee.
- [ ] F3 Semi-state: earns revenue from sales; answers to a sponsoring government department; publishes annual reports.
- [ ] F4 Private: funded by owners, shareholders, lenders and trading income; accountable mainly to shareholders and creditors.
- [ ] F5 Not-for-profit: donations, grants and fundraising; accountable to donors, funders and regulators such as the Charities Regulator.

Strengths and limits
- [ ] S1 Private: responsive and efficient because owners bear losses; may neglect services that make no profit.
- [ ] S2 Public: access for all regardless of ability to pay; can lack competitive pressure and face political interference.
- [ ] S3 Semi-state: State control plus commercial discipline; conflicting goals of profit and public service.
- [ ] S4 Not-for-profit: social goals and public trust; funding from donations and grants can be unpredictable.

Ethical, sustainability and digital angle
- [ ] X1 Ethics: public and not-for-profit bodies are judged on social outcomes and fairness of access; private firms face the question of profit versus the interests of other stakeholders. Charities depend on public trust, so honest use of donations is an ethical duty.
- [ ] X2 Sustainability: semi-state bodies hold the infrastructure (energy, forestry) that the shift to low-carbon activity depends on; state the point generally, with no figures or targets.
- [ ] X3 Digital: public services and charities increasingly deliver and fund themselves online (online services, online donations). One general sentence with no named platform.

Glossary
- [ ] G1 Define "not for profit enterprises" using the Appendix 2 wording, then link it to charities, social enterprises and NGOs.

## 3. Traps

- Do not use: "The HSE is not expected to make a profit" as a fact about its funding source, because only its public-service aim is safe; say its aim is service, not profit.
- Do not use: any percentage State shareholding in ESB, An Post or Coillte, because holdings change and only a 2021 figure was found.
- Do not use: "the ESB is a public enterprise", because in this subsection the ESB is a semi-state commercial body; a public enterprise is a department, local authority or the HSE, funded mainly by tax.
- Confusable: public versus semi-state. A public enterprise is funded mainly by taxation and exists to provide a service; a semi-state body earns its revenue from selling to customers and is expected to cover its costs. "Public sector" is the broad term covering both.
- Confusable: not-for-profit does not mean "no surplus". A not-for-profit body may make a surplus; it reinvests it instead of paying owners.
- Confusable: charity, social enterprise and NGO overlap. A charity is defined by its public-benefit purpose and registration; a social enterprise by trading for a social aim; an NGO by being independent of government. One body can be more than one.
- Confusable: "private enterprise" here means a commercial business owned by individuals or shareholders, not only a small firm.
- Do not use: privatisation or nationalisation examples here, because they belong to the notes on forms of ownership and how ownership changes.
- Do not use: the legal forms of private business (sole trader, partnership, Ltd, plc, franchise, co-operative), because they belong to the notes on forms of ownership.
- Do not use: regulators and corporate governance (Central Bank, CCPC, audit, ESG), because they belong to the notes on regulation, governance and ESG.
- Do not teach the old 1996 syllabus categories of industry or community development as units (Appendix A4).

## 4. What exams ask

The specification's command words are in Appendix 1: identify, distinguish, outline, explain, analyse, discuss, evaluate. The 2025 sample papers have no question aimed at this subsection (the outline records no Exam line for it), so these items come from the verbs in [1.2.1], not from a past question.

- Distinguish (make the differences clear): give a difference on each of owner, aim, funding and accountability, and name the type each point belongs to. Common mistake: describing each type separately and never stating the difference.
- Identify or outline: name the type and give one or two essential points, with a named example from the checked list. Common mistake: listing examples with no reason they fit the type.
- Analyse the contribution of each type locally and nationally: break it into jobs, services, infrastructure, tax and community, say whether the effect is local or national, and reach a conclusion. A full answer covers at least three of the four types and keeps the local and national points distinct. Common mistake: only national effects, or treating "contribution" as profit alone.
- Explain how an enterprise is funded and held to account: cause and reason, for example "funded by taxation, so it answers to the Oireachtas". Common mistake: swapping the funding of semi-state and public bodies.
- Discuss or evaluate (strengths and limits): a balanced view with a strength and a limit for each type, each tied to its effect on stakeholders, then a judgement. Common mistake: calling one type "best" without a context.
- Short true/false or fill-in items test the definitions (who owns it, whether surplus is paid to owners). Learn the one-line definitions exactly.
