# Package: ch17-s1 — Consumer rights, responsibilities and legislation

- **loId:** `ch17-s1` · **chapterId:** `ch17`
- **Syllabus:** Leaving Certificate Business specification (first examined June 2027), Strand 4, section 4.1, outcome [4.1.1]. Students learn how different pieces of legislation set out rights and responsibilities that consumers should be aware of regarding products, services and digital services and their promotion, and how these rights change when buying outside the EU; they investigate the rights and responsibilities of consumers using current consumer legislation. Footnote 2: students engage with current legislation; where it changes during the two years of the course, either version may be used.
- **Prepared by:** a higher model, 2026-11-23. The writer works only from this package and `course-outlines/NOTES_STANDARD.md`.

## 1. Must cover (one bullet or block for every item; tick each off)

Assumed knowledge
- [ ] A1 (assumed) A consumer is an individual who buys goods, services or digital content for personal use, not for a business; a trader is the seller. Rights are legal entitlements the trader cannot take away by contract.
- [ ] A2 (assumed) Goods (physical items), services (work or an activity done for the consumer), digital content (for example downloaded files, apps, streams) and digital services (for example cloud storage, streaming, social media platforms) are the four things bought.
- [ ] A3 (assumed) A statute (Act of the Oireachtas) sets legal rules; an EU directive must be turned into Irish law, an EU regulation applies directly (this is why the Consumer Protection Act 2007 "gives effect to" a directive).

Consumer Rights Act 2022
- [ ] C1 The Consumer Rights Act 2022 as the main Irish statute on consumer rights; it consolidated and modernised older law on the sale of goods and supply of services.
- [ ] C2 Scope: goods, services, digital content and digital services, so the consumer has rights whatever is bought.
- [ ] C3 Standards: goods of satisfactory quality, fit for purpose and as described; services with reasonable care and skill, and within a reasonable time where none was agreed; digital content and digital services of satisfactory quality, fit for purpose and matching the description.
- [ ] C4 Remedies for goods in sequence: short-term right to reject (full refund within 30 days); then repair or replacement; then price reduction or ending the contract for a refund if repair or replacement is impossible or fails.
- [ ] C5 The 12-month presumption: a fault that appears within the first 12 months is presumed to have existed at delivery unless the trader shows otherwise.
- [ ] C6 The time limit for a goods claim (use the checked six-year statement in section 2, not the old "two years").
- [ ] C7 Remedies for services (repeat or fix, then price reduction, then ending the contract where the failure is serious) and for digital content (repair or replace, then price reduction or refund).
- [ ] C8 Unfair contract terms: significant imbalance to the consumer's detriment, contrary to good faith; terms must be in plain, intelligible language; an unfair term does not bind the consumer but the rest of the contract can stand.

Consumer Protection Act 2007 and promotion
- [ ] P1 The Consumer Protection Act 2007 controls how traders market and sell; it gives effect to an EU directive on unfair commercial practices.
- [ ] P2 A practice is unfair if it is misleading or aggressive or fails the standard of care expected of a trader.
- [ ] P3 Misleading action (false or deceptive information) and misleading omission (leaving out information the consumer needs).
- [ ] P4 Aggressive practice: harassment, coercion or undue influence restricting freedom of choice.
- [ ] P5 Prohibited practices that are always unlawful, with bait advertising as the worked example.
- [ ] P6 The CCPC: enforces consumer protection and competition law; established under the Competition and Consumer Protection Act 2014; powers to investigate, seek court orders, prosecute; publishes guidance; deals with wider breaches rather than settling each individual complaint.
- [ ] P7 Advertising rules: the ASA code (the body was formerly the ASAI); misleading, offensive or unfair advertising can be ruled against and withdrawn or changed; fair comparative advertising; consumers can complain to the ASA.
- [ ] P8 Digital angle: promotion online (influencer posts, targeted ads, fake reviews, hidden fees) must still not mislead; digital content and services are covered by the 2022 Act (C2, C3, C7). Ethical angle: misleading green claims ("greenwashing") and exploiting vulnerable consumers breach the fair-dealing aim of the law (explain without naming a case).

Responsibilities and redress
- [ ] R1 Consumer responsibilities: keep receipts, order confirmations and warranties; read terms (cancellation, delivery, returns); act promptly on a fault; use goods properly (misuse is not the trader's responsibility); check the trader's identity and contact details.
- [ ] R2 Redress in order: complain to the seller in writing (problem, remedy sought, deadline); alternative dispute resolution; Small Claims procedure (see checked limit and fee); courts as last resort.
- [ ] R3 Definition of alternative dispute resolution (settling a complaint outside the courts using a neutral third party).

Buying inside and outside the EU
- [ ] E1 Inside the EU: EU-wide consumer rules apply to purchases from traders in other member states; the European Consumer Centre Ireland helps with cross-border complaints.
- [ ] E2 The UK after Brexit is outside the EU, so EU consumer rules do not automatically apply to UK traders.
- [ ] E3 Outside the EU: possible customs duty and import VAT; weaker, harder-to-enforce remedies; reliance on goodwill or the payment provider's protection; check location and returns policy before buying.
- [ ] E4 Compare how rights change: contrast the inside-EU position with the outside-EU position in a short comparison (rights, remedies, cost, enforcement).

## 2. Checked facts and examples

| Item | Checked facts |
|---|---|
| Consumer Rights Act 2022 | Act No. 37 of 2022; commenced 29 November 2022 (S.I. No. 596 of 2022, section 161 excepted; s.161 only adds a price-reduction and termination remedy for unfair commercial practices to the Consumer Protection Act 2007, so it does not affect the goods, services or digital-content remedies). It applies to goods, digital content and services bought on or after that date. Source: irishstatutebook.ie, ccpc.ie |
| Short-term right | 30 days from the relevant start point (the time of delivery, or completion of installation where the trader installs). Within it the consumer can reject faulty goods for a refund; a shorter period applies to goods that expire or deteriorate quickly. The consumer may instead choose repair or replacement. Source: irishstatutebook.ie (section 24), ccpc.ie |
| Presumption | A lack of conformity that appears within 12 months is presumed to have existed at delivery unless the trader proves otherwise (section 22). After 12 months the consumer must show the fault was there at delivery. Source: irishstatutebook.ie, ccpc.ie |
| Time limit for goods | The CCPC says consumers can seek a remedy for faulty goods for up to six years, if the goods could reasonably be expected to last that long under normal use, even after a warranty ends. Say "up to six years", not "two years". Source: ccpc.ie, citizensinformation.ie (as of 2026) |
| Remedy order for goods | After the short-term right: repair or replacement; then, where the trader cannot or will not, or the fault is serious, the "final right to terminate" (refund) or a price reduction. Source: ccpc.ie |
| Consumer Protection Act 2007 | Act No. 19 of 2007; implements the Unfair Commercial Practices Directive (Directive 2005/29/EC); Part 3 covers commercial practices, with chapters on misleading and aggressive practices; section 55 lists prohibited practices (substituted by the 2014 Act from 31 October 2014). Source: irishstatutebook.ie, gov.ie |
| Bait advertising | Well-established: advertising goods at a stated price with no reasonable grounds to believe the trader can supply them at that price is a prohibited practice (EU unfair practices list). |
| CCPC | Established 31 October 2014 under the Competition and Consumer Protection Act 2014 (No. 29 of 2014), merging the National Consumer Agency and the Competition Authority. The statutory body that promotes compliance with, and enforces, competition and consumer protection law. Source: ccpc.ie, enterprise.gov.ie |
| Small Claims | Claims of up to €2,000; application fee €25 (as of 2026); made online or at the District Court office; a Small Claims Registrar (court clerk) tries to settle before any hearing; both parties based in Ireland; debt claims excluded. Source: courts.ie (as of 2026). Do not state a higher limit. |
| ECC Ireland | European Consumer Centre Ireland, hosted by the CCPC, part of the European Consumer Centres Network; helps consumers with cross-border complaints against EU traders. Source: eccireland.ie, ccpc.ie |
| ASA (formerly ASAI) | Advertising Standards Authority, renamed in 2024 from Advertising Standards Authority for Ireland (ASAI); a self-regulatory body that administers the code of standards for advertising and marketing communications and handles complaints; its website is adstandards.ie. Source: adstandards.ie. Do not give a code edition number or complaint figures. |
| Unfair contract terms | Well-established: Irish consumer law bars unfair terms that cause a significant imbalance to the consumer's detriment, contrary to good faith; terms must be transparent; an unfair term does not bind the consumer. State this without section numbers. |
| Brexit | Well-established: the UK left the EU on 31 January 2020; the transition ended 31 December 2020. |

## 3. Traps

- Do not use: "Consumers have up to two years from delivery to claim that goods did not conform", because CCPC and gov.ie guidance gives up to six years (with the 12-month presumption inside it); two years is the EU minimum, not the Irish rule.
- Do not use: any fine, limit, fee or deadline other than those in section 2; amounts change.
- Do not use: "The Act consolidated ... older Irish law" as a precise claim about which Acts (Sale of Goods and Supply of Services Act 1980 and others) it replaced; say only that it modernised and consolidated the older law.
- Do not use: the EU online dispute resolution platform by name; its current status is unverified.
- Do not use: the "short-term right to reject" as the only remedy; repair or replacement may also be chosen in the first 30 days, then the order of remedies applies.
- Confusable: Consumer Rights Act 2022 (what the consumer is owed for the product) versus Consumer Protection Act 2007 (how traders must behave when selling and advertising). CCPC (regulator for the market) versus ASA, formerly ASAI (self-regulator for advertising) versus ECC Ireland (cross-border help). Rights (what you are owed) versus responsibilities (what you must do).
- Neighbouring subsections: ethical and sustainable buying, the circular economy, the shadow economy and the effects of digital technology on buying behaviour belong to the notes on ethical consumption; GDPR, personal data and why informed decisions matter belong to the notes on protecting personal data. GDPR cannot be the student's chosen EU regulation for the EU topic, so do not teach GDPR here.
- Do not mark anything "Higher Level only"; do not teach old-syllabus contract law or the Sale of Goods Acts.

## 4. What exams ask

The 2025 sample papers (outline) contain no question on this subsection, so nothing here is drawn from them. What the specification's verbs imply:
- "Investigate" and "explain" rights: name the Act, state the right, say what it means in practice (for example faulty goods within 30 days: full refund). Mistake: naming a right without the Act or the time limit.
- "Outline" responsibilities: give the essential points (keep receipts, read terms, act promptly), a short sentence each.
- "Describe" or "discuss" redress: the escalation order with the reason for each step; mistake: jumping to the courts.
- "Compare" buying inside and outside the EU: refer to both throughout (rights, enforcement, customs and VAT); mistake: describing only one side.
- "Distinguish" Consumer Rights Act from Consumer Protection Act: product remedies versus marketing conduct.
- Applied questions: a short stimulus (a faulty item, a misleading advert) asking the student to identify the breach and advise the consumer. A full answer names the Act, the breach, the remedy and the next step. Mistake: advising "sue" first.
- Higher Level answers need 3-4 developed points with the effect on the consumer or the business.
