# Package: ch16-s2 — Strategic planning and force-field analysis

- **loId:** `ch16-s2` · **chapterId:** `ch16`
- **Syllabus:** NCCA Leaving Certificate Business specification (first examined June 2027), Strand 3, section 3.4 (The rationale for planning), learning outcomes [3.4.5] to [3.4.7]. Students learn about the significance of strategic planning as an ongoing process, how it supports leading and managing change, and how a force-field analysis can support strategic decision-making. They should be able to outline strategic planning and why it is ongoing; describe its benefits; explain the importance of planning for change; and discuss how a force-field analysis could support strategic planning for change. Glossary term: force-field analysis. No Higher Level only content.
- **Prepared by:** a higher model, 2026-11-23. The writer works only from this package and `course-outlines/NOTES_STANDARD.md`.

## 1. Must cover (one bullet or block for every item; tick each off)

Assumed knowledge
- [ ] A1 (assumed) Strategic, tactical and operational planning: what each is, who does it and its time horizon (strategic long-term and senior management; tactical medium-term for departments; operational short-term day-to-day). Examples: a marketing campaign for the coming year (tactical); weekly rotas and stock orders (operational).
- [ ] A2 (assumed) Mission, vision, goals and objectives, and SMART objectives. Mission: why the organisation exists and what it does for customers. Vision: the future state it hopes to achieve. Goals are broad aims; objectives are specific targets that achieve them.
- [ ] A3 (assumed) SWOT (internal strengths and weaknesses, external opportunities and threats) and STEEPLE (external factors only), from earlier notes. One line each: they feed the analysis stage.
- [ ] A4 (assumed) The internal and external changes an organisation meets, from the previous subsection on change. Recall in a line.

Strategic planning [3.4.5]
- [ ] P1 Define strategic planning: the ongoing process of setting an organisation's long-term direction and deciding how to reach it; led by senior management; covers the whole organisation.
- [ ] P2 The strategic planning cycle: analysis (internal capabilities and external environment, with SWOT and STEEPLE); objective-setting (measurable objectives); strategy choice (for example entering a new market or cutting costs); implementation (resources, responsibilities and timetables assigned to named people); monitoring and review (performance measured against objectives, plan adjusted, which returns to analysis).
- [ ] P3 Why it is ongoing, not a one-off: the business environment keeps changing, so the plan needs repeated review.
- [ ] P4 Link the three levels: operational plans deliver tactical plans, tactical plans deliver the strategic direction.
- [ ] P5 Ethical, sustainability and digital angle: strategic objectives can include environmental targets and ethical standards (a "sustainability objective" with a measure and a date); digital tools (dashboards, data analytics) support monitoring and review; digital change is itself an item the analysis stage must consider.

Benefits of strategic planning [3.4.6]
- [ ] B1 Direction: everyone understands where the organisation is heading.
- [ ] B2 Coordination: departments align towards shared goals; less duplication and conflict.
- [ ] B3 Resource allocation: money, staff and equipment go to the priorities that matter most.
- [ ] B4 Anticipating change: likely threats and opportunities are seen early, leaving time to respond.
- [ ] B5 Motivation: staff who understand the purpose of their work tend to be more committed.
- [ ] B6 Control: benchmarks against which progress is measured and corrective action taken.
- [ ] B7 Each benefit explained with its effect on the business, for "describe" and "explain three benefits" answers.

Planning for change [3.4.6]
- [ ] C1 Why planning for change matters: change is risky and needs clear direction.
- [ ] C2 A change plan identifies what must change, when it will happen and who is responsible for each part.
- [ ] C3 Planning allows budgets and staff time to be allocated to the change rather than leaving it to chance.
- [ ] C4 A plan that involves the people affected shows them the reasons and timeline, which helps reduce resistance (the ways to overcome resistance are taught in the notes on change and resistance; do not repeat them in full).

Force-field analysis [3.4.7]
- [ ] F1 Quote the Appendix 2 definition (Checked facts, row G1) and explain it in plain words: a decision tool comparing forces pushing for a change with forces resisting it.
- [ ] F2 Driving forces: factors pushing towards the change (competitor action, rising customer demand). Restraining forces: factors opposing it (cost, fear of disruption, shortage of skills).
- [ ] F3 Forces can be internal (staff skills, culture) or external (regulation, market conditions).
- [ ] F4 Steps in order: identify the proposed change; list driving and restraining forces; score each force; total each side; decide; plan actions to shift the balance.
- [ ] F5 Scoring: each force scored for strength, normally 1 (weak) to 5 (strong), a teaching convention (the specification sets no scale); totals compared.
- [ ] F6 Reading the result: driving total higher suggests the change is more likely to be worth pursuing; close result means marginal, so act on the balance before committing; restraining total higher suggests reconsidering or weakening restraints first.
- [ ] F7 Using the result: strengthen drivers and weaken restraints (weakening a restraint is often more effective than adding drivers).
- [ ] F8 Fully worked example (the notes' sample bakery chain, row W1): decision, four driving forces with scores totalling 14, four restraining forces totalling 12, result narrowly in favour, then actions to shift the balance (a trial, retraining and a job guarantee, counter service kept in quiet periods, a maintenance contract).
- [ ] F9 Limitations: scores are subjective; forces are not weighted by importance (one strong restraint can outweigh several weaker drivers); forces change over time so the result dates; interactions between forces are not shown; no costs, benefits or financial return, so use alongside financial analysis.
- [ ] F10 How it supports strategic decision-making: makes the forces visible, forces a structured judgement, helps managers see which restraints to tackle (links to resistance), and supports a justified recommendation. Digital angle: a spreadsheet or shared online template can record and update the scores; ethical angle: staff and community interests appear as forces.
- [ ] F11 Choose between the proposal's options: go ahead, go ahead with changes, delay or abandon, with the reason.

Key terms
- [ ] K1 Force-field analysis; driving force; restraining force; strategic planning; strategic planning cycle; mission statement; vision statement; tactical planning.

## 3. Traps

- Scoring scale: the specification sets no scale; 1 (weak) to 5 (strong) is a teaching convention, so write "normally" and do not present it as part of the definition.
- Do not use: a date for Lewin's model, because the secondary sources disagree on details and no primary text was checked. Name him only, as in row L1.
- Confusable: strategic, tactical and operational planning (long, medium, short-term; whole organisation, department, day-to-day). A weekly rota is operational, not tactical.
- Confusable: mission (why the organisation exists and what it does) vs vision (the future state it wants). A statement beginning "To become..." is a vision.
- Confusable: goals (broad) vs objectives (specific, measurable, time-bound).
- Confusable: SWOT vs STEEPLE vs force-field analysis. SWOT looks at internal and external factors to understand a position; STEEPLE looks at the external environment only; force-field analysis weighs forces for and against one specific proposed change. Force-field analysis forces can be both internal and external.
- Confusable: driving forces vs restraining forces. Cost is usually a restraint on a proposal but can be a driver when the proposal saves money (rising wage costs driving a move to kiosks). Place each force from the point of view of the proposal.
- Confusable: the total score is not a final answer. A student must read the result, state a conclusion and propose how to shift the balance; the method does not decide by itself.
- Boundary: the notes on change and resistance own the internal and external changes, reasons for resistance and ways to overcome it. Here resistance appears only as a restraining force and a reason to plan with the people affected.
- Boundary: the notes on contingency planning and crisis management own planning for a sudden event and its cost, time and risk factors. Strategic planning here is about long-term direction and change, not emergency response.
- Boundary: SWOT and STEEPLE are taught in earlier notes; recall them only as inputs to the analysis stage.
- Boundary: the planning cycle is a strategic planning process, not the old syllabus's planning-organising-controlling framework (Appendix A2 of the outline). Do not teach that framework.

## 4. What exams ask

- **Use a force-field analysis to examine a decision (2025 sample paper, Higher Level Sample 2, Question 1(d): use a force-field analysis to examine a decision to close two restaurants).** Appendix 1: "examine" means "look closely at arguments, data, information, concepts and/or stories in order to uncover origins, assumptions, perspectives, trends and/or relationships". A full answer sets out the decision, lists driving and restraining forces taken from the stimulus (internal and external), scores each force, totals both sides, states which is stronger, then gives a justified conclusion and says how to strengthen drivers or weaken restraints. Common mistakes: listing forces with no scores or no totals; putting forces on the wrong side; ignoring the stimulus facts; stopping at the totals with no conclusion; only one or two forces per side.
- **True or false (Sample 1, Question 4(a)(ii): "strategic planning sets out long-term objectives").** The statement is true: strategic planning sets long-term direction and objectives. Common mistake: confusing it with operational planning (short-term). The answer is a single word; no marks for reasoning unless asked.
- **Likely applications (no marks implied):** distinguish tactical from operational planning with an example; explain three benefits of strategic planning for a named type of business; outline the strategic planning cycle and why it is ongoing; discuss how a force-field analysis could support planning for change (Appendix 1: "discuss" means "offer a considered, balanced review that includes a range of arguments, factors or hypotheses and is supported by appropriate evidence"), which must include limitations as well as benefits; explain the importance of planning for change.
- **What a "discuss" answer on force-field analysis contains:** what it is, two or three ways it helps (makes forces visible, quantifies the balance, shows which restraints to tackle), at least two limitations (subjective scores, no weighting, forces change, ignores interactions), and a judgement.
- **Cross-cutting angle:** mention staff, customers and the community as forces (ethical), sustainability goals as an objective, and digital tools for analysis and monitoring, where the stimulus allows.
- **Tip sources for the writer:** show the scores and totals; use forces from the stimulus; always conclude; give benefits with effects not just names; the cycle is continuous, so monitoring leads back to analysis.
