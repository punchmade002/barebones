# Package: ch8-s1 — The business plan, and ethics and sustainability in planning

- **loId:** `ch8-s1` · **chapterId:** `ch8`
- **Syllabus:** Strand 2, section 2.3 (Business planning), [2.3.1] and [2.3.2]. Students learn about the importance of a coherent business plan at different stages of business development and how it supports start-up, operations, funding and development, and about the need to consider ethical and sustainability factors when planning. They should be able to outline the key functions of a business plan and the importance of ethics and sustainability when planning.
- **Prepared by:** a higher model, 2026-11-23. The writer works only from this package and `course-outlines/NOTES_STANDARD.md`. The NCCA specification is first examined in June 2027; nothing in this subsection is Higher Level only.

## 1. Must cover (one bullet or block for every item; tick each off)

Assumed knowledge
- [ ] A1 (assumed) A stakeholder is any individual or group affected by a business or able to affect it (staff, customers, suppliers, owners, the community). Needed because the plan is communicated to stakeholders and ethics is about how they are treated.
- [ ] A2 (assumed) A cash-flow forecast is a projection of the money expected to come into and go out of a business over a future period. It is part of the financial plan; the technique itself belongs to the notes on finance.
- [ ] A3 (assumed) Viability: whether an idea can earn enough income to cover its costs and stay financially workable.
- [ ] A4 (assumed) A variance is the gap between planned and actual results; a benchmark is the planned figure that actual results are compared against.

Definition and purpose
- [ ] S1 Definition: a business plan is a written document that sets out what a business intends to do, how it will do it and what resources it needs; it is the main planning document, for a business starting up or already trading.
- [ ] S2 What it contains in outline: the idea, the product or service, the target customers and how the business will earn money.
- [ ] S3 It states goals and the steps to reach them, so managers know what to do and in what order.
- [ ] S4 It sets out the resources required: people, premises, equipment, technology and finance.
- [ ] S5 It is a working tool, reviewed and updated as the business learns what works in practice.

Functions (the five the outline names)
- [ ] F1 Direction and goals: a clear route and measurable targets for owners and staff.
- [ ] F2 Raising finance: persuades banks, investors and grant bodies that the business is viable and the owners have thought it through.
- [ ] F3 Benchmark for control: actual results compared with the plan so managers spot variances early and take corrective action.
- [ ] F4 Testing viability: writing the figures down shows whether the idea can cover its costs and earn a return before money is committed.
- [ ] F5 Communicating with stakeholders: employees, suppliers, landlords and advisers can see what the business is trying to do.
- [ ] F6 The emphasis on each function changes with the stage of the business.

Typical sections
- [ ] T1 Executive summary: a short overview of the whole plan, written last but placed first, so a busy reader can judge the proposal quickly.
- [ ] T2 Business description: legal form, founding idea, products or services offered, long-term vision.
- [ ] T3 Market analysis: target market, size and trends of demand, competitors.
- [ ] T4 Marketing plan: how the business will reach customers through its marketing mix and how much it will spend on promotion.
- [ ] T5 Operations and management: premises, production or service processes, the management team and staff needed.
- [ ] T6 Financial plan: start-up costs, sales forecasts, a cash-flow forecast, a profit forecast and the funding required.
- [ ] T7 Risks: main threats and the actions planned to reduce them or respond if they occur.
- [ ] T8 Length and detail of each section depend on the purpose of the plan (for example a bank may ask for its own format).

How the plan's role changes with the stage of the business (supports start-up, operations, funding and development)
- [ ] R1 Start-up: mainly tests viability and attracts initial finance; relies heavily on forecasts and the owner's assumptions.
- [ ] R2 Early operations: becomes a control benchmark; managers compare monthly results with the forecasts.
- [ ] R3 Growth: focuses on new markets, new products, expansion finance and the staff needed to deliver them.
- [ ] R4 Mature business: revises the plan regularly, especially when market conditions or costs change significantly.
- [ ] R5 Coherent plan: the sections must fit together (for example the marketing plan, the operations and the financial plan must all rest on the same assumptions).

Ethics in planning
- [ ] E1 Definition of the idea: ethical planning builds fair treatment of people and honesty into the business from the outset, not after problems appear.
- [ ] E2 Fair treatment of staff: pay, working conditions and training planned so employees are treated fairly and retained.
- [ ] E3 Fair treatment of suppliers: payment terms and purchasing agreements set so small suppliers are not exploited.
- [ ] E4 Honest marketing: promises in the plan are accurate and do not mislead buyers about what the product does.
- [ ] E5 Responsible sourcing: check materials are produced legally and without exploiting the workers who make them.

Sustainability in planning
- [ ] U1 Environmental impact: estimate energy use, waste, emissions and water use, and set targets to reduce them.
- [ ] U2 Circular design: products planned so they can be repaired, reused or recycled, cutting end-of-life waste. Link to the Appendix 2 definition of the circular economy (Section 2).
- [ ] U3 Social impact: effects on the local community, such as jobs created, local purchasing and community involvement.
- [ ] U4 Long-term viability: a business reliant on scarce resources or that damages its reputation may not survive, so sustainability protects future income.

Why investors and customers expect it (link to ESG in the notes on the business environment)
- [ ] X1 Investors often assess ESG factors when deciding whether to fund, because they affect long-term risk (quote the ESG definition, Section 2).
- [ ] X2 Customers may choose or avoid a brand because of its environmental and ethical record, so weak practice can reduce sales.
- [ ] X3 Banks and grant bodies may ask how a plan handles environmental and social risks before approving finance.
- [ ] X4 Poor ethical conduct can lead to reputational damage, staff loss and legal action, raising the cost of running a business.

Digital angle (new; the outline asks for it wherever one exists)
- [ ] D1 Digital tools: plans are now commonly written, shared and updated with spreadsheets and cloud tools, and a plan can be updated as live data (sales, website traffic) comes in. Keep it to one or two bullets, no named software.
- [ ] D2 Digital in the plan's content: a plan should say how the business will use digital channels, data and technology, and how it will protect customer data (a digital ethics point: honest and lawful use of customers' data).

## 3. Traps

- Do not use: any named company, grant scheme or figure as the example of a business plan or of ethical or sustainable practice, because none is verified in this subsection. Use unnamed sample businesses.
- Do not use: "physical evidence" or the old syllabus's idea of "management activities as planning, organising and controlling" as a topic (old syllabus only, Appendix A4). Planning here means the business plan, not the old management-functions unit.
- Confusable: business plan versus business model. The plan is the full document (market, operations, finance, risk); the model is one element within it that explains how the firm creates, delivers and captures value. The notes on the business model deal with the model; here mention it only as one part of the plan.
- Confusable: ethics versus sustainability. Ethics is about fair and honest treatment of people (staff, suppliers, customers); sustainability is about environmental and social impact and long-term survival. Keep the two headings separate.
- Confusable: executive summary is written last but placed first; do not say it is written first.
- Confusable: benchmark (the planned figure) versus variance (the gap from actual).
- Boundary: the technique of preparing a cash-flow forecast and sources of finance belong to the notes on finance; here the forecast appears only as one part of the plan. Market research methods and the marketing mix belong to the notes on the target market and the marketing mix. A SWOT analysis is a separate applied tool; do not teach it here. ESG reporting as a topic belongs to the notes on the business environment; use it here only to explain why investors expect it.
- Boundary: the nine canvas blocks and value creation, delivery and capture belong to the notes on business models and the canvas; the named business models belong to the notes on common business models; digital models belong to the notes on the digital economy.

## 4. What exams ask

The outline records no 2025 sample-paper question on this subsection, so everything below is derived from the specification's verbs and the style of the sample papers (applied questions on a short stimulus), not from a counted frequency.
- Outline (Appendix 1: "give the main points; restrict to essential points of information"): outline the functions of a business plan, or why ethics and sustainability matter in planning. A full answer gives three or four distinct, short points, each tied to the business. Common mistake: listing sections instead of functions, or giving one point twice in different words.
- Explain ("a detailed account including reasons or causes"): explain how a plan helps raise finance or control a business. A full answer states the function, then says why it works (for example the benchmark is compared with actual results so a variance is spotted early). Common mistake: naming the function with no reason.
- Identify: name the sections of a plan, or a stage at which the plan is used. One-line answers.
- Applied questions: a stimulus about a start-up or a growing firm asks which function the plan serves now. A full answer links the stage (start-up, early operations, growth) to the function that matters most then. Common mistake: generic answers that ignore the stimulus.
- Ethical and sustainability angle: expect to apply fair treatment of staff and suppliers, honest marketing, responsible sourcing, environmental impact and circular design to a given business, and to say why investors and customers expect them. Common mistake: stating "be ethical" with no specific practice or effect on the business.
- Tips to build: name the function then give its reason; for a stage question, name the stage first; always link ethics or sustainability to a specific effect (finance, sales, reputation, cost).
