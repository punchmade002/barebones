# Package: ch7-s2 — Design thinking

- **loId:** `ch7-s2` · **chapterId:** `ch7`
- **Syllabus:** NCCA *Curriculum Specification for Leaving Certificate Business*, Strand 2, section 2.2 "Idea development", learning outcome [2.2.2]: students learn about "the significance of design thinking* (including the four phases of clarify, ideate, develop, and implement) to support idea development", and should be able to "outline design thinking as an innovative approach to idea development and appreciate how the process is iterative and both solution- and person-centred". Outline scope: the glossary definition; each of the four phases with what happens and the tools used; what iterative, person-centred and solution-centred mean; benefits and drawbacks; a worked example of a product going through all four phases. Examined first in June 2027. Nothing here is Higher Level only.

## 1. Must cover (one bullet or block for every item; tick each off)

Background (assumed)
- [ ] A1 (assumed) A business idea has been generated and screened (covered in the notes on how business ideas are developed); design thinking is a way to develop it so that it fits what users need.
- [ ] A2 (assumed) The end user is the person who will actually use the product or service; the customer may be the same person or someone else (for example a parent buying for a child).
- [ ] A3 (assumed) The marketing mix elements named at launch (promotion, pricing, place) are taught in a later chapter; here they are only named.

Definition and approach
- [ ] D1 Glossary definition, word for word, with the term marked as a key term: "An innovative, non-linear approach to the development of products and services which focuses on solutions for the end user." (Appendix 2).
- [ ] D2 Innovative: treats problem solving as creative, generating new ideas.
- [ ] D3 Non-linear: teams move back and forth between phases; the phases are named in an order but are not completed once and finished.
- [ ] D4 Solution-centred: aims to produce a practical answer to the user's problem.
- [ ] D5 Person-centred: the user's needs, feelings and behaviour drive each decision.
- [ ] D6 Iterative: the process repeats in cycles, and each cycle refines the solution from the one before. Be able to explain why design thinking is called iterative (the spec's word).
- [ ] D7 Significance: supports idea development by reducing the risk of building something people do not want.

The four phases, in the specification's own names
- [ ] P1 Clarify: understand the user and define the real problem before choosing a solution. Tools: empathy and user research (observation, interviews), problem statement.
- [ ] P2 Ideate: generate a wide range of possible solutions. Tools: brainstorming with judgement held back, then sorting ideas against criteria such as user need and feasibility; mixed perspectives in the group.
- [ ] P3 Develop: turn the chosen idea into a prototype and test it with real users. Tools: prototyping (a low-cost early version), testing, feedback; repeat until the design works.
- [ ] P4 Implement: launch to customers (planned marketing mix: promotion, pricing and place), then monitor sales and customer feedback, which feeds later improvement.
- [ ] P5 The four phases form a loop: results of test or launch can send the team back to clarify, ideate or develop.
- [ ] P6 For each phase, give what the firm does and the tools used, so the answer can say "how a firm can use each phase to meet a client's expectations" (the exam wording is paraphrased here).

Worked example
- [ ] W1 One product taken through all four phases in order, with a return to an earlier phase after test feedback (the café-queue ordering app sample may stay as a "sample business").
- [ ] W2 A second sample for practice: a regional bakery launching a gluten-free range (it appears in the existing practice question; it may be used as a labelled sample).

Benefits and drawbacks
- [ ] B1 Benefits: products that meet real needs are more likely to succeed; early testing cuts the cost of fixing faults after launch.
- [ ] B2 Drawbacks: takes time, repeated testing can delay launch; research with a small group may not represent the market; needs staff with the right skills and perhaps training.
- [ ] B3 Evaluation: a small firm with a fast-moving competitor may find the delay costly; a firm with a complex product gains most.

Ethical, sustainability and digital angle
- [ ] T1 Ethical: user research must respect privacy and consent (personal data is protected by data protection law; do not name an Act or regulation here); design for inclusion and accessibility so the product works for all users.
- [ ] T2 Sustainability: clarify and develop can include designing for durability, less packaging and less waste, and testing the environmental cost of a design.
- [ ] T3 Digital: digital tools speed up the process (online surveys, clickable app prototypes, user analytics after launch, collaboration software), and digital products can be updated continuously after implementation.

## 3. Traps

- Do not use: the phase names empathise, define, prototype and test as the four phases, because the specification's phases are clarify, ideate, develop and implement. Empathy, problem statement, prototyping and testing are tools used inside the phases.
- Do not use: the Stanford d.school five-stage model, the IDEO method or the Double Diamond by name, because none is in the specification and none has been checked for this course.
- Do not use: "design thinking is a straight line of four steps", because the glossary says it is non-linear and the outcome says iterative. The four phases are named in order, but teams loop back.
- Do not use: any claim that a named Irish firm used design thinking, because none has been checked.
- Do not use: statistics on product failure rates or on the benefits of design thinking, because none is sourced.
- Confusable: solution-centred (aims at a practical answer for the user) versus person-centred (starts from the user's needs, feelings and behaviour). Both are needed; they are not the same word.
- Confusable: iterative (repeats in cycles, each refining the last) versus non-linear (steps can be revisited in any order). Distinguish them if asked; use "iterative" in the answer to the spec question.
- Confusable: the ideate phase uses brainstorming, which is also an internal source of ideas in the notes on how business ideas are developed. Here it is a phase tool.
- Confusable: a prototype (a test version used to learn) versus the launched product; the develop phase tests with a small group, implement is the real launch.
- Belongs to a neighbouring subsection: how ideas are sourced and screened, and the factors helping or hindering them, belong to the notes on how business ideas are developed; the parts of a feasibility study, break-even and the proceed, modify or abandon decision belong to the notes on the feasibility study; the marketing mix, branding and the product life cycle belong to the marketing chapters; the business plan and business model canvas belong to the notes on business planning and business models.

## 4. What exams ask

- The only recorded sample-paper link is Sample 1, Q1(d): outline how a firm can use each of the four phases to meet a client's expectations. "Outline" (Appendix 1: give the main points, restrict to essential points) means each phase named and one or two applied points, not a long essay.
- A full answer to a four-phases question: the four phases in the specification's names and order, for each what the firm does, the tool used, and a link to the client's expectations (the user's needs, what the client asked for, quality and cost, feedback). Use the case in the stem: name the firm's actual product and client.
- "Outline design thinking as an innovative approach": give the glossary meaning in the student's own words plus two features (non-linear, focused on the end user).
- "Explain why it is iterative" (Appendix 1: detailed account including reasons or causes): the process repeats, each test or launch gives feedback that sends the team back, and the solution improves with each cycle. State this cause and effect, not just "it repeats".
- "Discuss" benefits and drawbacks: three or four developed points, each with its effect on a small firm (cost of fixing faults, time to launch, representativeness of user research, skills needed), ending with a judgement.
- Common mistakes: using other models' phase names; describing the process as a one-off sequence; defining only "creative" and missing "focused on solutions for the end user"; leaving out the loop back after testing; giving a launch as the end rather than monitoring feedback; copying the phases with no link to the client in the question.
- Expect the themes to be assessed: user privacy, accessible and sustainable design, and digital prototyping or analytics.
