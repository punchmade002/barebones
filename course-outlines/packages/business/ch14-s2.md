## 1. Must cover (one bullet or block for every item; tick each off)

Assumed knowledge
- [ ] A1 (assumed) Employees' pay and conditions are set in a contract of employment; a manager (line manager) supervises and reviews staff.
- [ ] A2 (assumed) Performance means how well an employee meets the objectives and standards of the role; objectives should be clear and agreed (SMART-style goals).
- [ ] A3 (assumed) Feedback is information given to a person about how they are doing, so they can keep or change what they do.

Motivation [3.2.4]
- [ ] M1 Definition: the internal and external forces that make an employee willing to put effort into work.
- [ ] M2 Why it matters: motivated staff are more productive and committed, and less likely to leave (lower recruitment and training cost).
- [ ] M3 Intrinsic motivation (from within: enjoyment, achievement, growth) and extrinsic motivation (from outside: pay, bonuses, promotion, praise).
- [ ] M4 Intrinsic tends to last longer; extrinsic rewards can lose effect if expected or mismatched to effort.

Financial motivators
- [ ] F1 Pay: the basic reward; seen as unfair, it causes dissatisfaction even when other motivators are strong.
- [ ] F2 Bonuses: reward individual or team results over a period.
- [ ] F3 Profit-sharing: share of profits, shared ownership of results.
- [ ] F4 Share schemes: part-ownership, aligning interests with long-term growth.
- [ ] F5 Benefits (pension contributions, health insurance, company car): value beyond pay, attract and keep staff.

Non-financial motivators
- [ ] N1 Recognition (praise, thanks): valued at little or no cost.
- [ ] N2 Responsibility (leading a project): trust and ownership.
- [ ] N3 Job enrichment: more varied, skilled tasks, more challenge.
- [ ] N4 Job rotation: moving between tasks or departments reduces monotony and builds skills.
- [ ] N5 Flexible work (hours, remote working).
- [ ] N6 Teamwork, positive culture, clear career progression.

Factors that affect motivation [3.2.4 investigate]
- [ ] Q1 Individual needs and values differ (a young employee seeking progress vs an experienced one valuing stability).
- [ ] Q2 Perceived fairness: employees compare their rewards with colleagues'.
- [ ] Q3 Relationship with the line manager.
- [ ] Q4 Nature of the job: variety, autonomy, meaning.
- [ ] Q5 Clear goals and regular feedback.
- [ ] Q6 Workplace conditions: safety, job security, physical environment (a baseline; poor conditions demotivate even well-paid staff).

Appraisal [3.2.5, 3.2.6]
- [ ] P1 Purposes: review performance against agreed objectives, identify training needs, set goals, inform pay and promotion decisions.
- [ ] P2 Effective appraisal features: clear agreed criteria; regular, not one annual event; two-way; fair and consistent; linked to development.
- [ ] P3 Self-appraisal: employee assesses own performance against agreed criteria. Strengths: ownership, reflection, more open discussion. Weakness: over- or under-rating, so manager checks against evidence.
- [ ] P4 Formative appraisal: ongoing, developmental, regular feedback during the year. Strengths: supports learning, shows progress. Weakness: time-consuming; can become informal and unrecorded without follow-up.
- [ ] P5 Summative appraisal: formal end-of-period judgement of overall performance, used for pay, promotion, continued employment. Strengths: clear, documented record, supports fair decisions. Weaknesses: backward-looking, anxiety, a low rating can demotivate.
- [ ] P6 360-degree appraisal: feedback from line manager, peers, subordinates, customers and self; usually anonymous questionnaires summarised for employee and manager. Strengths: several angles, reveals blind spots, recognises contributions a manager does not see. Weaknesses: time-consuming and costly, hard to interpret without training, anonymous comments can be unfair, critical feedback can demotivate. Works best when it leads to a development plan, not blame.
- [ ] P7 Contribution of each approach to motivation (analyse): self-appraisal builds ownership; formative shows progress; summative gives recognition and reward link; 360 gives wider recognition.
- [ ] P8 Risks of poor appraisal: vague criteria or no follow-up; only-faults focus lowers confidence; inconsistency between managers erodes trust; box-ticking when not linked to development or reward.
- [ ] P9 Combining approaches (formative through the year plus a summative review) balances development with accountability.

Ethical, sustainability and digital angle
- [ ] X1 Ethics: fairness and consistency, avoiding bias (for example favouring people seen more often), honesty in feedback, confidentiality of results, and anonymity in 360 feedback.
- [ ] X2 Digital: appraisal and 360 surveys run on HR software; data from monitoring software used in appraisal raises privacy and fairness issues (judging visible activity rather than results).
- [ ] X3 Sustainability: wellbeing and sustainable workloads as a non-financial motivator; green or social goals can be part of objectives (no named example).

## 3. Traps

- Scope note (not a fact to drop): the specification names no motivation theorist and the current notes use none, so the rewrite adds none; explain the ideas without a name.
- Do not use: bonus, pay or share-scheme amounts or tax treatment, because they change and are unchecked.
- Do not use: "appraisal is only used to decide pay", because its purposes include development and training needs.
- Do not use: "360-degree feedback comes only from the manager", because the point is multiple sources.
- Confusable: formative (ongoing, developmental) vs summative (final, judgemental); self-appraisal (by the employee) vs 360-degree (by many people, including the employee).
- Confusable: intrinsic vs extrinsic motivation; financial vs non-financial motivators (a bonus is financial and extrinsic; recognition is non-financial and often extrinsic; enjoyment of work is intrinsic).
- Confusable: job enrichment (more depth: varied, skilled tasks) vs job rotation (moving between tasks); here they are motivators, whereas in the subsection on training and development rotation is a method of learning.
- Boundary: recruitment and selection (including interviews) belong to the subsection on workforce planning and recruitment; induction, training methods and human capital belong to the subsection on human capital management; corporate wellness initiatives and the leader's role in wellbeing belong to the subsection on corporate wellness; leadership styles belong to a different subsection of leadership. Mention wellbeing here only as one non-financial motivator.

## 4. What exams ask

- Sample paper 2, Question 2(b) (2025) asks students to explain two ways to ensure an effective appraisal process. "Explain" (Appendix 1) means a detailed account including reasons or causes. A full answer gives two distinct features from P2 (for example clear agreed criteria; two-way discussion; regular reviews; fairness and consistency; link to development), each with a reason it makes appraisal effective (the employee knows the standard; the employee feels heard and motivated) and, if possible, an effect on the business (better performance, lower turnover). Common mistakes: naming the approaches (360, summative) instead of ways to make the process effective; naming two ways without explaining either; repeating one idea in two forms.
- Likely verbs from the specification wording: describe (effective appraisal: give the main points in detail), investigate/analyse (factors affecting motivation; how an approach contributes to motivation), discuss (financial vs non-financial motivators: balanced, with evidence). For "analyse how the approach contributes to motivation" state the approach, its feature, then the effect on motivation and one limitation.
- Motivator answers must be linked to the employees in the case (their age, role, sector), and should show that a mix of financial and non-financial rewards usually works better than one. Common mistake: listing "pay" only.
- No question wording is copied here; use only the verbs and the task described above.
