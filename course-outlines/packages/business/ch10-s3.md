## 1. Must cover (one bullet or block for every item; tick each off)

Background (assumed)
- [ ] A1 (assumed) A business needs finance to start up, to fund day-to-day operation (working capital) and to buy long-lasting assets (fixed assets) and expand.
- [ ] A2 (assumed) Debt is borrowed money that must be repaid with interest; equity is money put in by owners and shareholders, which does not have to be repaid but shares control and profit.
- [ ] A3 (assumed) Security (collateral) means an asset a lender can claim if repayments are missed.
- [ ] A4 (assumed) The business life cycle: start-up, growth, maturity.

Short-term finance (normally repaid within a year)
- [ ] S1 Bank overdraft: lets a current account go below zero up to an agreed limit; interest only on the amount used; flexible, but the bank can usually ask for repayment at short notice.
- [ ] S2 Trade credit: paying a supplier after receiving goods; free within the agreed period; late payment can damage the relationship and bring charges.
- [ ] S3 Accrued expenses: costs such as wages or electricity incurred now and paid later, so they give free short-term finance.
- [ ] S4 Factoring: selling unpaid customer invoices to a factor for immediate cash, minus a fee; the factor usually collects the debts.
- [ ] S5 Invoice discounting: like factoring, but the business keeps responsibility for collecting from its customers.
- [ ] S6 Credit card: short-term borrowing; interest on unpaid balances often high, so suits small or temporary needs.

Medium-term finance (a few years, for vehicles and equipment)
- [ ] M1 Term loan: fixed period, usually from a bank, repaid in regular instalments of capital and interest; often secured on business assets.
- [ ] M2 Leasing: regular rentals to use an asset; the business does not own it; preserves cash but total payments can exceed the purchase price.
- [ ] M3 Hire purchase: deposit plus instalments; the business owns the asset once the final payment is made.
- [ ] M4 Leasing versus hire purchase: ownership at the end is the difference.

Long-term finance
- [ ] L1 Owner's equity: capital the owner puts in from savings or personal assets.
- [ ] L2 Share capital: money raised by issuing shares; investors become part-owners and may receive dividends.
- [ ] L3 Venture capital: equity from specialist investment firms backing high-growth businesses; expect a large return and a say in decisions.
- [ ] L4 Business angels: wealthy individuals investing their own money in early-stage businesses, often giving advice.
- [ ] L5 Retained earnings: profits kept in the business; no interest and no loss of control.
- [ ] L6 Long-term loans: borrowed over several years, usually for premises or major expansion.
- [ ] L7 Debentures: long-term loans with fixed interest paid to the holders, usually secured on the business's assets.
- [ ] L8 Grants: funds from state agencies or other bodies that need not be repaid but usually come with conditions. State schemes are covered in the notes on government supports; refer, do not repeat them.
- [ ] L9 Crowdfunding: small amounts from many people, usually online, either for a reward or in exchange for a share of the business.

Choosing and changing
- [ ] C1 Match the term of finance to the purpose: short-term for working capital, long-term for fixed assets.
- [ ] C2 Debt keeps ownership intact but interest and repayments must be met even in a weak year; equity has no obligation to repay or pay interest but dilutes ownership and profit.
- [ ] C3 Start-ups without assets or a trading record struggle to get bank lending and turn to equity, grants or crowdfunding.
- [ ] C4 Owners weigh cost, control and risk together; the cheapest source is not always the best.
- [ ] C5 Cost, control and security stated for each source (the outline asks for what it is, cost, control, security, advantages, disadvantages).
- [ ] C6 Start-up: owner's savings, family and friends, micro-loans, grants, because outside investors see high risk.
- [ ] C7 Growth: venture capital, bank loans, share issues, for larger sums.
- [ ] C8 Maturity: mostly retained earnings; long-term debt or share issues for large projects.
- [ ] C9 A track record makes lenders more willing to lend, which reduces risk for both sides.

Cross-cutting angle
- [ ] X1 Ethical: honest disclosure to lenders and investors; treating suppliers fairly by not paying late; investors who prefer ethical or sustainable businesses; the reputational and legal weight of late payment of suppliers (see section 2).
- [ ] X2 Sustainability: lenders and investors increasingly ask about environmental, social and governance (ESG) performance (definition in section 2); finance to fund energy-efficient or low-carbon investment; leasing equipment can support reuse and a circular economy.
- [ ] X3 Digital: fintech (definition in section 2) lets businesses apply for loans, invoice finance and crowdfunding online; online platforms and faster decisions; risks of unfamiliar online lenders and fraud.

## 3. Traps

- Do not use: a specific interest rate, fee, grant amount or loan limit (other than the dated Microfinance Ireland figure), because they change and were not verified.
- Do not use: a claim that debentures are always secured. Say "usually secured on the business's assets"; a debenture can be unsecured, so the word "usually" matters.
- Do not use: named banks, venture capital firms, crowdfunding sites or angel networks, because none was checked.
- Do not use: balance-sheet analysis, gearing or debt/equity ratios, because they are old-syllabus only (Appendix A4).
- Do not use: personal finance sources (personal loans, credit unions for households, mortgages), because they belong to the consumer chapter; this subsection is business finance only.
- Confusable: factoring (the factor collects the debts) versus invoice discounting (the business collects). Hire purchase (owns at the end) versus leasing (never owns). Overdraft (flexible, current account) versus term loan (fixed instalments). Venture capital (specialist firms, high-growth) versus business angels (wealthy individuals, early stage). Debentures (loan, fixed interest) versus shares (ownership, dividends).
- Confusable: retained earnings are profit kept in the business, not cash set aside in a bank account; grants are not loans.
- Confusable: equity is not repaid; share capital is not a loan.
- Boundary: state schemes, Enterprise Ireland and Local Enterprise Office supports belong to the notes on government supports; refer in one line and do not repeat them. Costs, revenue and break-even belong to the notes on costs and revenue streams. Forecasting cash and remedies belong to the notes on cash-flow analysis (invoice discounting and overdraft appear there as remedies; here they are the sources). The operational model's resources and partners belong to the notes on the operational model.

## 4. What exams ask

- Appendix 1 verbs: **identify** ("recognise patterns, facts, or details; provide an answer from a number of possibilities; recognize and state briefly a distinguishing fact or feature"), **outline**, **explain**, **discuss** and **evaluate** (for choosing a source). The syllabus word "explore" (used in the lifecycle outcome) is not in the Appendix 1 glossary; the author's own reading is to teach it to explain-depth.
- The outline records no 2025 sample-paper question on this subsection. Do not claim one. Finance questions in the SEC style are likely to give a business's situation (age, size, need) and ask which source suits and why.
- Identify answer: the source, its term (short, medium, long) and one reason it fits. Common mistake: naming a source with no link to the firm.
- Explain or discuss answer: three or four developed points that cover cost, control, risk or security and the term, with a balanced conclusion for this firm. Common mistake: listing advantages only, or giving the definition of the source without the fit.
- Lifecycle answer: stage, source, reason (risk as seen by lenders, size of sum, track record). Common mistake: naming sources for a stage without the reason investors or lenders behave that way.
- Matching finance to purpose: a short-term source for working capital, a long-term source for fixed assets. Common mistake: using an overdraft to buy premises.
- Mix-ups to avoid: confusing leasing with hire purchase, and equity with debt.
- Applying the cross-cutting themes: add an ethical, ESG or digital point (online lending, crowdfunding, sustainability-linked investment) where the stimulus allows.
- Do not invent marks or frequencies.
