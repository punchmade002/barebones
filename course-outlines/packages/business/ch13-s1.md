# Package: ch13-s1 — Leadership, management, organisational culture and leadership styles

- **loId:** `ch13-s1` · **chapterId:** `ch13`
- **Syllabus:** NCCA Leaving Certificate Business specification (first examined June 2027), Strand 3, section 3.1 outcomes [3.1.1] distinguish between leadership and management in organisations; [3.1.2] analyse the significance of organisational culture and innovation in successful organisations; [3.1.3] outline how leadership styles foster organisational culture and organisational innovation; and [3.5.1] analyse news about leadership ("Applying my learning"). Outline scope: leadership vs management with at least five differences; the roles of leaders and managers; organisational culture (values, norms, behaviours) and what shapes it; how culture drives innovation and success; the styles autocratic, democratic, laissez-faire, transformational, servant and transactional, each with its effect on culture and innovation and when it suits; one or two current leadership news stories. Nothing here is marked Higher Level only; write for both levels at discuss/evaluate depth.
- **Prepared by:** a higher model, 2026-11-23. The writer works only from this package and `course-outlines/NOTES_STANDARD.md`.

## 1. Must cover (one bullet or block for every item; tick each off)

Assumed knowledge
- [ ] A1 (assumed) An organisation is a group of people working towards shared objectives with limited resources; it needs both direction and day-to-day running.
- [ ] A2 (assumed) Stakeholders (owners, employees, customers) are affected by how an organisation is led.
- [ ] A3 (assumed) Innovation means introducing new or improved products, services or processes.

Leadership and management
- [ ] L1 Management defined: planning, organising, directing and controlling resources to achieve set objectives efficiently.
- [ ] L2 Leadership defined: setting a direction and influencing people to follow it, often to bring about change.
- [ ] L3 Roles of a manager: plan, organise, staff, direct and control operations so that targets for output, quality and budget are met.
- [ ] L4 Roles of a leader: set direction, inspire staff, champion change, shape the culture by modelling values, develop people.
- [ ] L5 A person can lead while managing, or manage without leading; the distinction is the activity, not the job title.
- [ ] L6 Difference 1, vision and execution: leaders set a vision; managers turn plans into day-to-day action and schedules.
- [ ] L7 Difference 2, influence and authority: leaders win followers through trust and inspiration; managers rely mainly on formal authority.
- [ ] L8 Difference 3, change and stability: leaders drive change and innovation; managers keep operations consistent and predictable.
- [ ] L9 Difference 4, questions asked: leaders ask what and why; managers ask how and when.
- [ ] L10 Difference 5, time horizon: leaders take the long-term view; managers focus on short- and medium-term targets and budgets.
- [ ] L11 Difference 6, relationships with staff: leaders motivate people to commit to goals; managers organise tasks, allocate resources, monitor performance.

Organisational culture and innovation
- [ ] C1 Organisational culture defined: the shared values, norms and behaviours that shape how people think and act in an organisation.
- [ ] C2 Values: beliefs about what matters (customer service, safety, innovation).
- [ ] C3 Norms: unwritten rules about acceptable behaviour (for example whether staff challenge decisions in meetings).
- [ ] C4 Behaviours: visible actions (how staff treat customers or respond to a mistake).
- [ ] C5 What shapes culture: founders, the actions of leaders, the firm's history, its industry and the wider national environment.
- [ ] C6 A culture that rewards experimentation and tolerates failure encourages new products and processes.
- [ ] C7 A culture that punishes mistakes makes staff hide problems and avoid new ideas, so it blocks innovation.
- [ ] C8 How a strong culture contributes to success: aligns behaviour with goals, consistent decisions without waiting for instructions, attracts and keeps staff, supports innovation. Give three developed points for an "analyse three ways" question.
- [ ] C9 Irish example of a culture linked to success: Ryanair's culture of cost control (see section 2).

Leadership styles (for each: what it is, when it suits, effect on culture, effect on innovation)
- [ ] S1 Autocratic: leader decides alone; fast, clear direction; suits emergencies, crises, inexperienced staff needing close supervision; culture: low trust, dependence, reluctance to speak up; innovation: ideas rarely sought, limited.
- [ ] S2 Democratic: staff views gathered (meetings, surveys, teams, sometimes a vote) before the leader decides; suits complex problems and where commitment matters; culture: trust, belonging, ownership; innovation: more ideas and support for change, but slow and may compromise.
- [ ] S3 Laissez-faire: broad goals then freedom, minimal supervision; suits highly skilled, experienced, self-motivated staff such as research teams; culture: independence but confusion, uneven standards, weak accountability; innovation: freedom to experiment but ideas may not be developed.
- [ ] S4 Transformational: inspires with a shared vision, challenges assumptions, develops staff; suits major change, intense competition, reinventing products; culture: commitment, shared purpose, learning culture; innovation: most closely linked; limitation: relies on the leader's credibility and stalls without practical plans.
- [ ] S5 Servant: needs of staff, customers and community first; leader listens, develops staff, removes obstacles; suits care, healthcare, education, social enterprises; culture: trust, loyalty, ethical, safe to speak up; innovation: staff willing to share ideas, but slower for radical change.
- [ ] S6 Transactional: rewards and penalties linked to targets, clear rules; suits routine operations, production lines, compliance-heavy work; culture: clear expectations and order, but staff controlled and target-focused; innovation: limited.
- [ ] S7 The spec says "one leadership style you have studied": give enough on all six that a student can choose any, and say a leader may mix styles to suit the situation.

Ethical, sustainability and digital angle
- [ ] E1 Ethical: leaders model ethical behaviour and set the ethical culture (honesty, fair treatment, speaking up safely); servant and democratic styles support an ethical culture; autocratic and strictly transactional pressure can tempt staff to cut corners to hit targets.
- [ ] E2 Sustainability: leaders set whether sustainability is a core value (ESG goals, waste and energy targets); a culture that values it shapes decisions; innovation in greener products and processes depends on a culture that rewards experimentation.
- [ ] E3 Digital: leaders drive digital transformation (new systems, automation, AI tools); transformational leaders sell the vision of the change, managers run the rollout; culture decides whether staff adopt or resist new technology; remote and digital teams need clear communication and trust.

Applying my learning [3.5.1]
- [ ] N1 One current Irish leadership news story, with month and year and source (section 2), and how to analyse it: who the leader is, which style or leadership-versus-management point it shows, and the effect on culture and stakeholders.

## 3. Traps

- Do not use: names of leadership theorists, books, surveys or study results (for example claims such as "research shows 70% of staff..."), because none is checked and the outline needs none.
- Do not use: figures for Ryanair (passengers, profit, fleet, number of staff) or quotations from its chief executive, because they change and are unchecked.
- Do not use: the old-syllabus framing of management as the topic "planning, organising and controlling" with organisation structures (functional, matrix, span of control), because Appendix A4 says the specification does not teach it. Planning, organising, directing and controlling may appear only inside the one-line definition of management and the manager's role.
- Do not use: Maslow, McGregor or other motivation theories as content, because they are old-syllabus topics; motivation belongs to the neighbouring subject of managing people.
- Confusable: leadership vs management. Not "leaders are good, managers are bad": organisations need both. Answers that give a one-sided list score poorly; give the matching pair for each difference.
- Confusable: democratic vs laissez-faire. In democratic leadership the leader still decides after consulting; in laissez-faire the staff decide how the work is done with little direction.
- Confusable: transformational vs transactional. Transformational inspires with a vision and challenges staff; transactional trades rewards and penalties for meeting targets.
- Confusable: servant vs democratic. Servant puts staff needs first and supports them; democratic involves staff in the decision itself.
- Confusable: culture vs climate/atmosphere; culture means the shared values, norms and behaviours, not just mood.
- Belongs to a neighbouring subsection: workplace conflict, its causes and effects, grievance and disciplinary procedures, negotiation, conciliation, mediation, arbitration, adjudication and industrial action belong to the notes on conflict in the workplace and its resolution. Motivation, appraisal and corporate wellness belong to the chapter on leading and managing people. Planning for change and resistance to change belong to the chapter on the rationale for planning.
- Do not name a style as "best". The notes should say each suits certain situations; a good answer matches the style to the situation.

## 4. What exams ask

- Sample paper (Higher, Sample 2): outline how one leadership style fosters innovation; examine three differences between leadership and management. Sample 1: analyse three ways culture contributed to a firm's success.
- Command words (Appendix 1): **distinguish** (make the differences clear: state both sides of each difference), **analyse** (break down into parts, relate each to the firm and reach a conclusion), **outline** (the main points only, restricted to essentials), **examine** (look closely at the idea, uncover relationships and assumptions), **identify** (state briefly).
- Distinguish/examine leadership and management: expect three differences. A full answer names the difference and states what the leader does and what the manager does, with a short example. Common mistakes: listing only leader qualities; giving two differences that are the same point reworded (for example "vision" and "long-term"); treating "leader" and "manager" as job titles.
- Outline how a style fosters innovation: choose one style, say what the leader does, then link to culture (ideas sought, risk tolerated, trust) and to innovation (new ideas, products, processes). A full answer has the mechanism, not just the style definition. Common mistakes: defining the style only; choosing a style and saying it does not foster innovation without saying why it still can; no link to culture.
- Analyse ways culture contributed to success: three developed points, each stating a feature of the culture (values, norms or behaviours), the effect on staff behaviour or customers, and the result for the firm. Common mistakes: describing culture in general; three points that overlap; no link to the firm in the stimulus.
- A short true/false or fill-in may test a definition, for example which style suits a crisis or which gives staff freedom; learn the six one-line definitions exactly.
- Higher-level answers evaluate: state when a style works and its limitation (speed, slow consultation, over-reliance on the leader), and justify a view.
- News questions: identify the leadership issue in a given headline, apply a style or the leader/manager distinction, and give an informed opinion on the effect for stakeholders.
- Tip material: "give a pair for every difference", "link style to culture to innovation", "name a limitation".
