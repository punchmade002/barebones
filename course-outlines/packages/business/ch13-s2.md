# Package: ch13-s2 — Conflict in the workplace and its resolution

- **loId:** `ch13-s2` · **chapterId:** `ch13`
- **Syllabus:** NCCA Leaving Certificate Business specification (first examined June 2027), Strand 3, section 3.1 outcomes [3.1.4] appreciate the range of reasons for conflict in the workplace and demonstrate an understanding of how conflict may impact on the workplace; [3.1.5] analyse how both employees and employers may deal with conflict internally; [3.1.6] outline different external approaches to conflict resolution (the specification names conciliation and arbitration as external approaches). Outline scope: causes (pay and conditions, workload, change, poor communication, personality clashes, discrimination, unclear roles); effects, positive and negative; internal approaches (grievance and disciplinary procedures, negotiation, collective bargaining, the shop steward, HR, internal mediation); external approaches (conciliation, mediation, arbitration, adjudication) through the Workplace Relations Commission and the Labour Court; types of industrial action as assumed knowledge; plus one or two current Irish news examples under "Applying my learning". Nothing is marked Higher Level only.
- **Prepared by:** a higher model, 2026-11-23. The writer works only from this package and `course-outlines/NOTES_STANDARD.md`.

## 1. Must cover (one bullet or block for every item; tick each off)

Assumed knowledge
- [ ] A1 (assumed) Employer, employee and trade union roles; a trade union is an organisation of workers that represents their interests.
- [ ] A2 (assumed) An employment contract sets pay, hours and duties.
- [ ] A3 (assumed) Industrial action: a strike is a withdrawal of labour; a work-to-rule means doing only what the contract strictly requires; an overtime ban means refusing extra hours. It is usually a last resort and Irish law sets conditions on when it is lawful.

Causes (explain each with an example)
- [ ] C1 Conflict arises when the interests, expectations or perceptions of employees and employers clash.
- [ ] C2 Pay and conditions: wages, bonuses, hours, holidays, pensions.
- [ ] C3 Workload: excessive or unevenly shared work.
- [ ] C4 Change: new technology, restructuring or new methods create fear of job loss and resistance.
- [ ] C5 Poor communication: unclear, late or missing messages lead to misunderstanding and rumour.
- [ ] C6 Personality clashes: differences in working style or temperament.
- [ ] C7 Discrimination: unfair treatment based on a protected characteristic (for example gender, race or age) creates grievances.
- [ ] C8 Unclear roles: overlapping or undefined responsibilities; disputes over who does what, or tasks left undone.

Effects
- [ ] F1 Positive: well-handled disagreement can bring new ideas, challenge poor practice and improve decisions.
- [ ] F2 Absenteeism reduces output.
- [ ] F3 Low morale reduces motivation, commitment and co-operation.
- [ ] F4 Staff turnover raises recruitment and training costs.
- [ ] F5 Industrial action halts or slows production and damages customer relationships.
- [ ] F6 Reputational damage makes customers and potential employees less willing to deal with the firm.

Internal approaches (analyse how employees and employers deal with conflict inside the organisation)
- [ ] I1 Internal approaches come first: usually faster, cheaper and more private.
- [ ] I2 Grievance procedure: formal process for an employee to raise a complaint; the employer must investigate and respond.
- [ ] I3 Disciplinary procedure: formal process for poor conduct or performance, with warnings and a chance for the employee to reply.
- [ ] I4 Negotiation: the parties discuss positions directly and seek a compromise.
- [ ] I5 Collective bargaining: negotiation between employers and trade unions over pay and conditions for a group of workers.
- [ ] I6 The shop steward: a trade union representative in the workplace who represents members and raises their concerns with management.
- [ ] I7 Human resources: advises on policy, handles complaints, helps managers deal with disputes fairly.
- [ ] I8 Internal mediation: a neutral person within the organisation helps the parties communicate and agree.
- [ ] I9 How the steps fit together: informal talk, then formal grievance or disciplinary procedure, with fair procedures at each stage (see section 2 for the Code of Practice).

External approaches (outline each; know who decides)
- [ ] X1 Conciliation: a neutral third party helps both sides reach their own agreement; meets the sides separately and together; imposes nothing and issues no recommendation; voluntary; in Ireland provided by the Workplace Relations Commission.
- [ ] X2 Mediation: voluntary process where a neutral mediator helps the parties communicate and find common ground; focus on the relationship; suits colleagues who must keep working together; can settle a dispute before a formal hearing.
- [ ] X3 Arbitration: a third party hears evidence and arguments from both sides and makes the decision; the parties do not write the settlement; usually agreed in advance, in which case the decision is generally binding; more formal, with hearings and a written decision.
- [ ] X4 Adjudication: a formal decision by an official body under the law (an adjudication officer of the Workplace Relations Commission hears an individual complaint and issues a decision); does not depend on advance agreement because the process is set by law.
- [ ] X5 Appeals and the Labour Court: decisions on individual complaints can generally be appealed to the Labour Court; the Labour Court also issues recommendations on industrial relations disputes referred to it.
- [ ] X6 External approaches are normally used when internal procedures have failed or the parties cannot agree.
- [ ] X7 Key contrast: conciliation and mediation, the parties control the outcome; arbitration and adjudication, a third party decides.
- [ ] X8 Industrial action (assumed, see A3) is a last resort after internal and external procedures.

Ethical, sustainability and digital angle
- [ ] E1 Ethical: fair procedures (the right to be heard and to be represented), confidentiality, non-victimisation of those who raise a grievance, treating discrimination and bullying complaints seriously; ethical cost of ignoring conflict (stress, harm to wellbeing).
- [ ] E2 Sustainability: a workplace that resolves conflict early keeps staff and skills (lower turnover), supports wellbeing and long-term viability; unresolved conflict and strikes can threaten a firm's survival and jobs.
- [ ] E3 Digital: digital communication can cause conflict (unclear messages, always-on expectations, remote-working misunderstandings, monitoring of staff) and can help resolve it (online hearings and mediation, HR case-management systems, clear records); data protection applies to complaint records.

Applying my learning
- [ ] N1 One or two current Irish news examples linked to conflict at work, with month and year and source (section 2), and how to analyse them: the cause, which procedure was used, who decides.

## 3. Traps

- Do not use: "conciliation produces a recommendation" or "conciliator decides", because it is wrong; in conciliation the parties reach their own agreement and the conciliator imposes nothing. (The sample paper true/false item tests exactly this and the right answer is false.) Labour Court recommendations come at a later stage, not from the conciliator.
- Do not use: "arbitration is always binding", because it is doubtful; say the decision is generally binding where the parties agreed in advance to accept it.
- Do not use: figures for WRC or Labour Court fees, case volumes, waiting times, redress amounts or the number of cases in a year, because they change and are unchecked.
- Do not use: the names of old bodies the WRC replaced (such as the Labour Relations Commission, Rights Commissioner Service, Equality Tribunal, National Employment Rights Authority), because the notes need not use them and they are unchecked; say "the WRC replaced several older bodies" at most.
- Do not use: specific Irish firms or strikes from memory as examples (for example named airline, bus or transport disputes), because none is checked; only the news example in section 2 is allowed.
- Do not use: the old-syllabus industrial relations machinery as a unit, such as the Rights Commissioner service, Employment Appeals Tribunal or contract-law remedies; Appendix A4 lists contract law as not taught.
- Confusable: conciliation vs mediation. Both are voluntary and the parties settle it themselves; conciliation is the WRC's service for industrial relations disputes, mediation focuses on communication and relationships, often between colleagues. Treat them as two terms with the definitions given.
- Confusable: conciliation vs arbitration. The parties decide in the first; the third party decides in the second. This is the most tested contrast.
- Confusable: arbitration vs adjudication. Arbitration is usually agreed in advance by both sides; adjudication is a formal process set by law and does not depend on advance agreement.
- Confusable: grievance vs disciplinary procedure. A grievance is the employee's complaint against the employer or a colleague; a disciplinary procedure is the employer's action against the employee's conduct or performance.
- Confusable: negotiation vs collective bargaining. Negotiation can be one-to-one; collective bargaining is between employers and trade unions for a group of workers.
- Belongs to a neighbouring subsection: the roles of leaders and managers, culture and leadership styles belong to the notes on leadership, management, culture and leadership styles (a leader's style affects how conflict is handled, but do not teach styles here). Stakeholder conflict between firms and groups such as customers or the community belongs to the stakeholders chapter. Employee rights under legislation, such as unfair dismissal, discrimination law and the employee protections, belong to the strand on employment and work; here mention discrimination only as a cause of conflict. Motivation and appraisal belong to the chapter on leading and managing people.

## 4. What exams ask

- Sample paper (Higher, Sample 1): a true/false item stating that conciliation is a process where a third party issues a recommendation (false). Sample 2: a fill-in-the-blank on the trade union representative in the workplace (shop steward).
- Command words (Appendix 1): **identify** (state briefly: shop steward, WRC), **outline** (main points only: what conciliation, mediation or arbitration is and who decides), **explain** (detailed account with reasons or causes: a cause of conflict and its effect), **analyse** (break down how internal procedures work in sequence and reach a conclusion), **distinguish** (make the differences clear: conciliation versus arbitration), **appreciate/demonstrate** (the spec's wording for [3.1.4]: show the range of causes and effects).
- Causes and effects: expect two or three causes, each with an effect on the business. A full answer states the cause, gives a short situation, then the effect (morale, absenteeism, turnover, industrial action, reputation). Common mistake: naming causes without effects, or listing effects without a cause.
- Internal approaches: a full answer follows a sequence, such as informal discussion, grievance procedure, negotiation (with the shop steward if the employee is a union member), HR support, internal mediation, and says why internal first (faster, cheaper, private, keeps relationships). Common mistake: jumping straight to the WRC; listing procedures with no application to the stated dispute.
- External approaches: for each, say who the third party is, what it does and who controls the outcome, and give the Irish body (the WRC, the Labour Court). Common mistakes: saying the conciliator decides; confusing arbitration with adjudication; omitting that the Labour Court hears appeals.
- Contrast questions: state both items in the same sentence or paragraph, then the key difference (who controls the outcome), and use one short example.
- Higher-level evaluation: weigh internal against external approaches (speed, cost, privacy, relationship, binding outcome versus control over the outcome) and give a justified view, noting that industrial action is a last resort.
- News questions: identify the cause of the dispute in the story, name the procedure used or the next step, and say how the stakeholders (employees, employer, customers) are affected.
- Tip material: "state who decides", "internal first, then external", "link every cause to an effect", "name the WRC and the Labour Court".
