# Package: ch18-s3 — Fintech, Central Bank Digital Currency and crypto-assets

- **loId:** `ch18-s3` · **chapterId:** `ch18`
- **Syllabus:** NCCA Leaving Certificate Business specification (first examined June 2027), Strand 4, section 4.2, learning outcomes [4.2.4] and [4.2.8]. Students examine the opportunities and challenges of fintech for consumers and explain how technology affects the provision of financial products; and describe the advantages and disadvantages of Central Bank Digital Currency (CBDC) as an electronic equivalent of cash, how it differs from stablecoins and crypto-assets, and the potential impact of digital currency on consumers and businesses. Nothing here is Higher Level only.
- **Prepared by:** a higher model, 2026-11-23. The writer works only from this package and `course-outlines/NOTES_STANDARD.md`.

## 1. Must cover (one bullet or block for every item; tick each off)

Assumed knowledge
- [ ] A1 (assumed) Money does three jobs: a means of payment, a store of value and a unit of account. Cash is issued by the central bank; the money in a bank account is a claim on a commercial bank.
- [ ] A2 (assumed) A central bank (for the euro area, the European Central Bank (ECB) with the national central banks such as the Central Bank of Ireland) issues euro banknotes and holds banks' reserves.
- [ ] A3 (assumed) Consumers already pay through banks, cards and online services; interest and fees are paid for them.

Fintech [4.2.4]
- [ ] F1 Fintech defined (glossary quote in section 2): the use of technology to deliver financial services and products to consumers.
- [ ] F2 Examples: mobile banking (balances, transfers, bill payments by app); payment apps (person-to-person and shop or online payments linked to a bank account or card); neobanks (digital-only banks, no branches, app as the main channel); robo-advisers (algorithms build and manage a simple investment portfolio, usually at a lower cost than a human adviser); open banking (a customer gives a regulated third party secure access to their bank data so rival services can compete); buy-now-pay-later at online checkout.
- [ ] F3 Opportunities: lower fees, faster transactions, round-the-clock access, wider choice, financial inclusion for people without a nearby branch or with a thin credit history.
- [ ] F4 Challenges: digital exclusion (older people, no internet or low confidence), fraud and security (account takeover, phishing, data breaches), weaker protection from unregulated or overseas firms, easy credit and gamified investing encouraging impulsive spending and risk-taking, so financial literacy matters.
- [ ] F5 How technology affects the provision of products: automation (cheaper processing, lending decisions in minutes); data analytics (pricing and creditworthiness using more data: can widen access or exclude some groups); digital channels replacing branches (lower provider costs, less personal contact); distributed ledger technology (a shared database maintained by many computers, underpinning crypto-assets and some payment systems).
- [ ] F6 Data privacy and algorithm fairness: fintech depends on personal data (ethical angle; the GDPR is the law on this and is taught in the notes on consumer and data protection, so name it only in one line).

CBDC [4.2.8]
- [ ] C1 CBDC defined (glossary quote in section 2): an electronic form of cash for the digitalised world; central bank money in digital format; complements banknotes and coins.
- [ ] C2 Because a CBDC is a liability of the central bank, holding it carries no credit risk, as with cash.
- [ ] C3 The ECB's digital euro project: a proposed CBDC for the euro area, designed to sit alongside cash. Status as of 2026 in section 2: preparation phase, no decision yet to issue.
- [ ] C4 Advantages: a safe digital payment option available to everyone; less reliance on private payment networks; possibly lower costs and faster settlement; inclusion for people without bank accounts.
- [ ] C5 Disadvantages: privacy concerns if payment data could be seen; cyber-security risk; cost and system change; disintermediation of banks.
- [ ] C6 Disintermediation: if people move large sums into a CBDC, banks lose deposits that fund their lending.

Stablecoins and crypto-assets
- [ ] D1 Stablecoin: a crypto-asset whose value depends on its issuer's pledge to redeem it at a stated value (glossary), usually backed by reserves.
- [ ] D2 Crypto-asset: no single identifiable entity is liable for it (glossary); price set by supply and demand on trading platforms, so volatile.
- [ ] D3 Comparison table (CBDC vs stablecoin vs crypto-asset): who issues it, who is liable, what backs the value, price stability, regulated protection, legal tender or not.
- [ ] D4 Two differences between CBDC and crypto-assets for the exam: issuer and liability (central bank vs no liable entity); stability of value (the digital euro would be euro, a crypto-asset's price swings); regulatory protection.

Impact of digital currency on consumers and businesses
- [ ] M1 Consumers gain: lower transaction costs, faster settlement, easier access to payments, possibly for people without bank accounts.
- [ ] M2 Consumers lose: privacy if payment data is collected; money lost to scams in unregulated crypto markets.
- [ ] M3 Businesses gain: faster settlement, possibly lower payment costs, new digital channels to reach customers.
- [ ] M4 Businesses face: cyber-security costs, changing systems to accept digital payments, learning cost.
- [ ] M5 Banks and payment firms: competition from fintech and a CBDC; business models may change.

Crypto risks and EU regulation
- [ ] X1 Risks: price volatility; fraud (fake exchanges, pump-and-dump schemes, phishing aimed at wallet holders); loss of access (lost private key or password may mean permanent loss); limited protection (outside the deposit guarantee scheme); money laundering and tax evasion risk (anti-money laundering rules).
- [ ] X2 MiCA: the EU Markets in Crypto-Assets Regulation sets authorisation and conduct rules for crypto-asset service providers and issuers of stablecoin-type tokens; the Central Bank of Ireland is the competent authority in Ireland.

Ethics, sustainability, digital
- [ ] E1 Ethical: financial inclusion vs exclusion; consumer privacy and surveillance in digital money; marketing of risky crypto and gamified apps to young people; responsibility of providers.
- [ ] E2 Sustainability: energy use of some crypto-assets (proof-of-work mining) compared with other systems (general statement; give no figures); fintech reducing paper and branch travel.
- [ ] E3 Digital: this subsection is itself the digital angle; link to cyber-security and digital transformation of businesses.

## 3. Traps

- Do not use: "MiCA fully applies from 2024 to all crypto, so crypto is now safe", because MiCA brings providers under authorisation and conduct rules but does not remove volatility or guarantee holders' money. State that protection is limited.
- Do not use: any statement that a digital euro "has been introduced", "is launching" or has a fixed launch date, because the ECB has not decided to issue it and the legislation is not adopted; the 2027 and 2029 dates are targets that depend on legislation.
- Do not use: any statement that the digital euro would replace cash, because the ECB and the specification's glossary both say it complements cash.
- Do not use: a MiCA transitional deadline of 1 July 2026 or any other transitional date, because it was not checked against a primary source.
- Do not use: any price, market value, user number or fee for any crypto-asset, fintech app or neobank, because they change and were not checked.
- Confusable: CBDC (issued by the central bank, its liability, digital cash) vs stablecoin (private issuer's pledge to redeem at a stated value) vs crypto-asset (no single liable issuer, value set by supply and demand) vs a commercial bank balance (a claim on a bank, covered by the deposit guarantee scheme up to a limit; the limit is in the notes on regulation).
- Confusable: fintech (the technology and the firms using it) vs a neobank (one type of fintech bank). Open banking (data sharing with consent) vs mobile banking (an app of your own bank). Digital currency vs electronic payment by card or app (which uses existing bank money, not a new kind of money).
- Confusable: distributed ledger technology vs "crypto": the ledger is the technology, a crypto-asset is one use of it.
- Boundary: the Central Bank's regulatory roles, the Consumer Protection Code, the deposit guarantee scheme, the Ombudsman and types of fraud (phishing, smishing, vishing) belong to the subsection on regulation and fraud. Here mention fraud and MiCA only as risks and rules for crypto.
- Boundary: product choice (saving, investing, borrowing, AER, APR) belongs to the first subsection; risk tolerance, reliable information, switching and credit ratings to the second. BNPL is a borrowing product there and a fintech example here.

## 4. What exams ask

- Appendix 1 verbs: **Describe**: "give a detailed account of the main points of a topic using words, diagrams, examples and/or images where appropriate". **Explain**: "give a detailed account including reasons or causes". **Examine**: "look closely at arguments, data, information, concepts and/or stories in order to uncover origins, assumptions, perspectives, trends and/or relationships". **Distinguish**: "make the differences between two or more concepts or items clear". **Discuss**: "offer a considered, balanced review that includes a range of arguments, factors or hypotheses and is supported by appropriate evidence".
- Seen on a 2025 sample paper (Sample 2): describe two differences between CBDC and crypto-assets; explain one impact of digital currency on consumers and one on businesses. A full answer for the differences gives each difference as a pair (CBDC: issued by the central bank and its liability; crypto-asset: no single identifiable entity liable) and developed with the effect on the holder (who is responsible if something goes wrong; whether value is stable). For the impacts, name the impact, explain why it happens, and say who it affects (for example, faster settlement means a business receives money sooner and improves cash flow). Common mistakes: describing two features of crypto only; saying CBDC and crypto are "both digital money" and stopping; mixing up stablecoin and CBDC; giving a benefit with no explanation; giving the same impact for consumers and businesses.
- Likely related asks (specification wording; no past frequency known): examine opportunities and challenges of fintech for consumers (give a mix, include inclusion and exclusion, security, protection); explain how technology affects the provision of a product; discuss advantages and disadvantages of CBDC.
- Tip: always give the reason ("because the central bank issues it, there is no credit risk").
- Tip: keep a balanced answer for "discuss": two points on each side, then a judgement.
- Common mistake: naming apps from memory instead of explaining the type of service.
