# Package: ch2-s2 — Forms of ownership and how ownership changes

- **loId:** `ch2-s2` · **chapterId:** `ch2`
- **Syllabus:** NCCA Leaving Certificate Business specification (first examined June 2027), Strand 1, section 1.2, learning outcome [1.2.2]. Students learn about the nature of business ownership and how it may evolve over the lifecycle of a business, including nationalisation and privatisation: sole traders, partnerships, private limited companies, public listed companies, franchises, co-operatives and public sector organisations; and outline how ownership differs and how it can change. Outline Cover: for each of the seven forms: ownership, liability (limited versus unlimited), control, finance, legal formation (Companies Act 2014 and the CRO), profit distribution, advantages and disadvantages, an Irish example; the typical path from sole trader to company to PLC (flotation); nationalisation and privatisation defined, with reasons, effects and Irish examples. No Higher Level only content.
- **Prepared by:** a higher model, 2026-11-23. The writer works only from this package and `course-outlines/NOTES_STANDARD.md`.

## 1. Must cover (one bullet or block for every item; tick each off)

Assumed knowledge
- [ ] A1 (assumed) Profit, dividend, share, shareholder, director: a share is a unit of ownership in a company; a dividend is a share of profit paid to shareholders; directors manage a company.
- [ ] A2 (assumed) Liability means responsibility for a business's debts. Unlimited liability: personal assets can be used. Limited liability: only the amount invested or agreed to be invested is at risk.
- [ ] A3 (assumed) Finance sources (savings, loans, retained profit, share capital) as one-line recall only; sources of finance are taught in their own chapter.
- [ ] A4 (assumed) The four enterprise types (private, public, semi-state, not-for-profit), as the setting for "forms of ownership".

Each form: ownership, liability, control, finance, formation, profit, advantages, disadvantages, example
- [ ] F1 Sole trader: one owner and decision-maker; unlimited liability; keeps all profit after tax; finance from savings, bank loans, retained profit; no company registration to start, but registration with the CRO if trading under a name other than the owner's own; advantages (simplicity, full control, few public filing requirements); disadvantages (unlimited liability, limited capital, business ends when owner stops). Example: a local plumber or hairdresser (unnamed sample).
- [ ] F2 Partnership: two or more owners sharing profit, losses and responsibility under a partnership agreement; each partner has unlimited liability, and partners are generally liable for debts another partner runs up on the firm's behalf; finance from partners' capital and loans; advantages (shared skills, capital, workload); disadvantages (disagreements, one partner's mistake harms all). Example: firms of solicitors and accountants (general statement, no named firm).
- [ ] F3 Private limited company: a separate legal person owned by shareholders; "Ltd" in the name; shares cannot be offered to the public; shareholders vote at general meetings, directors manage day to day; limited liability; formed by registration with the CRO under the Companies Act 2014; must keep records and file annual returns; profit paid as dividends in proportion to shares after costs and tax; finance from share capital, retained profit, bank loans; advantages (limited liability, separate legal identity, continuity); disadvantages (compliance costs, public filing of accounts). Example: family builders and retailers (unnamed).
- [ ] F4 Public limited company (plc): shares offered to the public and tradable on a stock exchange; ownership spread, so ownership and day-to-day control are separated; limited liability; raises large finance by issuing shares; profit paid as dividends and reflected in share price; advantages (large-scale capital, high profile); disadvantages (strict reporting and governance rules, listing cost, pressure for short-term results). Examples from the checked rows.
- [ ] F5 Franchise: franchisee pays a franchisor for the right to trade under its brand and system; franchisor owns the brand, product and operating system; franchisee owns and runs the outlet and funds premises and equipment; upfront fee plus ongoing royalties, usually a share of sales; must follow the franchisor's standards; franchisor gives training, marketing and support; advantages (proven brand, lower risk); disadvantages (royalties cut profit, limited independence). Example from the checked rows.
- [ ] F6 Co-operative: owned and democratically controlled by members who use, supply or work in it; one vote per member usually, regardless of capital; surplus shared in proportion to trade with the co-operative or reinvested; finance from member shares, loans and retained surplus, harder to attract outside investors; advantages (member loyalty, democratic control, member focus); disadvantages (slower decisions, conflict between members). Examples from the checked rows.
- [ ] F7 Public sector organisation: owned by the State, funded mainly by taxation, service aim not profit; ministers, boards or statute set objectives and powers; no shareholders and no profit distributed; advantages (universal service, stability); disadvantages (slower decisions, bureaucracy, political influence). Example from the checked rows.
- [ ] F8 A comparison table of the seven forms on ownership, liability, control, finance, profit and example (an easy exam revision tool).

How ownership changes
- [ ] O1 The typical path: sole trader, then partners added or incorporation as a private limited company (limited liability and more capital), then flotation as a plc.
- [ ] O2 Flotation: a private company offers its shares to the public for the first time and lists them on a stock exchange, becoming a plc.
- [ ] O3 Other changes: family succession, management buyout (managers buy the business), sale to another company, merger.
- [ ] O4 The reverse direction: a plc bought out by private investors and taken off the stock exchange.
- [ ] O5 Reasons for change: need for finance, reducing the founder's personal risk, succession planning, an exit for founders who want to sell their stake.

Nationalisation and privatisation
- [ ] N1 Nationalisation: transfer of ownership of a privately owned enterprise to the State, usually by buying its shares or by compulsory acquisition with compensation. Reasons: protect an essential service, control a natural monopoly, rescue a failing firm whose collapse would damage the economy. Effects: government control over prices and investment, a fiscal cost, greater public influence over services.
- [ ] N2 Privatisation: sale of a State-owned enterprise to private investors, for example through a public share offer or a trade sale. Reasons: raise funds, improve efficiency through competition, reduce the State's role in business. Effects: more competition and choice, but a risk of higher prices or weaker provision of services the public needs.
- [ ] N3 Irish examples from the checked rows (a bank rescue; a telecoms flotation; a current sale).

Ethical, sustainability and digital angle
- [ ] X1 Ethics: unlimited liability puts the owner's family home at risk; limited liability can shift losses onto creditors; directors' duties; fairness to staff and customers in a sale or buyout; whether essential services (health, energy, water) suit private ownership.
- [ ] X2 Sustainability: co-operatives and social-purpose structures can put member and community benefit ahead of short-term profit; plcs face short-term pressure but also the strictest sustainability reporting (link only by title to the notes on ESG).
- [ ] X3 Digital: forming a company and filing returns are done online with the CRO; a franchise or plc can scale through digital sales channels. One general sentence each, with no named platform.

## 3. Traps

- Do not use: "Shareholders of a private limited company vote at the annual general meeting" as a rule, because a private company may not need to hold an AGM; say shareholders vote on major decisions at general meetings.
- Do not use: "private limited companies must file public accounts" as a flat statement, because filing duties differ with company size under the Companies Act 2014; say "files annual returns with the CRO".
- Do not use: "Allied Irish Banks was nationalised" or any percentage for AIB, because only the rescue and the 2025 sale of the State's remaining directed shareholding were checked.
- Do not use: Bank of Ireland as a nationalisation example, because the State did not take it over; it only held a minority stake that it sold in 2022.
- Do not use: Subway fees, royalty percentages or start-up costs, because they conflict across sources.
- Do not use: "Telecom Éireann was sold to private investors" with any amount raised, because the sources disagree on proceeds.
- Do not use: a named Irish example of a partnership, a family company or a natural monopoly, because none was checked.
- Do not use: "limited by guarantee", "unlimited company" or "DAC" as extra forms to learn, because the specification lists seven forms; mention DAC only in one line, to stop students confusing it with an LTD.
- Confusable: private limited company (Ltd, shares cannot be offered to the public) versus public limited company (plc, shares can be offered and listed). "Public limited" does not mean State-owned.
- Confusable: public sector organisation (State-owned, tax-funded) versus public limited company (privately owned, shares tradable).
- Confusable: nationalisation (private to State) versus privatisation (State to private). Both change ownership, in opposite directions.
- Confusable: franchisor (owns the brand) versus franchisee (runs the outlet and pays).
- Confusable: flotation (first public sale of shares) versus any later share sale.
- Belongs to a neighbouring subsection: the four enterprise types and their contribution (the notes on public, private, semi-state and not-for-profit enterprises); regulation, audit, governance and ESG (the notes on regulation, governance and ESG); sources of finance and business plans (other chapters).
- Do not teach the old 1996 syllabus content on contract law or on final accounts and ratios (Appendix A4).

## 4. What exams ask

Appendix 1 verbs for [1.2.2]: outline (the main points only), distinguish, explain, discuss. The 2025 sample papers test this subsection once: a fill-in-the-blank in Question 3 on Sample 1 where the missing word is "privatisation" (selling off state-owned enterprises). No other question is recorded, so the rest is drawn from the verbs.

- Fill-in or true/false on definitions: privatisation (sale of State-owned enterprises), nationalisation (State takes over private ones), flotation, limited and unlimited liability. A full answer is the exact word. Common mistake: reversing nationalisation and privatisation.
- Outline how ownership differs between forms: for each form named, state who owns it, who is liable for debts, and who controls it. A full answer is a short, accurate point for each of ownership, liability and control, not a long account of one form. Common mistake: mixing up the liability of a partnership and a company.
- Distinguish between two forms (for example a private limited company and a plc, or a sole trader and a partnership): a difference on each point, with both forms named in each sentence. Common mistake: describing one form only.
- Explain why a business changes ownership: link a reason (needs finance, reduce personal risk, succession, exit) to the form it moves to, with its effect. Common mistake: listing forms with no reason.
- Explain or discuss nationalisation or privatisation: a definition, a reason, an effect on a named stakeholder (consumers, employees, taxpayers), and an Irish example from the checked list. Common mistake: only reasons, with no effects, or no example.
- Choose a suitable form for a described business (applied question): pick one form, justify it with the stated facts (capital needed, risk, control wanted), and say why a rival form is less suitable. Common mistake: choosing without using the facts given.
