# Package: ch14-s6 — Corporate wellness and the leader's role

- **loId:** `ch14-s6` · **chapterId:** `ch14`

## 1. Must cover (one bullet or block for every item; tick each off)

Assumed knowledge
- [ ] A1 (assumed) Motivation (internal and external forces that make staff willing to put in effort) and the difference between financial and non-financial motivators, from the notes on motivation.
- [ ] A2 (assumed) Organisational culture: shared values and behaviour in a business.
- [ ] A3 (assumed) A leader influences and directs people towards goals; leaders and managers set the tone of a workplace.
- [ ] A4 (assumed) Employee retention and absenteeism and their cost (recruitment and training cost, lost output).
- [ ] A5 (assumed) Equality, diversity and inclusion (EDI): equal treatment and opportunity, a varied workforce, everyone able to take part.

Corporate wellness defined [3.2.14]
- [ ] C1 Glossary: corporate wellness, with the Appendix 2 wording (section 2).
- [ ] C2 Covers physical health, mental health and wider social wellbeing, not only the absence of illness.
- [ ] C3 Linked to EDI, since supporting everyone's wellbeing means considering different needs.
- [ ] C4 A strategic choice: planned and resourced as part of how a business treats its people, not a one-off event.
- [ ] C5 Why it matters to employers: stressed or unwell staff are less productive and more often absent.

Typical initiatives
- [ ] I1 Employee assistance programmes: confidential counselling and advice on personal or work problems.
- [ ] I2 Mental-health supports: manager training, awareness campaigns, access to professional help.
- [ ] I3 Flexible working: flexible hours or remote working help staff manage pressures outside work.
- [ ] I4 Health promotion: health checks, screenings, information on nutrition, sleep, quitting smoking.
- [ ] I5 Physical-activity schemes: cycle-to-work scheme, subsidised gym membership.
- [ ] I6 EDI policies: fair treatment, fewer barriers to taking part in wellbeing programmes.

Impact on motivation [3.2.14]
- [ ] M1 Feeling supported raises motivation: staff feel valued and invest effort.
- [ ] M2 Less stress frees energy and eases concentration.
- [ ] M3 When support matches stated values, staff see the values as genuine, building trust.
- [ ] M4 Engagement: engaged staff contribute ideas and go beyond the job description.
- [ ] M5 Link to motivators: wellbeing as a non-financial motivator (ties to the subsection on motivation).

Impact on retention, absenteeism and culture
- [ ] R1 Retention: staff who feel cared for leave less, lowering recruitment and training costs.
- [ ] R2 Absenteeism: effective measures can reduce sickness absence, though the size of the effect depends on the initiatives.
- [ ] R3 Culture: wellbeing programmes reinforce values such as respect and inclusion.
- [ ] R4 Productivity: healthy staff make fewer errors and sustain performance.
- [ ] R5 Reputation: a strong record attracts applicants and strengthens the employer's image.

The leader's role [3.2.15]
- [ ] L1 Modelling: leaders who take breaks, respect working hours and talk about wellbeing set the norm.
- [ ] L2 Resourcing: leaders decide budget and time, so they decide whether programmes last beyond launch.
- [ ] L3 Policy: ensure wellbeing and flexible-working policies are applied consistently in practice.
- [ ] L4 Psychological safety: listening and responding without blame so staff raise problems early.
- [ ] L5 Workload management: realistic allocation of work reduces a common source of stress.
- [ ] L6 Early intervention: notice signs of stress and refer staff to support.

Limits and risks
- [ ] K1 Tokenism: programmes presented as caring but not backed by changes to workloads or culture breed cynicism.
- [ ] K2 Cost: needs a budget, hard to sustain in a downturn.
- [ ] K3 Measurement: the effect on motivation and retention is hard to isolate.
- [ ] K4 Individual blame: placing all responsibility on staff ignores organisational causes such as excessive workloads.
- [ ] K5 Low uptake: some initiatives reach only a minority of staff.

Ethical, sustainability and digital angle
- [ ] X1 Ethics: wellbeing programmes must not be used to pressure staff or hide poor conditions; confidentiality of health data; equal access for all staff; avoiding tokenism.
- [ ] X2 Sustainability: social sustainability (fair work, safe conditions, inclusion); sustainable workloads; active travel schemes (cycle-to-work) link to environmental aims.
- [ ] X3 Digital: wellbeing apps, online counselling and e-learning on mental health; flexible and remote work as a wellbeing measure; risks of always-on culture and the right to disconnect (code of practice); health data is personal data under the GDPR.

Applying my learning
- [ ] N1 One or two current Irish news examples linked to workplace wellbeing and flexible work, with month, year and source (section 2); link each to the chapter's ideas (motivation, culture, leadership, limits of wellness).

## 3. Traps

- Do not use: the current key term "Corporate wellness: An organisation's planned set of measures that support employees' physical, mental and social wellbeing" as the definition on its own, because it omits the equality, diversity and inclusivity element in the Appendix 2 definition.
- Do not use: figures on the cost of absenteeism, stress or burnout, return on wellness spending, or the share of Irish workers affected (other than the PwC figure as reported by the Southern Star, 6 May 2026), because they come from surveys that are unchecked or sponsor-driven.
- Do not use: "a new right-to-disconnect code was introduced in 2026" or "a statutory right to disconnect", because the 2021 code of practice is the current one and no statute exists.
- Do not use: "wellness programmes always raise productivity", because the notes themselves say the effect is hard to isolate and depends on the initiatives; claim only likely effects.
- Confusable: corporate wellness (planned, organisation-wide, includes EDI) vs one-off perks vs health and safety law (a legal duty on risk); wellbeing vs motivation (wellbeing is one influence on motivation); retention (staff stay) vs absenteeism (staff are off).
- Confusable: psychological safety (speak up without blame) vs mental-health support (services); modelling (leader's own behaviour) vs policy (written rules).
- Boundary: financial and non-financial motivators and appraisal belong to the motivation subsection; flexible and remote work in detail, the right to request it, and monitoring ethics belong to the digital technology subsection; sustainable workplace measures (energy, waste, travel) belong to the teamwork and sustainability subsection; leadership styles belong to a different subsection on leadership; employee rights and discrimination law belong to the chapter on employee rights.

## 4. What exams ask

- Sample paper 1, Question 4(d) (2025) asks students to discuss four ways motivation can be influenced by corporate wellness initiatives. "Discuss" (Appendix 1) means a considered, balanced review with a range of arguments supported by evidence. A full answer gives four distinct ways (feeling valued and supported, lower stress, trust when actions match values, higher engagement, plus links to retention or culture), each developed into an effect on motivation and, where useful, a named initiative (employee assistance programme, flexible working, health promotion) and a limit or balancing point (tokenism, cost, hard to measure). Common mistakes: listing initiatives instead of ways motivation is influenced, repeating one idea, writing only benefits, and not defining corporate wellness.
- "Outline corporate wellness" wants the Appendix 2 meaning (equality, diversity, inclusion, wellbeing) plus two or three typical initiatives. Common mistake: describing only gym memberships.
- "Identify the role of the leader" wants named roles (model, resource, apply policy, psychological safety) each with a few words of how it helps. Common mistake: describing a leadership style instead.
- "Investigate impact on motivation and culture" wants a cause-effect chain (initiative, effect on staff, effect on the business) and a cultural effect (respect, inclusion).
- Applied news tasks (the specification's "applying my learning") want a named current Irish example with its date and source, linked to a concept and a personal opinion with a reason. Use only the rows in section 2; never invent a news item.
