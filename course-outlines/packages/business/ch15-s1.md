## 1. Must cover (one bullet or block for every item; tick each off)

Assumed knowledge
- [ ] A1 (assumed) Communication is the exchange of information between a sender and a receiver through a medium, with the receiver's feedback completing the process; the parts: sender, message, mode (medium), receiver, feedback.
- [ ] A2 (assumed) A message is effective when the receiver understands it as the sender meant it and can act on it.
- [ ] A3 (assumed) An organisation's internal communication runs downward (managers to staff), upward (staff to managers) and horizontal (between colleagues); external communication runs to customers, suppliers and other stakeholders.
- [ ] A4 (assumed) Motivation and the leader's role in building teams (from the earlier notes on leading and managing people); engagement here means commitment to the organisation's goals.

Why communication matters: the six purposes [3.3.1] (each explained, each with a workplace example)
- [ ] P1 Supporting collaboration: shared information lets people coordinate tasks, hand work over and avoid duplication (construction crew sharing daily progress to coordinate deliveries).
- [ ] P2 Avoiding conflict: clear, timely explanation of decisions and expectations prevents misunderstanding and disputes (store manager explaining a new rota before it starts).
- [ ] P3 Increasing engagement: sharing results and goals shows staff how their work matters, raising commitment (quarterly results shared with staff).
- [ ] P4 Promoting positive culture: recognition and respectful communication reinforce shared values (team leader thanking staff in open meetings).
- [ ] P5 Managing change: explaining why, what and when, with training dates, reduces uncertainty and resistance (bank introducing an online system).
- [ ] P6 Promoting innovation: open channels let ideas from any level reach decision makers (suggestion scheme, junior employee's idea to cut packaging waste).
- [ ] P7 Effect on the business and its stakeholders: fewer errors, less wasted time, better staff retention and better service to customers (so each purpose is linked to an outcome, not just named).
- [ ] P8 Poor communication has the opposite effect: errors, rumour, disputes, low morale, missed ideas.

Barriers to effective communication (assumed knowledge)
- [ ] B1 (assumed) Barrier defined: anything that stops a message being understood.
- [ ] B2 (assumed) Jargon.
- [ ] B3 (assumed) Information overload.
- [ ] B4 (assumed) Poor listening.
- [ ] B5 (assumed) Wrong medium.
- [ ] B6 (assumed) Culture and language differences.
- [ ] B7 (assumed) Hierarchy (staff hesitate to question senior managers, so problems stay hidden).
- [ ] B8 (assumed) Timing.

How to improve communication [3.3.4] (each explained with what it involves and why it works)
- [ ] I1 Setting clear goals: define what the communication must achieve before sending; shapes the content; specific enough to check whether it was met; agreed team goals avoid confusion over who communicates what to whom.
- [ ] I2 Listening actively: full attention, no interrupting, eye contact and nods; paraphrasing (restating in your own words) to confirm understanding; open questions; builds trust so people raise problems.
- [ ] I3 Ensuring ongoing engagement: regular updates (weekly briefings) reduce rumour; two-way discussion keeps people involved; follow-up checks the message was understood and acted on.
- [ ] I4 Choosing the correct mode: match the mode to the message and the audience; urgent or sensitive messages need a fast direct mode; messages needing a record suit written modes; messages needing questions suit two-way modes. (Brief here; the full criteria and analysis of each mode belong to the next subsection.)
- [ ] I5 Feedback: information returned to the sender about how a message was received (questions, objections, a completed task).
- [ ] I6 Two-way channels: team meetings, suggestion boxes, staff surveys, open-door sessions with managers; versus one-way channels (noticeboard, circular, recorded announcement, email with no invitation to reply), which cannot show whether the message was understood.
- [ ] I7 Acting on feedback shows staff their views matter, which supports engagement and trust.
- [ ] I8 Link improvements to barriers: for example plain language overcomes jargon; active listening overcomes poor listening; an open-door session reduces the hierarchy barrier.

Key terms (each defined as used; also in keyTerms)
- [ ] K1 Active listening, paraphrasing, two-way communication, feedback, jargon, information overload, employee engagement, barrier to communication (the eight current key terms; definitions as in the current notes).

Ethical, sustainability and digital angle
- [ ] X1 Ethics: honest and timely communication, especially about change, treats staff fairly; hiding bad news or misleading staff damages trust; communication should be accessible (plain language, translation or adjustment for staff whose first language differs, or who have a disability) and respect confidentiality.
- [ ] X2 Digital: collaboration platforms and instant messaging make two-way communication quicker, but notification overload and always-on messaging add to information overload; digital channels need clear norms (which tool for what).
- [ ] X3 Sustainability: communicating by email and shared platforms instead of print and travel can cut paper and emissions, while data and devices still use energy (detail in the next subsection's notes on technology).

## 3. Traps

- Do not use: any statistic that "93% of communication is non-verbal" (the Mehrabian claim), because it is a misreading of a narrow experiment and is unverified; it is not in the current notes and should not be added.
- Do not use: any named real firm or survey figure as an example of good or bad internal communication, because none is checked for this subsection.
- Confusable: a barrier (stops understanding) versus a mode (the method used); feedback (what comes back) versus two-way channel (the route that lets it come back).
- Confusable: engagement (commitment to goals) versus motivation (the drive to work); the notes on leading people cover motivation.
- Confusable: active listening (a skill of the listener) versus hearing; paraphrasing (restating) versus repeating word for word.
- Boundary: the full criteria for choosing a mode (urgency, cost, confidentiality, audience, record, complexity, feedback), the types of verbal, non-verbal, written and visual modes, their efficacy and the impact of technology on communication belong to the next subsection on modes and technology. Here mention choosing the mode only as one improvement, with the short reasons in I4.
- Boundary: causes and handling of conflict and industrial relations, motivation, leadership styles, and the stages of managing change belong to the notes on leading and managing people. Use conflict and change here only as purposes of communication.
- Boundary: memos, business letters, agendas and minutes and the duties of a chairperson or secretary are old-syllabus only (Appendix A4); leave out.

## 4. What exams ask

- The 2025 Sample Paper 2 asks students to explain three reasons, other than avoiding conflict, why communication matters in an organisation. Appendix 1 "explain" is a detailed account with reasons or causes. A full answer names three distinct purposes from the six (collaboration, engagement, positive culture, managing change, innovation), and for each gives the reason and an effect on the business or its staff, tied to the business in the stimulus. Common mistakes: using "avoiding conflict" when the question excludes it; giving three labels with no explanation; three reasons that are really one (for example engagement and positive culture restated).
- The same paper asks for two ways to improve communication, other than choosing the mode of communication. A full answer names two of: setting clear goals, listening actively, ensuring ongoing engagement (plus feedback and two-way channels), and says how each works and what it improves. Common mistakes: choosing the mode when it is excluded; naming a barrier instead of an improvement; no link to how the improvement helps the business.
- "Discuss" versions need a balanced review: the benefits of each purpose or improvement and a limit or condition (for example, two-way channels only help if management acts on feedback; too many updates cause overload). Aim for three or four developed points.
- Questions may add a stimulus (a change in a firm, a news headline). Apply the answer to it: name the change, the staff affected, and the likely barrier.
- Include the ethical and digital angle when the stimulus gives one (honest communication about change; digital tools and overload).
