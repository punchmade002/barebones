# Package: ch5-s1 — Trading blocs and Ireland's membership of the EU

- **loId:** `ch5-s1` · **chapterId:** `ch5`
- **Syllabus:** NCCA Leaving Certificate Business specification (first examined June 2027), Strand 1, section 1.5 Irish business globally, outcomes [1.5.1–1.5.3]: the importance of trading blocs for Irish businesses; Ireland's membership of the EU and its impact on the economy, businesses and consumers. Students explain a trading bloc, identify the blocs most relevant to Irish business, and evaluate EU membership from the perspective of the economy, businesses and consumers. Nothing is marked Higher Level only. Glossary term used here: international trade (Appendix 2).
- **Prepared by:** a higher model, 2026-11-23. The writer works only from this package and `course-outlines/NOTES_STANDARD.md`.

## 1. Must cover (one bullet or block for every item; tick each off)

Background (assumed)
- [ ] A1 (assumed) A tariff is a tax on imports; a quota is a limit on the quantity that may be imported; both are trade barriers. Barriers raise prices for buyers and shelter home producers.
- [ ] A2 (assumed) Imports (bought from abroad) and exports (sold abroad); Ireland as a small economy that sells much of its output abroad (developed in the notes on trading internationally).
- [ ] A3 (assumed) The euro is the common currency of the euro-area members of the EU; interest rates for the euro area are set by the European Central Bank.
- [ ] A4 (assumed) International trade means the movement or exchange of goods or services between two jurisdictions or across borders, including trade on the island of Ireland between the Republic and Northern Ireland (Appendix 2 definition, quoted in section 2).

What a trading bloc is [1.5.1]
- [ ] S1 Definition: a group of countries that agree to reduce or remove trade barriers between members. Explain it, so give the definition and what it does for members.
- [ ] S2 Free-trade area: members remove tariffs and quotas between themselves but each sets its own tariffs on outsiders.
- [ ] S3 Customs union: a free-trade area plus a common external tariff, so all members charge the same duty on non-members; the members negotiate trade deals together.
- [ ] S4 Single market: a customs union that also allows free movement of goods, services, capital and people.
- [ ] S5 Deeper integration means more shared rules and less national control over trade policy.
- [ ] S6 Why blocs matter to Irish businesses: less paperwork, lower cost of exporting, access to a large market, common standards.

The blocs most relevant to Irish business [1.5.1]
- [ ] B1 The EU single market and customs union (Ireland's home bloc); the European Commission negotiates trade agreements for the whole EU.
- [ ] B2 USMCA (United States, Mexico, Canada): a free-trade agreement replacing NAFTA; the United States is a major market for Irish exports.
- [ ] B3 Mercosur (South American customs union) and the EU-Mercosur agreement: contested in Ireland, especially by beef farmers (no stage of the process stated).
- [ ] B4 ASEAN: Southeast Asian regional grouping with a free-trade area.
- [ ] B5 EFTA and the European Economic Area (EEA): who is in each, and how the EEA extends the single market.
- [ ] B6 The UK after Brexit: now outside the EU; separate trade terms with the EU; customs formalities between Ireland and Great Britain.
- [ ] B7 Trade on the island of Ireland and the Windsor Framework: rules for goods entering Northern Ireland; green lane and red lane; businesses may meet both UK and EU rules.
- [ ] B8 Rules of origin and differing standards can complicate exporting to blocs outside the EU.

Evaluating EU membership [1.5.2, 1.5.3]
- [ ] E1 Economy, benefits: large market, exports and economies of scale, attractiveness to foreign firms (FDI), EU research funding.
- [ ] E2 Economy, costs: loss of independent monetary policy, net contributor to the EU budget, EU fiscal rules limit borrowing.
- [ ] E3 Businesses, benefits: no tariffs or quotas inside the EU, common rules, passporting for financial firms.
- [ ] E4 Businesses, costs: compliance cost (heavier on small firms), limits on state aid.
- [ ] E5 Consumers, benefits: consumer rights (refunds, guarantees, price transparency), no roaming surcharges, free movement for study and work.
- [ ] E6 Consumers, costs: farm support can keep some food prices above world prices.
- [ ] E7 A balanced evaluation names who gains and loses, weighs benefits against costs and reaches a justified judgement (command words: evaluate, assess, discuss).

Ethical, sustainability and digital angle
- [ ] X1 Ethical and sustainability: EU trade deals can raise environmental and labour standards for imports, but critics, including Irish farming groups, argue imports produced to lower standards undercut Irish producers (the Mercosur dispute). Single-market rules on product safety and the environment protect consumers and the environment.
- [ ] X2 Digital: the single market covers digital services; EU rules on data protection and online consumer rights apply to Irish online sellers; digital customs documentation after Brexit.

## 3. Traps

- Do not use: "ASEAN has ten countries" (in the current notes), because Timor-Leste joined in October 2025 and the bloc has eleven members.
- Do not use: "Ireland uses the euro, which removes currency risk with all trade partners", because the risk is removed only with other euro-area members; trade with the UK, the United States and others still carries exchange-rate risk.
- Do not use: GDPR or the employment directives as the chosen EU regulation or directive, because the specification says the EU legislation studied under the EU strand must differ from the legislation used in the consumer and employee chapters.
- Confusable: a free-trade area has no common external tariff; a customs union does. A single market adds free movement of people, services and capital. The EEA is not EFTA; Switzerland is in EFTA but not the EEA.
- Confusable: a trading bloc (a group of countries) versus a trade agreement (a deal between two parties, such as EU-Mercosur). Mercosur is a bloc; the EU-Mercosur agreement is a deal between two blocs.
- Confusable: the European Central Bank sets euro-area interest rates; the European Commission negotiates trade deals.
- Belongs elsewhere: how Irish firms enter foreign markets and the balance of payments (trading internationally); social and environmental consequences, interdependence and technology (globalisation); foreign direct investment and IDA Ireland (FDI); the EU institutions and the choice of an EU regulation and directive (the notes on Irish government and EU policy). Mention FDI here only as a benefit of EU access.

## 4. What exams ask

- A short-answer part asks students to explain what a trading bloc is. A full answer gives the definition (a group of countries reducing trade barriers between members) and one example of the effect, such as no tariffs between members. Common mistake: naming the EU only, with no definition.
- A part asks students to identify one trading bloc other than the EU and Mercosur. A full answer names one bloc correctly, such as USMCA, ASEAN or EFTA. Common mistake: naming a trade agreement or a single country, or repeating the EU.
- A fill-in-the-blank item tests the term "trading bloc" in a sentence about countries reducing barriers.
- Evaluate and discuss questions on EU membership (appendix 1: evaluate means collect and examine evidence to make judgements; discuss means a considered, balanced review with a range of arguments). A full answer gives three or four developed points across benefits and costs, names the group affected (the economy, businesses or consumers), explains the effect with a reason, and ends with a justified judgement. Common mistakes: listing benefits only; confusing "the economy" with businesses; saying "free trade" without saying what it removes.
- Exam tip material: always say who gains or loses; link each point to a cost or benefit with "because"; give one benefit and one cost for each of the three perspectives.
