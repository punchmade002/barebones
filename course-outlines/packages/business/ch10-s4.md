## 1. Must cover (one bullet or block for every item; tick each off)

Background (assumed)
- [ ] A1 (assumed) Receipts (cash in) and payments (cash out) are different from sales and expenses: cash flow records money actually received and paid.
- [ ] A2 (assumed) Debtors are customers who owe the business money for goods or services supplied on credit; creditors are suppliers the business owes.
- [ ] A3 (assumed) Capital expenditure (equipment bought) and capital introduced (owner or investor cash put in) appear as payments and receipts in the month they happen.
- [ ] A4 (assumed) The sources of finance used as remedies are taught in the notes on sources of finance; here they are used as actions.

Purpose and layout
- [ ] P1 A cash-flow forecast predicts money in and out each month so the owner can see when cash will run short and arrange finance before a bill cannot be paid.
- [ ] P2 Uses: lenders expect it when a business applies for finance; it lets an owner test a decision (buying equipment, offering longer credit) before committing; it is usually prepared monthly for the next twelve months and updated as actual figures come in.
- [ ] L1 Layout in order each month: opening balance, receipts (cash sales, payments from debtors, capital introduced), payments (stock, wages, rent, loan repayments, equipment), net cash flow (receipts minus payments), closing balance (opening plus net cash flow), which becomes next month's opening balance.
- [ ] L2 Fully worked numerical example (all invented sample figures, a small café, January to March; see section 2) shown as a table of receipts, payments, net cash flow and balances.

Reading and analysing
- [ ] R1 A deficit month is one whose closing balance is negative, so the business needs an overdraft or other borrowing.
- [ ] R2 In the worked example February and March both close negative (-€3,500 and -€2,000); February has the negative net cash flow, caused by the oven purchase, a one-off, not normal trading. March stays negative because the deficit carries forward.
- [ ] R3 Causes of deficits: seasonality (predictable months), large capital payments, late debtors (sales on paper but no cash), repeated deficits pointing to a structural problem such as prices set too low.
- [ ] R4 Analyse means break down the forecast: find which months, how large, why, and whether the cause is one-off or recurring.

Remedies (recommend a course of action)
- [ ] M1 Arrange an overdraft in advance: agree the limit before needed; avoids the higher charges of an unarranged overdraft.
- [ ] M2 Delay or reschedule payments (for example longer terms with a supplier).
- [ ] M3 Chase debtors: prompt invoices, deposits, reminders, to bring receipts forward.
- [ ] M4 Lease instead of buy: worked sample, an oven leased at €500 a month from February instead of bought gives February closing €0 and March closing €1,000.
- [ ] M5 Cut costs (stock, staff hours), only where the saving does not reduce sales.
- [ ] M6 Inject capital (extra owner's equity or a share issue): adds cash with no loan repayments.
- [ ] M7 Invoice discounting or factoring: unpaid invoices turned into immediate cash for a fee.
- [ ] M8 Match the remedy to the cause: a one-off gap versus a recurring shortfall; recommend and justify one or two, giving the cost of each.

Cash versus profit
- [ ] V1 Profit records income and costs when earned or incurred; cash flow records money actually received and paid.
- [ ] V2 Credit sales count as revenue when made but cash arrives when the customer pays.
- [ ] V3 A profitable business can run out of cash if customers pay late or loan repayments are high.
- [ ] V4 Cash-flow forecasts manage liquidity (ability to pay short-term bills as they fall due); profit shows whether the business earns a return.

Evaluation
- [ ] E1 A forecast is an estimate; a small error in the sales estimate can change a month's position, so sales assumptions need most care.
- [ ] E2 A worst-case version shows the cash cushion needed.
- [ ] E3 Remedies have their own costs (overdraft interest, discounts for early payment).

Cross-cutting angle
- [ ] X1 Ethical: honest forecasts to lenders and investors; paying suppliers and staff on time even when cash is tight; the effect of late payment on small suppliers (see section 2).
- [ ] X2 Sustainability: forecast the up-front cost of energy-efficient or sustainable investments against lower running costs; leasing equipment instead of buying can protect cash.
- [ ] X3 Digital: cloud accounting software and online banking give real-time cash positions; automated invoicing and payment reminders bring receipts forward; fintech invoice finance (definition in section 2); cyber and payment fraud risk.

## 3. Traps

- Do not use: any figure from a past SEC paper or an old textbook cash-flow table, because they are old-syllabus or unverified. Use only the sample figures above.
- Do not use: the ISME prompt payments figures (average payment days, share favouring a 30-day regime), because the report PDF could not be read for this package, so they are unverified.
- Do not use: Atradius survey figures or "invoice finance accounts for x% of lending", because they were not checked against the original reports.
- Do not use: that parties may agree to extend payment terms up to 60 days, because it was not on the source read for this package.
- Do not use: "a profitable business cannot fail" or "negative cash means the business is loss-making", because cash and profit are different measures; a deficit can arise from timing.
- Do not use: liquidity ratios (current ratio, acid test) or final accounts, because they are old-syllabus only (Appendix A4); describe liquidity in words as in the definition.
- Do not use: cash-flow forecasts with depreciation as a payment, because depreciation is not a cash outflow and the outcome does not require it. If a student asks, say depreciation is not cash.
- Do not use: the figure for how many days late invoices are, or a claim that the 2012 Regulations set a fixed rate of interest, because the current interest rate was not checked.
- Confusable: cash versus profit; net cash flow (one month) versus closing balance (cumulative); receipts versus sales (credit sales are not receipts until paid); payments versus expenses; invoice discounting (business collects) versus factoring (factor collects); arranged versus unarranged overdraft.
- Confusable: capital introduced (receipt, from owners) is not revenue or profit; a loan received is a receipt but a debt to repay.
- Boundary: the definitions of sources of finance (overdraft, term loan, equity) belong to the notes on sources of finance; here they are remedies, so name them in one line each with their effect on the forecast. Fixed and variable costs and break-even belong to the notes on costs and revenue streams. The business plan's financial section is a different outcome (the notes on the business plan). Personal budgeting and consumer credit belong to the notes on personal finance.

## 4. What exams ask

- Appendix 1 verbs: **analyse** ("study or examine something in detail, break down in order to bring out the essential elements or structure; identify parts and relationships, and to interpret information to reach conclusions"), **calculate**, **interpret**, **suggest** ("propose solution(s)"), **evaluate**, **recommend** (in the syllabus wording) and **discuss**.
- The outline records no 2025 sample-paper question on this subsection. Do not claim one. The syllabus wording "analyse the cash flow and recommend a suitable course of action" is the likely shape: a short cash-flow table is given and the student interprets it.
- Analyse answer: state the months in deficit, the size, the cause (seasonal, one-off purchase, late debtors, structural), and whether it will repeat. Common mistake: restating the figures with no cause.
- Recommend answer: remedy matched to the cause, a justification, and its cost or limit (for example overdraft interest, loss of control or discount for early payment). Common mistake: listing every remedy with no recommendation or no link to the cause.
- Calculate answer (if a gap is left in the table): net cash flow, then closing balance, with working shown, carrying the closing balance forward as next month's opening. Common mistake: forgetting to carry the balance forward, or treating a loan received as profit.
- Cash versus profit: explain with credit sales or a large asset purchase. Common mistake: saying they are the same, or that a deficit means a loss.
- Evaluate: a forecast depends on assumptions; sales estimates are the weak point.
- Applying learning: use a current news item as the stimulus; show how a cash-flow concept explains the story.
- Do not invent marks or frequencies.
