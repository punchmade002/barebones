# Package: ch3-s1 — Economic indicators and their impact on business

- **loId:** `ch3-s1` · **chapterId:** `ch3`
- **Syllabus:** NCCA Leaving Certificate Business specification (first examined June 2027), Strand 1, section 1.3 "Business and the economy", learning outcome [1.3.1]: the relevance of inflation, employment rates, interest rates, economic growth and development, exchange rates and consumer confidence for business development and growth; students explain how they impact on business. ## 1. Must cover (one bullet or block for every item; tick each off)

Why indicators matter
- [ ] W1 An economic indicator is a measure of how the economy is performing; businesses use indicators to decide when to hire, borrow, invest, price or hold back.
- [ ] W2 Indicators are published regularly (CSO, ECB), so trends can be tracked; a business cannot control them but can plan for them (prices, staffing, borrowing, expansion).
- [ ] W3 Read indicators together: one can offset or amplify another (for example rising growth with rising inflation).
- [ ] W4 (assumed) A business's costs, revenue and profit; demand (how much customers want to buy at a price); exports and imports. The effects below rest on these.

Inflation
- [ ] I1 Definition: a sustained rise in the general price level, so money buys less over time (keyTerm).
- [ ] I2 How Ireland measures it: the CSO's Consumer Price Index, the average change in the prices of a basket of goods and services bought by households (keyTerm).
- [ ] I3 Effects on costs: dearer energy, materials and wages squeeze margins if costs cannot be passed on.
- [ ] I4 Effects on demand: lower purchasing power, so households cut non-essential spending.
- [ ] I5 Responses: raise prices (limited by competition), renegotiate supplier contracts, find cheaper inputs.

Employment and unemployment
- [ ] E1 Employment rate: share of the working-age population in paid work (keyTerm). Unemployment rate: share of the labour force without work, available for work and actively seeking it (keyTerm).
- [ ] E2 Measured in Ireland by the CSO's Labour Force Survey, a household survey.
- [ ] E3 Low unemployment: more income to spend so demand grows; but recruitment is harder and dearer, wages rise, labour-intensive firms may be limited.
- [ ] E4 High unemployment: weaker consumer spending and demand; staff easier and cheaper to hire.
- [ ] E5 (assumed) Labour force = people in work plus people unemployed and seeking work.

Interest rates
- [ ] R1 Definition: the cost of borrowing, a percentage of the amount borrowed, or the return on savings (keyTerm).
- [ ] R2 Ireland is in the euro area, so the European Central Bank sets the main policy rates; Irish banks set their own lending rates in response (also a factCard; exam fill-in).
- [ ] R3 Higher rates: dearer variable-rate loans and overdrafts, less business investment, less household spending on credit; saving becomes more attractive.
- [ ] R4 Lower rates: cheaper borrowing encourages investment in equipment, premises, expansion; supports spending on credit.
- [ ] R5 A heavily indebted business is most exposed to rises; know the terms of loans (fixed or variable).

Economic growth and development
- [ ] G1 Economic growth: an increase in the real value of goods and services produced over time, after allowing for inflation (keyTerm).
- [ ] G2 GDP: value of output produced within a country's borders in a period (keyTerm).
- [ ] G3 GNP: GDP adjusted for income earned abroad by residents and income paid to foreign owners.
- [ ] G4 GNI* (modified GNI): designed to remove distortions caused by multinational activity, so it gives a clearer view of the domestic Irish economy.
- [ ] G5 Why Ireland uses GNI*: GDP can overstate the size of the domestic economy because of multinational activity.
- [ ] G6 Effect of growth on business: more demand, so expansion, investment, hiring.
- [ ] G7 Economic development: broader than growth; improvements in living standards, health, education, environmental protection, social equality.
- [ ] G8 Why development matters to business: skills base, infrastructure, social stability, attractive for sustainable investment.
- [ ] G9 Growth versus development (factCard and exam question).

Exchange rates
- [ ] X1 Definition: the price of one currency in terms of another (keyTerm).
- [ ] X2 Stronger euro: Irish exports dearer abroad, imports cheaper; exporters less competitive.
- [ ] X3 Weaker euro: exports cheaper and more competitive; imported raw materials and fuel dearer in euro.
- [ ] X4 Currency risk: revenue earned in dollars or sterling changes in euro value; businesses may hedge (one line only; no technique needed).
- [ ] X5 Tourism: a weaker euro can make Ireland cheaper for visitors from the United States or the United Kingdom.

Consumer confidence
- [ ] C1 Definition: how optimistic households feel about their finances and the wider economy; measured through regular surveys (keyTerm).
- [ ] C2 High confidence: more discretionary spending (holidays, cars, dining out), boosting retail, hospitality, tourism.
- [ ] C3 Low confidence: more saving, deferred purchases; non-essential sellers hit hardest.
- [ ] C4 Businesses adjust stock, hiring and marketing, often before sales figures change.

Reading an indicator from a graph or infographic
- [ ] P1 Read title, units, scale, time period first.
- [ ] P2 Identify direction, rate of change, turning points.
- [ ] P3 Quote figures accurately; one data point is not a long-term trend.
- [ ] P4 Link the trend to business effects and say what the business should do.

Cross-cutting angles
- [ ] T1 Ethics and sustainability: rising prices fall hardest on lower-income households and on firms with little pricing power; ethical pricing (not exploiting a price spike) and fair treatment of staff when costs rise; the development side of growth (environment, equality) is the sustainability link.
- [ ] T2 Digital: indicators are published as open data (CSO, ECB) and read through dashboards and infographics; firms use digital tools to track costs and prices and to change prices quickly; the graph-reading skill applies to digital infographics.

## 3. Traps

- Do not use: any inflation, unemployment, interest-rate, GDP or confidence figure not in section 2 or with no date, because these change every month. Never copy figures from a past paper or old graph.
- Do not use: "GDP shows how well off Irish people are", because multinational activity inflates Irish GDP; that is why GNI* exists.
- Do not use: the ECB sets Irish bank mortgage or loan rates directly, because it sets policy rates and Irish banks set their own lending rates in response.
- Do not use: "a weaker euro is always good" or "a strong euro is always bad", because it depends on whether the firm exports or imports.
- Do not use: the old syllabus's household-management or ratio content on indicators; it is not in this course.
- Confusable: inflation (the price level rising) vs a rise in a single price; growth (output up) vs development (living standards, health, education, equality); employment rate (share of working-age population) vs unemployment rate (share of the labour force); GDP vs GNP vs GNI*; interest on loans vs interest on savings.
- Neighbouring subsection, the value of the business economy in Ireland: counts of enterprises, turnover, gross value added, SME and foreign-owned patterns belong there. Here, indicators only.
- Neighbouring chapters: the ECB as an EU institution and EU law (national and EU policy); trade, the balance of payments and international business (Irish business globally); sources of business finance and loan terms (business finance); household saving and borrowing (consumer finance). Mention rates and exchange rates only for their business effects.

## 4. What exams ask

The 2025 sample papers test this in three ways (no marks or frequencies stated).
- **Explain the impact of three indicators on a business's growth** (Explain: a detailed account including reasons or causes). A full answer picks three named indicators, and for each gives the direction of change, the mechanism (costs, demand, borrowing, exports) and the effect on the business's growth decision, ideally tied to a business type. Common mistakes: listing the indicator's definition only; giving a cause with no business effect; effects for all three that are really the same point; leaving out the link to growth.
- **Fill in who sets interest rates** (Identify): the European Central Bank sets the euro-area rates; common mistakes: central bank of Ireland, the government or the CSO.
- **Explain the indicator shown in an infographic** (Explain, Interpret: use knowledge to recognise trends and draw conclusions from given information). A full answer names the indicator, says what it measures, reads the trend from the figures shown (direction, size, time period), and links it to costs, demand, borrowing or exports. Common mistakes: naming the wrong indicator, ignoring the units, treating one data point as a trend, giving no business impact.
- Fuller answers for "discuss" or "evaluate" (Discuss: a considered, balanced review; Evaluate: make judgements about ideas using evidence) give the positive and negative effects, say which business is affected most and why, and reach a justified conclusion.
- Tips: always link an indicator to a specific cost or revenue effect; show you know a rise can help one business and hurt another (an exporter versus an importer).
