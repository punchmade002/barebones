# Package: ch12-s1 — The risks of enterprise and why risk management matters

- **loId:** `ch12-s1` · **chapterId:** `ch12`
- **Syllabus:** Strand 2, section 2.7 (Managing risk), outcomes [2.7.1] and [2.7.2]: the nature of risk within entrepreneurship and the importance of risk management; outline the challenges and risks of enterprise and entrepreneurship, and the importance of assessing and managing risks. ## 1. Must cover (one bullet or block for every item; tick each off)

Assumed knowledge
- [ ] A1 (assumed) An enterprise is a business venture; an entrepreneur is the person who brings together money, premises, people and ideas to pursue an opportunity, and who bears the loss if it fails.
- [ ] A2 (assumed) Profit is often seen as the reward for bearing risk, which is why sensible risk-taking is central to enterprise.
- [ ] A3 (assumed) Uncertainty: the outcome of a new venture cannot be known in advance, so risk cannot be removed, only identified, reduced and managed.
- [ ] A4 (assumed) A stakeholder is anyone affected by a business (owner, staff, lenders, customers, suppliers); risks fall on different stakeholders.

The nature of risk
- [ ] R1 Definition of risk: the chance that an event will harm a business or stop it reaching its objectives.
- [ ] R2 Risk management defined: identifying, assessing, responding to and monitoring risks so the business can protect itself and pursue its goals.
- [ ] R3 Likelihood (how probable) and impact (size of the damage) as the two measures of a risk.

Challenges of starting a business (all five, each with its effect)
- [ ] C1 Finance: raising start-up capital and keeping enough cash flowing until sales cover costs.
- [ ] C2 Competition: established firms and new rivals take market share and force prices down.
- [ ] C3 Time: long hours and the struggle to balance the business with other demands.
- [ ] C4 Skills: a founder may lack marketing, accounting or people-management skills, so mistakes are costlier.
- [ ] C5 Regulation: licences, employment law, tax and health and safety rules add cost and paperwork from the start.

Categories of risk (all eight, each defined with at least one concrete cause)
- [ ] T1 Financial: poor cash flow, rising borrowing costs, bad debts, exchange-rate movements.
- [ ] T2 Market: falling demand, changing tastes, competitor actions that cut sales or prices.
- [ ] T3 Operational: process failure, equipment breakdown, supplier problems, loss of key staff.
- [ ] T4 Legal and compliance: breaking contract, employment, consumer, tax or data protection law.
- [ ] T5 Reputational: damage to the brand from poor quality, a safety incident or a public complaint, losing customers.
- [ ] T6 Cyber and technological: hacking, ransomware, data loss, system failure that halts trading or exposes customer data.
- [ ] T7 Environmental and climate: flooding, extreme weather, raw-material shortages, new carbon rules affecting costs and premises.
- [ ] T8 Personal risk to the entrepreneur: illness, loss of income, personal guarantees to lenders that put the owner's own assets at risk.

Risk assessment
- [ ] P1 Five steps as a repeating cycle: identify, evaluate likelihood and impact, prioritise in a risk matrix, act, monitor.
- [ ] P2 Identify: list risks using the categories and the knowledge of staff.
- [ ] P3 Evaluate: estimate likelihood and impact of each risk.
- [ ] P4 Risk matrix: grid with likelihood on one axis and impact on the other; high-likelihood, high-impact risks are dealt with first.
- [ ] P5 Act: choose a response (avoid, reduce, transfer or accept); the strategies themselves are developed in the notes on risk management strategies.
- [ ] P6 Monitor: review the risk register regularly because likelihood and impact change.

Why managing risk matters
- [ ] W1 Survival: one uninsured fire, lawsuit or cyber attack can close a small business.
- [ ] W2 Lower costs: good controls can help a firm obtain lower insurance premiums.
- [ ] W3 Legal duty: the Safety, Health and Welfare at Work Act 2005 requires employers to manage risks to employees' safety and health; the Health and Safety Authority enforces it.
- [ ] W4 Investor and lender confidence: banks and investors prefer firms that identify and control their risks.
- [ ] W5 Continuity: planning for disruption (supplier failure, flood) allows quick recovery.
- [ ] W6 Better decisions: knowing the risks helps managers judge whether an opportunity is worth its downside.

Ethical, sustainability and digital angle
- [ ] X1 Sustainability: climate and environmental risk (extreme weather, carbon-reduction rules) and its cost to premises and operations.
- [ ] X2 Ethics: an ethical lapse (for example unsafe conditions in a supplier's workplace) creates legal and reputational risk for the buyer.
- [ ] X3 Digital: moving sales online adds cyber risk; customer payment data must be protected and data protection law complied with.
- [ ] X4 Planning for risk from the start, not reacting to it, helps a business last.

## 3. Traps

- Do not use: any claim that employer's liability insurance is compulsory in Ireland, because it is not (William Fry, 2015, noting it is not compulsory in Ireland though most employers hold it). The current s2 notes say it is; this subsection must not repeat it.
- Do not use: figures for insurance premiums, borrowing costs or fines, because they change and are unchecked.
- Do not use: the old-syllabus ratio analysis, contract law or insurance principles as part of risk, because they are old-syllabus only (Appendix A4).
- Confusable: risk (chance of harm, may be quantified) versus uncertainty (outcome unknowable); likelihood versus impact; a risk (a possible event) versus a loss (the damage after it happens).
- Confusable: risk assessment (find, evaluate, prioritise) versus risk response (avoid, spread, prevent, transfer, retain). The responses and all insurance belong to the notes on risk management strategies and insurance; here only name "act" as a step.
- Boundary: do not teach premiums, excess, policies or insurance products here; they belong to the neighbouring subsection on strategies and insurance.
- Boundary: do not teach SWOT or STEEPLE in depth; those belong to the chapters on business environment and planning.
- Do not name "physical evidence" or any other external risk framework not in the notes.

## 4. What exams ask

- The 2025 Sample Paper 2 asks students to explain two reasons why managing risk is important, with a stimulus on Storm Éowyn and rising insurance costs. A full answer gives two separate reasons, each with an explanation of cause and effect on the business (for example survival, because a single uninsured event can close a small firm; lower premiums or lender confidence), linked to the stimulus. Common mistakes: listing two words with no explanation; two reasons that are really the same; ignoring the stimulus; defining risk instead of giving reasons.
- "Identify" and "outline" ask for risks or challenges briefly with the main point only. "Explain" needs reasons or causes (Appendix 1: give a detailed account including reasons or causes). "Analyse" needs a break-down: the risk, its likelihood, its impact and a consequence.
- Possible further question types (from the syllabus wording, not past papers): outline challenges facing a start-up; outline the stages of risk assessment; classify a stimulus into risk categories. Name the category and tie it to the facts given (a ransomware attack is cyber and technological risk with a reputational knock-on).
- Common mistake: describing a risk in general terms when the stimulus asks about a specific business; use the business and sector given.
- Applying a stimulus: say who is affected (owner, staff, customers, lenders) and how, to reach discuss or evaluate depth with three or four developed points.
