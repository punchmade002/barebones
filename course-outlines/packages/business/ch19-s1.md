# Package: ch19-s1 — Employee rights and responsibilities: legislative and non-legislative protection

- **loId:** `ch19-s1` · **chapterId:** `ch19`
- **Syllabus:** Strand 4, section 4.3 (Being an informed employee), [4.3.1] and [4.3.2]. Students learn about the rights and responsibilities of employees set out in legislation (pay, conditions of employment, health and safety, discrimination, security of employment and dismissal) and about how employees have both legislative and non-legislative protection. They should be able to examine both, and investigate how current employment legislation sets out the rights and responsibilities of employees and employers. Footnote 3 of the specification: use current employment legislation. Outline scope: the Acts on pay, conditions, health and safety, equality and dismissal; redress through the WRC and the Labour Court; employees' responsibilities; contracts, collective agreements, HR policies, codes of practice and grievance procedures.
- **Prepared by:** a higher model, 2026-11-23. The writer works only from this package and `course-outlines/NOTES_STANDARD.md`.

## 1. Must cover (one bullet or block for every item; tick each off)

Assumed knowledge
- [ ] A1 (assumed) An employer hires an employee under a contract of employment; an employee works under the employer's direction and is paid wages or salary, unlike a self-employed person.
- [ ] A2 (assumed) Rights come from the law (legislation, which no contract can reduce) and from agreements and procedures (non-legislative). Rights have matching responsibilities for both sides.
- [ ] A3 (assumed) Gross pay, deductions and take-home pay (the payslip is taught in the notes on the Budget; here only "wages and deductions" as protected by law).

Pay
- [ ] P1 National Minimum Wage Act 2000: a legal minimum hourly rate; an employer cannot pay less than the rate that applies. Reviewed by the government, usually after recommendations from the Low Pay Commission; a change affects employers' costs and workers' take-home pay.
- [ ] P2 Payment of Wages Act 1991: wages paid on time, written statement of pay and deductions; deductions allowed only where the law permits or the employee has agreed in writing.
- [ ] P3 A worker not paid properly can complain to the Workplace Relations Commission (WRC).

Conditions of employment
- [ ] C1 Organisation of Working Time Act 1997: limit on average weekly hours, rest breaks, daily and weekly rest, annual leave.
- [ ] C2 Terms of Employment (Information) Acts: written statement of core terms (pay, hours, place of work).
- [ ] C3 Employment (Miscellaneous Provisions) Act 2018: restricts zero-hours contracts; minimum payment when called in but not given expected work; banded hours for those whose contract does not reflect the hours they actually work.
- [ ] C4 Sick Leave Act 2022: statutory right to paid sick leave for eligible employees; the number of paid days is set in law.
- [ ] C5 Work Life Balance and Miscellaneous Provisions Act 2023: right to request remote working and flexible working; extra leave for family and carers (medical care leave).

Health and safety
- [ ] H1 Safety, Health and Welfare at Work Act 2005: the main workplace safety law, enforced by the Health and Safety Authority (HSA).
- [ ] H2 Employer duties: identify hazards, assess risks, prepare a safety statement, provide training, information and safe equipment.
- [ ] H3 Employee duties: take reasonable care of own and others' safety, use protective equipment, report hazards and accidents.
- [ ] H4 An employee who reasonably believes there is a serious and imminent risk can refuse to work on it and cannot be penalised; breaches can be prosecuted.

Discrimination
- [ ] D1 Employment Equality Acts 1998 to 2015 protect employees and applicants: recruitment, pay, training, promotion, conditions, dismissal.
- [ ] D2 The nine grounds: gender, civil status, family status, sexual orientation, religion, age, disability, race, membership of the Traveller community. Race includes colour, nationality, ethnic or national origins. (Exam: list three apart from gender.)
- [ ] D3 Direct discrimination versus indirect discrimination (a rule applying to all that disadvantages a group, unless objectively justified).
- [ ] D4 Reasonable accommodation for a disabled employee unless a disproportionate burden.
- [ ] D5 Claims go to the WRC.

Security of employment and dismissal
- [ ] S1 Unfair Dismissals Acts 1977 to 2015: protect employees with the required service.
- [ ] S2 Fair grounds (capability, conduct, redundancy, legal prohibition, another substantial reason) plus fair procedures.
- [ ] S3 Automatically unfair reasons, such as trade union membership or raising a safety complaint.
- [ ] S4 Constructive dismissal: resignation forced by the employer's conduct, treated as dismissal.
- [ ] S5 Redress: reinstatement, re-engagement or compensation, decided by the WRC or the Labour Court.

Redress and responsibilities
- [ ] R1 WRC investigates and adjudicates (pay, discrimination, dismissal, working time); the Labour Court hears appeals and certain industrial relations disputes.
- [ ] R2 Time limits: complaints must usually be made within a set period, so act promptly.
- [ ] R3 Employees' responsibilities: follow reasonable, lawful instructions; attend as rostered; duty of good faith (no disclosure of confidential information, no unfair competition); give required notice on resigning.
- [ ] R4 Employers' responsibilities mirror the rights: pay, safe workplace, non-discrimination, fair procedures (the outline asks for both sides).

Non-legislative protection
- [ ] N1 Employment contract (express terms; statutory rights cannot be signed away).
- [ ] N2 Collective agreement between a trade union and an employer for a group of workers.
- [ ] N3 HR policies in a staff handbook (cannot reduce statutory rights).
- [ ] N4 Codes of practice, such as the Code of Practice on Grievance and Disciplinary Procedures; bodies such as the WRC take them into account.
- [ ] N5 Grievance procedure: informal then formal steps.
- [ ] N6 Legislative versus non-legislative protection compared (the outline says examine both).

Ethical, sustainability and digital angle
- [ ] E1 Ethical: equality and fair treatment, dignity at work, and employers who go beyond the legal minimum (inclusive workplaces; the glossary's "Corporate wellness"). Law sets the floor, ethics asks for more.
- [ ] E2 Sustainability: safe, decent, secure work as part of the social side of a sustainable business (the "S" in ESG).
- [ ] E3 Digital: the right to request remote working and digital working practices; employees' time and pay records kept electronically.

## 3. Traps

- Do not use: "10 paid sick days a year" or any figure above 5, because the original phase-in to 10 days by 2026 did not go ahead; the WRC page states 5. Do not state a figure for any year the WRC page does not cover.
- Do not use: "the Labour Relations Commission code" as a current body, because the Labour Relations Commission was replaced by the WRC; the code survives under the WRC.
- Do not use: "the Act bans zero-hours contracts" without qualification, because the restriction has exceptions (emergencies and short-term relief cover).
- Do not use: a "6 months' service" condition for requesting remote working, because the six months applies only before an approved arrangement can start, and the remote and flexible working provisions took effect in March 2024, not July 2023.
- Do not use: any figure for a notice period, a rest-break length, a weekly hours limit, annual leave days or compensation caps, because none is checked here.
- Do not use: the "Applied Business Question", contract law (offer and acceptance), or the old syllabus headings on industrial relations, because they are old syllabus only.
- Do not use: "physical evidence" as a P; not relevant here.
- Confusable: legislative protection (law, applies to all workers, cannot be signed away) versus non-legislative protection (contracts, agreements, handbooks, codes). A code of practice is guidance, not an Act.
- Confusable: direct versus indirect discrimination; unfair dismissal versus redundancy (redundancy is a fair ground when procedures are followed); WRC (first hearing) versus Labour Court (appeal).
- Neighbouring subsection: what a trade union is and does, shop stewards, ICTU, and the gig economy belong in the notes on trade unions and the gig economy. Here a union appears only as a party to a collective agreement.
- Neighbouring subsection: the Budget, tax, USC, PRSI and the payslip belong in the notes on the annual Budget and informed decisions. Here pay means the minimum wage and wage protection only.
- Neighbouring chapters: consumer protection law and GDPR belong elsewhere; do not teach them here.

## 4. What exams ask

- Sample paper 2, Question 4 (a), asks students to list three grounds, other than gender, on which discrimination is illegal. A full answer gives three of the nine grounds exactly (for example age, disability, race); mistakes: repeating gender, writing "colour" as a separate ground, inventing grounds such as "political opinion" or "weight".
- The verbs in the specification's Appendix 1 shape the answers. "Identify" needs a brief named fact; "outline" the main points only; "explain" a detailed account with reasons or causes; "discuss" a balanced review with a range of arguments supported by evidence; "examine" looks closely at arguments and concepts. Likely forms (from the verbs, not from any paper): identify an Act or a right, outline the rights of employees in one area, explain how an employer breaches or respects a right, discuss whether legislation or non-legislative protection protects employees better.
- Full answer: name the Act (with year), state the right or duty, say who enforces it or where to complain (WRC, HSA, Labour Court), and link it to the effect on the employee and the business. A "discuss" answer gives three or four developed points, with a conclusion.
- Common mistakes: naming an Act without saying what it does; mixing up the WRC and the Labour Court; treating a handbook policy as law; giving only the employee's rights and forgetting responsibilities; unsupported figures.
- Applying the specification's ethics and sustainability angle: a "discuss" answer may weigh the cost to the business of meeting standards against fairer, safer and more productive staff.
