# Package: ch0-s1 — Developing research questions, managing information and acknowledging sources

- **loId:** `ch0-s1` · **chapterId:** `ch0`
- **Syllabus scope:** Unifying Strand (Investigating Business), U1.1, U2.1–U2.6 and U6.1 of the NCCA *Curriculum Specification for Leaving Certificate Business* (first examined June 2027). Students learn about: the need to develop and refine questions on topics to ensure clarity and the availability of sufficient, quality information and/or data, using SMART objectives; the importance of using a range of information and data, a variety of perspectives, the credibility, context and accuracy of sources, and assessing information for bias, reliability and value; the need to analyse information from a range of perspectives and make sure data are representative and comprehensive. Students should be able to: develop a range of questions to research (U1.1); identify relevant data, evidence and information from a range of sources (U2.1); assess accuracy, credibility and reliability (U2.2); accurately record and acknowledge sources (U2.3); identify key points in each source (U2.4); analyse sources to determine the importance of an issue for different individuals or groups (U2.5); identify gaps in information and data and assess their impact on drawing informed conclusions (U2.6); acknowledge sources of information (U6.1). No outcome is Higher Level only.
- **Neighbouring subsections (by subject):** project planning; analysing, evaluating and presenting findings; the Investigative Study itself (requirements, sections, marks).
- **Prepared by:** a higher model, 2026-11-23. The writer works only from this package and `course-outlines/NOTES_STANDARD.md`.

## 1. Must cover (one bullet or block for every item; tick each off)

Assumed knowledge
- [ ] A1 (assumed) Junior cycle Business Studies: a business must make decisions using information, and information can be a fact or an opinion.
- [ ] A2 (assumed) The difference between a fact, an opinion and an advertisement or promotional claim.
- [ ] A3 (assumed) What a questionnaire, an interview and a sample are (used before these are judged).

Developing a question [U1.1]
- [ ] Q1 Why a research question is refined before data collection: clarity, and the availability of sufficient, quality information and/or data.
- [ ] Q2 Glossary term: SMART objectives (for research), with the Appendix 2 definition (see section 2).
- [ ] Q3 Each letter applied to a research question: specific, measurable, achievable, relevant, timebound.
- [ ] Q4 A weak question versus a refined question, with a worked refinement (add a business, group, place, period, measurable outcome).
- [ ] Q5 A range of questions: developing several possible questions and testing each against SMART before choosing one.
- [ ] Q6 A question may be refined again during the study, and the student notes why (NCCA guidelines).

Using a range of information, sources and perspectives [U2.1]
- [ ] S1 Glossary term: sources of information, with the Appendix 2 definition (see section 2): primary and secondary.
- [ ] S2 Primary methods: surveys/questionnaires, interviews, observation and trial; advantages and disadvantages of primary data.
- [ ] S3 Secondary sources: reports, news and media, government statistics, company websites; advantages and disadvantages of secondary data.
- [ ] S4 Why combine primary and secondary data (each checks or explains the other).
- [ ] S5 Why a variety of perspectives is needed (owners, employees, customers, regulators, community).

Assessing sources [U2.2]
- [ ] C1 Credibility, context and accuracy of a source.
- [ ] C2 Five checks: author and expertise; date (how recent); purpose and bias; evidence; corroboration.
- [ ] C3 Fact versus opinion, and relevance to the question (NCCA guidelines' prompts).
- [ ] C4 Reliability and value: a source may be reliable but of little value to this question.

Recording and key points [U2.3, U2.4]
- [ ] R1 Record every item with source, date and method at the moment of collection; record the hyperlink and date accessed for online sources.
- [ ] R2 Identify the key points or ideas in each source, grouped under the research question or sub-questions.

Importance for different groups, representative data and gaps [U2.5, U2.6]
- [ ] G1 The same finding matters differently to different groups (stakeholders); state who is affected.
- [ ] G2 Representative and comprehensive data: a sample reflecting the group being studied; sampling bias (friends only, one location, one customer type).
- [ ] G3 Information gaps: name the gap, then assess its impact on the conclusion.

Acknowledging sources [U6.1]
- [ ] K1 All sources, primary or secondary and in any format (books, newspapers, journals, reports, online sources, video, podcasts, specialist organisations, people), are recorded and acknowledged.
- [ ] K2 In-text citation plus a reference list at the end; enough detail for the reader to find and authenticate the source; no formal style is required, but be consistent.
- [ ] K3 How to reference print (author, title, year, page) and online (hyperlink and date accessed) sources.
- [ ] K4 Primary data is acknowledged too (when and how it was collected).
- [ ] K5 Plagiarism: work other than your own used without clear acknowledgement; includes AI-generated material; penalties.
- [ ] K6 AI use must be acknowledged: tool name, date generated, brief explanation of how it was used, and the shareable URL if the tool gives one, otherwise the prompt.
- [ ] K7 Honest, ethical and responsible research practice: do not mislead respondents, keep respondents' details private, collect data safely (ethical angle).

Cross-cutting angle
- [ ] X1 Ethics: informed, voluntary and anonymous responses; no copying; honest reporting of limitations; credit given to sources.
- [ ] X2 Digital: online sources need a check on who runs the site and when it was updated; search engines and AI tools can give wrong or invented answers, so every claim is checked against a source; digital survey tools collect primary data but need a representative sample.
- [ ] X3 Sustainability: a source on a business's environmental claims is checked for greenwashing (a claim with no evidence).

## 3. Traps

- Do not use: classifying "annual reports" or "government statistics" flatly as secondary sources, because the glossary says primary sources involve "original documents", so a firm's own accounts can be primary and a news article interpreting them is secondary. Say a source is secondary when it interprets or reports on information collected by others.
- Do not use: "Harvard is required" or any single mandatory referencing style, because the NCCA says no particular formal style is required. A consistent style, with in-text citation and a reference list, is what matters.
- Do not use: "primary data is always more reliable than secondary data", because a small or unrepresentative sample makes primary data weak, and well-sourced secondary data can be stronger.
- Do not use: "the SEC treats unacknowledged AI output as cheating", because the checked wording is plagiarism, with penalties up to and including withholding of results.
- Confusable: a primary source (first-hand or original) versus primary data (collected by the student); reliable (consistent, trustworthy) versus valid or relevant (answers this question); bias (one-sided slant) versus an error; a representative sample versus a large sample (large but unrepresentative is still biased); corroboration versus repetition (two copies of the same press release are one source).
- Confusable: acknowledging a source (crediting) versus quoting (using the exact words); a reference list (every source used) versus a bibliography (also things only read).
- Belongs to a neighbouring subsection: the parts of a project plan, risk assessment, Gantt charts and monitoring (project planning); describing trends, choosing charts, analysis versus evaluation versus synthesis, conclusions and limitations (analysing and presenting findings); the report's word limit, five sections, marks and deadline (the Investigative Study). Mention SMART and sources only as they apply to the question and the source stage.
- Do not use: any real named firm, survey or statistic as an example of a source, because none are checked here.

## 4. What exams ask

The outline records no 2025 sample-paper (Higher Level Sample 1 or Sample 2) question for this subsection. The strand is assessed mainly through the Investigative Study (40%), and the specification says the written exam also assesses "the learning described in ... the unifying strand", so it can appear as the skills behind stimulus questions (judging a news headline, company profile or graph). The command verbs below come from Appendix 1; the forms are expected uses, not recorded questions; no marks or frequencies are claimed.
- **Identify / state** (briefly recognise): the type of source (primary or secondary) of a described item; which SMART letter a question fails; the information gap in a short extract. Full answer: the answer plus one phrase of reason.
- **Outline / describe** (main points; detailed account): the steps to refine a research question; how to acknowledge sources. Full answer: three or four distinct points, each tied to the Investigative Study.
- **Explain** (reasons or causes): why bias in a source or sample matters; why a gap weakens a conclusion. Full answer: the point, the reason, and the effect on the conclusion.
- **Assess / evaluate a source** (judge value): given a source with a clear interest (a firm's own report), judge it on author, date, purpose and bias, evidence and corroboration, and reach a judgement about how far it can be used. Full answer: at least three checks applied to the described source, then a conclusion.
- **Distinguish / compare** primary and secondary data: both named, with a method and a strength and weakness of each, with the contrast made explicit.
- Common mistakes: listing the five checks without applying them to the given source; saying "the sample is too small" without stating what it would make the study unable to show; calling any online source reliable; confusing referencing with a quotation; saying "a SMART question is a good question" without defining each letter; treating Harvard as compulsory.
