# Package: ch8-s3 — Common business models

- **loId:** `ch8-s3` · **chapterId:** `ch8`
- **Syllabus:** Strand 2, section 2.3 (Business planning), [2.3.5]. Students learn about a range of business models including retail, manufacturing, subscription, franchise and affiliate. They should be able to identify and compare the most common business models.
- **Prepared by:** a higher model, 2026-11-23. The writer works only from this package and `course-outlines/NOTES_STANDARD.md`. The NCCA specification is first examined in June 2027; nothing here is Higher Level only.

## 1. Must cover (one bullet or block for every item; tick each off)

Assumed knowledge
- [ ] A1 (assumed) What a business model is: how a firm creates, delivers and captures value, and earns income (the notes on business models and the canvas). Each model below is described by its revenue stream.
- [ ] A2 (assumed) Basic terms: revenue, profit, mark-up, fixed costs, stock, brand, scalability (ability to grow without a matching rise in costs).
- [ ] A3 (assumed) A manufacturer makes goods; a wholesaler buys in bulk and sells on; a retailer sells to the final consumer.

For each of the five models the notes give: how it earns money, key features, advantages, disadvantages and an example.

Retail
- [ ] R1 Definition: sells finished goods directly to end consumers, usually buying them from manufacturers or wholesalers.
- [ ] R2 Earns income by selling goods above purchase cost; profit depends on mark-up and volume sold.
- [ ] R3 Key features: shop or online store, a range chosen for the target market, direct contact with customers.
- [ ] R4 Advantages: direct customer feedback; wide reach through high street or online; flexibility in what to stock.
- [ ] R5 Disadvantages: high premises and stock costs; intense competition; sales fall quickly when consumer spending weakens.
- [ ] R6 Example: Tesco Ireland (Section 2).

Manufacturing
- [ ] M1 Definition: converts raw materials or components into finished products sold to retailers or other businesses.
- [ ] M2 Earns income by selling what it makes at a price covering production cost plus profit margin.
- [ ] M3 Key features: factories or plants, machinery, skilled labour, direct control of quality and processes.
- [ ] M4 Advantages: control over product and quality; lower unit costs possible at high volume; ability to build a brand.
- [ ] M5 Disadvantages: heavy investment in equipment and buildings; machinery can become obsolete; exposure to changing raw material prices.
- [ ] M6 Example: Kerry Group (Section 2).

Subscription
- [ ] U1 Definition: a recurring fee for continued access to a product or service rather than a one-off sale.
- [ ] U2 Earns income through regular payments, making revenue more predictable and easier to forecast than one-off sales.
- [ ] U3 Key features: ongoing customer relationship; access renewed monthly or annually; strong focus on keeping existing customers.
- [ ] U4 Advantages: predictable income; usage data to target offers; lower cost of earning repeat business.
- [ ] U5 Disadvantages: customers can cancel easily so value must keep being delivered; early costs high before enough subscribers join.
- [ ] U6 Example: Spotify (premium monthly fee, removes adverts, offline listening).

Franchise
- [ ] F1 Definition: a franchisor licenses its brand, system and know-how to independent franchisees, who run outlets in return for fees.
- [ ] F2 Income: the franchisor earns from franchisees' fees; the franchisee earns profit from its own outlet after paying them.
- [ ] F3 Key features: proven system, shared brand, training and support, standardised products.
- [ ] F4 Advantages for the franchisee: established brand and system lower start-up risk (but some freedom is given up).
- [ ] F5 Advantages for the franchisor: rapid expansion with less capital, since franchisees fund outlets.
- [ ] F6 Disadvantages: franchisor loses some control of local outlets; a poorly run outlet can damage the brand for all franchisees.
- [ ] F7 Example: Subway in Ireland (Section 2).
- [ ] F8 Franchisor versus franchisee in detail: franchisor provides brand, operating system, training and supply arrangements and keeps ownership of the brand; franchisee provides premises, most capital and local management and follows standards.
- [ ] F9 Fees and royalties: an initial fee paid once to join; royalties paid regularly, usually as a share of the outlet's sales.
- [ ] F10 Control: franchisor sets rules on products, pricing and appearance, limiting the franchisee's decisions.
- [ ] F11 Risk: franchisee carries most of the outlet's financial risk; franchisor's main risk is damage to brand reputation.

Affiliate
- [ ] L1 Definition: earns commission by promoting another firm's products and sending customers to it, usually through a tracked link.
- [ ] L2 Income: commission on each sale or lead, so paid only when a result occurs.
- [ ] L3 Key features: tracked referral link; reliance on a website, social media channel or blog; no stock to hold.
- [ ] L4 Advantages: low start-up cost; little or no need for premises or stock; can work from home.
- [ ] L5 Disadvantages: income depends on the merchant's programme terms, which can change; commission rates often modest.
- [ ] L6 Example: an unnamed blogger reviewing kitchen appliances (sample example).

Comparison of the five models
- [ ] K1 Revenue: subscription gives predictable recurring income; retail and manufacturing depend on each sale.
- [ ] K2 Cost: retail and manufacturing carry high fixed costs; an affiliate has very low costs; a franchisor's own costs are modest.
- [ ] K3 Control: retail and manufacturing give the owner full control of the offer; franchise and affiliate depend on others.
- [ ] K4 Risk: retail and manufacturing carry more financial risk (stock, capital tied up); an affiliate risks very little money.
- [ ] K5 Scalability: franchising and affiliate can grow quickly with less capital; manufacturing growth needs new plant and equipment.
- [ ] K6 A business should choose the model fitting its goals and resources.

Ethics, sustainability and digital angle
- [ ] E1 Ethics: franchisors must be honest about costs and support when recruiting franchisees; affiliates must disclose that they earn commission so recommendations are honest; subscription businesses should make cancelling easy and billing clear. State as general principles.
- [ ] E2 Sustainability: retailers and manufacturers can reduce waste and packaging, source responsibly and design for repair and recycling (link to circular design in the notes on the business plan); manufacturers face raw material and energy impacts.
- [ ] E3 Digital: retail can sell online as well as in store; subscriptions are managed through apps and online accounts; affiliate marketing depends on tracked online links and data. Keep digital-first models for the notes on the digital economy.

## 3. Traps

- Do not use: any fee, royalty, investment or market-share figure for Subway or any franchise, because the sources disagree or are dated and the notes do not need numbers.
- Do not use: Kerry Group as a seller of branded dairy or as an Irish dairy business, because it has sold its Irish dairy arm to Kerry Co-op, with no year (state it only as a business-to-business ingredient maker).
- Do not use: Tesco store counts, delivery fees or minimum-order values, because they change.
- Confusable: franchise versus affiliate. A franchisee runs its own outlet under the franchisor's brand and pays fees; an affiliate sells nothing itself and earns commission for sending customers to another firm.
- Confusable: franchisor versus franchisee roles; keep each party's income, obligations and risk separate.
- Confusable: royalty (regular payment, usually a share of sales) versus initial fee (paid once).
- Confusable: retail (sells goods bought in) versus manufacturing (makes goods). A manufacturer may also sell directly; keep the main model clear.
- Confusable: subscription (recurring payment for access) versus a one-off sale; do not describe a loyalty scheme as a subscription.
- Boundary: digital-economy models (marketplace, digital subscription, crowdfunding, advertising-supported), network effects and churn belong to the notes on the digital economy. Here, subscription is treated as a general model; keep the Spotify example short and leave the digital comparison to those notes.
- Boundary: how a model fits into the plan and the nine canvas blocks belong to the notes on business models and the canvas. The notes on types of business ownership (including franchises as ownership forms) belong elsewhere; here franchise is a model.
- Boundary: sources of finance, growth strategies (including franchising out as expansion) and marketing mix belong to later notes; do not teach them here.

## 4. What exams ask

The outline records no 2025 sample question on this subsection. Points are derived from the verbs "identify" and "compare" and the SEC's applied style, not from a counted frequency.
- Identify the most common models, or which model a described firm uses. A full answer names the model and gives the feature that shows it (recurring fee, brand licence, commission). Common mistake: naming a model with no signal from the stimulus.
- Compare two or more models ("give an account of the similarities and differences, referring to both throughout"). A full answer uses headings such as revenue, cost, control, risk and scalability, and refers to both models under each. Common mistake: describing each model in turn without comparing.
- Franchise detail: franchisor versus franchisee roles, fees and royalties, control and risk. A full answer gives both sides, the payment structure, and the effect on risk and control. Common mistake: only giving advantages for the franchisee.
- Advantages and disadvantages: three or four developed points for a chosen model, each with its effect on the business (for example predictable income helps forecasting).
- Ethics, sustainability and digital angle: apply honest disclosure, responsible sourcing, waste reduction or online tools to a model in the stimulus; state the effect on customers or reputation.
- Tips to build: for any model, give how it earns money first; always name a feature tied to cost, control, risk or scalability; use a comparison table in your head (revenue, cost, control, risk, scalability).
