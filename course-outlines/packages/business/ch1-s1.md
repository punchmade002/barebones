# Package: ch1-s1 — Stakeholders, their importance and interdependence

- **loId:** `ch1-s1` · **chapterId:** `ch1`
- **Syllabus:** NCCA Leaving Certificate Business specification (first examined June 2027), Strand 1, section 1.1, learning outcome [1.1.1]. Students learn about key stakeholders in business (business owners, investors, employees, consumers, suppliers, local community and government); their importance in business; their interdependencies; and the needs and wants of different stakeholders at different stages of business development. They should be able to outline the key internal and external stakeholders and demonstrate their importance in the business environment. Key terms: stakeholder, internal and external stakeholder, interdependence. No Higher Level only content.
- **Prepared by:** a higher model, 2026-11-23. The writer works only from this package and `course-outlines/NOTES_STANDARD.md`.

## 1. Must cover (one bullet or block for every item; tick each off)

Definitions and classification
- [ ] S1 What a stakeholder is: any person or group affected by a business or able to affect it (also the keyTerm).
- [ ] S2 Internal stakeholders: work within or own the business (owners, managers, employees).
- [ ] S3 External stakeholders: outside the business but affected by it or able to affect it (consumers, suppliers, local community, government).
- [ ] S4 Investors can be internal (when also owners) or external (providing money through shares or loans). State this as the notes already do; explain that classification depends on the relationship.
- [ ] S5 Managers run the business day to day and carry out the owners' strategy (assumed knowledge; the outline lists managers as assumed).
- [ ] S6 Interest groups (such as trade unions or lobby organisations) press particular concerns on the business (assumed knowledge).
- [ ] S7 (assumed) The difference between a business owner, a shareholder and a lender: owner takes the decisions and bears the loss, shareholder owns part through shares, lender is repaid with interest.

The seven named stakeholders: who, role, why they matter, needs and wants
- [ ] O1 Business owners: take the main decisions, bear the loss if the business fails; want profit, growth and control.
- [ ] O2 Investors: provide money through share purchases or loans; want security of their capital and a return (dividends or interest).
- [ ] O3 Dividend and interest explained as the investor's return (keyTerm Dividend).
- [ ] O4 Employees: supply the labour that produces goods and services; need fair wages, safe working conditions, job security, training and progression.
- [ ] O5 Employees' skills and motivation shape productivity, customer service and quality, so affect sales and reputation (their importance).
- [ ] O6 Managers: coordinate employees and carry out the owners' strategy; must balance the needs of owners and employees.
- [ ] O7 Consumers: buy the goods and services; expect quality, a fair price, safety, reliability and, increasingly, ethical and sustainable production.
- [ ] O8 Consumers provide the revenue, so a fall in trust can reduce sales quickly (their importance).
- [ ] O9 Suppliers: provide the inputs; want timely payment, reliable orders, a fair price and often a long-term relationship (keyTerm Supplier).
- [ ] O10 Local community: wants employment, a clean environment, safe roads and local spending; may object to noise, pollution or heavy traffic.
- [ ] O11 Government: collects taxes from businesses (corporation tax, VAT), sets laws on employment and safety, funds public services.
- [ ] O12 Agencies (Enterprise Ireland, the Local Enterprise Offices) support businesses with funding and advice.
- [ ] O13 An applied example for each of the seven stakeholders: use the sample (unnamed) examples in section 2.
- [ ] O14 Importance of each stakeholder to the business environment (what the business loses without them), and how a business contributes to its local economy (jobs, spending, taxes, suppliers' orders).

Stages of development
- [ ] D1 Start-up stage (keyTerm): owners and investors prioritise survival and security; employees accept uncertainty in exchange for growth potential.
- [ ] D2 Growth stage: owners and investors want funding for expansion and rising returns; employees seek progression and new roles.
- [ ] D3 Maturity: investors look for steady dividends and stability; consumers and suppliers value consistency and reliability over novelty.
- [ ] D4 Government and community interests intensify as a business grows, because a larger firm affects tax revenue, employment and the environment more.
- [ ] D5 The same stakeholder may move from supporting the business to demanding more from it.

Interdependence
- [ ] I1 Definition of interdependence (quote the Appendix 2 definition in section 2).
- [ ] I2 Rise in supplier prices squeezes owners' profit, which may lead to lower staff pay or higher consumer prices.
- [ ] I3 A new environmental regulation raises costs, may reduce investor returns and change how the community views the firm.
- [ ] I4 A staff strike cuts output, delays consumer orders and strains supplier relationships.
- [ ] I5 A business that focuses on one stakeholder and ignores another risks losing the support of both.
- [ ] I6 Mutual dependence: the stakeholder pairs depend on each other (a business depends on suppliers' goods, suppliers on the business's orders).

Ethical, sustainability and digital angle
- [ ] E1 Ethical and sustainability: consumers increasingly expect ethical and sustainable production; communities and government expect environmental responsibility; fair treatment of employees and fair dealing with suppliers is an ethical expectation as well as a commercial one.
- [ ] E2 Digital: online reviews and social media let consumers and communities exert influence quickly (a loss of trust spreads fast); digital tools let businesses communicate with stakeholders (e-mail, websites, online supplier ordering). Keep this as reasoning, not a named example.

## 3. Traps

Do not use:
- Do not use: "Investors are always internal stakeholders", or that they are always external. The current notes say either, depending on whether the investor is also an owner. Keep that conditional wording.
- Do not use: any tax or grant rate, amount or threshold (for example the corporation tax rate), because rates change and are unverified here.
- Do not use: a named firm for the founder, hotel, bakery or mill examples, because they are unnamed samples. The hotel is in Galway, but no hotel is named.
- Do not use: "managers" as one of the seven named stakeholders; the specification names owners, investors, employees, consumers, suppliers, local community and government. Managers and interest groups are assumed knowledge.
- Do not use: the Google or Killala rows to claim the final outcome of any judicial review; the decisions may be challenged.

Confusable terms
- Stakeholder vs shareholder: a shareholder owns shares; a stakeholder is anyone affected by or able to affect the business. All shareholders are stakeholders, not the reverse.
- Interdependence vs dependence: interdependence is mutual (each group relies on the other for goods and services).
- Internal vs external depends on relationship to the business, not on importance. An external stakeholder (government, a major customer) can have more power than an internal one.
- Owner vs investor vs manager: owner takes risk and control; investor supplies money for a return; manager runs it day to day.

Belongs to neighbouring subsections (mention only as stated)
- Conflicts between stakeholders, how to avoid and resolve them (negotiation, mediation and so on): the notes on stakeholder interaction and conflict. Here: only the ripple effects under interdependence, not causes or resolution methods.
- Stakeholder mapping, the power-interest grid, prioritising stakeholders: the notes on stakeholder mapping. Here: do not place stakeholders in grid quadrants.
- Consumer and employee rights and legislation: the later chapters on consumer and employee law. Here: needs and wants only.
- Forms of business ownership (sole trader, company, co-operative): the notes on forms of business.

## 4. What exams ask

(Source: SEC 2025 sample papers, Higher Level; marks and wording not invented.)
- Identify or outline: name or outline key internal and external stakeholders (Appendix 1: identify = recognise and state briefly; outline = give the main points, restrict to essentials). A full answer names the stakeholder, says whether internal or external and gives its main need.
- Explain the importance of a relationship: one sample paper asks about a founder's relationship with suppliers. A full answer gives reasons or causes (explain = detailed account with reasons): reliable supply, quality, credit terms, a mutual dependence. Common mistake: listing what suppliers do without saying why it matters to the business.
- How a firm contributes to its local economy: one sample paper asks this. A full answer gives developed points: jobs and wages spent locally, orders to local suppliers, taxes funding services, community sponsorship. Common mistake: one-word points ("jobs") without the effect.
- Demonstrate (prove or make clear with examples): use a named or sample business and show how a change in one stakeholder affects another (interdependence).
- Needs at different stages of development: show that needs change, for example investors want security early and returns later. Common mistake: giving the same needs at every stage.
- Applying ethical, sustainability and digital themes: the written exam assesses applying them, so link a stakeholder's needs to ethical sourcing, environmental concerns or online reviews.
- For higher-order command words (discuss, evaluate) give three or four developed points, each with its effect on the business or its stakeholders.
