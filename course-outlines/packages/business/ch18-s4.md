# Package: ch18-s4 — Regulation of financial services and financial fraud

- **loId:** `ch18-s4` · **chapterId:** `ch18`
- **Syllabus:** NCCA Leaving Certificate Business specification (first examined June 2027), Strand 4, section 4.2, learning outcomes [4.2.6], [4.2.7] and [4.2.9]. Students learn about the regulation of the financial products market by the Irish government and the EU, and managing risk, including pensions, mortgages, credit-based products and crypto-finance; investigate how the industry is regulated and discuss the consequences of under-regulation; outline financial fraud and how consumers can protect themselves; and discuss finance stories in the news. Nothing here is Higher Level only. This is the last subsection of the chapter, so it ends with the "Applying my learning" news examples.

## 1. Must cover (one bullet or block for every item; tick each off)

Assumed knowledge
- [ ] A1 (assumed) Why financial markets are regulated: consumers know less than providers (information asymmetry), failures harm many people at once, and trust in finance underpins the economy.
- [ ] A2 (assumed) A bank takes deposits and lends them out; a bank run or failure harms depositors and lending.
- [ ] A3 (assumed) A directive sets goals each member state puts into national law; a regulation applies directly across the EU.

The Central Bank of Ireland [4.2.6]
- [ ] B1 Regulates banks, insurers, credit unions and other financial firms operating in Ireland; its roles are separate.
- [ ] B2 Consumer protection: the Consumer Protection Code sets standards on how firms treat customers (fair terms, clear information, complaints handling). The revised Code (2025) is in force; its new protections are in section 2.
- [ ] B3 Prudential supervision: checks that firms hold enough capital and manage risks soundly, so they can meet obligations to customers.
- [ ] B4 Mortgage measures: limits on how much a borrower can borrow relative to income and to the value of the property, to reduce the risk of over-borrowing (macroprudential policy).
- [ ] B5 Authorisation: firms and advisers must be authorised; operating without authorisation is illegal; it also keeps a register consumers can check.

Redress and protection
- [ ] R1 Financial Services and Pensions Ombudsman (FSPO): an independent body that investigates unresolved complaints about financial service providers and pension providers.
- [ ] R2 The route: complain to the firm first; go to the FSPO only if the firm's response does not resolve it.
- [ ] R3 Deposit guarantee scheme: repays eligible deposits in covered institutions up to a set limit per depositor if a bank fails.
- [ ] R4 Investor compensation: some protection for investors when an authorised investment firm fails and cannot return client assets (generic; no named scheme).

EU supervision and rules
- [ ] U1 European Central Bank: supervises the significant banks in the euro area directly, alongside national authorities such as the Central Bank of Ireland.
- [ ] U2 European Banking Authority (EBA): sets common rules and standards for banks across the EU (a level playing field).
- [ ] U3 European Securities and Markets Authority (ESMA): oversees securities markets and investment firms and promotes consistent rules.
- [ ] U4 EU rules that Irish law must implement: directives on payment services and consumer credit set minimum standards; MiCA for crypto (met in the notes on fintech).
- [ ] U5 Why EU level matters: integrated financial markets, so one country's weakness can spread.

Managing risk in specific products [4.2.7]
- [ ] P1 Pensions: investment returns, inflation, charges, changes to pension rules or retirement age.
- [ ] P2 Mortgages: rising repayments on variable-rate loans; negative equity if property values fall below the debt.
- [ ] P3 Credit products (credit cards, BNPL): easy to use, high cost, can trap people in debt.
- [ ] P4 Crypto-finance: high volatility, limited protection; EU rules have only recently come into application.
- [ ] P5 Managing risk: spread investments, keep borrowing affordable, check the terms before committing.

Consequences of under-regulation
- [ ] G1 The 2008 banking crisis: lax lending, a property bubble and weak oversight left Irish banks with heavy losses; the State injected large sums to keep them open.
- [ ] G2 The State guarantee of bank liabilities (September 2008) shifted the cost of bank failures onto taxpayers.
- [ ] G3 The tracker mortgage scandal: some lenders moved borrowers off tracker rates in ways the Central Bank found unacceptable; redress was paid.
- [ ] G4 General effects: mis-selling spreads, trust in finance falls, consumers are unprotected when firms fail.
- [ ] G5 The balancing point: over-regulation raises costs and can limit access to credit; the aim is proportionate regulation.

Financial fraud [4.2.9]
- [ ] Z1 Phishing (fake emails imitating a bank or official body); smishing (the same by SMS text, often a link to a fake site); vishing (a caller pretends to be from a bank to get security codes or persuade the consumer to move money).
- [ ] Z2 Investment scams (promise high returns with little risk, via social media adverts or fake trading platforms); identity theft (personal details used to open accounts or take credit); money muling (a person lets their account receive and pass on criminal proceeds, often recruited through a fake job offer, and can face prosecution); card fraud (stolen card details used online or copied).
- [ ] Z3 Protection: never share a PIN, password or one-time security code (a genuine bank will not ask); verify contact by ringing the number on the bank's official website or the back of the card; two-factor authentication and updated devices; check statements regularly and report unknown transactions at once; report scams to the Gardaí as well as the bank.
- [ ] Z4 Why reporting matters (quick reporting improves the chance of recovery; section 2 has a checked statistic).
- [ ] Z5 Digital angle: AI-generated adverts and deepfake investment ads, scam texts and social media; cyber-security habits.

Applying my learning: finance stories in the news [4.2.9]
- [ ] N1 A method for any finance story: identify the product or practice and whether it is regulated; identify who is affected (savers, borrowers, investors, taxpayers); decide whether it shows a regulatory failure, a firm's failure or a consumer's decision; consider the response (regulator action, redress, rule change).
- [ ] N2 Two current Irish news examples linked to the chapter (section 2, "News"), each applied with that method: what happened, who is affected, what it shows about regulation, and what a consumer should do.

Ethics, sustainability, digital
- [ ] E1 Ethical: firms' duty to treat customers fairly; the tracker scandal as an ethical failure as well as a regulatory one; scams exploit trust and vulnerable people (older people, young people recruited as money mules).
- [ ] E2 Sustainability: regulators and EU rules increasingly require firms to disclose sustainability risks in investment products and to avoid misleading "green" claims (general statement; name no rule).
- [ ] E3 Digital: digital finance extends fraud risk; regulators now cover digitalisation, scams and unregulated activities in consumer protection (section 2).

## 3. Traps

- Do not use: "160 million euro in 2024, up 24.5 per cent", because it conflicts with the Central Bank's September 2026 statistics (140.8 million euro in 2024); use 179 million in 2025, up 27 per cent.
- Do not use: any total figure for the 2008 bank guarantee (sources gave 375 billion and 440 billion euro), because the figures conflict. Say only that the State guaranteed the liabilities of the main Irish banks.
- Do not use: any figure for the amount the State injected into the banks, because it was not checked.
- Do not use: "the FSPO was established in 2018", because the date was not verified; say it was set up under the Financial Services and Pensions Ombudsman Act 2017.
- Do not use: the names of the banks fined over tracker mortgages and the size of fines, because they were not checked.
- Do not use: "the Consumer Protection Code is the 2012 Code", because the revised 2025 Code replaced it from 24 March 2026.
- Do not use: that the deposit guarantee scheme covers investments, shares, pensions or crypto, because it covers deposits only.
- Do not use: any claim of the number of scams in a year other than those in section 2, because the figures change each year.
- Do not use: Coinbase or any named firm's fine, because the reports were not checked against the Central Bank's own notice.
- Confusable: regulation (rules; the Central Bank, the EBA, ESMA) vs supervision (checking firms follow the rules) vs redress (the Ombudsman and compensation schemes). Prudential supervision (firm's soundness) vs consumer protection (how customers are treated). Macroprudential mortgage limits (system-wide) vs a lender's own affordability test.
- Confusable: phishing (email), smishing (SMS text), vishing (voice call). Identity theft vs card fraud. A money mule (lets an account be used) vs the criminal who sends the money.
- Confusable: ECB (euro-area monetary policy; direct supervision of significant banks) vs the Central Bank of Ireland (Irish regulator and national central bank) vs EBA (rule-setting) vs ESMA (securities).
- Boundary: the consumer rights law (the Consumer Rights Act, GDPR) belongs to the chapter on consumer rights and the notes on data protection, not here. Risk tolerance, sources of information, switching and the Central Credit Register belong to the second subsection; fintech, CBDC and crypto-assets (what they are, MiCA detail) belong to the third. Use crypto here only as a risk to regulate.
- Boundary: company regulation (the Companies Act, the CCPC's enforcement on business) belongs to other chapters.

## 4. What exams ask

- Appendix 1 verbs: **Investigate**: "observe, study, or make a detailed and systematic examination, in order to establish facts and/or reach new conclusions". **Discuss**: "offer a considered, balanced review that includes a range of arguments, factors or hypotheses and is supported by appropriate evidence". **Outline**: "give the main points; restrict to essential points of information". **Explain**: "give a detailed account including reasons or causes". **Evaluate**: "make judgments about the ideas, solutions, or methods" (ethical judgement version: "collect and examine evidence to make judgments and appraisals").
- The outline records no 2025 sample-paper question for this subsection. Stimulus-based questions in the written exam use a short real news headline; expect a finance news story to be set and ask for analysis.
- Likely asks (specification wording; no past frequency known): outline how financial services are regulated in Ireland and the EU (name the body and its role); discuss the consequences of under-regulation (use the 2008 crisis and the tracker scandal with cause and effect on consumers and taxpayers); outline types of financial fraud and how a consumer protects themselves; discuss a finance story in the news.
- A full answer on regulation names the body, states its role in one clause and gives the effect on the consumer (for example, the Ombudsman investigates a complaint the firm has not resolved, so a consumer has an independent route without going to court). A full "discuss under-regulation" answer gives three or four developed points and a balancing point.
- A full fraud answer names the type, says how it works and gives one protective step. Common mistakes: describing phishing and smishing as the same thing without the channel; "protect yourself" answers that say only "be careful"; confusing the ECB with the Central Bank of Ireland; saying the deposit guarantee covers all money.
- Tip: for a news story apply the four-step method (product and regulation, who is affected, whose failure, response) and finish with a justified viewpoint.
- Tip: for a stimulus about a scam, state the type of fraud, who the target was and what the person should do (do not share codes; ring the official number; report to the bank and the Gardaí).
