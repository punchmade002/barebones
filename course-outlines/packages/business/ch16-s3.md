# Package: ch16-s3 — Contingency planning and crisis management

- **loId:** `ch16-s3` · **chapterId:** `ch16`
- **Syllabus:** NCCA Leaving Certificate Business specification (first examined June 2027), Strand 3, section 3.4 (The rationale for planning), learning outcomes [3.4.8] and [3.4.9]; also "Applying my learning" [3.5], which asks students to analyse business news and give an informed opinion. Students learn about the importance of contingency planning for effective crisis management, which may arise locally or nationally, and the need to consider factors including cost, time and risk when developing a contingency plan. They should be able to explain contingency planning in terms of crisis management and discuss the factors to consider. No Higher Level only content. This is the last subsection of the chapter, so it also carries the chapter's current Irish news examples.

## 1. Must cover (one bullet or block for every item; tick each off)

Assumed knowledge
- [ ] A1 (assumed) Risk in business: an event that might harm the business, with a likelihood and an impact (the notes on enterprise and finance already used risk).
- [ ] A2 (assumed) Stakeholders (customers, employees, suppliers, the public) and why their interests matter when priorities are set.
- [ ] A3 (assumed) Strategic planning as a continuous process (previous subsection): contingency planning supports it but covers specific events, not long-term direction.
- [ ] A4 (assumed) Cloud storage, backups and basic cybersecurity as everyday business technology.

Definitions [3.4.8]
- [ ] D1 Crisis: a sudden, unexpected event that threatens an organisation's people, operations, reputation or survival and needs an urgent response.
- [ ] D2 Crisis management: the process of preventing, preparing for, responding to, recovering from and reviewing such events.
- [ ] D3 Contingency plan: a prepared course of action to follow if a specified event occurs (such as failure of the main computer system); written in advance so decisions are made calmly, before pressure builds.
- [ ] D4 Distinguish a crisis (the event) from a contingency plan (the prepared response) and from crisis management (the whole process).

Local and national crises [3.4.8]
- [ ] L1 Local crisis: affects one site or business (fire at a production plant; sudden loss of a key member of staff; a cyber-attack on one firm's systems exposing customer data and disrupting sales).
- [ ] L2 National crisis: affects many businesses at once (a severe storm that damages power networks; a pandemic that closes workplaces).
- [ ] L3 Product recall: may start with one supplier but affects every retailer that stocked the product (a contaminated food product).
- [ ] L4 Supply failure: a disruption to imported components can spread from one supply chain to whole sectors.
- [ ] L5 Cyber-attack: can be local to a business or hit a whole public body; use the checked Irish example.
- [ ] L6 Irish examples from the checked table (cyber-attack on the HSE, 2021; Storm Éowyn, 2025).

Stages of crisis management [3.4.8]
- [ ] S1 Prevention: reduce the chance of a crisis (safety procedures, regular checks of systems and equipment).
- [ ] S2 Preparation: write contingency plans, train staff, arrange backups before a crisis.
- [ ] S3 Response: immediate action once a crisis begins, guided by the plan and clear lines of command.
- [ ] S4 Recovery: restore normal operations (repair systems, serve customers again, support affected staff).
- [ ] S5 Review: examine what happened, what worked and what failed; feed lessons back to prevention and preparation. The five stages form a cycle.

What a contingency plan contains [3.4.8]
- [ ] P1 Triggers: events or warning signs that activate the plan.
- [ ] P2 Responsibilities: who decides and who does each task.
- [ ] P3 Communication plan: who is told what, through which channels, how quickly (staff, customers, suppliers).
- [ ] P4 Backups: copies of data and spare equipment.
- [ ] P5 Alternative suppliers: a second source of essential goods or services.
- [ ] P6 Keep it specific, tested and up to date.

Factors to weigh when developing a plan [3.4.9]
- [ ] F1 Cost: expense of each measure weighed against the losses it prevents.
- [ ] F2 Time: some measures (such as qualifying a standby supplier) take time, so arrange them before needed.
- [ ] F3 Risk likelihood and impact: a likely, severe risk deserves more attention than a rare, minor one.
- [ ] F4 Resources: staff, money and equipment limit what can be prepared.
- [ ] F5 Legal duties: the Safety, Health and Welfare at Work Act 2005 (see Checked facts) and data protection law may require specific measures.
- [ ] F6 Stakeholders: customers, employees, suppliers and the public shape priorities.
- [ ] F7 Each factor explained with its effect on the plan, for "discuss" depth.

Benefits and limits
- [ ] E1 Benefits: faster decisions; protects reputation (a prompt, planned response looks competent); reduces costs by limiting losses and downtime.
- [ ] E2 Limits: plans cost money and time to prepare and maintain; a business may judge the cost too high for rare events; an untested plan goes out of date and may fail; no plan anticipates every event, so judgement is still needed.

Digital, ethical and sustainability angle
- [ ] G1 Digital: greater reliance on online systems means an outage quickly stops sales; backups kept in a separate location allow recovery when a local system is compromised; staff training on phishing and security procedures reduces the chance an attack succeeds; a communications plan covers online channels.
- [ ] G2 Ethical: duty of care to staff, customers and the public in a crisis; honest, prompt communication; protecting personal data.
- [ ] G3 Sustainability: climate-related events (storms, flooding) are a growing risk; plans can include energy resilience and reducing waste from recalls and spoiled stock.

Applying my learning: Irish news [3.5]
- [ ] N1 Two current Irish news examples linked to the chapter's topic (Checked facts, rows N1 and N2), each with the month, year and source, and a short opinion-style analysis: what the crisis was, what planning would have helped (backups, a communication plan, alternative arrangements), and the factors (cost, time, risk) at stake.
- [ ] N2 How to analyse a news item: identify the event, its local or national reach, the stakeholders affected, the plan measures that would reduce the damage, and an informed opinion with a reason.

Key terms
- [ ] K1 Crisis; crisis management; contingency plan; trigger; backup; alternative supplier; recovery; risk likelihood and impact.

## 3. Traps

- Do not use: any cost figure, ransom amount or number of affected patients for the HSE attack, because reports differ and none is verified here.
- Do not use: a named firm for a product recall, supply failure or fire, because none is checked.
- Do not use: the SuperValu cyber-attack report, because it rests on secondary sources and an unconfirmed AI attribution.
- Confusable: crisis vs contingency plan vs crisis management. A crisis is the event, the contingency plan is the prepared response to a specified event, and crisis management is the whole five-stage process.
- Confusable: contingency planning vs strategic planning. Strategic planning sets long-term direction and is ongoing; contingency planning prepares for specified unwanted events.
- Confusable: prevention vs preparation. Prevention lowers the chance of the event; preparation readies the response if it happens.
- Confusable: response vs recovery. Response is immediate action; recovery restores normal operations.
- Confusable: risk likelihood vs impact. Likelihood is how probable, impact is how severe; both are weighed together.
- Confusable: local vs national crisis. Local affects one business or site; national affects many businesses at once. A cyber-attack can be either, depending on reach.
- Boundary: the notes on strategic planning and force-field analysis own the planning cycle, the benefits of strategic planning and the force-field tool. Do not repeat them here.
- Boundary: the notes on change and resistance own the internal and external changes and overcoming resistance. A crisis here is a sudden event, not a planned change.
- Boundary: the notes on consumer protection and on employee rights own product-safety and workplace law in detail. Here the legal duties are only a factor in the plan.
- Boundary: insurance is not a heading of this section; one line may say insurance can transfer part of the cost, but teach no insurance principles (old-syllabus content).

## 4. What exams ask

- **No 2025 sample-paper question is recorded for this subsection in the outline.** Questions are likely in the same style as the rest of the paper: a short stimulus (a news headline or company profile) followed by command-word parts. Do not invent marks or frequencies.
- **Explain contingency planning in terms of crisis management.** Appendix 1: "explain" means "give a detailed account including reasons or causes". A full answer defines both terms, names the five stages, says what a plan contains (trigger, responsibilities, communication, backups, alternative suppliers) and shows with the stimulus how the plan would reduce the damage. Common mistakes: defining a crisis but not saying how a plan helps; listing plan contents with no link to the stimulus; mixing up contingency planning with strategic planning.
- **Discuss the factors to consider.** Appendix 1: "discuss" means "offer a considered, balanced review that includes a range of arguments, factors or hypotheses and is supported by appropriate evidence". A full answer covers at least three or four of cost, time, risk likelihood and impact, resources, legal duties and stakeholders, each applied to the stimulus, with a balanced point (a measure that is costly may still be worth it if the risk is likely and severe). Common mistakes: naming factors only; ignoring the balance; one-sided answers.
- **Likely applications (no marks implied):** distinguish a crisis from a contingency plan with an example; identify a local and a national crisis; explain benefits (and limits) of contingency planning for an online business; outline stages of crisis management; give a view on a news item (apply the [3.5] approach: event, reach, stakeholders, plan measures, informed opinion with reason). "Identify" means "recognise and state briefly".
- **Cross-cutting angle:** digital (backups, phishing training, system outages), ethical (duty of care, honest communication, personal data) and sustainability (climate events, energy resilience) points show applied understanding; use them where the stimulus allows.
- **Tip sources for the writer:** a plan must be written before the crisis; name at least three plan contents; weigh cost against risk; mention limits (untested plans fail); apply to the stimulus.
