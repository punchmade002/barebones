# Package: ch3-s2 — The value of the business economy in Ireland

- **loId:** `ch3-s2` · **chapterId:** `ch3`
- **Syllabus:** NCCA Leaving Certificate Business specification (first examined June 2027), Strand 1, section 1.3 "Business and the economy", learning outcome [1.3.2]: the value of the business economy in Ireland, including turnover and employment patterns; students outline it. The chapter's "Applying my learning" rule also applies: this last subsection ends with current Irish news examples. No Appendix 2 glossary term is named for this chapter.

## 1. Must cover (one bullet or block for every item; tick each off)

What the business economy is
- [ ] B1 The business economy is the market-based part of the economy in which firms produce goods and services for sale, as measured by the CSO (keyTerm).
- [ ] B2 What the CSO's Business in Ireland statistics cover (see section 2): most industry, construction, trade, transport, accommodation and food, information and communication, finance, real estate, professional, administrative, education, health, arts and other services; public administration is left out.
- [ ] B3 The sector list is set by the CSO and can change between releases; give the CSO as the source.
- [ ] B4 (assumed) An enterprise is a business organisation producing goods or services for sale, counted as one unit in statistics (keyTerm); the CSO is the national statistics office.

Headline measures
- [ ] H1 Number of enterprises: businesses operating; dominated by very small firms.
- [ ] H2 Turnover: total sales revenue from trading in a period (keyTerm); shows scale of commercial activity.
- [ ] H3 Gross value added (GVA): the value a business adds to the inputs it buys from others, output minus the cost of materials and services bought in (keyTerm); shows contribution to national output.
- [ ] H4 Persons employed: people working in enterprises (keyTerm); shows jobs supported.
- [ ] H5 Productivity as GVA per person employed; compare sectors.
- [ ] H6 Headline figures for the latest year with dates (section 2); give them as examples, not as numbers to learn.

Size pattern
- [ ] Z1 SME = micro, small or medium enterprise, classified by the EU mainly on staff numbers, with limits on turnover and balance sheet total (keyTerm; thresholds in section 2).
- [ ] Z2 SMEs are almost all enterprises and about two thirds of employment.
- [ ] Z3 Large firms (250 or more staff) are a tiny share of enterprises but a large share of employment and the majority of turnover and GVA.
- [ ] Z4 SMEs' lower GVA per worker than large firms points to a productivity gap that policy tries to close.
- [ ] Z5 SMEs are central to local employment (retail, hospitality, construction).

Ownership pattern
- [ ] O1 Foreign-owned firm: ultimate owners are outside Ireland, often a multinational with a subsidiary here (keyTerm). Multinational company: owns or controls operations in more than one country (keyTerm).
- [ ] O2 Foreign-owned multinationals are a very small share of enterprises but produce most turnover and GVA.
- [ ] O3 Share of value added far higher than share of jobs for foreign-owned firms.
- [ ] O4 Irish-owned firms, mostly SMEs, provide most of the rest of employment and tend to serve the domestic market; some Irish-owned firms are multinationals with operations abroad (optional, section 2).
- [ ] O5 Foreign-owned firms have stronger links to global supply chains; Irish-owned firms to local suppliers and customers.

Sector pattern
- [ ] S1 Sectors differ in GVA per worker and in jobs.
- [ ] S2 High-value manufacturing (such as pharmaceuticals) and information and communication technology: high GVA per worker.
- [ ] S3 Retail, hospitality and construction: many jobs, lower GVA per worker.
- [ ] S4 Food and nutrition: two Irish groups named in the current notes (section 2).
- [ ] S5 Professional services: concentrated in urban areas; domestic and international clients.

Regional pattern
- [ ] G1 Activity is unevenly spread; Dublin and Cork hold the most enterprises (section 2).
- [ ] G2 Regions outside Dublin rely more on SMEs, agri-food and tourism, so are more exposed to change in those sectors.
- [ ] G3 Rural areas depend on small firms for employment; losing one local business has a large local effect.

Recent trends
- [ ] R1 Enterprise numbers and employment grew in 2023 (section 2); large firms grew fastest, small enterprises contracted.
- [ ] R2 Digital services and online trade change how firms sell and create demand for digital skills (general trend, no figure).
- [ ] R3 A tight labour market can raise wages and push firms to automate (general, no sector named as fact).
- [ ] R4 The green transition creates demand for energy-efficiency and low-carbon investment (general).
- [ ] R5 New firms and survival (section 2): many start, fewer than half last five years.

What the patterns mean for Irish business
- [ ] M1 Dependence on a few large foreign firms exposes the economy to decisions made abroad on investment, location and tax; a small number of firms pay a large share of corporation tax (section 2).
- [ ] M2 SMEs give resilient local employment and entrepreneurship, but lower productivity limits their contribution to output growth.
- [ ] M3 Closing the productivity gap: digital transformation, skills, access to finance.
- [ ] M4 Ethics and sustainability: customers, investors and regulators expect firms of every size to manage environmental and social impact; fairness of the tax contribution and job quality are the ethical angle; digital capability is the digital angle.

Applying my learning (news)
- [ ] N1 One or two current Irish news examples linked to the chapter (section 2), each with month, year and source, tied to an indicator or to the business-economy patterns.

## 3. Traps

- Do not use: "the business economy leaves out the health and education sectors", because the CSO's coverage (sections B to S excluding public administration and S94) includes education and health; only public administration is excluded.
- Do not use: "Dublin and the east hold the largest share of turnover and employment", because only county enterprise counts are checked, not turnover or jobs by region. Say Dublin and Cork hold the most enterprises.
- Do not use: "Irish-owned firms account for most turnover", or any figure for Irish-owned firms' turnover and GVA, because only the foreign-owned shares are checked.
- Do not use: "labour shortages in hospitality and construction have raised wages", because no source was checked; say a tight labour market can raise wages.
- Do not use: "Kerry Group and Glanbia have a large presence in global markets" as a statistic or market-share claim, because no figures are checked; call them Irish food and nutrition groups with international operations.
- Do not use: "a small number of firms pay a large share of corporation tax" with any figure other than the Revenue figures above, because other published shares (for example "almost 60%" or "three firms about half") use different years or definitions and were not checked.
- Do not use: figures from earlier years as if current, or mix years in one comparison. Always state the year.
- Confusable: turnover (sales revenue) vs GVA (value added: output minus inputs bought in); persons employed vs number of enterprises; SME (by staff numbers) vs "small business" in everyday speech; foreign-owned firm vs multinational (an Irish-owned firm can be a multinational); the business economy vs the whole economy (GDP).
- Neighbouring subsection, economic indicators: inflation, employment rate, interest rates, GDP, GNP, GNI*, exchange rates and consumer confidence belong there. Mention GDP or GNI* here only in passing. Do not define the indicators again.
- Neighbouring chapters: EU law and the ECB (national and EU policy); Irish exports, the balance of payments and international trade (Irish business globally); business forms and company types (business and its regulation); entrepreneurship and enterprise support agencies (the first chapter on enterprise). Do not teach them here.

## 4. What exams ask

The 2025 sample papers do not set a question on this outcome directly; the outline records no Exam line for it. Likely forms follow the Appendix 1 verbs (no marks or frequencies stated).
- **Outline the value of the business economy** (Outline: give the main points, restricted to essentials). A full answer names the headline measures (enterprises, turnover, GVA, persons employed), gives one or two dated figures, and states one pattern. Common mistakes: confusing turnover with GVA; undated or invented figures; describing the whole economy instead of the business economy.
- **Describe the patterns** (Describe: a detailed account of the main points with examples). A full answer covers size (SMEs vs large), ownership (foreign vs Irish) and sector or region, with the contrast in each (few firms, big output; many firms, many jobs). Common mistakes: patterns with no contrast; one pattern only.
- **Explain what the pattern means** (Explain: reasons or causes), for example dependence on a few large foreign firms: risk from decisions abroad, concentrated tax receipts, but jobs, exports and investment. Common mistakes: only positives or only negatives; not linking to SMEs and local employment.
- **Analyse a news headline or graph** (Analyse; Interpret): name the measure, read the trend, link to business effects. Fuller answers evaluate: judge whether the economy is more exposed or resilient and justify it.
- Tip: for every pattern state the evidence (a dated figure) and its consequence for a stakeholder (workers, SMEs, the State).
