# Package: ch7-s1 — How business ideas are developed

- **loId:** `ch7-s1` · **chapterId:** `ch7`
- **Syllabus:** NCCA *Curriculum Specification for Leaving Certificate Business*, Strand 2, section 2.2 "Idea development", learning outcome [2.2.1]: students learn about how business ideas are developed, and should be able to "determine the factors that impact on the development of business ideas". Outline scope: internal sources (staff and intrapreneurs, research and development, brainstorming, existing products, sales data); external sources (customers, competitors, market research, trends, technology, travel, hobbies, government and university research); idea generation and screening; factors that help or hinder development (market demand, competition, finance, skills and experience, technology, legal and regulatory constraints, economic conditions, ethics and sustainability, timing); Irish examples from each source. Examined first in June 2027. Nothing here is Higher Level only. "Determine" (Appendix 1) means establish or ascertain based on information, usually after investigation: students weigh up which factors apply to a given idea, not just list them.
- **Prepared by:** a higher model, 2026-11-23. The writer works only from this package and `course-outlines/NOTES_STANDARD.md`.

## 1. Must cover (one bullet or block for every item; tick each off)

Background (assumed)
- [ ] A1 (assumed) A business idea is a concept for a product or service that meets a need; many ideas never become businesses, so firms generate several and filter them.
- [ ] A2 (assumed) Entrepreneurs start new businesses; intrapreneurs are employees who develop new ideas inside an existing firm; innovation means turning an idea into something new that is used.
- [ ] A3 (assumed) Primary research (gathered first-hand, such as surveys, interviews, focus groups) and secondary research (existing published data) are the two kinds of market research. Detail belongs to the notes on market research and the target market.
- [ ] A4 (assumed) Fixed costs, revenue and profit are known from the notes on costs and break-even; this subsection only names finance as a factor.

Internal sources
- [ ] I1 Staff: employees see customer problems and work practices first-hand, so they can suggest improvements and products.
- [ ] I2 Intrapreneurs: employees who propose and lead new ventures inside the firm, giving innovation without a new start-up.
- [ ] I3 Research and development: structured testing and experiment that produces new or improved products and processes (definition as in the current key term).
- [ ] I4 Brainstorming: a group generates many ideas quickly with judgement held back.
- [ ] I5 Existing products: extensions, new sizes, flavours or uses of a product the firm already sells.
- [ ] I6 Sales data: shows what sells, to whom and when; reveals gaps and changing demand. Digital angle: online sales and website analytics make this data fast and detailed.

External sources
- [ ] E1 Customers: complaints, reviews, direct requests and feedback show unmet needs.
- [ ] E2 Competitors: show what the market offers and where it is weak or overcrowded.
- [ ] E3 Market research: surveys and focus groups test whether an idea has demand before the firm invests.
- [ ] E4 Trends: lifestyle, population, spending (for example interest in plant-based food) point to new markets. Include sustainability trends (reuse, lower carbon, circular economy).
- [ ] E5 Technology: makes products possible or cheaper (mobile payments, apps, artificial intelligence, automation).
- [ ] E6 Travel: exposes entrepreneurs to products and services not yet available at home.
- [ ] E7 Hobbies and personal interests: founder understands the need well enough to judge the idea.
- [ ] E8 Government and university research: findings firms can commercialise through partnership or licensing; named Irish supports in section 2.
- [ ] E9 Internal versus external compared: internal is cheap, fast and builds on what the firm knows; external widens the view and guards against a product customers do not want.

Generation and screening
- [ ] G1 Idea generation aims for quantity first; judgement is postponed.
- [ ] G2 Screening tests ideas against criteria (fit with goals, cost, demand, legality, ability to make and sell).
- [ ] G3 A simple screening matrix: score each idea against each criterion, giving the most important criteria the highest weighting; add the scores.
- [ ] G4 Ideas that pass screening move on to a feasibility study (the study itself is covered in the notes on the feasibility study).

Factors that help development
- [ ] H1 Market demand: a strong, growing need.
- [ ] H2 Competition: number and strength of rivals affect how easily customers are won.
- [ ] H3 Finance: money is needed for development, production and marketing before revenue arrives.
- [ ] H4 Skills and experience: the right skills reduce the risk of poor execution.
- [ ] H5 Technology: determines whether the product can be made at reasonable cost.

Factors that hinder development
- [ ] X1 Legal and regulatory constraints (for example food safety rules, planning permission, data protection for apps) delay or stop a product.
- [ ] X2 Economic conditions: high interest rates, weak consumer spending, inflation reduce demand and raise borrowing costs.
- [ ] X3 Ethics and sustainability: an idea that harms the environment, exploits workers or breaks ethical standards may be rejected or lose customers. Include the positive side: a sustainable idea can attract customers and funding.
- [ ] X4 Timing: too early (market not ready) or too late (market has moved on).
- [ ] X5 Each factor can help or hinder depending on the idea: students must say which way it cuts for the business in the question, and give the effect on the business.

Irish examples (see section 2)
- [ ] R1 At least one checked Irish idea from an internal source and one from an external source (or supports that turn research into ideas), each with the source of the idea named.
- [ ] R2 Sample (unnamed) examples labelled as samples may be kept.

Glossary
- [ ] K1 No Appendix 2 term is defined in this subsection. Define the key terms (internal source, external source, screening, brainstorming, market research, research and development, sales data) in plain wording as the current notes do.

## 3. Traps

- Do not use: "Stripe, founded by two Irish brothers, ... an example of an idea shaped by digital trade" with a founding year, funding figure or valuation, because only the checked row above (Tipperary upbringing, ages 21 and 19 in 2010, simpler online payments); the holiday-prototype and Peter Thiel pitch claims are unconfirmed and dates for Thiel's investment conflict (2010 vs 2011) has been verified.
- Do not use: any list of other well-known Irish start-ups or "unicorns" from memory, because none has been checked.
- Do not use: the Innovation Voucher at the old value of €5,000 or 12 months, because it is out of date; and do not describe it as covering market research, because it excludes it.
- Do not use: "physical evidence" or any other marketing-mix item here; the marketing mix is a later chapter.
- Confusable: internal versus external source (where the idea comes from) versus market research (a method that is itself an external source and is also used to test ideas later). Brainstorming as an idea source here is the same technique as the ideate phase of design thinking, but the notes on design thinking own the four phases.
- Confusable: screening (a quick comparison of ideas against criteria) versus a feasibility study (a detailed evidence-gathering report on one idea).
- Confusable: intrapreneur (inside an existing firm) versus entrepreneur (starts a new firm).
- Belongs to a neighbouring subsection: the four phases of clarify, ideate, develop and implement and what "iterative", "person-centred" and "solution-centred" mean belong to the notes on design thinking; the parts of a feasibility study, break-even, outcomes (proceed, modify, abandon) and funding supports belong to the notes on the feasibility study; surveys and sampling methods belong to the notes on market research; the business plan belongs to the notes on business planning.

## 4. What exams ask

- The SEC 2025 sample papers (Higher Level Samples 1 and 2) contain no question recorded in the outline on this subsection. Do not claim one. The exam style is real, current, named Irish firms and news stories with parts using the specification's command words.
- "Identify" (recognise and state briefly): name sources or factors, for example two internal and two external sources of ideas. One word or short phrase is enough, so do not explain.
- "Outline" (give the main points, restricted to essentials): a source or factor plus one line of what it contributes.
- "Explain" (a detailed account with reasons or causes): a source or factor, why it produces ideas, and its effect on the firm. Give three or four developed points when marks are high.
- "Determine the factors that impact" is the spec verb: a full answer names the factor, says whether it helps or hinders in the situation given, and states the effect on the business (cost, demand, delay, reputation). Link each factor to the case in the stem.
- "Discuss" or "analyse": balanced; one helping and one hindering factor, or internal versus external sources, ending with a judgement on which matters more for the named business.
- Common mistakes: listing sources with no link to the case; treating "market research" as only a hindrance check rather than also a source of ideas; mixing up screening and a feasibility study; giving a factor with no effect on the business; naming a firm with a wrong detail.
- The written exam rewards applying the cross-cutting themes: expect a sustainability or ethics factor and a digital source (data, apps, AI).
