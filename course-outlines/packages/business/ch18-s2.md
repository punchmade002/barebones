# Package: ch18-s2 — Risk tolerance, reliable information, switching providers and credit ratings

- **loId:** `ch18-s2` · **chapterId:** `ch18`
- **Syllabus:** NCCA Leaving Certificate Business specification (first examined June 2027), Strand 4, section 4.2, learning outcomes [4.2.2], [4.2.3] and [4.2.5]. Students analyse the risks when acquiring financial products and why consumers should consider switching providers; explain risk tolerance and the risks facing consumers of financial products, and how to find reliable financial information; outline the importance of switching; and describe how a credit rating is established, what affects it, and the consequences of a poor rating, which travels with a person over time. Nothing here is Higher Level only.
- **Prepared by:** a higher model, 2026-11-23. The writer works only from this package and `course-outlines/NOTES_STANDARD.md`.

## 1. Must cover (one bullet or block for every item; tick each off)

Assumed knowledge
- [ ] A1 (assumed) Financial products (current and savings accounts, loans, mortgages, insurance, investments) are bought from regulated providers; a consumer is an individual buying them for personal use.
- [ ] A2 (assumed) Risk and return go together: a higher potential return usually means a higher risk of loss. Inflation and interest rates were met in the notes on saving, investing and borrowing.
- [ ] A3 (assumed) Credit means borrowing against a promise to repay; a lender decides whether to lend by assessing the likelihood of repayment.

Risk tolerance
- [ ] T1 Risk tolerance: the degree of uncertainty about the value of money that a person is willing to accept in pursuit of a higher return.
- [ ] T2 Risk capacity: the ability to absorb a loss without serious hardship; it can be lower than willingness (a young family on a tight budget).
- [ ] T3 Matching tolerance to product: low tolerance suits deposit accounts and State-backed savings; a higher tolerance may suit shares or funds held for many years.
- [ ] T4 Risk tolerance changes with age, income, dependants and experience, so review it when circumstances change.

Risks facing consumers of financial products
- [ ] K1 Loss of capital: an investment falls in value so less is returned than was put in.
- [ ] K2 Inflation risk: money held in low-return products shrinks in real value.
- [ ] K3 Interest rate risk: repayments on a variable-rate loan rise when the lender's rate rises.
- [ ] K4 Mis-selling: a product sold to someone whose needs or attitude to risk it does not suit.
- [ ] K5 Hidden charges: fees, exit penalties and small-print costs reduce returns or raise the cost of borrowing.
- [ ] K6 Over-indebtedness: borrowing more than can be repaid leads to arrears, repossession and a damaged credit record.
- [ ] K7 Digital risks: fraud and scams when buying products online (met in detail in the notes on regulation and fraud; one line here).

Reliable financial information
- [ ] I1 Regulated and independent sources: the Central Bank of Ireland's consumer hub, the Competition and Consumer Protection Commission (CCPC) consumer pages, the Money Advice and Budgeting Service (MABS) for people in debt, a regulated financial adviser authorised by the Central Bank.
- [ ] I2 Regulated adviser: must be authorised and must assess a client's needs before recommending a product.
- [ ] I3 Unreliable sources: unregulated social media commentators ("finfluencers") may promote products without disclosing payments and without knowing the follower's circumstances.
- [ ] I4 Comparison websites help, but check whether the site earns commission from the products it lists.
- [ ] I5 A three-question test for any source: is it regulated, does it gain from a sale, does it explain the risks as well as the rewards?

Switching providers
- [ ] W1 What switching is: moving a product (current account, savings account, mortgage, insurance) to a different provider to get a better rate, lower charges or better service.
- [ ] W2 Why it matters: it is the main way a consumer ends a poor deal; loyalty can be penalised because new customers are sometimes offered better rates than existing ones.
- [ ] W3 The process: compare products, check exit charges, apply to the new provider, then close or transfer the old product.
- [ ] W4 Barriers: early repayment charges, exit fees, lock-in periods on fixed-rate products, time and paperwork, inertia.
- [ ] W5 Switching a mortgage can involve valuation and legal costs, so the saving must outweigh them.

Credit ratings
- [ ] C1 A credit rating (credit history) is a record of how reliably a person has handled borrowing.
- [ ] C2 How it is established: lenders report to the Central Credit Register (CCR), run by the Central Bank of Ireland; lenders can check it before lending; the individual can request their own credit report.
- [ ] C3 What is recorded: loans and credit cards, repayment history, whether payments were late or missed (set out in section 2).
- [ ] C4 How long: loan records are kept for up to five years after the loan is repaid, written off or discharged; applications for six months.
- [ ] C5 Factors that affect the rating: repayment history, arrears, level of debt, number of applications in a short period, legal action or court judgments.
- [ ] C6 Consequences of a poor rating: loan, card or mortgage refused; credit offered at higher interest rates; some landlords and service providers may check; effects last for years and ease as entries age off the record.
- [ ] C7 Improving a record: pay on time, reduce debt, avoid unnecessary applications, seek early help from MABS or the lender.

Ethics, sustainability, digital
- [ ] E1 Ethical angle: mis-selling and undisclosed commission are ethical failures by providers; finfluencers' lack of disclosure; lenders' duty to treat borrowers in difficulty fairly; the fairness of "loyalty penalties" on existing customers.
- [ ] E2 Digital angle: switching and comparison are quicker online; credit history is held digitally and can be requested online; social media is now a common route to financial information and to scams, so check its source.
- [ ] E3 Sustainability angle: ask whether the product's provider publishes how it invests (for example sustainable funds or green mortgages); name none.

## 3. Traps

- Do not use: "MoneySense" as the name of the Central Bank's consumer information service, because I could not verify that name. Use "the Central Bank of Ireland's consumer hub" and the CCPC.
- Do not use: "the CCR gives a credit score" or a number-based rating, because the Irish CCR provides a credit report, not a score (ccpc.ie); "credit rating" is the specification's term for the history lenders see.
- Do not use: the claim that negative entries "stay for a period set by the rules" without the figure; use five years from the loan being discharged, and six months for applications.
- Do not use: any named Irish credit bureau as a current register, because the notes' source for the national register is the CCR and no other bureau was checked.
- Do not use: any particular switching incentive, cashback or rate, because they change.
- Confusable: risk tolerance (willingness to accept uncertainty) vs risk capacity (ability to absorb a loss). Credit rating (the record) vs credit score (a number; not what the CCR shows). Switching a provider vs refinancing a loan.
- Confusable: an adviser who is authorised and must assess your needs vs a commentator or comparison site that earns commission.
- Boundary: the Consumer Protection Code, the Central Bank's other roles, the Financial Services and Pensions Ombudsman, the deposit guarantee scheme and the types of fraud belong to the subsection on regulation and fraud; mention only that regulation exists. How to choose between saving, investing and borrowing and the factors in each belong to the subsection on saving, investing and borrowing. Fintech, apps and digital currency belong to the subsection on fintech and digital currency.
- Boundary: this is the consumer's credit history, not a business's creditworthiness.

## 4. What exams ask

- Appendix 1 verbs: **Outline**: "give the main points; restrict to essential points of information". **Explain**: "give a detailed account including reasons or causes". **Analyse**: "study or examine something in detail, break down in order to bring out the essential elements or structure; identify parts and relationships, and to interpret information to reach conclusions". **Describe**: "give a detailed account of the main points of a topic using words, diagrams, examples and/or images where appropriate". **Identify**: "recognise and state briefly a distinguishing fact or feature".
- Seen on a 2025 sample paper (Sample 1): outline three factors that can affect a person's credit rating. A full answer states three distinct factors in a short phrase each, with one clause on why it counts (for example, missed payments are recorded and weigh against the borrower; a large debt relative to income suggests difficulty in meeting more repayments; many applications in a short time can suggest financial stress). Common mistakes: giving the consequence (loan refused) instead of the factor; repeating "paying late" three ways; leaving out the reason when the verb is outline and the mark scheme expects a developed point; confusing credit rating with income.
- Likely related asks (specification wording, no past frequency known): explain risk tolerance and one risk of a financial product; outline why switching is important and a barrier to it; explain how to judge whether a source of financial information is reliable (regulated, no commission, explains risks); describe what a poor credit rating leads to.
- Tip: for "explain" give the reason or cause and its effect on the consumer; for "outline" give a point, then stop.
- Tip: use the three-question test (regulated, gains from a sale, explains the risks) to evaluate an unfamiliar source in a stimulus.
