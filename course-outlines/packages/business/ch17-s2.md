# Package: ch17-s2 — Ethical and sustainable consumption, the shadow economy and digital technology

- **loId:** `ch17-s2` · **chapterId:** `ch17`
- **Syllabus:** Leaving Certificate Business specification (first examined June 2027), Strand 4, section 4.1, outcomes [4.1.2] and [4.1.3]. Students learn the importance of ethics and sustainability, including the consequences of participating in the shadow economy for the consumer, the business and the economy, and how consumers contribute to the circular economy; how technology affects consumers positively and negatively. They demonstrate how consumer behaviour might be informed by ethical and sustainability concerns, and investigate how digital technology affects consumer behaviour.
- **Prepared by:** a higher model, 2026-11-23. The writer works only from this package and `course-outlines/NOTES_STANDARD.md`.

## 1. Must cover (one bullet or block for every item; tick each off)

Assumed knowledge
- [ ] A1 (assumed) Consumer behaviour is shaped by price, quality, convenience, values, advertising and budget.
- [ ] A2 (assumed) Ethics means judging a choice by whether it is fair to people, animals and communities; sustainability means meeting present needs without damaging the environment for the future.
- [ ] A3 (assumed) Tax revenue pays for public services; businesses that follow the law carry costs (tax, safety testing) that illegal competitors avoid.

Ethical consumption
- [ ] E1 Ethical consumption: choosing products by how they were produced and how workers, animals and communities are treated, not only price and quality.
- [ ] E2 Fair trade: fairer prices and better trading conditions for producers in developing countries.
- [ ] E3 Labour standards: fair pay, reasonable hours, freedom to organise; consumers can favour firms that meet them.
- [ ] E4 Animal welfare: buying from suppliers with high animal-treatment standards.
- [ ] E5 Buying local: supports nearby producers, shorter transport, local employment and environmental benefit.
- [ ] E6 The limit on ethical choice: it can cost more, so values must be weighed against budget.
- [ ] E7 Ethics and sustainability as drivers of behaviour: avoiding a brand because of its environmental record can cut its sales and damage its reputation; labels and certification help consumers identify ethical and sustainable products; each consumer balances price, quality and values.

Sustainable consumption
- [ ] S1 Reduce (buy less; largest benefit), reuse (refillable containers, passing items on), repair (keeps materials and labour in service), recycle (last of the preferred actions, still uses energy and resources).
- [ ] S2 Buying second-hand extends an item's life and avoids resources for a new one.
- [ ] S3 Energy labels let consumers compare running costs and environmental impact.

Circular economy
- [ ] CE1 Definition (quote in section 2).
- [ ] CE2 Linear (make, use, throw away) versus circular (design for reuse, repair, recycling; value kept longer).
- [ ] CE3 What consumers can do: choose durable products; return items for recycling; buy repaired or refurbished goods; borrow, share or rent instead of owning; use the deposit return scheme. (The exam asks for four ways; give at least five.)
- [ ] CE4 Deposit return scheme: a deposit paid on a drinks container is refunded when the empty container is returned for recycling (the Irish scheme: see section 2).

Shadow economy
- [ ] SH1 Definition (quote in section 2).
- [ ] SH2 Examples: counterfeit goods; cash work not reported for tax; goods sold without required safety testing.
- [ ] SH3 Consequences for consumers: no consumer rights or guarantees, safety risks.
- [ ] SH4 Consequences for businesses: legal firms undercut on price, unfair competition.
- [ ] SH5 Consequences for the economy: lost tax revenue, weaker funding for public services; hard to trace, so hard to protect consumers.
- [ ] SH6 Ethical angle: buying from the shadow economy can support exploitative labour and unsafe products.

Digital technology and consumer behaviour
- [ ] D1 Positive: wider choice online, price comparison sites and apps, convenience (any time, from home), online reviews, digital budgeting tools and subscription tracking.
- [ ] D2 Negative: impulse buying (one-click purchase, saved payment details, instant deals); targeted advertising using browsing and purchase data; dark patterns (such as hidden subscription sign-ups); fraud (fake websites, phishing); data shared with third parties; fake reviews and influencers not disclosing a commercial relationship.
- [ ] D3 Digital and sustainability: online shopping makes returns, delivery and fast-fashion overbuying easy, but digital tools also support second-hand marketplaces, repair guides and sharing (give a balanced point).
- [ ] D4 Evaluate: a considered judgement on net effect, for example that benefits are real for an informed consumer but depend on digital skill and on the law limiting manipulative practices.

## 3. Traps

- Do not use: any claim that the deposit return scheme covers glass, milk or small containers, because it does not.
- Do not use: deposit amounts as current beyond the dated figures above; they can be changed by the Minister.
- Do not use: statistics on the size of the shadow economy, counterfeit trade or online fraud; none is checked.
- Do not use: a named influencer, platform, app or brand as an example of dark patterns, targeted ads or fake reviews; none is checked.
- Confusable: sustainable (environmental and long-term) versus ethical (fairness to people and animals); the two overlap but are not the same. Recycle is not the same as the circular economy: recycling is one part, and repair and reuse rank higher. Shadow economy versus black market: use the specification's term; it includes unreported legal work, not just illegal goods. Tax avoidance (legal) is not the shadow economy.
- Neighbouring subsections: consumer legislation, remedies and the CCPC belong to the notes on consumer rights and responsibilities; GDPR, data subject rights and protecting your data belong to the notes on protecting personal data. Mention data collection here only as an effect of digital technology on behaviour, without GDPR detail.
- Do not teach the 7 Ps or marketing mix here.

## 4. What exams ask

From the outline's Exam lines (2025 sample papers):
- Analyse three ways digital technology influences consumer behaviour (Sample 1). A full answer gives three distinct ways, each with an explanation of how it changes what the consumer does, for example comparison sites shifting the choice towards the cheapest, with an effect on the consumer or the business. Mistake: listing "convenience, choice, reviews" in a word each with no analysis.
- Evaluate the impact of digital technology on consumer behaviour (Sample 2). "Evaluate" means weighing evidence and making a judgement: give positive and negative effects, then a conclusion on overall impact with a reason. Mistake: only listing benefits, or no conclusion.
- Four ways a consumer can contribute to the circular economy (Sample 2). A full answer gives four separate actions (durable purchases, recycling or the deposit return scheme, buying refurbished or second-hand, borrowing or renting), each briefly explained. Mistake: naming "reduce, reuse, recycle" as one point, or describing what businesses do.
- A fill-in-the-blank on "circular economy" (Sample 2): the student must know the term and its meaning (use the Appendix 2 definition).
- Command verbs: "demonstrate how consumer behaviour might be informed" asks for examples with reasoning; "investigate" asks for evidence from the student's own study.
- Consequences of the shadow economy are asked for three parties; a full answer addresses consumer, business and economy separately.
