# Package: ch4-s3 — Evaluating one EU regulation and one EU directive

- **loId:** `ch4-s3` · **chapterId:** `ch4`
- **Syllabus:** NCCA Leaving Certificate Business specification (first examined June 2027), Strand 1, section 1.4, learning outcome [1.4.6], and the "Applying my learning" outcome [1.6] for this chapter. Students learn about how the EU affects business in Ireland, focusing on a directive's or regulation's purpose, how it has been implemented in Ireland, its positive and negative impact on different stakeholders, and whether it fosters ethical and sustainable practice and/or the use of technology. They should be able to evaluate one regulation and one directive of their choice, different from the legislation used in the consumer and employee chapters (so not GDPR and not the employment directives).
- **Prepared by:** a higher model, 2026-11-23. The writer works only from this package and `course-outlines/NOTES_STANDARD.md`.

## 1. Must cover (one bullet or block for every item; tick each off)

Assumed knowledge
- [ ] A1 (assumed) A regulation applies directly in Ireland and needs a national authority to supervise it; a directive is transposed by an Act or statutory instrument by a deadline (from the notes on EU decision-makers; repeat in one line each).
- [ ] A2 (assumed) A stakeholder is anyone affected by a business or able to affect it; the impacts are judged for named stakeholders (the stakeholder notes in the first chapter).
- [ ] A3 (assumed) ESG: environmental, social and governance factors, the pillars of ESG frameworks (glossary term), which firms may be expected to report on.

How to evaluate
- [ ] V1 Command word: "evaluate" means collect and examine evidence to make judgments; identify the limitations of evidence; make judgments about ideas, solutions or methods (Appendix 1, "Evaluate (ethical judgement)"). So a full answer ends in a reasoned verdict.
- [ ] V2 The five-part frame: name the instrument and its type, purpose, Irish implementation, impact on named stakeholders (positive and negative, with a reason), and a verdict on ethics, sustainability and technology with one strength and one limitation.
- [ ] V3 Choice rules: one regulation and one directive; prepare at least two options of each; avoid GDPR and employment directives; choose instruments different from those used in the consumer and employee chapters.
- [ ] V4 Check currency: rules in this area are being changed by EU simplification measures; a student writes only what they can stand over.

Option 1 regulation: Digital Services Act
- [ ] DSA1 What it is, and that it is a regulation: duties for online intermediaries (platforms, marketplaces, search engines).
- [ ] DSA2 Purpose: safer online platforms, less illegal content, more user rights (a reason given when content is removed, routes to appeal).
- [ ] DSA3 Irish implementation: applies directly; Coimisiún na Meán as Digital Services Coordinator; another authority for online marketplaces; Irish Act 2024 gives powers and penalties.
- [ ] DSA4 Positive for consumers: reporting and appeal routes; limits on profiling-based advertising to minors.
- [ ] DSA5 Positive for smaller sellers: clearer dispute process with platforms, one set of rules across the EU.
- [ ] DSA6 Negative for smaller platforms and start-ups: compliance cost (some duties are lighter for micro and small firms).
- [ ] DSA7 Negative argument on free expression: fear of fines leading to over-removal of lawful content (present as an argument).
- [ ] DSA8 Verdict: strong on transparency and technology governance (recommender systems, advertising); weak on sustainability.
- [ ] DSA9 Ethical angle (fairness, child protection) and digital angle (it governs digital technology) stated explicitly.

Option 2 regulation: AI Act
- [ ] AI1 What it is: sorts AI systems by risk and sets duties that rise with the risk.
- [ ] AI2 Purpose: safe, rights-respecting, transparent AI while supporting innovation.
- [ ] AI3 Banned practices (examples: social scoring, emotion recognition in workplaces and schools).
- [ ] AI4 High-risk uses (recruitment, credit scoring, access to education) need documentation, risk management and human oversight.
- [ ] AI5 Irish implementation: applies directly; Ireland has designated national authorities; a national bill was published in 2026.
- [ ] AI6 Timing: the Act applies in phases and the high-risk dates have been changed by EU simplification; do not state dates beyond those in section 2.
- [ ] AI7 Positive for consumers: protection from manipulative or discriminatory AI; knowing when they deal with an AI system.
- [ ] AI8 Negative for start-ups: compliance cost for small firms using AI in hiring or lending.
- [ ] AI9 Verdict: strong on ethics and on directly regulating technology; weak on sustainability.
- [ ] AI10 Keyterm: risk-based regulation (strictness depends on the harm possible).

Option 1 directive: Corporate Sustainability Reporting Directive (CSRD)
- [ ] CS1 What it is, and that it is a directive: large companies report on sustainability to common standards.
- [ ] CS2 Purpose: reliable, comparable information on ESG performance for investors, lenders and other stakeholders.
- [ ] CS3 Double materiality (keyTerm): how sustainability issues affect the firm and how the firm affects people and the environment.
- [ ] CS4 Irish implementation: transposed by statutory instrument amending company law; the 2026 EU simplification changed who is covered.
- [ ] CS5 Positive for investors and consumers: comparable data on emissions, labour standards, governance.
- [ ] CS6 Positive for firms: efficiency savings, better reputation (links to ESG).
- [ ] CS7 Negative for smaller suppliers: requests for data from large customers; the 2026 changes limit what can be demanded.
- [ ] CS8 Verdict: strong on ethical and sustainable practice; limitations are administrative burden and box-ticking.
- [ ] CS9 Digital angle: reporting depends on data collection and digital reporting; no checked detail on format (see section 3).

Option 2 directive: Single-Use Plastics Directive
- [ ] SU1 What it is: targets plastic items most often found as litter on beaches and in the sea.
- [ ] SU2 Purpose: reduce marine litter by banning some products and making producers pay for the clean-up of others.
- [ ] SU3 Banned from July 2021: cutlery, plates, straws, stirrers, cotton bud sticks, balloon sticks, expanded polystyrene food containers.
- [ ] SU4 Extended producer responsibility (keyTerm): producers fund collection and clean-up (tobacco filters, fishing gear, wet wipes).
- [ ] SU5 Irish implementation: transposed by statutory instrument in 2021; Ireland's plastic bag levy since 2002 as earlier national policy.
- [ ] SU6 Positive for the environment and coastal communities and tourism.
- [ ] SU7 Negative for cafes, takeaways and small retailers: replace stock; alternatives cost more.
- [ ] SU8 Negative argument for disabled people and some consumers: alternatives less convenient (present as an argument).
- [ ] SU9 Verdict: strong on sustainability and ethical product choice; limitation: alternatives can have a larger footprint over their life cycle (an argument, no figures).
- [ ] SU10 Technology angle: little direct; mention product redesign and reuse systems generically.

Choosing, comparing and applying
- [ ] C1 Comparison table across the four options: type | who must act | Irish route | main gain | main cost | verdict on ethics/sustainability/technology.
- [ ] C2 Keep each impact specific: stakeholder, effect, reason, example.
- [ ] C3 Applying my learning [1.6]: read a current Irish news story about the chapter's topic and give an informed opinion with evidence; one worked example in section 2 (news).

## 3. Traps

- Do not use: GDPR, the Work-Life Balance Directive, the Platform Work Directive or any employment directive as the student's choice, because they belong to the consumer and employee chapters.
- Do not use: "ban on targeted advertising to minors" as a flat statement, because the Article 28 rule concerns advertising based on profiling to users known to be minors.
- Do not use: "social scoring by public authorities", because the AI Act bans social scoring generally; say "social scoring".
- Do not use: "Ireland must designate national authorities" for the AI Act, because Ireland has already designated them (S.I. 366 of 2025, as amended).
- Do not use: "the Act applies in phases, with the bans first and the duties for other systems following over several years" with any dates other than 2 February 2025, because the high-risk dates were postponed in 2026.
- Do not use: "reporting phased in by company size, starting with the largest" for the CSRD, because the 2026 Omnibus I directive narrowed the scope to companies above 1,000 employees and EUR 450 million turnover and needs transposing; say the scope has been narrowed and state the figures with "as of 2026".
- Do not use: "reports must be tagged digitally" for the CSRD, because the digital format is not checked here.
- Do not use: "applies double materiality" as part of the current standards, because the standards were being simplified; use the keyTerm definition only.
- Do not use: "SMEs in the supply chain are asked for data they may not hold" without the 2026 limit on what large firms can demand, because it overstates the burden.
- Do not use: fines "up to 6 per cent of global annual turnover" as the reason lawful content is removed; present it as a criticism that fines may encourage over-removal.
- Do not use: "Digital Services Act: a ban on all targeted advertising" or a claim that it covers sustainability, because it largely does not.
- Do not use: any plastic bag levy rate, fine amounts or thresholds not in section 2.
- Confusable: Digital Services Act (platform duties) vs Digital Markets Act (competition rules for gatekeepers) vs GDPR (personal data). Do not use the last two.
- Confusable: regulation vs directive. The DSA and AI Act are regulations; the CSRD and Single-Use Plastics Directive are directives, and Irish statutory instruments that transpose them are titled "Regulations".
- Confusable: sustainability reporting (CSRD) vs ESG as a concept; CSRD is the legal duty to report.
- Belongs to a neighbouring subsection: how the institutions work and the three types of EU law (the notes on EU decision-makers and types of EU law); Irish policy and lobbying (the notes on Irish government policy and legislation). Do not re-teach them; give one line each as the assumed point.
- Belongs to a neighbouring chapter: Ireland's trade and trading blocs (international business chapter); consumer data protection (consumer chapter); employee rights (employee chapter).

## 4. What exams ask

- The 2025 Sample 1 paper asks students to name an EU regulation, explain how it was implemented in Ireland, and examine two positive and two negative impacts on stakeholders. The 2025 Sample 2 paper asks students to name an EU directive, explain its purpose and evaluate its impact on business in Ireland.
- "Examine" (look closely at arguments, data or concepts to uncover assumptions and relationships): two positives and two negatives, each tied to a named stakeholder with a reason and a business example. Common mistake: general effects such as "good for the environment" with no stakeholder, or only positives.
- "Explain" Irish implementation: for a regulation, that it applies directly and which Irish authority supervises it; for a directive, the transposing Act or statutory instrument and the deadline. Common mistake: saying a directive applies automatically.
- "Explain" purpose: the problem the EU wanted to solve and who it protects, in one or two sentences.
- "Evaluate" impact on business in Ireland: balanced gains and costs for different stakeholders (consumers, large firms, small firms, workers, the environment), then a judgement that weighs them and states one limitation. Never leave out the verdict.
- "Identify" or "name": give the full name of the instrument and say whether it is a regulation or a directive.
- Ethics, sustainability and technology: the question may ask whether it fosters ethical and sustainable practice or the use of technology; answer each in turn with a reason.
- Applying my learning: an unseen headline on an EU rule; identify the instrument, the Irish authority, the stakeholders and give a reasoned opinion.
- Never invent marks, frequencies or examiner quotes in the tips.
