# Package: ch11-s1 — Competition, competitive advantage and Porter's five forces

- **loId:** `ch11-s1` · **chapterId:** `ch11`
- **Syllabus:** Strand 2, section 6 (Growth, development and expansion), outcomes [2.6.1] and [2.6.2]. Students learn about "the importance of identifying potential competition and competitors and the significance of a business's competitive advantage and how a business can capitalise on this." They should be able to demonstrate an understanding of the importance of identifying competition in the market, and use Porter's five forces model to identify and analyse competition in the market and use these findings to identify the competitive advantage of a business. Glossary term: Porter's five forces model. Applied tool: Porter's five forces (use the Skills block).

## 1. Must cover (one bullet or block for every item; tick each off)

Background (assumed)
- [ ] A1 (assumed) A market is where buyers and sellers meet; a business earns profit only if it wins customers from other businesses that want the same spending.
- [ ] A2 (assumed) Profit margin and price: lower costs or higher prices raise margin; customers compare price, quality and service.
- [ ] A3 (assumed) The target market and the marketing mix (product/service, pricing, promotion, people, packaging, process, place) from the marketing chapters: advantage is delivered through the mix.
- [ ] A4 (assumed) Economies of scale: average cost per unit falls as output rises (also in the notes on expansion strategies).

Identifying competition [2.6.1]
- [ ] C1 What a competitor is: any business that competes for the same customers or spending.
- [ ] C2 Direct competitors: same or very similar products to the same customers (the notes' coffee-shop example).
- [ ] C3 Indirect competitors: meet the same customer need in a different way (the juice bar against the coffee shop).
- [ ] C4 Potential competitors: firms that could enter the market matter as much as current ones.
- [ ] C5 Why identify competitors: set realistic prices, spot gaps in the market, anticipate price cuts or new products; it shapes pricing, marketing and strategy.

Competitive advantage [2.6.1, 2.6.2]
- [ ] D1 Competitive advantage: a feature that lets a business outperform its rivals; the basis of a sustainable strategy.
- [ ] D2 Cost leadership: producing at lower cost than rivals, so the business can price lower or earn a higher margin.
- [ ] D3 Differentiation: offering something customers value that rivals do not match (brand, quality, design, service).
- [ ] D4 Focus: concentrating on a narrow market segment, by cost or by differentiation, and serving it better than wider competitors.
- [ ] D5 How a business capitalises on its advantage: protect it, promote it to customers, reinvest the extra profit in further strengths.

Porter's five forces [2.6.2]
- [ ] P1 Definition of Porter's five forces model (quoted in section 2), the five forces named, and what the model is for: judging how competitive forces shape profit in an industry and where a firm's advantage should lie.
- [ ] P2 Threat of new entrants: high when barriers to entry are low. Low-threat factors: high capital requirements, economies of scale, brand loyalty and switching costs, licences, patents and regulation, expected retaliation by incumbents. High-threat factors: low start-up costs, easy access to customers such as online selling.
- [ ] P3 Bargaining power of suppliers: high with few suppliers or a unique input, high switching costs, a supplier able to sell direct to the business's customers (forward integration). Low with many suppliers of standard inputs and volume buying.
- [ ] P4 Bargaining power of customers: high with a few large buyers buying in volume (a major supermarket group), standard products with many substitutes, low switching costs, easy online price comparison. Low with many fragmented buyers and a differentiated, hard-to-replace product.
- [ ] P5 Threat of substitutes: a substitute meets the same need in a different way (bottled water replacing a soft drink; online services replacing high-street services). High when it offers similar value at a lower price or an improving price-performance ratio; low when no close alternative exists or switching is costly. Industries with many close substitutes must keep prices low and keep innovating.
- [ ] P6 Competitive rivalry: intensity of competition among existing firms. Intense with many similar-sized competitors, slow market growth, high fixed costs (price cuts to fill capacity), low differentiation (price becomes the basis of competition), high exit barriers (specialised assets that cannot be sold).
- [ ] P7 Using the model: score each force high, medium or low; attractiveness is greatest where entry barriers are high, supplier and customer power are low, substitutes are few and rivalry is moderate.
- [ ] P8 Using the findings to identify the firm's competitive advantage: choose the advantage that best fits the forces faced (cost leadership where buyers are price-sensitive; differentiation or focus where rivalry is high and products are alike).
- [ ] P9 Steps of the applied tool, numbered, as a Skills block: (1) define the industry and the firm; (2) rate each of the five forces and give the reasons; (3) judge industry attractiveness; (4) identify the firm's competitive advantage; (5) conclude and recommend a strategy.

Worked analysis: the Irish grocery market (general, qualitative judgements, no named firms)
- [ ] W1 New entrants low to medium (costly sites, buying scale, brand loyalty; online grocers face lower site costs).
- [ ] W2 Suppliers medium (large food brands bargain strongly; the main chains buy in volume and can switch for standard goods).
- [ ] W3 Customers high (easy price comparison, low switching costs, chains compete on price).
- [ ] W4 Substitutes medium (restaurants, convenience stores, markets, online delivery meet food needs in part).
- [ ] W5 Rivalry high (several large chains and discount operators in a mature market where growth comes from taking share).
- [ ] W6 Conclusion: only moderately attractive, margins squeezed; a firm needs a clear advantage such as the discounters' low-cost model or a focused niche (premium fresh food, convenience formats).

Cross-cutting themes
- [ ] X1 Ethical angle: competing fairly. Irish competition law bans cartels and abuse of a dominant position, and the CCPC enforces it; rivals fixing prices harms consumers. Fair marketing claims (do not mislead about the advantage).
- [ ] X2 Sustainability angle: ethical sourcing, low-waste or low-carbon products can be a differentiation advantage; growing customer demand for sustainable goods lowers the power of price-only comparison; supplier power rises when a firm depends on a scarce certified input.
- [ ] X3 Digital angle: online price comparison and marketplaces raise customer power; online selling lowers entry barriers (more new entrants); data and personalisation can support differentiation.

## 3. Traps

- Do not use: any named Irish grocery chain, market share, price-comparison finding or sales figure, because none is checked and market shares change.
- Do not use: "government" or "complementors" as a sixth force, because the specification's definition names exactly five forces.
- Confusable: an indirect competitor offers a different product for the same need; a substitute in the five-forces model is the same idea seen as a force on the whole industry. Teach both, with the link: indirect competitors are the firms that supply substitutes.
- Confusable: "threat of new entrants" is about firms not yet in the market; "competitive rivalry" is about firms already in it. Barriers to entry are not exit barriers.
- Confusable: buyer power is about customers negotiating price and quality; it is not demand or customer loyalty alone. Customer is the specification's word; "buyers" is Porter's.
- Confusable: the five forces analyse the external industry; SWOT (strengths, weaknesses, opportunities, threats) and STEEPLE are separate tools. STEEPLE is taught in the notes on adapting to external drivers.
- Belongs to a neighbouring subsection: the list of expansion strategies (organic and inorganic growth), the technology that supports growth, the cost-benefit analysis tool, and adapting the marketing mix to external drivers. Mention the marketing mix here only as the way advantage is delivered.
- Marketing-mix reminder: the 7 Ps are product/service, pricing, promotion, people, packaging, process and place. Never "physical evidence".
- Do not present the grocery example as a verified industry study; call it a worked example using general judgements.

## 4. What exams ask

- Applied analysis (2025 Higher Level Sample 1, Question 1(e)(i)): "analyse" the competitive forces in a given market using Porter's five forces. Appendix 1: analyse means study in detail, break down, identify parts and relationships, reach conclusions. A full answer takes each force in turn, gives a high/medium/low rating, gives a reason tied to the stimulus (who the suppliers, buyers, substitutes and rivals are), and ends with a conclusion on how attractive the market is. Common mistakes: listing the five names without a rating or reason; describing the firm instead of the industry; skipping the conclusion; muddling new entrants with rivalry.
- Same question, part (ii): "identify" a competitive advantage and recommend a strategy. Identify means recognise and state, so name cost leadership, differentiation or focus, then link it to the findings and give a recommendation (what to do and why). Common mistake: naming an advantage with no link to the forces, or a recommendation that ignores the stimulus.
- Direct versus indirect competitors and why identify competition: short "identify"/"outline" items; give the type with a one-line example and the benefit to pricing or strategy.
- Command-word depth: for discuss or evaluate, give three or four developed points, each with an effect on the business.
- Never invent mark allocations; use the stimulus the paper provides.
