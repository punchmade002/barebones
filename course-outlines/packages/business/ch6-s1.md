# Package: ch6-s1 — Enterprise, innovation, intrapreneurship, entrepreneurship and the competencies of innovators

- **loId:** `ch6-s1` · **chapterId:** `ch6`
- **Syllabus:** NCCA Leaving Certificate Business specification (first examined June 2027), Strand 2, sections 2.1 and 2.8, learning outcomes [2.1.1], [2.1.2] and [2.8.1]. Students learn about the range of reasons for starting an enterprise; how enterprise and innovation are present in, and important for, government, society, business, the community and their own lives; and the competencies that support innovation and enterprise development. They should be able to identify examples of innovation, intrapreneurship and entrepreneurship locally, nationally and internationally; explain the importance of innovation for business, the economy and society; identify the competencies of innovators and outline why they matter when starting a business; and analyse news about enterprise and innovation. Glossary term: competencies (of entrepreneurs). No Higher Level only content.
- **Prepared by:** a higher model, 2026-11-23. The writer works only from this package and `course-outlines/NOTES_STANDARD.md`.

## 1. Must cover (one bullet or block for every item; tick each off)

Definitions
- [ ] D1 Enterprise in its broad sense: spotting an opportunity and organising people, money and ideas to pursue it. It is not only starting a company: it applies to charities, community groups and public bodies too (also the keyTerm).
- [ ] D2 Innovation: introducing something new or significantly improved (also the keyTerm). Three types by what changes: product (what is sold), process (how it is made or delivered), business model (how the firm earns money), each with an everyday example.
- [ ] D3 Entrepreneur: starts and runs a new venture and carries the financial risk personally (keyTerm).
- [ ] D4 Intrapreneur: an employee who develops new products or processes inside an existing organisation, using its resources, so the firm carries most of the financial risk (keyTerm). Intrapreneurship lets an established firm innovate while keeping the stability of its existing business.
- [ ] D5 Entrepreneurship and innovation are linked but not identical: an entrepreneur may run a business with nothing new in it, and an established firm may produce an innovation.
- [ ] D6 Entrepreneur compared with intrapreneur: setting, who carries the risk, who owns the idea (factCard).
- [ ] D7 (assumed) A business model describes how a business operates and earns money (needed to explain business-model innovation). Appendix 2 wording is in section 2.

Reasons for starting an enterprise [2.1.1]
- [ ] R1 Financial reasons: earning more than paid employment offers, building personal wealth, a business that could later be sold (factCard).
- [ ] R2 Independence: being your own boss, controlling how, when and where you work.
- [ ] R3 A gap in the market: an unmet customer need that existing firms are not serving (keyTerm).
- [ ] R4 A social goal: solving a community problem, the business run to achieve that aim.
- [ ] R5 Redundancy or no suitable job: push factor.
- [ ] R6 Passion for a hobby or skill turned into a business.
- [ ] R7 Recognition, or the wish to prove an idea works.
- [ ] R8 Most founders have more than one reason, financial and non-financial. Push factors (redundancy) against pull factors (gap in the market).

Enterprise in society and personal life [2.1.2]
- [ ] E1 Government: state agencies give advice and funding to business (detail belongs elsewhere; one line only).
- [ ] E2 Community enterprise: run by local people for the benefit of their area, often using surplus income to fund local services.
- [ ] E3 Social enterprise: trades to achieve a social or environmental purpose and reinvests most of its surplus in that aim (keyTerm).
- [ ] E4 Business: enterprise inside firms (intrapreneurship) and in new ventures.
- [ ] E5 Students' own lives: planning a school fundraiser, managing the budget for a part-time job.
- [ ] E6 Enterprise education in schools builds planning, teamwork and decision-making.
- [ ] E7 Local, national and international examples of innovation, intrapreneurship and entrepreneurship, from the checked rows only (section 2).

Why innovation matters
- [ ] I1 For the business: competitive advantage (hard for rivals to copy quickly) and growth through new markets and more sales to existing customers (keyTerm Competitive advantage).
- [ ] I2 For the economy: jobs in new sectors, export sales, tax revenue on profits and wages from growing firms (factCard).
- [ ] I3 For society: solutions to problems in health, energy use and waste; more sustainable production; new services for people who lacked them (factCard).
- [ ] I4 Ethical, sustainability and digital angle: innovation can cost jobs through automation, raise privacy risks through digital services, and have an environmental impact; firms weigh these up ethically. Innovation can also serve sustainability (circular, low-waste production) and digital innovation (online payments, online ordering) opens new markets.

Competencies of innovators [2.1.2; 2.8.1]
- [ ] C1 Definition of competencies (of entrepreneurs), word for word from Appendix 2 (section 2), with the notes' plain gloss: knowledge, skills, values and dispositions.
- [ ] C2 Creativity: generate many ideas, look at problems from new angles.
- [ ] C3 Risk-taking: accept uncertain outcomes for possible rewards; needed to launch a product with no proven market.
- [ ] C4 Resilience: recover from setbacks; many start-ups stumble early.
- [ ] C5 Self-confidence and decisiveness: make choices quickly with incomplete information.
- [ ] C6 Vision: a clear picture of where the business is going, which helps attract staff, investors and customers.
- [ ] C7 Initiative: acting without waiting to be told, with the second-supplier example.
- [ ] C8 Networking: contacts with mentors, suppliers and customers for advice and early sales.
- [ ] C9 Adaptability: changing a plan when customer feedback or market conditions change; essential at start-up.
- [ ] C10 For each competency, why it matters at start-up (the "outline why they matter" part of [2.1.2]). Nine competencies in all (factCard).

Applying my learning [2.8.1]
- [ ] N1 How to analyse enterprise news: what problem the firm solves, who benefits, what risks it faces. Add: is the innovation product, process or business model; which competencies does the founder show; ethical, sustainability and digital effects.
- [ ] N2 Give an informed opinion, supported by a reason (spec wording: "informed opinion").

## 3. Traps

- Do not use: "Kerry Group ... competes internationally by developing food ingredients that it adapts for its customers", because the checked source says only "taste and nutrition"; use the checked row.
- Do not use: "Stripe was founded by two Irish brothers" or "Irish-founded digital innovation" as a stated fact, because no source in this check gave their nationality; the checked wording is co-founded by the brothers Patrick and John Collison, with a Dublin headquarters. Do not give a founding year, valuation or number of countries.
- Do not use: "Supermac's ... has grown ... by building a brand around its own menu and outlet network", because it is unsupported; use the checked row (first restaurant 1978, Ballinasloe, founder Pat McDonagh).
- Do not use: any employee, outlet, revenue or valuation figure for these firms, because they change.
- Do not use: "an entrepreneur ... carries the financial risk" as the only difference; an intrapreneur can still lose their job or standing. The point is who carries the financial risk, not that an intrapreneur carries none.
- Confusable: enterprise (the process or attitude) against an enterprise (a business); entrepreneurship (starting) against intrapreneurship (inside an existing firm); invention (a new idea) against innovation (the new idea put into use).
- Confusable: social enterprise (trades for a social or environmental aim, reinvests most of its surplus) against a charity (relies on donations) and a private firm (aims at profit for owners).
- Confusable: competencies (the glossary term) against "competition" or "competitive advantage". They are separate.
- Belongs to a neighbouring subsection: the state agencies, grants, tax reliefs and loans that support enterprise (the notes on the role of government in supporting enterprise). Give only a one-line pointer here.
- Belongs elsewhere in the course: the idea-development tools (design thinking, the business model canvas, the generation of ideas), sources of finance and the 7 Ps. Do not teach them here.
- Old syllabus only: do not teach the "categories of industry" or "community development" units, or the old Unit 1 enterprise topics, as topics (Appendix A4).

## 4. What exams ask

- The 2025 Higher Level Sample 1 paper asks students to identify four key competencies of innovators. Verb: identify, so a short list of four named competencies earns the marks; a one-phrase gloss is enough. A common mistake is to give two near-identical competencies (for example confidence and self-belief) or to name a business skill such as bookkeeping instead of a personal competency.
- Likely question shapes, from the specification's command verbs (no frequencies are known): identify competencies; outline reasons for starting an enterprise; explain the importance of innovation for a business, the economy and society; distinguish an entrepreneur from an intrapreneur; analyse a short news item about an enterprise.
- Outline or explain: give the main points, then for explain add the reason or cause. For a "why it matters at start-up" part, pair each competency with its effect on a new business (resilience: the founder keeps going after early setbacks).
- Importance of innovation: three levels (business, economy, society); give a developed point for each, with its effect (jobs, exports, tax revenue; competitive advantage). A common mistake is to treat "the economy" as only "more profit".
- Reasons for starting: split financial from non-financial and show you know a founder usually has several.
- Analyse enterprise news: break the story into what the firm does, what is new (product, process or business model), who gains, what the risk is. Close with a one-sentence justified opinion. The exam uses real, current Irish stories as stimulus, so use only what the stimulus says.
- Ethical, sustainability and digital angle: the written exam assesses applying these themes, so add one sentence on the ethical cost or the digital or sustainability gain wherever innovation is discussed.
- Common mistake: naming a firm without saying what the firm did that was entrepreneurial or innovative. Always link the example to the point.
