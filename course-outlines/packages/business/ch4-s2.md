# Package: ch4-s2 — EU decision-makers and types of EU law

- **loId:** `ch4-s2` · **chapterId:** `ch4`
- **Syllabus:** NCCA Leaving Certificate Business specification (first examined June 2027), Strand 1, section 1.4, learning outcomes [1.4.4] and [1.4.5]. Students learn about the key actors in EU policy development (the Council of the European Union, the European Commission, the European Parliament and the European Central Bank), and the difference between EU regulations, directives and opinions based on how the Irish government must respond. They should be able to identify the decision-makers and distinguish the three types of law.
- **Prepared by:** a higher model, 2026-11-23. The writer works only from this package and `course-outlines/NOTES_STANDARD.md`.

## 1. Must cover (one bullet or block for every item; tick each off)

Assumed knowledge
- [ ] A1 (assumed) The EU is a union of 27 member states, Ireland among them, with a single market in which goods, services, people and capital move freely. (Ireland's membership is evaluated in the international business chapter; give one line only.)
- [ ] A2 (assumed) Member states pass national law through their own parliament; EU law sits above and works alongside it.
- [ ] A3 (assumed) The Irish government and the Oireachtas decide how EU law applies in Ireland (links back to the notes on Irish policy and legislation).
- [ ] A4 (assumed) A statutory instrument is secondary legislation made by a minister; it is the common route for transposing a directive (keyTerm in the previous subsection, repeat in one line).
- [ ] A5 (assumed) Recommendations and decisions exist as EU legal acts alongside the three the syllabus names; they are assumed knowledge (the outline says so).

The four decision-makers [1.4.4]
- [ ] D1 Council of the European Union: ministers from each member state meet by policy area; adopts laws (with Parliament); the ministers depend on the topic (agriculture ministers on farm policy, finance ministers on budgets and tax).
- [ ] D2 Voting in the Council: mostly by qualified majority; some areas, such as taxation, need unanimity.
- [ ] D3 European Council (not the same body): heads of state or government set political direction and pass no laws. Flag as the commonest confusion.
- [ ] D4 European Commission: one Commissioner per member state; proposes laws, enforces EU law, manages much of the EU budget; competition law (cartels, abuse of market power).
- [ ] D5 European Parliament: directly elected every five years; its members (MEPs) represent citizens; shares law-making power with the Council.
- [ ] D6 European Central Bank: based in Frankfurt; sets monetary policy for the euro area; price stability its primary aim; its rate decisions change the cost of borrowing for Irish firms.
- [ ] D7 What the ECB does not do: it does not propose or adopt the EU laws regulating business. Make each institution's role distinct in one sentence a student can recall.
- [ ] D8 Ireland's part: Irish ministers sit in the Council; Irish MEPs sit in Parliament; Ireland has a Commissioner.
- [ ] D9 Definitions as keyTerms: Council of the European Union, European Commission, European Parliament, European Central Bank, ordinary legislative procedure.

The ordinary legislative procedure
- [ ] O1 Commission proposes (after consulting stakeholders and member states).
- [ ] O2 Parliament votes and may amend.
- [ ] O3 Council adopts; both must agree, so it is called co-decision.
- [ ] O4 The procedure used for most EU laws; show it as numbered steps with the institution named at each.
- [ ] O5 Business angle: firms and representative bodies can influence the draft at the consultation and amendment stages (link to the notes on how business influences policy), and the Commission's proposal is where the policy aim is set.

Types of EU law [1.4.5]
- [ ] R1 Regulation (keyTerm EU regulation): binding in its entirety and directly applicable in all member states; no Irish law is needed.
- [ ] R2 A regulation still needs a national authority to supervise and enforce it in Ireland.
- [ ] R3 Why regulations matter to business: uniform rules across the single market.
- [ ] R4 Directive (keyTerm): binding as to the result; each member state chooses the method; transposed by a deadline through an Act or statutory instrument (keyTerm Transposition).
- [ ] R5 If Ireland does not transpose on time, the Commission can begin infringement proceedings.
- [ ] R6 After the deadline, an individual may in limited circumstances rely on the directive against the State.
- [ ] R7 Why directives matter to business: the detailed rules can differ between member states.
- [ ] R8 Opinion (keyTerm): not binding; a view from an EU institution; creates no legal obligation but can shape national policy.
- [ ] R9 Recommendation: not binding (assumed).
- [ ] R10 Decision: binding only on its addressee, which may be a member state, a company or an individual (assumed).
- [ ] R11 The comparison table: instrument | binding? | how it reaches Ireland | Irish government must... (the syllabus's "based on how the Irish government must respond").
- [ ] R12 Ethical, sustainability and digital angle: EU law is how common standards on environment, data and digital services reach Ireland. Give one example of each of the three types of instrument from section 2 where there is one (the Digital Services Act and AI Act are regulations; the Corporate Sustainability Reporting Directive and the Single-Use Plastics Directive are directives); the full evaluation belongs to the next subsection.
- [ ] R13 Answering the exam question: state the instrument, say whether it binds, say how it reaches Irish law, name the institution at each step, apply to a business.

## 3. Traps

- Do not use: "ECB interest rate decisions affect borrowing costs" together with the claim that "the ECB does not make EU laws on business regulation" as a flat statement, because the ECB does adopt some legal acts in its own field. Say it sets monetary policy and does not propose or adopt the general laws that regulate business.
- Do not use: the number of euro area countries or the number of members of the ECB Governing Council, because the count changes and sources differ.
- Do not use: Council votes described as "qualified majority" without the term being explained, or any figure other than 55% of countries and 65% of the population.
- Do not use: "co-decision" as the official name; it is the older name for the ordinary legislative procedure. Use ordinary legislative procedure and mention co-decision as the informal term.
- Do not use: a simplified "Parliament votes first, Council second" as if the order is fixed in every case, because the procedure can include several readings. Present the three steps as the outline of the procedure only.
- Confusable: Council of the European Union vs European Council vs Council of Europe. The Council of Europe is not an EU body; do not use it.
- Confusable: European Commission (proposes and enforces) vs European Parliament (elected, co-legislates) vs Council (ministers, adopts).
- Confusable: regulation vs directive. A regulation applies directly; a directive must be transposed. "Opinion" has no force.
- Confusable: the Oireachtas vs the European Parliament; MEPs are not TDs.
- Confusable: "regulation" (EU law) vs "regulations" in an Irish statutory instrument's title (the S.I. that transposes a directive is called "Regulations" but is Irish secondary legislation).
- Belongs to a neighbouring subsection: Irish policy, Acts of the Oireachtas and lobbying (the notes on Irish government policy and legislation); the evaluation of one named regulation and one directive with stakeholder impacts (the notes on evaluating one regulation and one directive). Give examples here in one line only.
- Belongs to a neighbouring chapter: Ireland's EU membership and trading blocs (international business chapter). The single market only as a one-line assumed point.
- GDPR and the employment directives are used in the consumer and employee chapters; do not use them as the examples here.

## 4. What exams ask

- The 2025 sample papers do not set a stand-alone question on the decision-makers; the topic is tested through the next subsection's regulation and directive questions, which ask students to name an instrument and explain its Irish implementation. So the notes' tips should show how this subsection's facts support those answers.
- "Identify" (Appendix 1: recognise and state briefly a distinguishing fact): name the institution that proposes EU laws (Commission), that adopts them with Parliament (Council), that is directly elected (Parliament), that sets monetary policy (ECB). A full answer is a name plus its one distinguishing role.
- "Distinguish" (make the differences clear) regulation, directive and opinion: a full answer states binding or not, how each reaches Ireland (direct application; transposition by Act or statutory instrument; no legal effect), and what the Irish government must do. Common mistakes: saying a directive applies automatically, calling an opinion binding, and mixing the Council with the European Council.
- "Explain" how a directive becomes Irish law: instrument, deadline, an Act or statutory instrument, and what happens if the State is late. Add a business example.
- "Outline" the steps of the ordinary legislative procedure: three steps, each with the institution named. Common mistake: leaving out the Commission's proposal or reversing the roles.
- "Explain" how an ECB decision affects Irish business: monetary policy, interest rates, borrowing cost; avoid saying the ECB makes business law.
- Applying to unseen stimulus: a headline about a new EU rule; state whether it is a regulation or directive, which institution proposed it, and how Ireland must respond.
- Never invent marks, frequencies or examiner quotes in the tips.
