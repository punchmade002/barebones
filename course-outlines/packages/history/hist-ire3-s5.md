## 1. Must cover (one bullet or block for every item; tick each off)

Background (assumed)
- [ ] A1 (assumed) The Treaty (signed 6 December 1921) gave Ireland dominion status within the Commonwealth, an oath to the Crown, British retention of three ports, and partition (Northern Ireland could opt out; Boundary Commission). The Dáil approved it 64-57 on 7 January 1922. Detail belongs to the Treaty notes; one or two lines of recall only.
- [ ] A2 (assumed) The War of Independence (1919–21) had ended in a truce in July 1921; the IRA had fought as the army of the Dáil.
- [ ] A3 (assumed) Pro-Treaty vs anti-Treaty: the argument was over the oath and the Crown (a republic or dominion status), not mainly over partition.

Key concepts (each defined as used in this topic; also in keyTerms)
- [ ] K1 Republic: the form of state the anti-Treaty side demanded; it rejected the Crown and the oath.
- [ ] K2 Physical force: the tradition of armed struggle; the anti-Treaty IRA used it against the Free State; the state met it with its own force and emergency law.
- [ ] K3 Dominion status: the Free State as a self-governing member of the Commonwealth, as Canada was; "freedom to achieve freedom" was the pro-Treaty argument.
- [ ] K4 Essential terms (also keyTerms): Provisional Government, Free State, Civil War, Cumann na nGaedheal, Garda Síochána, Public Safety Acts, Electoral Amendment Act 1927, Fianna Fáil, Shannon Scheme.

The split and the road to war, January–June 1922
- [ ] S1 The two authorities: Provisional Government (Collins) and the Dáil government (Griffith); National Army recruited.
- [ ] S2 The IRA split: the March 1922 convention; O'Connor and Lynch; barracks taken as British troops left.
- [ ] S3 Four Courts occupied, 14 April 1922.
- [ ] S4 Collins-de Valera pact (20 May) and its repudiation.
- [ ] S5 The June 1922 election and its result; the draft Constitution published on polling day.
- [ ] S6 The Wilson assassination (22 June) and British pressure.
- [ ] S7 The O'Connell kidnapping (26 June).

The course of the Civil War, 1922–1923
- [ ] W1 Shelling of the Four Courts (28 June), surrender (30 June), the Public Record Office explosion.
- [ ] W2 Dublin fighting and Brugha's death (July).
- [ ] W3 National Army advantages; capture of Limerick and Waterford; the August landings at Fenit and Cork; loss of the towns.
- [ ] W4 Shift to guerrilla war: ambushes, destruction of roads, bridges and railways.
- [ ] W5 Deaths of Griffith (12 August) and Collins (22 August, Béal na Bláth); Mulcahy and Cosgrave in charge.
- [ ] W6 Emergency powers (September 1922) and the executions: the first four (17 November), Childers (24 November), the four on 8 December and why (reprisal for Hales).
- [ ] W7 Total executed: about 80, number disputed.
- [ ] W8 Atrocities and reprisals: Ballyseedy and the Kerry killings (March 1923).
- [ ] W9 Lynch killed (10 April 1923); Aiken ceasefire and the dump-arms order (24 May 1923); de Valera's message.
- [ ] W10 Why the anti-Treaty side lost: lack of public support, the Church's condemnation, weaker arms, the National Army's British guns and supplies, loss of the towns.
- [ ] W11 Costs: dead (range disputed), damage, bitterness, prisoners.

State building under Cosgrave
- [ ] B1 Cumann na nGaedheal founded 1923; the government's aim of continuity, law and order.
- [ ] B2 The 1922 Constitution: date, Oireachtas, PR, Executive Council, Governor-General, the oath; later removal of the Referendum and Initiative (1928).
- [ ] B3 The Governor-Generals: Healy, then McNeill.
- [ ] B4 The Garda Síochána: origins, unarmed, O'Duffy, absorbed the DMP (1925).
- [ ] B5 The courts: Courts of Justice Act 1924.
- [ ] B6 Civil service and local government continuity; Ministers and Secretaries Act 1924.
- [ ] B7 International standing: League of Nations (1923), legation in Washington (1924).
- [ ] B8 Land Act 1923.
- [ ] B9 The Shannon Scheme and the ESB.

Threats to the state, 1924–1927
- [ ] T1 The Army Mutiny (March 1924): causes, leaders, Mulcahy's resignation, result (civil control of the army).
- [ ] T2 The Boundary Commission crisis (Morning Post leak, MacNeill's resignation, the Tripartite Agreement of 3 December 1925).
- [ ] T3 O'Higgins and the Public Safety Acts; the 1931 constitutional amendment (Article 2A) as the last of the emergency measures of this period.
- [ ] T4 O'Higgins's assassination (10 July 1927).
- [ ] T5 The government's response: Public Safety Act 1927 and the Electoral Amendment Act.

Consolidating democracy
- [ ] D1 Fianna Fáil: the split from Sinn Féin and founding (1926); the oath "empty formality".
- [ ] D2 The June and September 1927 elections.
- [ ] D3 Fianna Fáil entering the Dáil (12 August 1927).
- [ ] D4 Why Fianna Fáil rose by 1932 (Depression, spending cuts, protection, annuities, oath).
- [ ] D5 The February 1932 election and the transfer of power on 9 March 1932; the army and police accepted it.

Legacy of the Civil War
- [ ] L1 The party system (Fine Gael and Fianna Fáil from opposite sides); the August 1923 election result.
- [ ] L2 De Valera's arrest and release; the 1923 prisoners and hunger strike.
- [ ] L3 Divided families and communities; silence in public life.

Key personality: W.T. Cosgrave (one block, every point)
- [ ] P1 Who he was: Dublin publican's son, Sinn Féin councillor (1909), 1916 (South Dublin Union, sentence commuted).
- [ ] P2 What he did, with dates: Kilkenny 1917; Minister for Local Government from 1919; head of government from August 1922; President of the Executive Council December 1922 to March 1932.
- [ ] P3 As participant in events: Civil War executions, the Mutiny, the Boundary Commission crisis, O'Higgins's assassination, the 1932 handover.
- [ ] P4 Shaped by events: pushed to harsher measures by the war and the murder of Hales and O'Higgins; his cautious style suited a state needing stability, but not the Depression.
- [ ] P5 Contemporaries: supporters (stability, honesty, law) vs critics (conservative, authoritarian, "the Treaty government", the executions).
- [ ] P6 Historians: Garvin vs Regan; Laffan's balanced view.
- [ ] P7 Later life: Fine Gael leader 1935–44; death 1965 (not first leader of Fine Gael).

Assessment (paired judgement points, final block)
- [ ] J1 Achievements: survival, working institutions, civil control of the army, the Shannon Scheme, peaceful handover in 1932.
- [ ] J2 Limits: repression and executions; economic conservatism; the Boundary outcome; the Treaty symbols left in place.
- [ ] J3 Contemporary vs later views; success vs failure; "counter to militarism" vs "conservative counter-revolution".

## 3. Traps

Do not use (wrong or doubtful facts in the current notes):
- Do not use: Cosgrave as "first leader of Fine Gael" (after 1933), because Eoin O'Duffy was the first leader (1933–34); Cosgrave led it from 1935 to 1944.
- Do not use: "Cathal Brugha was killed in action on 7 July", because he was wounded on 5 July and died on 7 July.
- Do not use: "the Governor-General's role was left unchanged" in 1928, as a point about the removal of the Referendum and Initiative; it is vague and unsupported.
- Do not use: "allowed the government to be seen as pro-British" and "relied on a narrow group of ministers" as contemporary views of Cosgrave; they are not specific or well attested. Use instead the specific criticisms: the executions, the emergency laws and economic conservatism.
- Do not use: a total of Civil War dead; give the range only. Do not use a single figure for the war's cost.
- Do not use: the Civil War "began" on 14 April (the Four Courts occupation); the fighting began on 28 June 1922 when the National Army attacked the Four Courts.

- Do not use: any account of who fired the shot that killed Collins; state only that he was killed in an ambush.
- Do not use: an exact date for the Electoral Amendment Act; say Fianna Fáil entered the Dáil on 12 August 1927 under the threat of it.
- Do not use: any quotation attributed to Collins or Cosgrave.

Confusable people, terms and dates
- Provisional Government (Collins, January 1922) vs the Dáil government (Griffith, as President of the Dáil) vs the Executive Council of the Free State (from December 1922; Cosgrave).
- Free State (the state, from 6 December 1922) vs Irish Free State Constitution vs Constitution of 1937 (later).
- Cumann na nGaedheal (pro-Treaty party, 1923) vs Fine Gael (1933) vs Sinn Féin (the republican rump after 1926).
- Army Mutiny (1924, ex-IRA/IRB officers) vs the Curragh incident (1914, British officers): never mix them.
- Civic Guard (1922) vs Garda Síochána (name from 1923) vs DMP (absorbed 1925) vs RIC (disbanded 1922).
- The Boundary Commission (1924–25, set up under the Treaty) vs the Tripartite Agreement (3 December 1925, which ended it).
- Kevin O'Higgins (Justice, killed 1927) vs Joe McKelvey, Seán Hales, and other people killed in 1922: do not confuse the killings.
- The anti-Treaty IRA in 1922–23 vs the IRA of 1927 (killers of O'Higgins acted without sanction).
- Liam Lynch (chief of staff, killed April 1923) vs Frank Aiken (successor) vs Liam Mellows (executed December 1922).
- Cosgrave (head of government 1922–32) vs de Valera (head of Fianna Fáil, in power from 1932) vs Cosgrave the Fine Gael leader (1935–44).

## 4. What exams ask

- Higher 100-mark essay on the Cosgrave governments' foundation of the Free State, consolidation of democracy, or threats to the state: marks go to a clear thesis at the start (the state survived and democracy took root, with limits) and a sustained line. Cover institutions (Constitution, Garda, courts), threats (Civil War, Mutiny, Boundary crisis, O'Higgins) and the 1932 handover. A narrative of the war with no link to state building scores poorly.
- Strong answers organise by theme: security (executions, Public Safety Acts), institutions, control of the army, acceptance by opponents (Fianna Fáil in the Dáil, 1932), and give dated evidence for each. The peaceful handover of power in 1932 is the usual climax and should be explained as a test of democracy.
- "Threats to the state" questions need each threat explained as a threat (who, what, date), how the government responded, and the outcome; weak answers list events only.
- Ordinary Level 30-mark questions: the start of the Civil War (the Four Courts, the dates of occupation and shelling, Collins's role) and the death of Collins and Griffith; each dated, correct fact earns a mark.
- Ordinary Level 40-mark questions on Cosgrave: career dates (1909, 1916, 1917, 1922–32), what his government did (Garda, Mutiny, Shannon Scheme, O'Higgins), and a judgement. Use the four to five specific points; do not give an unfocused list.
- Short answers on a document: who Cosgrave was, the Garda Síochána (unarmed, 1923), the Shannon Scheme (Ardnacrusha, 1929), or the Mutiny (1924).
- Cosgrave or de Valera comparisons (Anglo-Irish relations): the answer needs Cosgrave's achievements within the Commonwealth and his limits; pointers here, detail in the de Valera notes.
- Examiners reward paired judgement: achievements vs limits, contemporary vs later views, with a named historian (Garvin or Regan) and the point he makes.
- Common errors to warn against: confusing the Mutiny with the Civil War; dating the war from the Four Courts occupation (April) instead of the attack (June); giving Cosgrave as leader of Fine Gael from its founding; giving a single death toll for the war.
