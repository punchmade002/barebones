## 1. Must cover (one bullet or block for every item; tick each off)

What history is, evidence and repositories
- [ ] H1 History: the past itself, the surviving evidence, and the written accounts historians build from it. The past cannot be examined directly.
- [ ] H2 Historical knowledge is provisional: accounts change with new evidence or new questions; two historians can write different histories of the same events.
- [ ] H3 Source vs evidence: a source is the item itself; it becomes evidence when used to answer a particular question. The same source can be evidence for different questions.
- [ ] H4 Varieties: public records (official policy, statistics); private institutions' records (interests of members); letters; memoirs; eyewitness accounts; newspapers, speeches, pamphlets; maps; photographs; political cartoons; oral testimony; radio and TV transcripts (usually recorded long after events).
- [ ] H5 Repositories: archives (state records, organisations' papers), libraries (books, newspapers, pamphlets), museums (objects, banners, photographs); which kinds of source each holds.

Interrogating evidence and research
- [ ] I1 The five questions: who made it, why, when, in what circumstances, for what audience; each with a brief example.
- [ ] I2 Stages of research: question, locate sources, evaluate, compare, interpret, test the interpretation.
- [ ] I3 Primary vs secondary; primary does not mean reliable; a source can be primary for one question and secondary for another (a historian's book is primary evidence of the thinking of its own time).
- [ ] I4 Corroboration: testing a source's claims against independent evidence (assumed knowledge behind reliability).

Fact, opinion, bias, propaganda, objectivity
- [ ] F1 Fact vs opinion, each with an example from the case studies.
- [ ] F2 Bias: a consistent tilt, shown by loaded words, selective detail, omission.
- [ ] F3 Propaganda: persuades a mass audience by emotion, slogans and selected facts; example.
- [ ] F4 A biased source is still useful evidence of what its author and audience believed.
- [ ] F5 Objectivity: the aim of fair, evidence-based judgement; no historian is free of assumptions.
- [ ] F6 Strengths and limitations of sources (closeness, first-hand knowledge, corroboration vs bias, narrow view, gaps, partial survival).
- [ ] F7 Revising views: new sources, harder testing of old ones, new questions; example the Pigott forgeries.

The DBQ and method for each part
- [ ] D1 Format: Question 1 is the documents-based question; documents (written or visual, including cartoons and photographs) come from one of the three case studies (the elections of 1885–86, Dublin 1913, the GAA to 1891); four parts: comprehension, comparison, criticism, contextualisation; part (d) is worth 40 marks.
- [ ] D2 Part (a) comprehension: answer from the named document only, in own words, with a detail for each point; no outside knowledge.
- [ ] D3 Part (b) comparison: name the point, give a specific similarity or difference with evidence from both documents; link words; compare like with like (private letter vs public speech).
- [ ] D4 Part (c) criticism: provenance, purpose, viewpoint, reliability, with a cited line for each; balanced verdict; usefulness measured against a stated enquiry.
- [ ] D5 Typical part (a)-(c) question types: usefulness, primary/secondary, fact/opinion, propaganda, bias, whether a cartoon is hostile to a group, which document is more favourable or more effective.
- [ ] D6 Reading cartoons: identify figures and symbols, read caption and labels, note exaggeration, message, audience; a cartoon is opinion, evidence of attitudes not of events.
- [ ] D7 Reading photographs: what it shows; framing, staging and caption can mislead; good evidence of conditions but not of blame.
- [ ] D8 Part (d) contextualisation: a short essay on the case study's subject using own knowledge; background, events in order with dates and names, outcomes, both sides, judgement; documents as support, not retold; draw on the rest of the chapter.
- [ ] D9 Time and effort: spend the largest share of the time on part (d) (no minutes or mark splits for (a)-(c) may be stated; see Traps).

- Do not use: "Question 1 is worth 100 marks of the 400" or "part (a) 20, part (b) 20, part (c) 20", because the outline confirms only that part (d) is 40 marks and that the chapter is examined only by Question 1; past papers show shorter marks for earlier parts (about 10 each) and the 2027 split is not confirmed. Say only that (d) carries the most marks.
- Do not use: "four equal questions in about 170 minutes, roughly 40 minutes for Question 1", because the paper's timing is unconfirmed. Advise giving part (d) the biggest share of time without minutes.
- Do not use: "return to work in January 1914" as a precise date; write "early 1914".
- Do not use: "Church Street ruins of September 1913" as an unqualified date; the collapse was 2 September 1913 (use only as in section 2).
- Do not use: "accounts by participants in Bloody Sunday" without a year; Bloody Sunday in 1913 means 31 August, and the name is also used for 1920 and 1972. Say "the baton charges of 31 August 1913".
- Do not use: Cusack's removal "1886–87" as a range; give 1886.
- Do not use: "the balance of power, the Hawarden Kite or Salisbury" as an unqualified cue for late 1885 only; Salisbury was prime minister in 1885–86 and again from July 1886. Use the cues as in section 2.

Confusable terms
- Source (the item) vs evidence (the item used to answer a question). Primary (from the time) vs reliable (trustworthy): they are not the same.
- Fact vs opinion: "the Act was passed in 1881" is fact; "it was a betrayal" is opinion. Bias (a tilt in the source) vs propaganda (deliberate mass persuasion).
- Provenance (origin) vs reliability (trustworthiness): provenance is one test of reliability.
- Comprehension (what it says) vs criticism (how far to trust it) vs contextualisation (own knowledge of the period).
- Hibernia/Erin (Ireland) vs John Bull/Britannia (Britain).
- Documents-based question (Part I, this chapter) vs the research study (Appendix B, not examined here).

## 4. What exams ask

- Part (a)-(c) questions in past DBQs ask: what a document says or shows on a stated point (answer from the document only); which of two documents is more favourable, more critical, more effective or more useful, with reasons and cited detail from both.
- Comparison parts reward a named point with a specific agreement or disagreement and evidence from each document; two separate summaries earn little.
- Criticism parts: strengths and weaknesses of a document as a source (author's position, purpose, audience, date, whether it is first-hand or an extract); whether a source is fully objective; whether a cartoon is anti-Irish as well as anti-Home Rule, with details of how figures are drawn.
- Classification parts: say whether a source is primary or secondary, with a reason from the source note; pick out an example of fact and of opinion; say how language or imagery is propaganda.
- Part (d) is on the case study's subject (a leader's strengths and weaknesses; how an organisation dealt with its difficulties); it rewards dated, named, relevant points from the whole chapter and a judgement, not a retelling of the documents.
- Concrete tips: quote or point to a line for every judgement; never say only "it is biased"; say what a biased source is still good evidence of; use the source note (author, date) before reading; do not bring in outside knowledge in part (a); in part (d), refer to documents by letter only as support.
