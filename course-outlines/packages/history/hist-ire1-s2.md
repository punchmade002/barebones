## 1. Must cover (one bullet or block for every item; tick each off)

Background (assumed)
- [ ] A1 (assumed) The Act of Union (1801): Irish MPs sat at Westminster; Ireland was governed through Dublin Castle.
- [ ] A2 (assumed) Catholic Emancipation, 1829: the Relief Act let Catholics sit in Parliament; the county franchise was raised to £10; O'Connell won it through the Catholic Association and Rent and the Clare election, and was called "the Liberator".
- [ ] A3 (assumed) The Church of Ireland was the established church, though most Irish people were Catholic.
- [ ] A4 (assumed) The parties: Whigs (Grey, Melbourne, Russell) and Tories/Conservatives (Peel).
- [ ] A5 (assumed) The Famine began in 1845 (detail belongs elsewhere; here it is only the event that ended the Repeal movement).

Key concepts (each defined as used in this topic; also in keyTerms)
- [ ] K1 Nation: a people bound by shared history, culture and language, not religion (Young Ireland's idea).
- [ ] K2 Physical force republicanism: belief that independence needs armed rebellion; Young Ireland's refusal to rule out force and 1848 passed it to the Fenians.
- [ ] K3 Repeal: ending the Union and restoring an Irish Parliament under the Crown (essential term).
- [ ] K4 Moral force: O'Connell's method of peaceful mass pressure within the law, against Young Ireland's readiness to use force (essential term).

O'Connell at Westminster and the Whig alliance, 1830–1841
- [ ] W1 Entry to the Commons (1830); he led the Irish Repeal MPs; supporters funded him by the O'Connell Tribute; he backed the Whigs over parliamentary reform.
- [ ] W2 The Irish Reform Act 1832: 105 Irish MPs, a small electorate.
- [ ] W3 The 1832 election returned about 40 Repealers; his Repeal motion of April 1834 was heavily defeated.
- [ ] W4 The Lichfield House Compact (1835): informal bargain; O'Connell's MPs kept Melbourne's Whigs in office for reforms and fair appointments.
- [ ] W5 Drummond's administration (Mulgrave, Morpeth, Drummond): Catholics appointed, stipendiary magistrates, the 1836 Irish Constabulary, Drummond's 1838 remark on property's duties.
- [ ] W6 The Municipal Corporations (Ireland) Act 1840: most closed corporations abolished.
- [ ] W7 O'Connell Lord Mayor of Dublin (1841), the first Catholic since the reign of James II.
- [ ] W8 Limits of the alliance (no Repeal, limited tithe and municipal reform); Peel's Conservatives in office in 1841; O'Connell returned to agitation.

The Tithe War, 1830–1838
- [ ] T1 Tithe as a grievance: a tax of about a tenth of produce for the Church of Ireland, paid by Catholic farmers.
- [ ] T2 The non-payment campaign from 1830–31 (Graiguenamanagh), organised by the Catholic clergy; seizures of cattle by proctors, police and soldiers.
- [ ] T3 Violent clashes: Newtownbarry (1831), Carrickshock (1831), Gortroe near Rathcormac (1834).
- [ ] T4 Effect: tithe uncollectable in much of the south; arrears; government advances to clergy.
- [ ] T5 The Whig split over appropriation of Church revenue delayed a settlement.
- [ ] T6 The Tithe Rentcharge Act (1838): cut by about a quarter, paid by landlords through rent; limits (the Church survived to 1869).

The Poor Law, 1838
- [ ] L1 Before 1838: no national system; charity, a few houses of industry and county infirmaries.
- [ ] L2 The Whately commission (1833–36) and its recommendations; rejected as too costly.
- [ ] L3 George Nicholls's 1836 tour and his recommendation of the English workhouse model.
- [ ] L4 The Act (July 1838): 130 unions, boards of guardians (elected, with magistrates ex officio), workhouses, poor rate.
- [ ] L5 The workhouse test: relief only inside, harsh conditions, families separated, stigma.
- [ ] L6 Limits: capacity about 100,000; built for normal destitution; 1843 change to rating of small holdings; not ready for the Famine.

The Repeal campaign, 1840–1847
- [ ] R1 The Repeal Association (April 1840) and the penny-a-month Repeal Rent.
- [ ] R2 What Repeal meant (an Irish Parliament under the Crown) and O'Connell's hints of a compromise.
- [ ] R3 Repeal Year 1843 declared after Dublin Corporation's debate.
- [ ] R4 The clergy as organisers; Father Mathew's temperance movement as a source of crowds and bands.
- [ ] R5 The monster meetings: about forty, at historic sites (Tara 15 August, Mullaghmast, Clontarf).
- [ ] R6 The Nation publicised the meetings.
- [ ] R7 The government's response: Peel's May 1843 statement, dismissal of Repeal magistrates, more troops.
- [ ] R8 The Clontarf ban (7–8 October 1843) and O'Connell's cancellation.
- [ ] R9 Arrest (October 1843), conspiracy trial (1844), sentence, Lords reversal (September 1844).
- [ ] R10 Peel's conciliation: Devon Commission, Charitable Bequests Act, Maynooth grant, Queen's Colleges.
- [ ] R11 Decline: his age and health, federalism (1844), the Famine, return to the Whigs under Russell (1846).
- [ ] R12 Why Repeal failed (paired causes).

Young Ireland, 1842–1848
- [ ] Y1 Who they were; the founders (Duffy, Davis, Dillon) and The Nation (15 October 1842).
- [ ] Y2 The Nation's reach and content (history, ballads, essays).
- [ ] Y3 Cultural, non-sectarian nationalism: nation not tied to religion; the Irish language.
- [ ] Y4 The Library of Ireland (Duffy).
- [ ] Y5 Disagreements with O'Connell: federalism (1844), the Whig alliance, the Queen's Colleges (1845).
- [ ] Y6 The peace resolutions (July 1846), Meagher's Sword Speech and the walk-out.
- [ ] Y7 The Irish Confederation (January 1847) under William Smith O'Brien.
- [ ] Y8 Lalor (land and nation) and Mitchel (open revolution; United Irishman, February 1848).
- [ ] Y9 Build-up to 1848: the Paris revolution, the Treason Felony Act and Mitchel's conviction, suspension of habeas corpus.

Key personality: Daniel O'Connell, part 2 (one block, every point)

## 3. Traps

Do not use (wrong or doubtful facts in the current notes):
- Do not use: John Blake Dillon as "from Mayo", because his birthplace is on the Roscommon-Mayo border and sources differ. Say only "a Catholic barrister".
- Do not use: "the Tithe Composition Act extended tithe to pasture", because this is doubtful; say only that it converted tithe into money payments.
- Do not use: "Ireland had no statutory system of poor relief before 1838", because houses of industry had a statutory basis; say "no national, rate-funded system".
- Do not use: the Repeal Association "first called the National Association for Full and Prompt Justice or Repeal", because it blurs two bodies; say only that the Repeal Association was founded in April 1840.
- Do not use: "39 MPs" for the 1832 Repealers, because counts vary; say "about 40".
- Do not use: "November 1841" for the Lord Mayor, because the exact date is uncertain; say "1841".
- Do not use: "the best-selling paper in Ireland" for The Nation; say "one of the most widely read papers".
- Do not use: Carrickshock "more than ten policemen" as an exact count; say about ten or eleven.
- Do not use: the Orange Order dissolving itself in 1836 as a main point; it belongs to the notes on sectarianism and is not on the outline.
- Do not use: any figure for the numbers at Tara other than "supporters claimed up to a million"; estimates vary widely.

## 4. What exams ask

- Higher 100-mark essay on O'Connell, the commonest form (strengths and weaknesses, why Repeal failed, how far his leadership explains success and failure, his achievements): marks go to a clear judgement stated at the start, with dated evidence such as Compact (1835), Repeal Association (1840), Repeal Year and Clontarf (1843), and a comparison with the Emancipation campaign. A list of events with no verdict scores poorly.
- Why Repeal failed: strong answers pair external causes (both British parties, Peel's firmness, Protestant Ulster) with internal ones (reliance on bluff, ill health, the Young Ireland split, the Famine); naming the cancellation of Clontarf as the turning point earns credit.
- Young Ireland essays (aims, achievements, contribution to nationalism, or compared with Fenianism): state the cultural, non-sectarian idea of the nation first, then The Nation, then the split and 1848; give a judgement of long-term influence and name the physical force link via Stephens and O'Mahony.
- Thomas Davis (Higher profile or Ordinary 30 marks): his background, joining the Repeal Association and founding The Nation, his ideas on nationality and language, his ballads, the Queen's Colleges clash, and his early death. Each correct dated point earns marks; contrast with Kickham or Stephens or Cullen needs the other man's contribution stated explicitly.
- Tithe War (Ordinary 30 marks; Higher paired with Liberal reforms): define tithe, where the campaign began, one or two clashes with dates, the 1838 Act and what it left unsolved. Say why Catholics resented it, not only what happened.
- Poor Law (Ordinary 30 marks; Higher "relief of distress" comparison): 1838 Act with the 130 unions, boards of guardians, the workhouse test, and why it was not equal to the Famine; naming Nicholls over Whately shows depth.
- Ordinary Level 40-mark paragraph on Repeal: founding in 1840, the Rent, the clergy, about forty monster meetings with Tara, Clontarf, arrest, and the outcome. Four or five dated, relevant statements are needed.
- Ordinary Level document questions on O'Connell's meetings: say who the speaker or crowd are, what the document shows about crowd discipline and enthusiasm, and link to the Repeal or Emancipation campaign; short answers copied from the document are enough for 6 marks.
- Comparisons of Young Ireland and O'Connell, or of Young Ireland and the Fenians: give the difference in methods and ideas and a clear point on what each contributed; weaker answers confuse 1848 with 1867.
