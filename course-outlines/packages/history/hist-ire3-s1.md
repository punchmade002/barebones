## 1. Must cover (one bullet or block for every item; tick each off)

Background (assumed)
- [ ] A1 (assumed) Home Rule meant a devolved parliament in Dublin within the United Kingdom; the First (1886) and Second (1893) Home Rule Bills failed; the Irish Parliamentary Party (IPP) under John Redmond was the main nationalist party.
- [ ] A2 (assumed) Unionists, strongest in Ulster, opposed Home Rule; Edward Carson and James Craig led them (detail in the notes on the movements for reform before 1914).
- [ ] A3 (assumed) The 1913 Dublin Lockout and the founding of the Irish Citizen Army; the Gaelic League (1893) and cultural nationalism.
- [ ] A4 (assumed) The Fenian tradition of physical force and the IRB, the secret oath-bound society founded in 1858.

Key concepts (each defined as used in this topic; also in keyTerms)
- [ ] K1 Physical force: armed violence to win political aims, the tradition the Rising revived against the constitutional methods of the IPP.
- [ ] K2 IRB/IRA: the IRB planned the Rising; the Volunteers were later called the IRA (from about 1919; later subsections).
- [ ] K3 "Blood sacrifice": the idea, linked with Pearse's 1915 writings, that a willing sacrifice of life in rebellion would renew the nation; the phrase is a later critics' label.

The Third Home Rule Bill, 1912–1914
- [ ] H1 Why Home Rule became possible: the 1910 elections left the Liberals dependent on the IPP; Redmond's price was Home Rule.
- [ ] H2 The Parliament Act 1911: the Lords' veto replaced by a two-year delay; three Commons passages.
- [ ] H3 The Bill introduced by Asquith, 11 April 1912.
- [ ] H4 Terms: Dublin parliament (Senate of 40, Commons of 164) with a responsible executive; what Westminster kept; 42 Irish MPs at Westminster.
- [ ] H5 Passage: Commons January and July 1913, Lords rejections, Commons third passage May 1914.

Unionist and nationalist mobilisation (brief)
- [ ] U1 Ulster Covenant, 28 September 1912, and the women's Declaration; Carson and Craig.
- [ ] U2 The UVF (January 1913); the Curragh incident (March 1914); Larne gun-running (April 1914).
- [ ] U3 The Irish Volunteers founded November 1913 (MacNeill, IRB involvement); Howth gun-running and Bachelor's Walk (July 1914).

The crisis and suspension, 1914
- [ ] S1 County option (March 1914); Redmond's nominees on the Volunteers' committee (June 1914).
- [ ] S2 The Amending Bill (June 1914) and the Buckingham Palace Conference (July 1914); the Tyrone and Fermanagh problem.
- [ ] S3 War, then the Home Rule Act's royal assent (18 September 1914) with the Suspensory Act.

World War I and nationalist politics, 1914–1916
- [ ] W1 Redmond's offer of the Volunteers (3 August 1914) and the Woodenbridge speech (20 September 1914); the split into National Volunteers and Irish Volunteers.
- [ ] W2 Recruitment: numbers who served and died (ranges, section 2).
- [ ] W3 The 10th (Irish) Division at Suvla Bay; the 16th (Irish) Division at Guillemont and Ginchy (Somme); the 36th (Ulster) Division at Thiepval, 1 July 1916; Tom Kettle.
- [ ] W4 Economic effects: farmers' gains, Belfast industry, rising prices against wages, urban poverty.
- [ ] W5 Redmond's loss of ground; separatists' anti-war feeling; the war as the IRB's opportunity.

Planning the Rising
- [ ] P1 The IRB Military Council (1915) and its seven members; Connolly brought in (January 1916).
- [ ] P2 The O'Donovan Rossa funeral (1 August 1915).
- [ ] P3 Casement and Germany: arms, the Irish Brigade (little success).
- [ ] P4 The Aud and Casement's landing at Banna Strand (Good Friday 1916); the Ballykissane drownings.
- [ ] P5 MacNeill's countermand (Easter Sunday) and the postponement to Easter Monday; the smaller turnout.
- [ ] P6 (assumed) The Castle Document: how MacNeill was brought to accept the plan, and why he then reversed.

Easter Week, 24–29 April 1916
- [ ] E1 24 April: the GPO seized as headquarters; the Proclamation of the Irish Republic read; its content and its seven signatories.
- [ ] E2 The garrisons and their commanders (GPO, Boland's Mill, South Dublin Union, Jacob's, St Stephen's Green and the College of Surgeons, Four Courts); what the rebels did not take.
- [ ] E3 The British response: martial law, reinforcements, Maxwell; Mount Street Bridge; shelling and fires.
- [ ] E4 Events outside Dublin (Ashbourne, Enniscorthy, Galway).
- [ ] E5 The GPO evacuated, Moore Street, the surrender (29–30 April).
- [ ] E6 Casualties and damage (estimates).
- [ ] E7 (assumed) Women's part: Cumann na mBan, the Citizen Army; Markievicz (see M-block).

Executions, martial law and the shift in opinion
- [ ] X1 Initial hostility in Dublin and from the IPP and most papers.
- [ ] X2 Arrests, internment, Frongoch.
- [ ] X3 Courts-martial; the fifteen executions, 3–12 May 1916 (who and when).
- [ ] X4 Maxwell's role, Bishop O'Dwyer's protest, Birrell's resignation, Asquith's visit, Lloyd George's failed settlement.
- [ ] X5 Casement's trial and execution (3 August 1916).
- [ ] X6 Release of prisoners (December 1916; amnesty June 1917) and their role in the new Sinn Féin (point to the notes on the second Sinn Féin).

Key personality: Patrick Pearse (one block, every point)
- [ ] R1 Who he was: born Dublin 1879, Gaelic League from 1896, barrister, editor of An Claidheamh Soluis, founder of St Enda's, The Murder Machine.
- [ ] R2 What he did with dates: IRB 1913, Director of Military Organisation, Military Council 1915, Rossa oration, 1916 President and Commandant-General, Proclamation, surrender, execution.
- [ ] R3 Ideas: Gaelic and separatist nationalism; the cleansing power of war and "blood sacrifice" (and the caution about that label).
- [ ] R4 Shaped events and was shaped by them: educator turned revolutionary; the war as opportunity.

- [ ] R6 Contemporaries' views (condemned in April, honoured as a martyr by autumn).

Key personality: Countess Markievicz, part 1 to 1916 (one block)
- [ ] M1 Who she was: Gore-Booth family, Lissadell, art training, marriage 1900.
- [ ] M2 Career to 1916: Inghinidhe na hÉireann and Sinn Féin 1908, Fianna Éireann 1909, Citizen Army, Lockout soup kitchen.
- [ ] M3 Participant: second-in-command at St Stephen's Green and the College of Surgeons.
- [ ] M4 Court-martial, death sentence commuted, imprisonment, release June 1917 (her later career belongs to the next subsection).
- [ ] M5 Contemporaries' and historians' views.

Assessment: the Rising's achievements and limits (final block, paired points)
- [ ] Y1 Military failure vs political success.
- [ ] Y2 Contemporary views (condemnation, then shift) vs later views (revisionist stress on small support and civilian deaths; others on the British reaction).
- [ ] Y3 Yeats's "changed utterly" / "a terrible beauty is born" and the question whether Ireland was changed.
- [ ] Y4 Home Rule overtaken: the Act suspended and never operated; link to partition (point to the notes on partition).
- [ ] Y5 Legitimacy and commemoration (1966, 2016).

## 3. Traps

Do not use (wrong or doubtful facts in the current notes):
- Do not use: the Parliament Act "removed the House of Lords' power to block bills for good", because it left a two-year delay (and the Lords had earlier rejected the Bill twice).
- Do not use: "Sean O'Casey was critical of her" as a view of Markievicz, because it is vague and not safe as a one-line claim; use only the admiration-and-ridicule point stated generally, or the 1916 court-martial facts.
- Do not use: "the police stayed under British control for six years" without saying the Bill reserved the police for six years; it never operated.
- Do not use: "Pearse ... blood sacrifice" as his own slogan (see R3).
- Do not use: "Easter Monday 1916 ... 1,200" as the total number who fought; it is the first-day figure. Say "about 1,200 on the first day".
- Do not use any exact figure for the damage to Dublin, the number of Irish Volunteers who fought in total, or the British force total; none is checked.

## 4. What exams ask

- Higher 100-mark essays on the Rising: its events and whether Ireland was "changed utterly" or its military and political significance. Marks go to events in order with dates, places and people, then a stated judgement: military failure, political success through the executions and the shift in opinion, with the limits (small support, civilian deaths, little effect until 1917–18).
- Higher essays on tensions or a decade of change (Home Rule Bill, World War I, then 1918–22): use this subsection for how the Bill, unionist resistance and the war split nationalism and set up Sinn Féin's rise; link causes across 1912–1916 with dated detail.
- Pearse essays (aims and achievements; Pearse against another figure): two or three aims (Gaelic identity, a republic by force), what he achieved (St Enda's, the Proclamation, the Rising, his posthumous influence) and what he did not live to see. Name the 1913 IRB oath, the Military Council and his Rossa speech, and give both contemporary and historian views.
- Ordinary Level 30-mark parts on the Third Home Rule Bill: who introduced it and when, why the Liberals needed the IPP, its main terms, the Parliament Act, the Ulster reaction, the suspension. Each correct dated point earns marks.
- Ordinary Level 30-mark parts on the Rising: planning by the Military Council, the Aud and countermand, Easter Monday, GPO and Proclamation, surrender, executions. A short narrative with named leaders and dates scores well.
- Ordinary Level 40-mark profiles: Pearse (education, Gaelic League, IRB, Rising, execution) and Markievicz (background, 1908 to 1916, St Stephen's Green, sentence, release). Cover each stage of the career in order.
- Short-answer and document parts: Redmond's war policy and why the Volunteers split; why public opinion changed after the executions; what the Proclamation said; who the leaders were. One clear fact per mark.
- Comparing views: contemporaries (unionists and Redmond's party condemning a "German-backed" rebellion, then a shift) against later historians; naming a historian adds credit.
- World War I effects on Ireland (recruitment, the 16th and 36th Divisions, economic effects) are asked as part of Rising or tension questions; give both nationalist and unionist sides.
