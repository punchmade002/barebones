## 1. Must cover (one bullet or block for every item; tick each off)

Background (assumed)
- [ ] A1 (assumed) Post-war reconstruction and the welfare state (Britain's National Health Service, 1948) made new treatments available to ordinary people.
- [ ] A2 (assumed) The Second World War drove research: penicillin, radar, code-breaking and the atomic bomb (1945).
- [ ] A3 (assumed) The Cold War: both blocs competed in science and technology, and each held nuclear weapons.
- [ ] A4 (assumed) The 1973 oil crisis made governments seek energy independence.

Key concept
- [ ] K1 Information technology: the use of computers and electronic systems to store, process and transmit information. Also define (keyTerms): genetic engineering, recombinant DNA, microchip, mainframe, personal computer, anti-nuclear movement, nuclear deterrence, Chernobyl.

(a) Biological sciences and medicine
- [ ] B1 Antibiotics: penicillin (Fleming 1928; Florey and Chain at Oxford; mass-produced 1943–44, mainly in the USA); streptomycin against tuberculosis; effect on deaths from pneumonia and TB in the 1950s.
- [ ] B2 Vaccination: the polio vaccines of Salk (1955) and Sabin (early 1960s); wider campaigns (BCG, diphtheria, measles); smallpox eradication (1980).
- [ ] B3 The structure of DNA (1953): Watson and Crick, Franklin and Wilkins, Nobel Prize 1962.
- [ ] B4 The contraceptive pill (1960): a medical advance with social effects (detail belongs to the women and the family notes).
- [ ] B5 Transplants and surgery: the first kidney transplant (1954), the first heart transplant (1967), and other advances (pacemaker, dialysis, hip replacement, CT scanner).
- [ ] B6 IVF: Louise Brown, 1978, Edwards and Steptoe.
- [ ] B7 Genetic engineering: recombinant DNA (1973), DNA sequencing (1977), genetically engineered insulin (1978), the biotechnology industry.
- [ ] B8 Ethics and risk: thalidomide (1961); the ethics of IVF and embryo research (Warnock Report 1984, Church objections); DNA fingerprinting (Jeffreys 1984, first criminal case 1986); regulation.
- [ ] B9 Effects: better health and longer lives, but unequal access, new costs and moral questions.

(b) Nuclear power
- [ ] N1 From weapons to electricity: Atoms for Peace (1953), the IAEA (1957), Euratom (1957), CERN (1954).
- [ ] N2 The first plants: Obninsk (1954) and Calder Hall (1956, also made plutonium).
- [ ] N3 The French programme: the Messmer Plan (1974); nuclear electricity's share of French supply.
- [ ] N4 Wider expansion after the 1973 oil crisis (West Germany, Sweden, the USSR and others).
- [ ] N5 Early accident: Windscale fire (1957).
- [ ] N6 The anti-nuclear movement: CND (1958), Greenpeace (1971), the protests of the late 1970s and early 1980s (including Carnsore Point in Ireland), referendums (Austria, Sweden).
- [ ] N7 Three Mile Island (28 March 1979).
- [ ] N8 Chernobyl (26 April 1986): causes, the cloud, the Soviet delay, evacuation and exclusion zone, consequences.
- [ ] N9 Results of the accidents: tighter safety, slower growth, policy changes (Italy, 1987), debate on risks and waste.
- [ ] N10 Nuclear deterrence: nuclear weapons as the military side (brief; detail belongs to the Cold War notes).

(c) The computer
- [ ] C1 Early machines: Turing's ideas, Colossus (1943–44), ENIAC (1945), the Manchester Baby (1948).
- [ ] C2 The transistor (1947) and the integrated circuit / microchip (1958–59); the microprocessor (1971).
- [ ] C3 Mainframes: LEO (1951), IBM System/360 (1964), business and government use.
- [ ] C4 Personal computers in the 1980s: Apple II (1977), IBM PC (1981), British home micros (ZX Spectrum, 1982), France's Minitel (1982).
- [ ] C5 Networks: ARPANET (1969), the World Wide Web (1989, public 1991).

Effects on work and society
- [ ] E1 Work: automation of routine office and factory tasks, fewer clerical and manufacturing jobs, growth of service jobs, deindustrialisation.
- [ ] E2 Everyday life: cash machines (1967), bar codes, word processors and spreadsheets, home computers.
- [ ] E3 The Cold War: COCOM export controls; the Soviet bloc's lag in computing and microelectronics and its role in the push for reform.
- [ ] E4 Western Europe's lag behind the USA and Japan; ESPRIT (1984).

Assessment (final block)
- [ ] S1 Achievements: health, cheap energy, communication and productivity.
- [ ] S2 Limits: accidents and waste, ethical dilemmas, job losses, inequalities, dependence on the USA and Japan.
- [ ] S3 Contemporary optimism (1950s) vs the questioning of the 1970s and 1980s.
- [ ] S4 Which of the three had the greatest impact on Europeans by 1992 (a judgement, with reasons on both sides).

## 4. What exams ask

- Higher 100-mark essay (asked in 2010, 2014, 2023): a statement that the three advances were highly significant, to be assessed. Marks go to a judgement on which had most impact (or how they differed), supported by dated examples from all three fields. Cover biology, nuclear power and the computer; an answer on one or two scores poorly.
- Strong answers pair benefits with problems in each field: medicine (cures and longer lives against thalidomide and IVF ethics); nuclear power (cheap electricity and French independence against Windscale, Three Mile Island, Chernobyl); computers (productivity and new industries against lost jobs and the Soviet and European lag).
- Include the Cold War link: nuclear technology came from weapons research; computing technology was restricted by COCOM; Soviet failure to keep up fed pressure for reform.
- Ordinary Level 30-mark questions give a short passage on one field and ask for two or three facts and an effect. Each correct, dated statement earns marks: Colossus (code-breaking) and the transistor (smaller, cheaper computers); Obninsk, Calder Hall, the Messmer Plan; penicillin and the polio vaccine with the date.
- Ordinary Level 40-mark questions ask for a paragraph on medicine or on the computer's effect on work: name the advance, the person, the date and its effect on health or jobs.
- Typical short answers: who developed penicillin into a drug (Florey and Chain); what the first station to supply a public grid was (Obninsk); what the Messmer Plan committed France to; what the computer did to routine office work (automated it and cut some jobs).
- Effects questions reward two-sided answers: for example, automation destroyed clerical and factory jobs but created service and technology jobs.
- Dates are marks: DNA 1953, Calder Hall 1956, IVF 1978, Three Mile Island 1979, Chernobyl 1986 and the IBM PC 1981 are the most-tested anchors.
