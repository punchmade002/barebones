# Package: hist-eur3-s8 — Case study: the Jarrow March, October 1936

- **loId:** `hist-eur3-s8` · **chapterId:** `hist-eur3` · **syllabusRef:** E3 S-CS
- **Syllabus:** Dictatorship and democracy in Europe, 1920–1945, Society and economy: case
  study **the Jarrow March, October 1936**. Outline scope: Jarrow and Palmer's shipyard; its
  closure by National Shipbuilders' Security (1933–34) and the steelworks refusal; unemployment
  levels; Ellen Wilkinson MP and the town council; the organisation of the crusade (the 200
  marchers, the cross-party approach, the Labour and TUC attitudes, the churches); the march
  (5 October–31 October 1936), the route and reception; the petition; Baldwin's refusal to meet
  them; outcome (the return home, the dole cut); later effects (new industries, Wilkinson's
  The Town That Was Murdered 1939, rearmament); its place in memory; success or failure. Key
  concepts: the Depression; democracy. No key personality is assigned to this subsection
  (Keynes belongs to the notes on inter-war Britain), but Ellen Wilkinson needs a full block
  because the documents questions turn on her. It is a documents-based case study (the 2016,
  2017 and 2025 DBQs), so the notes need enough detail to place any document about the march
  in context.
- **Prepared by:** a higher model, 2026-11-22. The writer works only from this package and
  `course-outlines/NOTES_STANDARD.md`.

## 1. Must cover (one bullet or block for every item; tick each off)

Background (assumed knowledge)
- [ ] A1 (assumed) The Depression in Britain: the world slump after 1929; registered unemployment highest in 1932–33 and falling to under two million by 1936; unemployment concentrated in the staple-industry regions (the north of England, South Wales, central Scotland) while the south-east and Midlands recovered. One or two bullets; the detail belongs to the notes on inter-war Britain.
- [ ] A2 (assumed) The National Government under Stanley Baldwin (Prime Minister from 1935): orthodox finance, balanced budgets, and the belief that general recovery would in time reach the depressed areas. One bullet.
- [ ] A3 (assumed) The dole and the means test: long-term unemployed paid by the Unemployment Assistance Board (set up under the Unemployment Act 1934) after a household means test. One bullet.
- [ ] A4 (assumed) The Special Areas Act 1934: Jarrow lay in one of the designated areas (Durham and Tyneside), but the commissioners had small budgets and little power, so the town got little help. One bullet.
- [ ] A5 (assumed) The hunger-march tradition of the Communist-led National Unemployed Workers' Movement (NUWM), which the government and the Labour leadership distrusted. One bullet; it explains why Jarrow called its march a "crusade".

Jarrow and Palmers (causes)
- [ ] B1 Jarrow: a one-industry town on the south bank of the Tyne in County Durham; its whole economy (shops, trades, families) depended on the yard's wages.
- [ ] B2 Palmers: the shipyard founded by Charles Mark Palmer in 1851, which grew into a combined shipyard and iron and steel works that dominated the town.
- [ ] B3 The shipbuilding slump: world trade fell, foreign competition grew, and British yards had far more capacity than orders.
- [ ] B4 Palmers' collapse: its last ship launched 1932; the company in receivership 1933.
- [ ] B5 National Shipbuilders Security (NSS): who formed it, how it was financed, what it did; it bought Palmers in 1934, dismantled the yard and banned shipbuilding on the site for forty years.
- [ ] B6 The steelworks refusal: the 1934 scheme for a modern steelworks on the site, put forward by T. Vosper Salt, abandoned after opposition from the British Iron and Steel Federation, which wanted no new competitor.
- [ ] B7 Unemployment levels in Jarrow, compared with the national level (use the range in section 2).
- [ ] B8 Social conditions: poverty, poor and overcrowded housing, ill health and high death rates; J.B. Priestley's description of the town in English Journey (1934) as a source.
- [ ] B9 The government's attitude before the march: the remark by Walter Runciman, President of the Board of Trade, that Jarrow must work out its own salvation (give no year for the remark).

Organising the Crusade
- [ ] C1 The town council's initiative and the cross-party approach: Labour, Conservative and Liberal support in the town; called a "crusade", not a hunger march, to show it was non-political and respectable.
- [ ] C2 Ellen Wilkinson: one block headed "Key figure: Ellen Wilkinson" (she is not a syllabus key personality): who she was, her career to 1935, her role in the march (publicity, speeches, the petition), her absence for the Labour conference, her book, her later career, how contemporaries and historians see her.
- [ ] C3 David Riley: the councillor who was marshal and leader of the marchers on the road.
- [ ] C4 The aims: a petition asking the government to provide work for Jarrow, above all by bringing new industry (a steelworks), and to make the town's distress known.
- [ ] C5 Selection and discipline: about 200 men chosen from many more volunteers, medically checked, organised and disciplined to show that the unemployed were respectable men who wanted work.
- [ ] C6 Money and supplies: a public appeal for funds; supporters gave boots, clothing and food.
- [ ] C7 The petition: about 11,000 signatures, carried in an oak box.
- [ ] C8 Labour and TUC attitudes: no official backing from the national Labour Party or the TUC, which feared Communist links and preferred action through Parliament; criticism at the Labour conference in Edinburgh, October 1936; but many local Labour parties and trades councils helped.
- [ ] C9 The churches: a service and the Bishop of Jarrow's blessing at the start; the Bishop of Durham, Hensley Henson, condemned the march as mob pressure; churches along the route gave food and shelter.
- [ ] C10 Kept apart from the NUWM and the Communists, to protect the march's cross-party, respectable image.

The march, 5–31 October 1936
- [ ] D1 Departure from Jarrow on 5 October 1936 after a church service and a send-off by crowds.
- [ ] D2 The route and the daily routine: about 300 miles by way of the towns in section 2; marching in stages with rests, Sundays off, nights in drill halls, schools, church halls and sometimes workhouses; the banner and the mouth-organ band.
- [ ] D3 The reception along the route: welcomed and fed by councils, Co-operative societies, churches and local parties of all sides; boots repaired at Leicester; some coolness from officials and some Conservatives.
- [ ] D4 Publicity: wide press and newsreel coverage, mostly sympathetic, made the march a national story.
- [ ] D5 Arrival in London on 31 October 1936 and the Hyde Park meeting on 1 November.

The government's response
- [ ] E1 The government's public statement of October 1936 discouraging marches on London: grievances should be raised through MPs, and processions had no constitutional influence.
- [ ] E2 Baldwin's refusal to receive a deputation of the marchers.
- [ ] E3 The petition presented in the Commons by Wilkinson on 4 November 1936: only a brief exchange, and no action.
- [ ] E4 The dole cut: the Unemployment Assistance Board reduced the marchers' benefit because they had not been available for work while marching.
- [ ] E5 The return home by train on 5 November 1936; a welcome in Jarrow but bitter disappointment among the men.

Outcomes
- [ ] F1 Short term: no steelworks, no shipyard, no new commitment; unemployment in Jarrow stayed high.
- [ ] F2 New industries: Sir John Jarvis, Conservative MP for Guildford, organised help through a fund raised in Surrey and brought shipbreaking and small metal-working firms to the old Palmers site; they employed only a fraction of the jobless.
- [ ] F3 The Special Areas (Amendment) Act 1937: tax and rent reliefs for firms setting up in the special areas; no evidence that the march caused it.
- [ ] F4 Rearmament from the later 1930s and the Second World War brought back work to Tyneside; this, not the march, ended mass unemployment in Jarrow.
- [ ] F5 Wilkinson's The Town That Was Murdered (1939): her account blaming NSS, the steel interests and the government.
- [ ] F6 Wilkinson's later career: Minister of Education in the Labour government of 1945; died 1947. One bullet.

Significance, concepts and memory
- [ ] G1 Memory: the march became the best-known symbol of the "hungry thirties" and of government failure on unemployment.
- [ ] G2 Influence on later politics: memories of the 1930s fed the post-1945 commitment to full employment and the welfare state (one clause; the welfare state belongs to the notes on wartime society).
- [ ] G3 Re-enactments (1986, 2011) and the march's place in the labour movement's memory.
- [ ] G4 Concept, the Depression (keyTerm): Jarrow as the extreme case of structural unemployment in a staple industry.
- [ ] G5 Concept, democracy (keyTerm): the march as a peaceful, lawful protest within a parliamentary democracy, in contrast to the extremism of the dictatorships and the violence of Cable Street in the same week; but also the limits of democracy, since a petition could be ignored.
- [ ] G6 Contemporary vs later views: supporters (moral victory, dignity) vs critics (government, Henson, Labour leaders: political theatre, no solution); historians (a failure in its immediate aims but a lasting success in publicity and memory; some note that the NUWM marches were larger but are less remembered).

Documents and judgement
- [ ] H1 Placing a document: who wrote it, when and why. Wilkinson's book (primary, a participant, partisan, written in 1939 with hindsight); government statements (aimed to show recovery and discourage marches); newspaper editorials written during the march (opinion, not a full account); photographs (the disciplined column, the banner); later textbooks (secondary). Wilkinson did not share the men's sleeping quarters.
- [ ] H2 The wider context of autumn 1936 for dating sources: Cable Street (4 October 1936), the Spanish Civil War in the news, the NUWM's own national hunger march reaching London in November, and the abdication crisis in December pushing Jarrow from the headlines.
- [ ] H3 "Assessment: success or failure?" block: paired points (immediate aims vs long-term effect; government response vs public sympathy; contemporary vs later views).

## 3. Traps

Do not use (doubtful or wrong in the current notes):
- Do not use "Palmers Shipbuilding and Iron Company was founded in 1851": Palmer founded the yard in 1851; the company took that name later. Use B2.
- Do not use "it launched its thousandth ship in July 1930": not checked.
- Do not use "creditors appointed a receiver in June 1933" or "last ship on 19 July 1932" with day and month: give the years only (B4).
- Do not use "about 17 per cent in the north-east in 1936 and about 33 per cent in shipbuilding": not checked.
- Do not use any single exact Jarrow unemployment figure (and never the figure printed in a past-paper document): give the range in B7.
- Do not use "the American businessman" for Vosper Salt, or "Wilkinson later said that it had been strangled at birth" as a quotation: not checked.

- Do not use "David Riley, the Labour leader on the council" or "Riley and some councillors briefly left the Labour Party after the march": not checked. Call him the marshal and leader of the marchers.
- Do not use "chosen from more than a thousand volunteers; records name fewer": say "chosen from many more volunteers".
- Do not use "about 290 miles" or "about 15 miles a day": say "about 300 miles" and give no daily figure.
- Do not use "Baldwin declined to meet a deputation on 29 October 1936, and cited a fall in Jarrow's unemployment" or "Wilkinson answered that the fall was due to combining the figures with South Shields": the date and the exchange are not checked. Say only that Baldwin refused to receive them (E2).
- Do not use "it was not debated" as a flat statement: say it led only to a brief exchange and no action (E3).
- Do not use "by 1939 about 100 men worked in a furniture factory and up to 500 in metal industries": not checked. Give no job numbers (F2).
- Do not use "one called it a waste of time", "Labour later adopted its banners", or the Daily Herald's stance: not checked.
- Do not use "the Special Areas Act of 1934 had given it little help" with figures; keep it general (A4).

Doubtful claims to avoid:
- Do not say the march caused the Special Areas (Amendment) Act 1937 or rearmament orders.
- Do not say Wilkinson walked the whole way, or that she shared the men's sleeping quarters.
- Do not call the Jarrow march a hunger march or a Communist march; it was cross-party and deliberately kept apart from the NUWM.
- Do not say Labour opposed the cause: the national leadership withheld official support from the march; local Labour parties helped.
- Do not date Runciman's "Jarrow must work out its own salvation" remark (not 1936, not 1934): the year is disputed and unchecked. Attribute it to Runciman, President of the Board of Trade, and say it shows the government's attitude before the march.
- Do not invent quotations from the petition, Wilkinson, Baldwin, Henson or the marchers. Only Runciman's remark (B9) may be quoted.
- Do not give totals for money raised, men who finished, or miles per day.

- Sir John Jarvis (Conservative MP who helped bring firms to Jarrow) vs David Riley (march leader) vs Charles Mark Palmer (founder of the yard, 1851).
- The Jarrow Crusade (about 200 men, cross-party, October 1936) vs the NUWM's national hunger march (Communist-led, reached London in November 1936).
- The Special Areas Act 1934 vs the Special Areas (Amendment) Act 1937.

## 4. What exams ask (topics only)

- Documents question, Higher (2017, 2025): comparing two documents on the town's problems or on the value of marching (do both agree, which is stronger); judging a government statement's arguments (MPs, constitutional influence) against the town's distress; the value of an editorial or of Wilkinson's book as a primary source (written during the march or soon after, by a participant, opinion not a full account, hindsight). Marks come from citing the document for each point, so the notes must make clear who issued the government's case, who wrote the 1939 book, and when.
- Documents part (d), Higher 40 marks: the march's aims and how far they were achieved (2017); whether it was a total failure (2025). Marks reward: the aims stated precisely (work, new industry, a steelworks); each failure with a fact (Baldwin's refusal, the petition ignored, the dole cut, no steelworks); each partial success (national publicity, cross-party sympathy, Jarvis's firms, the 1937 Act as part of a changing climate, memory and post-war influence); a clear overall judgement, usually "a failure in its aims but not a total failure".
- Documents question, Ordinary (2016, 2025): Wilkinson's role (organiser, publicist, joined the march each morning but did not sleep with the men); whether the march was enjoyable or hard (food, band, halls); whether the marchers deserved public support; primary vs secondary (Wilkinson's own account vs a later textbook); part (d) on results or on Britain's inter-war problems.
- Ordinary Level picture or document parts (6 marks each; 2009, 2011, 2024): where the march started and finished, what the men protested about (unemployment, poverty), the band and the support cart, critics' view of the unemployed as idle and how a writer changed his mind, what they hoped to win, the daily routine, where they slept, why the yard closed (the Depression).
- Ordinary Level paragraph (40 marks, set very often): why the march took place (yard closed, NSS, steelworks blocked, unemployment), how it was organised (council, cross-party, 200 men, petition), the march (dates, route, reception), the response (Baldwin, petition, dole cut) and results. Each accurate dated fact counts; the dates 5 and 31 October 1936, NSS 1934, Wilkinson and the petition are the most rewarded.
- Higher Level essay (100 marks), causes and consequences (2007): causes must go beyond "unemployment" to the single-industry town, NSS, the steel refusal and government inaction; consequences in the short and long term, with a judgement.
- Higher Level essay (2014): how far the march illustrated inter-war Britain's social and economic problems. Link Jarrow to staple-industry decline, regional unemployment, the dole and means test, poverty and health, and the limits of government policy; note that the south-east was prospering at the same time.
- Higher Level comparison essays (2011, 2021, 2023): Keynes and the Jarrow March (sometimes with Churchill) as responses to unemployment. Marks reward a judgement of relative impact: the march gave publicity but won nothing at the time, Keynes's ideas shaped later policy; avoid two unconnected narratives.
- Judgement material examiners reward: success vs failure (paired), contemporary vs later views, how democracy dealt with a peaceful protest, and the real causes of Jarrow's recovery (rearmament and war).
