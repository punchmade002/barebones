## 3. Traps

Do not use (wrong or doubtful facts in the current notes):
- Do not use: "Reagan's own adviser Jack Matlock argued that Gorbachev's reforms drove the change", because Matlock's role and exact view are muddled (he was ambassador to Moscow, and credited both sides). Give the argument without naming him.
- Do not use: "Britain, France and Ireland sent teams" as a precise claim of government policy; say that some national teams, including Britain's, attended despite government pressure, or leave it out.
- Do not use: "the two leaders declared that the Cold War was ending" at Malta as a quotation or formal declaration, because it was not a signed statement. Say they spoke of a new era.
- Do not use: "the Soviet Union rejected the link", because the claim is unspecific; say only that the amendment tied trade to emigration and angered Moscow.
- Do not use: Reagan saying "a nuclear war cannot be won" as an exact quotation; give it as reported speech.
- Do not use: the Central America material (Nicaragua, El Salvador, Contras, Boland Amendments) and the nuclear freeze movement. They are not in this subsection's outline; Iran-Contra belongs with the Presidency notes. If the writer mentions them at all, one sentence, no dates.
- Do not use: Reagan "dropped SALT II restraint in 1986" without hedging; say Reagan's government ceased to observe the SALT II limits in 1986.

Confusable people, terms and dates
- SALT I (1972, Moscow, Nixon) vs SALT II (1979, Vienna, Carter, never ratified). ABM Treaty is part of SALT I.
- SALT vs INF (START came later, 1991; not on this list). SALT limited growth; INF eliminated missiles.
- SDI (1983, missile defence) vs the ABM Treaty (1972, limits on defence); critics said SDI undermined it.
- Détente (Nixon/Ford, 1972–75) vs "new Cold War" or end of détente (1979–83).
- Paris Peace Accords (1973) vs the end of the war (1975): the US left in 1973; South Vietnam fell in 1975.
- Gulf of Tonkin Resolution (1964) vs War Powers Resolution (1973).
- Helsinki (1975, CSCE, 35 states) vs Reykjavik (1986) and Geneva (1985).
- Ford was president at Saigon's fall, not Nixon.
- Gorbachev (1985–91) vs Brezhnev, Andropov and Chernenko.

## 4. What exams ask

- Higher essay on US foreign policy 1973–1989 as a whole: a clear line through retreat from Vietnam, détente at its height, collapse after 1979, Reagan's build-up, then negotiation with Gorbachev. Marks go to a judgement on continuity or change and to dated events in order.
- Higher essay on SALT and Star Wars (often paired with Korea or Cuba): students must explain what SALT I and SALT II limited, why SALT II failed, and what SDI was and why it mattered. Explaining that SALT slowed growth but did not cut warheads is a concrete mark-earner.
- Higher essay on Star Wars (paired with the Moon landing): technological development as a theme; SDI's announcement, doubts about feasibility, Soviet fears, role at Reykjavik.
- Ordinary Level paragraph on détente, SALT I and SALT II (about 30 marks): define détente, give date and place of both treaties, name ABM Treaty and Interim Agreement, say SALT II was never ratified and why (Afghanistan).
- Ordinary Level paragraph on SDI: date and medium of announcement, what it proposed, nickname, critics, never deployed.
- Judgement items: Reagan versus Gorbachev as the cause of the Cold War's end; success versus failure of détente. Strong answers weigh economic collapse in the Soviet Union against US pressure and name specific events (INF, Reykjavik, 1989).
- Cause-and-result items: why the US left Vietnam in 1973 and why Saigon fell in 1975; why détente collapsed (Afghanistan, Angola, Iran, Soviet buildup claims by critics).
- Each correct dated point earns marks; a sound answer names the leader, the event, the date and one consequence.
