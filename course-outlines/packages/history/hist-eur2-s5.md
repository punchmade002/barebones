## 1. Must cover (one bullet or block for every item; tick each off)

Background (assumed)
- [ ] A1 (assumed) The two sides: the Allies (Entente) of France, Russia and Britain, later joined by Italy (1915), Romania (1916) and the USA (1917, as an "associated power"); the Central Powers of Germany and Austria-Hungary, joined by the Ottoman Empire (1914) and Bulgaria (1915). One line; the alliance system belongs elsewhere.
- [ ] A2 (assumed) The short-war expectation of 1914 and the Schlieffen Plan in outline (defeat France quickly through Belgium, then turn on Russia), as the starting point; its pre-war origins and Belgium's role in Britain's entry belong to the notes on the outbreak of war.
- [ ] A3 (assumed) Army terms used in the notes: front, offensive, salient (a bulge in the line surrounded on three sides), division and corps (large army units), casualties (killed, wounded, missing and captured, not just deaths), BEF (British Expeditionary Force).

Key concepts (defined as used in this topic; also in keyTerms)
- [ ] K1 World war: a war fought on several continents and at sea, drawing in empires, whole economies and populations; show this through the many fronts (Western, Eastern, Italian, Balkan, Middle East, colonies, the sea).
- [ ] K2 War of attrition: winning by wearing down the enemy's men and resources rather than by one decisive battle; Verdun, the Somme and Passchendaele as the examples; the blockade as attrition at sea.
- [ ] K3 Conscription: compulsory military service; already normal on the Continent, new to Britain in 1916, never applied in Ireland.
- [ ] K4 Stalemate and trench warfare: why defence beat attack on the Western Front (also a keyTerm: trench warfare).

The war of movement, 1914
- [ ] M1 German invasion of Belgium (4 August 1914) and Liège; the Schlieffen Plan as modified by the younger Moltke.
- [ ] M2 The BEF: a small professional army; Mons (23 August 1914) and the retreat with the French.
- [ ] M3 The First Battle of the Marne (September 1914): halted the German advance near Paris; the Germans withdrew to the River Aisne and dug in; the Schlieffen Plan had failed.
- [ ] M4 The "race to the sea" and the trench line from the North Sea (Belgian coast) to the Swiss frontier by the end of 1914.
- [ ] M5 First Ypres (October–November 1914): Allied defensive success; the old regular BEF largely destroyed, so Britain depended on volunteers.
- [ ] M6 The Christmas truce of 1914: unofficial, in parts of the line; the commands forbade repeats.

Trench warfare: conditions, weapons and tactics
- [ ] T1 The trench system: front line, support and reserve trenches, communication trenches, dugouts, barbed wire, no man's land.
- [ ] T2 Conditions: mud, rats, lice, cold, shelling and snipers; trench foot; shell shock (now called combat stress or post-traumatic stress).
- [ ] T3 Why defence beat attack: machine guns, quick-firing artillery, barbed wire, railways bringing defenders' reserves faster than attackers could advance; most battle casualties caused by artillery.
- [ ] T4 Preliminary bombardment (warned the enemy, churned the ground, often failed to cut wire) and the creeping barrage (from 1916).
- [ ] T5 Poison gas: first large-scale use by Germany, chlorine, at Second Ypres (22 April 1915); then phosgene and mustard gas; gas masks reduced its effect.
- [ ] T6 Tanks: first used by Britain at Flers-Courcelette (15 September 1916); slow and unreliable; massed at Cambrai (November 1917).
- [ ] T7 Aircraft: reconnaissance and artillery spotting, then fighters and bombing; Zeppelin and Gotha raids on British cities.
- [ ] T8 By 1918, combined arms: German stormtrooper (infiltration) tactics; Allied coordination of tanks, aircraft, artillery and infantry.

The Western Front, 1915–1917: attrition
- [ ] W1 1915 offensives: Neuve Chapelle, Artois, Loos; none broke the line; the British shell shortage and political crisis (May 1915).
- [ ] W2 Verdun (21 February–18 December 1916): Falkenhayn's attack and his later claimed aim to "bleed France white"; losses of hundreds of thousands on each side; the French held.
- [ ] W3 Pétain at Verdun: rotation of divisions, the Sacred Way (Voie Sacrée) supply road; Verdun as the symbol of French endurance.
- [ ] W4 The Somme (1 July–18 November 1916): joint Anglo-French, partly to relieve Verdun; the week-long bombardment; first day about 57,000 British casualties, about 19,000 killed, the worst day in the British Army's history.
- [ ] W5 The Somme's results: a few kilometres gained; over a million casualties across all armies; Kitchener's volunteer "Pals" battalions; first use of tanks (T6); German losses also heavy.
- [ ] W6 Irish divisions on the Western Front: the 36th (Ulster) Division at Thiepval on 1 July 1916; the 16th (Irish) Division at Guillemont and Ginchy (September 1916); both side by side at Messines (June 1917).
- [ ] W7 Nivelle's offensive on the Chemin des Dames (April 1917) failed; French army mutinies (spring 1917); Pétain replaced Nivelle and restored morale by improving conditions and avoiding big attacks.
- [ ] W8 Arras (April 1917): British offensive, including the Canadian capture of Vimy Ridge.
- [ ] W9 Messines (7 June 1917): mines under the ridge, a limited success before Passchendaele.
- [ ] W10 Passchendaele (Third Ypres, 31 July–November 1917): aims (break out of the Ypres salient, reach the Belgian coast and U-boat bases); rain and shelling turned the ground to mud; a gain of about 8 kilometres.
- [ ] W11 Cambrai (20 November 1917): hundreds of tanks in a surprise attack; gains mostly lost to a German counter-attack.

The Eastern Front and Russia's exit
- [ ] E1 Tannenberg (26–30 August 1914): Hindenburg and Ludendorff destroyed the Russian Second Army, taking about 90,000 prisoners; the Masurian Lakes (September 1914) against the First Army; Russia's invasion drew German troops east at the time of the Marne.
- [ ] E2 Gorlice–Tarnów (May 1915) and the great Russian retreat from Poland and Galicia.
- [ ] E3 The Brusilov Offensive (June 1916) against Austria-Hungary: the most successful Allied attack of 1916; took many prisoners and forced Germany to move troops east; could not be sustained; very heavy Russian losses.
- [ ] E4 Romania joined the Allies (August 1916) and was largely overrun by the end of 1916.
- [ ] E5 Defeats and shortages contributed to the February Revolution (1917); the Provisional Government fought on; the Kerensky Offensive (July 1917) collapsed. One or two lines; the revolutions belong to the notes on Tsarist Russia.
- [ ] E6 The Bolshevik armistice (December 1917) and the Treaty of Brest-Litovsk (3 March 1918): Russia gave up Poland, the Baltic lands, Finland and Ukraine; Germany could move many divisions west, but still kept large forces in the east.

Other fronts
- [ ] O1 Gallipoli: the Ottoman entry (October–November 1914); the naval attempt on the Dardanelles (February–March 1915); landings on 25 April 1915 by British, French and ANZAC troops; Irish battalions at the landings and at Suvla Bay (August 1915); stalemate; evacuation completed by 9 January 1916.
- [ ] O2 Gallipoli's significance: damaged Churchill, who had promoted it (he left the Admiralty in May 1915); a founding moment for Australian and New Zealand national identity (Anzac Day, 25 April).
- [ ] O3 Italy: the Treaty of London (April 1915) and entry (May 1915); eleven battles of the Isonzo; Caporetto (October 1917); recovery on the Piave and victory at Vittorio Veneto (October–November 1918).
- [ ] O4 The Middle East: Mesopotamia and the surrender at Kut (April 1916); Palestine, Allenby's capture of Jerusalem (December 1917) and Megiddo (September 1918); the Arab Revolt (from 1916) and T. E. Lawrence.
- [ ] O5 The Balkans: Serbia overrun (autumn 1915, after Bulgaria joined the Central Powers); the Allied front at Salonika; Bulgaria's armistice (29 September 1918).
- [ ] O6 The colonies: most German colonies taken by the Allies early; German East Africa under Lettow-Vorbeck held out until after the armistice in Europe.

War at sea
- [ ] S1 The British blockade of Germany: food and raw material shortages; the "turnip winter" of 1916–17.
- [ ] S2 Jutland (31 May–1 June 1916): Jellicoe (British Grand Fleet) vs Scheer (German High Seas Fleet); Britain lost more ships and men (figures in section 2) but kept control of the North Sea; the German fleet stayed mostly in port afterwards. Link in one line to the pre-war naval race (notes on the naval policy of Wilhelm II).
- [ ] S3 Submarine warfare from 1915: the sinking of the Lusitania (7 May 1915); American outrage; Germany restricted its campaign (the Sussex pledge, 1916).
- [ ] S4 Unrestricted submarine warfare resumed on 1 February 1917, to starve Britain out within months; very heavy British shipping losses in spring 1917.
- [ ] S5 Convoys from 1917 and better anti-submarine methods cut losses; the campaign failed and brought in the USA.

Conscription and manpower
- [ ] C1 Continental conscription: Germany, France, Russia and Austria-Hungary called up trained reservists under their pre-war systems.
- [ ] C2 Britain: Kitchener's volunteer campaign (over two million volunteers by the end of 1915); the Military Service Act (passed January 1916, in force 2 March 1916) for unmarried men aged 18 to 41; extended to married men in May 1916.
- [ ] C3 Conscientious objectors: tribunals; about 16,000 claimed the status; some jailed, others sent to non-combatant work.
- [ ] C4 Ireland: excluded from the 1916 Act; the April 1918 attempt to extend conscription caused the Conscription Crisis (the anti-conscription pledge, the one-day general strike of 23 April 1918); never enforced. Two lines; the effect on Irish politics belongs to the Irish history notes.
- [ ] C5 Other states: the US Selective Service Act (May 1917); Germany's Auxiliary Service Law (December 1916), compulsory war work for men aged 17 to 60.
- [ ] C6 Reserved occupations: skilled workers kept in munitions, mines and railways; women replaced many men (one line; the notes on women in the workforce cover it).

The USA's entry and the end of the war, 1917–1918
- [ ] U1 Why the USA entered: unrestricted submarine warfare (American ships sunk) and the Zimmermann Telegram (a German offer of alliance to Mexico, intercepted by British intelligence and published in March 1917).
- [ ] U2 The US declaration of war (6 April 1917) as an "associated power"; Pershing and the American Expeditionary Forces arriving in strength in 1918; the effect on Allied morale and German calculations.
- [ ] U3 Ludendorff's Spring Offensive (Kaiserschlacht), from 21 March 1918 (Operation Michael): large gains, close to Amiens, no breakthrough; Haig's "backs to the wall" order (11 April 1918).
- [ ] U4 Unified Allied command under Foch (spring 1918); German exhaustion and supply problems; the Second Battle of the Marne (July 1918) turned the tide.
- [ ] U5 The Hundred Days: Amiens (8 August 1918), Ludendorff's "black day of the German army"; the Hindenburg Line broken in late September 1918.
- [ ] U6 Collapse of Germany's allies (Bulgaria 29 September, the Ottomans 30 October, Austria-Hungary 3 November 1918) and Ludendorff's demand for an armistice (29 September 1918); the request to Wilson on the basis of the Fourteen Points (October 1918).
- [ ] U7 The armistice: signed in a railway carriage at Compiègne at about 5 a.m. on 11 November 1918, in effect at 11 a.m. Its terms and the German revolution belong to the notes on war and revolution in Germany; give only the signing here.
- [ ] U8 The human cost: military deaths of about 9 to 10 million (estimates vary), many more wounded, and millions of civilian deaths.

Key personality: Douglas Haig
- [ ] H1 Who he was: born in Edinburgh in 1861 into the Haig whisky family; Oxford and Sandhurst; cavalry officer (7th Hussars); Staff College; service in the Sudan (Omdurman, 1898) and the Boer War.
- [ ] H2 1914–15: commanded I Corps in the BEF (Mons, the retreat, First Ypres), then the First Army (1915, including Loos).
- [ ] H3 Commander-in-chief of the BEF from December 1915 (succeeding Sir John French) to the end of the war; field marshal from 1 January 1917.
- [ ] H4 His battles: the Somme (1916); Arras and Passchendaele (1917); the defence against the 1918 Spring Offensive and the "backs to the wall" order; the Hundred Days, in which the BEF played the leading part and took many prisoners.
- [ ] H5 His methods and beliefs: a believer in attrition and in the decisive breakthrough with cavalry to exploit it; confidence before battle (his diary on the eve of the Somme); support for new weapons such as tanks.
- [ ] H6 Participant, and shaped by events: the need to support France (Verdun, the French mutinies), the strength of defences and poor communications (no radio at the front) limited what any commander could do.
- [ ] H7 After the war: made Earl Haig (1919) and voted £100,000 by Parliament; founding president of the British Legion (1921) and the poppy appeal (the Haig Fund); died in 1928; huge public mourning at his funeral.
- [ ] H8 How contemporaries saw him: trusted by George V and much of the army; distrusted by Lloyd George, who thought his offensives wasteful and kept reserves in Britain over the winter of 1917–18; mourned by ex-servicemen and the public in 1928.
- [ ] H9 How historians judge him, critics: Lloyd George's War Memoirs (1930s); Alan Clark's The Donkeys (1961) and "lions led by donkeys"; Oh! What a Lovely War (1963 stage, 1969 film) and Blackadder Goes Forth (1989) fixed the popular image; Denis Winter and Gerard De Groot as critical biographers ("butcher", callous, unimaginative).
- [ ] H10 How historians judge him, defenders: John Terraine (Douglas Haig: The Educated Soldier, 1963); Gary Sheffield (Forgotten Victory, 2001); the "learning curve" argument that the BEF improved its tactics and won in 1918 under Haig.

Assessment
- [ ] X1 Assessment: how the war was won and lost, as paired points: Allied strengths (blockade, attrition, US troops and resources, combined-arms victory of 1918) vs limits (1915–17 casualties out of proportion to gains); German successes (held the Western Front four years, knocked Russia out, nearly won in spring 1918) vs failures (the Schlieffen Plan, the U-boat gamble that brought in the USA, an exhausted army by 1918).
- [ ] X2 Assessment of Haig: achievements vs limits (victory in 1918, adaptation, coalition loyalty vs the first day of the Somme, Passchendaele, over-optimism and slowness to stop failing attacks); contemporary vs later views ("donkeys" vs "learning curve").

## 3. Traps

Do not use (wrong or doubtful facts in the current notes):
- Do not use: the Marne halted the Germans "about 40 kilometres from Paris", because the distance varies by source; say the Germans came close to Paris.
- Do not use: gas at Second Ypres "released against French colonial and Canadian troops" as one event, because on 22 April the chlorine hit French and Algerian troops; the Canadians were gassed two days later. Use the wording in section 2.
- Do not use: the Somme "wiped out the male population of some local communities", because it overstates; say the Pals battalions brought heavy losses to particular towns, such as Accrington, at once.
- Do not use: the Brusilov Offensive "cost Russia about a million casualties" as a fixed figure, because estimates range from about half a million to over a million; say very heavy losses.
- Do not use: Brest-Litovsk "let Germany move over a million men west", because the figure is doubtful; say it freed dozens of divisions for the West.
- Do not use: "Britain had food stocks for only weeks" in spring 1917, because the claim rests on contested statements; say Britain faced a serious food crisis.
- Do not use: the Lusitania's "128 Americans" as exact, because sources give slightly different figures; say more than 120 Americans.
- Do not use: "From the 1920s memoirs, notably Lloyd George's, criticised him", because Lloyd George's War Memoirs appeared in the 1930s. Critical memoirs began in the late 1920s; Lloyd George's came in the 1930s.
- Do not use: Clark "misattributed" the phrase "lions led by donkeys" to German generals as if a real source existed; Clark presented it as an exchange between German generals, but no such source has been found, and similar phrases were used of other armies before 1914.
- Do not use: Haig "founded the British Legion" alone; he was one of its founders and its first president.
- Do not use: the US declared war "after the submarine campaign sank American ships and the Zimmermann Telegram" as a single date chain without the date of Wilson's request (2 April) vs the declaration (6 April); keep both dates distinct.
- Do not use: the Military Service Act "of January 1916" as the start of call-ups without the 2 March 1916 date when it came into force; both are correct for different things (passed vs in force).
- Do not use: Foch's unified command "in April 1918" alone if the reason is given as the March crisis; the Doullens agreement was 26 March 1918, the formal title came in April.

Quotations
- Do not quote Haig's diary, the Zimmermann Telegram, Wilson's war message or a soldier's letter; describe them. Past papers use extracts from these as documents.
- "They shall not pass" (Ils ne passeront pas) is associated with Nivelle at Verdun, not Pétain; leave it out.
- "Lions led by donkeys" is a phrase about British soldiers and generals; it is not a quotation from Haig or Ludendorff.

Confusable people, terms and dates
- Moltke the younger (chief of staff 1906–14) vs Moltke the elder (1860s–70s); Falkenhayn (chief of staff 1914–16, Verdun) vs Hindenburg and Ludendorff (from August 1916, in charge in the West and of the war economy).
- Sir John French (BEF 1914–15) vs Haig (from December 1915) vs Kitchener (War Secretary, drowned June 1916 when HMS Hampshire sank) vs Robertson (Chief of the Imperial General Staff 1915–18).
- Joffre (French commander 1914–16) vs Nivelle (December 1916–May 1917) vs Pétain (from May 1917) vs Foch (Allied supreme commander 1918).
- First Ypres (1914), Second Ypres (1915, gas), Third Ypres (1917, Passchendaele). First Marne (September 1914) vs Second Marne (July 1918).
- Tannenberg (August 1914) vs the Masurian Lakes (September 1914) vs Gorlice–Tarnów (1915).
- Treaty of London 1915 (Italy) vs Treaty of London 1839 (Belgian neutrality, outbreak of war notes).
- Verdun (German attack, French defence) vs the Somme (Anglo-French attack). The Somme lasted until 18 November 1916; Verdun until 18 December 1916.
- Casualties vs deaths: the 57,000 figure is casualties; about 19,000 of them were killed.
- Armistice (11 November 1918) vs the Treaty of Versailles (1919) vs Brest-Litovsk (3 March 1918).
- World war vs total war: total war (the home front, state control) belongs to the notes on the impact of war on society.
- Associated power (the USA's status) vs ally.

## 4. What exams ask

- Higher 100-mark essay on what a student learned from studying Haig, Owen and women in the workforce, or the conduct of war with women, Wilson or war literature (combined-topic essays, often). Marks go to a stated lesson about the war (attrition at huge cost, defence stronger than attack, a war won by mobilising whole societies) backed by dated evidence from each element, not narrative. For the conduct-of-war element: the Marne and stalemate (1914), the weapons that made defence strong, Verdun and the Somme (1916), Passchendaele (1917), and the 1918 offensives and Hundred Days.
- Higher essay judging Haig's generalship, sometimes paired with Wilson's role: a clear verdict sustained throughout, with achievements vs limits (victory in 1918, learning and new tactics, holding the coalition together vs the first day of the Somme, Passchendaele's mud, over-optimism) and contemporary vs later views (Lloyd George; Clark's "donkeys" vs Terraine and Sheffield). Naming at least one historian on each side earns credit.
- Ordinary 30- and 40-mark paragraphs on Haig (asked often): each accurate dated statement earns marks: succeeded French (December 1915), the Somme (1916) with the first-day losses, Arras and Passchendaele (1917), field marshal (1917), the "backs to the wall" order (April 1918), the Hundred Days, the British Legion and poppy appeal, his death (1928), and the debate about his reputation.
- Ordinary document questions (DBQ style) on: Haig's diary before the Somme (his confidence and the men's spirits, then a question on what actually happened: very heavy losses, little ground); a German soldier's account of the first day of the Somme (machine guns brought up from deep dugouts after the bombardment and firing as the British advanced), which tests why the bombardment failed; the Zimmermann Telegram (alliance with Mexico, unrestricted submarine warfare, why it drew the USA in); an extract on conscription and women's work (conscription in force from March 1916). Students should read the document carefully, answer only from it where asked, and add one outside fact where asked, such as Verdun as another 1916 battle.
- Cause and result points examiners reward: why the war of movement became stalemate (Schlieffen Plan failure, the Marne, the race to the sea, defensive weapons); why offensives failed (bombardment warning, uncut wire, mud, machine guns, reserves moved by rail, poor communications); why the USA entered (U-boats, the Zimmermann Telegram); why Germany lost in 1918 (blockade, exhausted army after the Spring Offensive, American troops, Allied combined arms, collapse of its allies); why Britain needed conscription (losses and falling volunteer numbers by 1915).
- Judgement points: attrition as a deliberate strategy vs the result of failed breakthroughs; the Somme as failure (first-day losses, small gains) vs success (relieved Verdun, wore down the German army, taught the BEF); Gallipoli as failure for the Allies but a source of national identity for Australia and New Zealand; Jutland as a German tactical success (more British losses) but a British strategic success (control of the North Sea kept the blockade in place).
