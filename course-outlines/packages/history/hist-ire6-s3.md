## 1. Must cover (one bullet or block for every item; tick each off)

Background (assumed)
- [ ] A1 (assumed) The 1950s: stagnation, emigration and a state that did little to plan; the turn to economic planning after 1958 (see the notes on economic planning and free trade, and the Programme for Economic Expansion).
- [ ] A2 (assumed) The churches (Catholic Church above all) owned and managed most schools and many hospitals; the state paid but did not run them.
- [ ] A3 (assumed) Means-tested provision: help depended on proving low income.

Key concept
- [ ] K1 Equality of opportunity: access to education, health care and welfare should not depend on wealth, class or place of residence (also in keyTerms).

Education before the reforms
- [ ] E1 Before the 1960s: most children left school at 14 with only primary education; secondary schools charged fees and were mostly run by religious orders; vocational schools (Vocational Education Act 1930) were run by local committees and had lower status.
- [ ] E2 No state policy for expanding education; the Department of Education as passive administrator; the economic need for skills after 1958.
- [ ] E3 Investment in Education (1965): commissioned under Hillery with OECD help; findings (class and regional differences, teacher and skills shortages); argued education was an investment in growth; recommendations.

Reforms of the 1960s
- [ ] E4 Hillery and comprehensive schools: announced 1963; academic and practical subjects, all abilities, state-funded; first opened 1966 (Carraroe, Cootehill).
- [ ] E5 O'Malley and free secondary education: becomes minister July 1966; announcement 10 September 1966; start September 1967; fees abolished in schools that joined.
- [ ] E6 Free school transport (1967) and its effect on rural pupils; rising enrolments.
- [ ] E7 Other changes: Primary Certificate abolished 1967; school-leaving age raised from 14 to 15 in 1972.
- [ ] E8 Community schools from the early 1970s (merged secondary and vocational schools; adult use).
- [ ] E9 Regional Technical Colleges from 1970.
- [ ] E10 Third level: Higher Education Authority (1968), higher education grants scheme (1968), NIHE Limerick (1972); growth in student numbers, more women and more rural and working-class students.
- [ ] E11 The new primary curriculum, 1971: child-centred; activity learning; retraining and difficulties.
- [ ] E12 Hillery and O'Malley compared: Hillery the planner, O'Malley the political breakthrough (one block, every point in section 2).
- [ ] E13 Pressures and limits: church control of management, overcrowding and teacher shortages, academic vs vocational status, remaining costs, regional gaps.

Health
- [ ] H1 The Health Act 1953: replaced the Mother and Child Scheme (the crisis itself belongs elsewhere) with means-tested maternity and child services; eligibility for hospital care by income.
- [ ] H2 Dispensary system and medical cards: rural primary care; free GP care for lower-income card holders.
- [ ] H3 The Voluntary Health Insurance Board, 1957: private insurance for those above the income limit.
- [ ] H4 The Fitzgerald Report (1968): fewer, larger regional hospitals; local resistance.
- [ ] H5 The Health Act 1970: eight regional health boards (working from 1971) replacing county authorities.
- [ ] H6 Comhairle na nOspidéal (1972): consultant appointments and hospital structure.
- [ ] H7 Hospital building: the Hospitals Sweepstake funding; Beaumont Hospital's long delays.

Social welfare
- [ ] W1 Children's allowance (1944): extended in later decades.
- [ ] W2 The Social Welfare Act 1952: consolidation of insurance schemes.
- [ ] W3 Contributory old age pension (1961); free travel (1967); electricity allowance (1968).
- [ ] W4 New benefits for women raising children alone: deserted wife's benefit (1970), unmarried mother's allowance (1973).
- [ ] W5 Pay-related benefit (1974).
- [ ] W6 Rising welfare spending in the 1970s with unemployment (point to the notes on the economic challenges of the 1970s and 1980s).

Housing and amenities
- [ ] G1 Local authority building from the 1950s: replacing tenements and slums; new estates at the city edges.
- [ ] G2 The Housing Act 1966: tenant purchase and home ownership.
- [ ] G3 Ballymun (1966–69): flats and seven towers; problems; demolition and rebuilding.
- [ ] G4 Suburban growth: new towns planned for Tallaght, Clondalkin and Blanchardstown; growth around Dublin, Cork and Limerick.
- [ ] G5 Rural electrification (from 1946, nearly complete by the mid-1970s) and group water schemes (grants from the 1960s).
- [ ] G6 Effects on daily life, especially for women, and on rural population.

Assessment (paired judgement points, final block)
- [ ] S1 Achievements: wider access to second level; a national health board structure; welfare reached more families, including lone mothers.
- [ ] S2 Limits: class gaps persisted; two-tier health care (VHI, medical cards, public wards); Ballymun and similar schemes created new problems.
- [ ] S3 Contemporary view: welcomed by parents, teachers and unions; churches accepted expansion but kept control.
- [ ] S4 Later view: driven by economic need as much as by social ideals; the middle classes benefited first; yet a major change of direction.

## 4. What exams ask

- Higher 100-mark essay on developments in education, health, housing and welfare (past papers also combine education with women, language or broadcasting): marks go to a clear judgement (how far did the state change, and for whom), with dated points. A list of reforms without assessment scores poorly.
- Education is the commonest and best-rewarded topic: students should give Investment in Education (cause and findings), the 1966 announcement and 1967 start, free transport, comprehensive and community schools, and third-level changes, each with a date.
- Strong answers link cause and result: economic planning created the need for skills, so the report led to the reforms; the reforms widened access but class and regional gaps remained.
- Health and welfare points earn marks when dated and named (Health Act 1953, VHI 1957, health boards 1970-71, children's allowance, unmarried mother's allowance 1973); a single sentence on each is not enough for a full essay.
- Ordinary Level 30-mark paragraphs ask for a short account of education before and after the reforms: the age of leaving, fees, Investment in Education, O'Malley's announcement, free transport, comprehensive schools; and separately health and welfare (1953 Act, VHI, health boards). Each correct dated statement earns marks.
- Short answers also ask for one cause, one result or one named person (who commissioned the report, who announced free education).
- Assessment of achievements against limits, with at least one limit (costs, church control, class gaps, regional gaps, Ballymun), is rewarded as paired points.
