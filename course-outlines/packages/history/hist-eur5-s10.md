# Package: hist-eur5-s10 — Culture and religion: indigenous and colonial culture, Islam and Christianity, English as a world language and post-colonial literature

- **Syllabus:** Later Modern Europe, Topic 5 (European retreat from empire and the aftermath, 1945–1990), Culture and religion: "tensions between indigenous culture and colonial culture; the spread of Islam and Christianity in Africa; the Islamic faith in Europe; English as a world language; post-colonial literature." Outline scope: colonial education and the "civilising mission"; négritude (Senghor) and cultural nationalism; missions and African churches; the spread of Islam in West and East Africa; Islam in Europe (mosques, communities in Britain and France, the Rushdie affair 1989); English as a world language (colonial legacy, the Commonwealth, American power, media); post-colonial literature: Achebe (Things Fall Apart 1958, the language debate), Gordimer (apartheid South Africa in her novels, censorship, the Nobel prize 1991), Ngũgĩ, Soyinka. Key personalities: Nadine Gordimer; Chinua Achebe. Key concepts: identity; cultural diversity; assimilation. Write in full Higher Level detail.

## 1. Must cover (one bullet or block for every item; tick each off)

Background (assumed)
- [ ] A1 (assumed) Colonial rule in Africa: European powers (Britain, France, Belgium, Portugal) governed most of the continent from the late 19th century; independence came mostly 1956–1975.
- [ ] A2 (assumed) British indirect rule (through local rulers) versus French assimilation (making subjects French in language and culture); Belgian rule relied heavily on missions.
- [ ] A3 (assumed) Apartheid: South Africa's system of legal racial segregation from 1948 (needed to place Gordimer).
- [ ] A4 (assumed) The Biafran War (1967–70) followed the Nigerian coups of 1966 and Igbo secession (needed to place Achebe).

Key concepts (each defined as used in this topic; also in keyTerms)
- [ ] K1 Identity: a person's or community's sense of who they are, shaped by language, religion, ethnicity and history, and contested under colonial rule.
- [ ] K2 Cultural diversity: many languages, religions and traditions within one society, which post-colonial states and European states with migrant communities had to manage.
- [ ] K3 Assimilation: the expectation that subjects adopt the colonial power's language and culture (French policy most fully).
- [ ] K4 Civilising mission (essential term): the European claim that rule was justified by bringing Christianity, education and European values.
- [ ] K5 Négritude (essential term).
- [ ] K6 Post-colonial literature (essential term): writing from former colonies, in European or local languages, on colonial rule, identity and its aftermath.
- [ ] K7 Other essential terms: Sufi brotherhood, censorship, African Independent Churches, fatwa, lingua franca.

Colonial education and the civilising mission
- [ ] E1 The civilising mission and the French version (mission civilisatrice); education as the main tool of cultural change.
- [ ] E2 Mission schools in British and Belgian Africa: literacy, scripture, European history; African languages and customs discouraged.
- [ ] E3 French education: a small elite trained in French (Lycée Faidherbe, École William Ponty); assimilation.
- [ ] E4 British colonies: clerks, teachers and interpreters; girls' education lagged.
- [ ] E5 Curricula centred on Europe; African history and literature largely absent until after independence; the tension between indigenous and colonial culture.

Négritude and cultural nationalism
- [ ] N1 Founders (Senghor, Césaire, Damas), Paris in the 1930s; the journal L'Étudiant noir (1934).
- [ ] N2 Ideas: pride in black and African values; rejection of the claim that European culture was superior.
- [ ] N3 Césaire's Cahier d'un retour au pays natal (1939); Senghor's poetry.
- [ ] N4 Senghor as politician: president of Senegal 1960–80; Académie française 1983; African socialism; close ties with France.
- [ ] N5 Criticism (Soyinka, Mphahlele): too tied to European thought; romanticised Africa.

Christianity, Islam and indigenous religion in Africa
- [ ] R1 Christian missions (Catholic and Protestant) as agents of expansion: schools, hospitals, churches; examples (White Fathers, Church Missionary Society, Irish orders).
- [ ] R2 African leadership in the Church: Crowther (1864); African Independent Churches (Kimbanguist Church, Aladura churches).
- [ ] R3 Islam in West Africa: trade, Sufi brotherhoods, the Mouride brotherhood and Amadou Bamba; the Sokoto Caliphate.
- [ ] R4 Islam on the East African coast and Swahili culture.
- [ ] R5 Northern Nigeria: indirect rule through the emirs, missions kept out, uneven spread of western education north and south.
- [ ] R6 Syncretism: many Africans combined Christian or Muslim belief with older practice; religion and political authority varied.

Islam in Europe
- [ ] I1 The Grand Mosque of Paris (1926).
- [ ] I2 Muslim migration to Britain and France from the 1950s–60s; mosques and cultural centres; France's Muslim community mainly of North African origin.
- [ ] I3 The Rushdie affair: The Satanic Verses (September 1988), Bradford book burning (January 1989), Khomeini's fatwa (14 February 1989), Rushdie in hiding.
- [ ] I4 What the affair raised: freedom of expression versus religious identity; Britain's blasphemy law covering only Christianity (1991 court ruling); link to the French headscarf debate (point to the notes on race relations in France).

English as a world language
- [ ] L1 Spread through empire and Commonwealth; official language in Nigeria and Kenya; English as the language of government, courts and education in Nigeria.
- [ ] L2 After 1945: American economic and cultural power, media, business, international organisations.
- [ ] L3 Pidgin English as a lingua franca (West Africa).
- [ ] L4 Alternatives: Tanzania's choice of Swahili; India (Hindi and English).
- [ ] L5 French equivalent: La Francophonie (1970).
- [ ] L6 The language question for African writers.

Key personality: Chinua Achebe (one block, every point)
- [ ] P1 Who he was: born 1930, Ogidi; Ibadan; Nigerian Broadcasting Corporation.
- [ ] P2 What he did, with dates: Things Fall Apart (1958) and what it shows; later novels (1960, 1964, 1966, 1987).
- [ ] P3 Shaped by events: the Biafran War; disillusion with independent Nigeria.
- [ ] P4 Participant role: Biafra's advocate; first editorial adviser of the African Writers Series (1962).
- [ ] P5 The language debate: Obi Wali versus Achebe's reply (English remade for African experience).
- [ ] P6 The Heart of Darkness lecture (1975).
- [ ] P7 Contemporaries and historians: praised as the founder of modern African fiction; criticised for writing in English; studied as a foundational post-colonial text. Death 2013.

Key personality: Nadine Gordimer (one block, every point)
- [ ] G1 Who she was: born 1923, Springs; white, English-language, Johannesburg.
- [ ] G2 Novels with dates: The Lying Days (1953), A World of Strangers (1958), The Conservationist (1974), Burger's Daughter (1979), July's People (1981), My Son's Story (1990).
- [ ] G3 Censorship: bans on A World of Strangers and Burger's Daughter.
- [ ] G4 Political involvement: ANC supporter; Mandela's Rivonia speech (1964).
- [ ] G5 Honours: Booker Prize 1974 (shared); Nobel Prize 1991; death 2014.
- [ ] G6 Contemporaries and critics: seen as an opponent of apartheid; some black writers saw white liberal perspectives as limited.

Post-colonial literature beyond Achebe and Gordimer
- [ ] W1 Ngũgĩ wa Thiong'o: English early work, 1977–78 detention after Gikuyu play, switch to Gikuyu, Decolonising the Mind (1986).
- [ ] W2 Wole Soyinka: Nobel 1986, first African; detention in the civil war; The Interpreters (1965).
- [ ] W3 African Writers Series (Heinemann, 1962).
- [ ] W4 Themes: tradition versus new faiths, village versus city, moral failure of the independence generation.
- [ ] W5 French-language writers: Cheikh Hamidou Kane (1961), Mariama Bâ (1979).

Assessment (paired judgement points, final block)
- [ ] S1 Achievement: new cultural forms combining African, European and Islamic influences; African literature won a world audience.
- [ ] S2 Limit: colonial education reduced African languages; dependence on English or French in higher education.
- [ ] S3 Achievement: Tanzania showed an African language could run government and education.
- [ ] S4 Limit: the language debate (Achebe versus Ngũgĩ) shows an unresolved tension between identity and colonial legacy.
- [ ] S5 Contemporary view (officials and missionaries saw African culture as backward; nationalists argued for revival) versus later view (Achebe and Ngũgĩ on language).

## 3. Traps

## 4. What exams ask

- Higher 100-mark essay, the commonest form: how far did religion shape Africa and Europe, or the impact of Islam in Africa and Europe. Marks go to a stated judgement, balanced between Africa (Christianity and Islam reshaped by African societies, education, politics, identity) and Europe (mosques, Rushdie affair, freedom of expression versus religious feeling), each with dated examples.
- Higher essay on the legacy of colonialism in language and literature: valuable but not unqualified. Credit for naming English's reach and a world audience for African writers, set against the loss of African languages and the Achebe-Ngũgĩ disagreement.
- Higher essay on Gordimer and/or Achebe (often with a third personality): what each wrote, when, what it showed about colonial or apartheid society, and how far they mattered. Link each novel to a point about identity, damage from colonialism or moral struggle; naming one specific title and date per point earns more than general praise.
- Compare the two writers: Achebe (colonial arrival, then disillusion with independence) versus Gordimer (apartheid, white liberal viewpoint, censorship); both used English, both gained world audiences.
- Ordinary Level 30/40-mark paragraph on Achebe or Gordimer: born when and where, main works with dates, what they were about, honours, one critic or reaction. Each correct, dated statement earns marks, so open with birth date and place and finish with the prizes.
- Short answers on Islam and Christianity in Africa: how each spread (trade and Sufi brotherhoods; missions and schools), one named example each, and how Africans reshaped them (independent churches, Mouride brotherhood).
- Short answers on the Rushdie affair: what the book was, the Bradford protest, the fatwa and why it mattered in Britain.
- Short answers on English as a world language: empire and Commonwealth, American power after 1945, and the Tanzanian alternative.
- Pairing: examiners reward achievements versus limits and contemporary versus later views; naming Achebe's and Ngũgĩ's differing positions on language is a ready contrast.
