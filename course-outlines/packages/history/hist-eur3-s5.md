## 1. Must cover (one bullet or block for every item; tick each off)

Background (assumed knowledge)
- [ ] A1 (assumed) Stalin's position by 1934: General Secretary from 1922; in the succession struggle he defeated Trotsky (expelled from the party 1927, deported 1929), Zinoviev and Kamenev (the Left / United Opposition) and Bukharin, Rykov and Tomsky (the Right, 1928–29). One or two bullets; the struggle itself belongs to the Communism in Russia notes.
- [ ] A2 (assumed) The First Five-Year Plan and collectivisation, and their costs: shortages, accidents, missed targets and the famine of 1932–33. One bullet.
- [ ] A3 (assumed) The secret police line: Cheka, then OGPU, absorbed into the NKVD in 1934; the Gulag camp system from 1930. One bullet.

Why Stalin purged (causes)
- [ ] W1 Stalin's suspicion (often called paranoia) and his wish for unchallenged personal power.
- [ ] W2 Rivals: old Bolsheviks such as Zinoviev, Kamenev and Bukharin kept prestige from 1917 and Lenin's time; with Trotsky abroad they could be presented as an alternative leadership.
- [ ] W3 Scapegoats for economic failure: managers, engineers and officials accused of "wrecking" and sabotage.
- [ ] W4 Fear of war and of a "fifth column" of traitors as Nazi Germany and Japan grew stronger.
- [ ] W5 The pattern set by earlier show trials: Shakhty (1928), the Industrial Party (1930), Metropolitan-Vickers (1933).
- [ ] W6 Signs of discontent inside the party: the Ryutin platform (1932); the 17th Party Congress (1934), the "Congress of Victors".
- [ ] W7 Party purges (chistka) of 1933 and the check of party documents of 1935: expulsions, not yet executions.
- [ ] W8 Historians' debate on motive: Stalin's central direction vs pressure and initiative from below and real fears (see section 2).

The Kirov murder, 1934–1935
- [ ] K1 Sergei Kirov: who he was; shot 1 December 1934 at the Smolny, Leningrad, by Leonid Nikolaev.
- [ ] K2 The decree of 1 December 1934 on terrorism cases (fast investigation, no defence, no appeal, immediate execution).
- [ ] K3 Blame moved from "white guards" to Zinoviev's supporters; Nikolaev shot (December 1934); Zinoviev and Kamenev tried in secret and imprisoned (January 1935).
- [ ] K4 The debate on whether Stalin arranged the murder; no proof found.
- [ ] K5 Stalin used the murder as a pretext: a supposed network of conspirators justified the trials.

The three Moscow show trials
- [ ] T1 First trial, 19–24 August 1936 ("Trotskyite-Zinovievite Terrorist Centre"): sixteen defendants led by Zinoviev and Kamenev; charges (terrorist centre directed by Trotsky, Kirov's murder, plots to kill Stalin and others).
- [ ] T2 Confessions after months of pressure and a reported promise to spare their lives; all sixteen sentenced to death and shot on 25 August 1936.
- [ ] T3 Trotsky and his son Lev Sedov condemned in their absence; Tomsky's suicide (22 August 1936); mass arrests of former oppositionists followed.
- [ ] T4 Second trial, 23–30 January 1937 ("Anti-Soviet Trotskyite Centre"): seventeen defendants including Pyatakov and Radek; charges moved to industrial wrecking, railway accidents and plotting with Trotsky, Germany and Japan, explaining the failures of the plans.
- [ ] T5 The Oslo flight inconsistency; sentences (thirteen shot; Radek and Sokolnikov ten years; two others eight years); Radek's death in prison (1939).
- [ ] T6 Ordzhonikidze's death (February 1937), Pyatakov's superior.
- [ ] T7 Yagoda replaced by Yezhov as head of the NKVD (September 1936); Yagoda himself later a defendant.
- [ ] T8 Third trial, 2–13 March 1938 ("Bloc of Rights and Trotskyites"): twenty-one defendants including Bukharin, Rykov, Yagoda and Krestinsky; charges (treason, spying, wrecking, plans to break up the USSR, murder plots including against Lenin in 1918, and the deaths of Gorky and others by "medical murder").
- [ ] T9 Krestinsky's retraction and reversal; Bukharin's partial defence; eighteen shot on 15 March 1938, three imprisoned.

How the trials were staged and confessions obtained
- [ ] S1 Andrei Vyshinsky as prosecutor (former Menshevik); confession treated as enough proof; his demands for death sentences.
- [ ] S2 Vasily Ulrikh as presiding judge; Stalin's personal oversight of charges and testimony.
- [ ] S3 Methods: sleep deprivation, endless interrogation ("the conveyor"), threats to families, false promises of leniency, and from 1937 torture approved by Stalin.
- [ ] S4 Why loyal communists confessed: the argument that confession served the party; rehearsed testimony.
- [ ] S5 Public staging: the House of Unions in Moscow, invited audience, foreign journalists and diplomats present, transcripts published in several languages; Soviet newspapers and meetings demanding death for "enemies of the people" before the verdicts.
- [ ] S6 Weaknesses in the evidence noticed abroad: the Oslo flight, the Hotel Bristol in Copenhagen (first trial), near-identical confessions, no documentary evidence.

The military purge, 1937–1938
- [ ] M1 Marshal Tukhachevsky: arrested May 1937; secret military tribunal 11 June 1937; shot the next day; fabricated charges of treason and spying for Germany.
- [ ] M2 Scale: three of the five marshals; most army commanders; tens of thousands of officers dismissed, many arrested, some later reinstated; political commissars' control restored.
- [ ] M3 Effects and debate: weak leadership in the Winter War (1939–40) and in June 1941; historians debate how far the purge explains 1941. Tukhachevsky rehabilitated 1957.

The wider Great Terror, 1937–1938
- [ ] G1 Yezhov and the "Yezhovshchina" (1937–38); the NKVD's role.
- [ ] G2 NKVD Order 00447 (30 July 1937): regional quotas, two categories (shoot / camp), local requests to raise the quotas.
- [ ] G3 The national operations against groups with foreign links (Poles, Germans, Latvians and others); the Polish operation the largest.
- [ ] G4 Numbers from the NKVD archives (see section 2), with the caution that camp deaths add to the total.
- [ ] G5 Ordinary victims: workers, peasants, minorities as well as officials; denunciations of "enemies of the people"; families of the arrested stigmatised and often arrested.
- [ ] G6 The party itself: the fate of the 1934 Congress delegates and Central Committee, as given by Khrushchev in 1956.
- [ ] G7 The Gulag: well over a million prisoners by 1939; forced labour on the economy's projects.
- [ ] G8 The end of the mass phase: Yezhov removed November 1938, replaced by Beria; Yezhov arrested 1939 and shot 1940.

Foreign reactions
- [ ] F1 Supporters and believers: Joseph Davies (US ambassador), Lion Feuchtwanger; Western communist parties; Soviet diplomats abroad defending the trials.
- [ ] F2 Sceptics: many diplomats and journalists, liberals and socialists who saw frame-ups; some communists left their parties.
- [ ] F3 The Dewey Commission (1937): hearings with Trotsky in Mexico, verdict that the charges were false.
- [ ] F4 Damage to the Soviet Union's reputation among Western liberals and the left, at the time of the Popular Fronts against fascism.
- [ ] F5 Trotsky's murder in Mexico (August 1940) by an NKVD agent: the end of the campaign against him.

Impact on Soviet Russia (the 2024 DBQ part (d) topic)
- [ ] I1 The party: the old Bolshevik leadership destroyed; a new generation promoted who owed everything to Stalin; no organised opposition left.
- [ ] I2 The army: loss of experienced commanders; caution and fear in the officer corps.
- [ ] I3 The economy: loss of managers, engineers and planners; fear of "wrecking" charges made officials cautious, avoid decisions or falsify results; Gulag labour used.
- [ ] I4 Society: fear, denunciation, broken families, silence; the relatives of "enemies of the people" stigmatised.
- [ ] I5 Stalin's personal power made absolute; history rewritten (the Short Course, 1938) to make the old Bolsheviks traitors from the start and Stalin Lenin's true heir.
- [ ] I6 Later: Khrushchev's secret speech (1956) and rehabilitations (Tukhachevsky 1957, Bukharin 1988).

Key concepts and terms (keyTerms)
- [ ] C1 Totalitarianism, as shown by the trials (courts, press and police under one man's control; leading communists made to confess to invented crimes).
- [ ] C2 Propaganda, as shown by the trials (published transcripts, press campaigns, mass meetings, confessions explaining shortages and accidents).
- [ ] C3 Personality cult, as shown by the trials (Stalin as the vigilant protector and Lenin's true heir; the old Bolsheviks written out of the revolution).
- [ ] C4 Other terms: show trial; purge; chistka; Great Terror; Yezhovshchina; NKVD; Gulag; Old Bolsheviks; wrecking; "enemy of the people"; Order 00447; Dewey Commission; rehabilitation.

Assessment (end with this block, headed "Assessment: …")
- [ ] J1 Achievement for Stalin (total control, rivals gone, loyal new elite) vs cost to the state (army, economy and party weakened before 1941).
- [ ] J2 Contemporary views: supportive (Soviet press, foreign communists, Davies, Feuchtwanger) vs critical (Dewey Commission, many diplomats, liberals and socialists).
- [ ] J3 Later views: after the archives opened, historians agree the trials were fabrications; they still debate central planning by Stalin (Conquest, Khlevniuk) vs chaos and local initiative (Getty), and paranoia vs calculation.
- [ ] J4 The link to the economy: the trials as an explanation of the plans' failures (wrecking), which exams pair with Stalin's economic policy.

## 3. Traps

Do not use (doubtful or wrong in the current notes):
- Do not use "held in the Hall of Columns" (current notes on the first trial): the hall used for each trial is reported inconsistently. Say "the House of Unions in Moscow".
- Do not use "most of its delegates did not survive the Terror" about the 1934 Congress: Khrushchev's figure is for arrests (1,108 of 1,966), not deaths. Use G6.
- Do not use "about 1.6 million arrests" as a single exact figure: archive totals differ by count. Use "about 1.5 to 1.6 million" (G4).
- Do not use "the decree ordered that terrorism cases be heard in days": the decree set ten days for investigation. Use K2.
- Do not use "in December reported that the charges were false; its report was titled Not Guilty" as one event: the verdict was announced December 1937, the book Not Guilty published 1938. Use F3.
- Do not use "Ordzhonikidze shot himself" as plain fact: say "officially heart failure; generally believed to have shot himself" (T6).
- Do not use "Stalin promised that their lives would be spared" without "reportedly": the source is later testimony, not a document.
- Do not use "roughly 30,000 to 35,000 officers were dismissed or arrested" as if all were arrested: they were dismissed; many but not all were arrested and some were reinstated. Use M2.
- Do not use "Tukhachevsky, Deputy Defence Commissar, was arrested": he had been demoted shortly before his arrest. Say "former First Deputy Commissar for Defence".

Doubtful claims to avoid:
- Do not state as fact that Stalin arranged Kirov's murder. Present it as a debate (K4).
- Do not say that Kirov won more votes than Stalin in the 1934 Central Committee election, or that delegates struck Stalin's name off: the story is disputed.
- Do not give total death figures for the Stalin era, Conquest's older estimates, or any Gulag death total: none are checked here.
- Do not give numbers for corps or divisional commanders lost, or for the national operations.
- Do not invent quotations from the confessions, from Vyshinsky beyond "mad dogs", from Bukharin's last plea or letters, or from Davies's reports. Paraphrase.
- Do not say all defendants were tortured: methods varied and the evidence for physical torture is strongest from 1937.

Confusables:
- Zinoviev and Kamenev (first trial, August 1936) vs Pyatakov and Radek (second, January 1937) vs Bukharin, Rykov, Yagoda and Krestinsky (third, March 1938). The military purge (Tukhachevsky, June 1937) was a secret tribunal, not a public show trial.
- Yagoda (NKVD head 1934–36, defendant 1938) vs Yezhov (NKVD head 1936–38, shot 1940) vs Beria (from November 1938).
- Rykov (former head of government, third trial) vs Radek (journalist, second trial, prison).
- Tomsky (trade union leader, suicide August 1936) vs Tukhachevsky (marshal, shot June 1937).
- A purge (expulsion or removal, chistka) vs a show trial (public scripted trial) vs the Great Terror (the whole mass campaign of 1937–38).
- Vyshinsky (prosecutor) vs Ulrikh (judge).
- Stalin's purges vs Hitler's Night of the Long Knives (1934): different regimes; do not mix them up in comparison answers.
- The Kirov decree of 1 December 1934 vs Order 00447 of 30 July 1937.

Stalin's Key personality block belongs there; do not write one here.

## 4. What exams ask (topics only)

- Documents question (Higher and Ordinary, 2024): comprehension of sources by visitors and officials about the trials; comparing two documents (do both show concern or doubt?); judging a source as primary or secondary and why (eyewitness at the trial, written at the time); spotting propaganda in a Soviet official's account (blaming foreign enemies, using the Kirov murder as "proof"). Marks come from quoting or citing the document for each point, so the notes must make clear who was present at the trials (journalists, diplomats, invited Soviet audience) and who defended them abroad.
- Documents part (d) (40 marks Higher, 20 Ordinary): the impact of the trials on Soviet Russia. Marks reward separate points on the party (old guard gone, new loyal elite), the army (purged commanders, 1941), the economy (lost specialists, fear of wrecking charges) and society (fear, denunciations, families), each with a dated fact, ending in a judgement.
- Ordinary Level paragraph (40 marks) on the show trials: why Stalin held them (rivals, scapegoats, Kirov pretext), what happened (the three trials with dates and named defendants, confessions, executions) and results. Each accurate dated fact counts; Kirov's date and the 1936 defendants are the most often rewarded.
- Ordinary Level document (6-mark parts): who was tried, what they were charged with (plots to kill Stalin, spying), how they pleaded (guilty, confessed), who attended (foreign journalists and diplomats), whether a writer's claim was certain or hearsay, and why Stalin held trials.
- Higher Level essay (100 marks) on Stalin's economy and/or show trials: link the two. The trials explained economic failures as wrecking; the purge of managers harmed the economy. Marks reward a clear judgement and dated evidence on both halves, not two separate narratives.
- Higher Level essay on Stalin's leadership or rule: the trials and Terror as evidence of ruthlessness and of totalitarian control; weigh against the cost to the army and economy before 1941.
- Higher Level essays on propaganda and terror in the dictatorships: the trials combine both (public confessions as propaganda, the NKVD as terror).
- Judgement material examiners reward: causes ranked (power and rivals vs scapegoating vs fear of war); success for Stalin vs damage to the state; contemporary belief vs scepticism; later historians' debate (central plan vs pressure from below).
- Precision: give the three trials with their dates and principal defendants; name Vyshinsky as prosecutor; date Kirov's murder; keep the military purge separate from the public trials; give Terror numbers only as "about".
