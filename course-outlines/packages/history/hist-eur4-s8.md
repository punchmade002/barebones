## 1. Must cover (one bullet or block for every item; tick each off)

Background (assumed)
- [ ] A1 (assumed) After 1945 the Soviet Union dominated eastern Europe; communist parties took power there in 1945–48 (the political takeover belongs to the Sovietisation notes).
- [ ] A2 (assumed) Capitalism (private ownership, markets, profit) versus communism (public ownership, state direction) as the economic rivalry of the Cold War.
- [ ] A3 (assumed) The Marshall Plan (1947) and the division of Europe; the Soviet bloc refused Marshall aid.
- [ ] A4 (assumed) Stalin died in 1953; Khrushchev led the USSR 1953–64, Brezhnev 1964–82, Gorbachev 1985–91.

Key concepts (each defined as used in this topic; also in keyTerms)
- [ ] K1 Communism: one-party rule with a publicly owned, centrally planned economy.
- [ ] K2 Sovietisation: remodelling eastern European states, economies included, on the Soviet pattern.
- [ ] K3 Central planning, five-year plan and Gosplan (essential terms).
- [ ] K4 Collectivisation; Comecon; second economy; stagnation; goulash communism; perestroika (essential terms).

The Soviet planning model and its export
- [ ] P1 How planning worked: Gosplan, five-year plans, output targets, state-set prices, no market signals.
- [ ] P2 Priority to heavy industry (steel, coal, machinery, armaments); consumer goods and housing neglected.
- [ ] P3 The Soviet origins: first Five-Year Plan from 1928; forced collectivisation in the early 1930s; the famine of 1932–33; the human cost.
- [ ] P4 Stakhanovite campaigns: pressing workers to exceed norms (Soviet origin, copied in the bloc).
- [ ] P5 Export to eastern Europe: nationalisation of industry, banks and large estates 1945–48; Soviet-style plans from about 1949–51.
- [ ] P6 Showpiece new industrial towns (Nowa Huta, Stalinstadt).
- [ ] P7 Collectivisation in the satellites; Poland the major exception (most farms stayed private).
- [ ] P8 Comecon (January 1949): aims, response to the Marshall Plan, how it worked (trade with the USSR, planner-set prices, non-convertible currencies), dependence on Soviet raw materials and energy.
- [ ] P9 Soviet extraction in the early years: reparations, joint companies, trade turned from West to USSR.

Problems: shortages, inefficiency and low innovation
- [ ] Q1 Gross-output targets: quantity over quality; heavy, shoddy goods.
- [ ] Q2 Shortages, queues, black markets; consumer goods, meat, housing, cars.
- [ ] Q3 State-fixed prices: shortages in shops alongside unsold goods in warehouses.
- [ ] Q4 Low innovation: no incentive for managers to adopt technology or take risks.
- [ ] Q5 Poor Soviet agriculture; grain imports from the 1960s.
- [ ] Q6 Housing: standard blocks, overcrowding; the long wait for a Trabant.
- [ ] Q7 Environmental damage (northern Bohemia, Upper Silesia, Aral Sea).
- [ ] Q8 Extensive rather than intensive growth: when extra labour and raw materials ran out, growth slowed.
- [ ] Q9 The arms burden: military spending took a large share of Soviet output at the cost of consumers and investment.

Reform attempts
- [ ] R1 Khrushchev 1957: regional economic councils replaced ministries; reversed after his fall.
- [ ] R2 Khrushchev's consumer and housing drive and his boast of overtaking the USA; targets missed.
- [ ] R3 Kosygin reform (September 1965): more enterprise freedom over profits and pricing; central control of investment stayed; reform faded.
- [ ] R4 Czechoslovakia's New Economic Model (1960s, Ota Šik); ended with the August 1968 invasion.
- [ ] R5 Hungary's New Economic Mechanism (1968): limited market prices, private services.
- [ ] R6 Goulash communism under Kádár: small private enterprise, more consumer goods; the second economy; state still held main industries and prices.
- [ ] R7 Yugoslavia: outside Comecon, self-management and market socialism, travel and work abroad; debt and inflation in the 1980s.
- [ ] R8 Why reforms stopped: fear of losing party and ministry control; the 1968 invasion as a warning.

The Brezhnev era and stagnation
- [ ] B1 Brezhnev 1964–82; slowing growth in the 1970s; "stagnation".
- [ ] B2 Oil and gas revenues after 1973 and 1979 let leaders postpone reform and buy Western grain and technology; the support vanished when prices fell in the mid-1980s.
- [ ] B3 Rough nuclear parity and heavy military spending; Afghanistan (from December 1979).
- [ ] B4 Ageing leadership, corruption, party loyalty rewarded over performance.
- [ ] B5 Everyday life: shortages, black market, bribery, blat.

Poland: price rises, protest and Western debt
- [ ] L1 December 1970: food price rises, protests on the Baltic coast, Gomułka replaced by Gierek.
- [ ] L2 Gierek's strategy: borrow from the West to import technology and consumer goods.
- [ ] L3 June 1976: price rises, strikes, withdrawal.
- [ ] L4 Why the strategy failed: goods not competitive, debt unpayable, agriculture starved of investment, shortages.
- [ ] L5 Summer 1980: price rises, strikes, Gdańsk shipyard, Solidarity founded.
- [ ] L6 Link forward: economic collapse in the 1980s and the Round Table talks of 1989.

Gorbachev, the East–West gap and the end of the system
- [ ] G1 Perestroika (1986): restructuring; falling oil prices in 1986 cut hard-currency earnings.
- [ ] G2 Law on State Enterprise (1987) and law on cooperatives (1988): loosened control without creating a market.
- [ ] G3 Soviet economy 1989–90: shortages, deficit, rationing in some cities, falling output.
- [ ] G4 Romania: Ceaușescu repaid foreign debt by austerity and cuts to imports; shortages of food, heat and electricity.
- [ ] G5 East Germany: strongest bloc economy but dependent on West German credit (the 1983 credit); 1990 monetary union exposed its industry.
- [ ] G6 The East–West gap by 1989: living standards, TV and travel made it visible.
- [ ] G7 Legacy: obsolete industry, pollution, unemployment for the successor states.

The satellite economies compared
- [ ] C1 Czechoslovakia: industrialised before the war, high living standards in the 1950s, stagnation under rigid planning.
- [ ] C2 East Germany: advanced, showcase, lost skilled workers until the Wall (1961).
- [ ] C3 Bulgaria and Romania: agrarian in 1945, rapid industrialisation.
- [ ] C4 Hungary and Poland: more flexible, but dependent on Western credit.

Assessment (paired judgement points, final block)
- [ ] S1 Achievements: rapid industrialisation, literacy, free health care and education, full employment including women, cheap housing and basic goods.
- [ ] S2 Limits: shortages, poor quality, environmental damage, falling behind the West.
- [ ] S3 Contemporary views: Western stress on failure of planning; bloc officials on social gains.
- [ ] S4 Later historians: real success in mobilising resources, but structural limits; the system could build basic industry but not adapt to an information economy.
- [ ] S5 Comparison: reform within the bloc was partial; economic failure was one cause, not the only cause, of 1989.

## 3. Traps

Do not use (wrong or doubtful in the current notes)
- Do not use: "consumer goods were rationed in the early 1950s" as a general bloc statement, because rationing differed by country and date; say only that shortages were common.
- Do not use: "the government withdrew most of the increases within days" for June 1976, because the increases were withdrawn the next day.
- Do not use: the claim that East Germany was "the most productive economy of the bloc" as flat fact; say "among the most advanced" or "the strongest industrial economy in the bloc, by common assessment".
- Do not use: Czechoslovakia's living standards were "the highest in the bloc in the 1950s" as flat fact; say "among the highest".
- Do not use: that Kosygin's reform was "abandoned within a few years because ministries and the party feared the loss of control" as settled fact; say it was watered down by the late 1960s and that fear of losing control is a common explanation.
- Do not use: "Yugoslavia outside Comecon" as a bare statement without the date; it left Soviet alignment in 1948 and was never a full member.
- Do not use: a figure for the arms burden (for example a percentage of Soviet output), because estimates vary widely; say "a large share".
- Do not use: "Trabant" waiting times in years, or Polish debt servicing percentages, or counts of Romanian villages.

## 4. What exams ask

- Higher 100-mark essay (2013 style): what economic challenges did communist governments face, 1945–92, and how did they respond. Marks go to a thematic answer: rebuilding, feeding and supplying people, keeping up with the West, the arms race, and the response (planning, then partial reform, then collapse), with dated examples from several states. A single-country narrative scores lower.
- Essays also use communist economic failure as a cause in questions on why communism collapsed in eastern Europe; the strongest answers link shortages, debt, the East–West gap and the loss of Soviet will to prop up the system.
- Explaining the planning system: Gosplan, five-year plans, gross output targets, priority to heavy industry, neglect of consumer goods and housing. These are the recurring Ordinary Level points: name the body, the type of plan and two effects.
- Explaining effects: shortages, queues, black market, poor quality, low innovation. State each effect together with its cause (for example fixed prices or gross output targets).
- Comparing reforms: Hungary (NEM 1968, goulash communism) against the Soviet Union (Khrushchev, Kosygin); say why Hungary went further and why even Hungary stayed within communism.
- Poland: three price-rise crises with dates and what followed each; Western debt as the reason Gierek's plan failed.
- Judgement: achievements (industrialisation, welfare, full employment) against failures (shortages, environment, falling behind); stating a clear verdict and naming one cause of long-run decline scores above listing.
- Short document questions may give a Soviet or bloc source on planning or consumers: identify the system, the problem described and one effect, using dates from the notes.
