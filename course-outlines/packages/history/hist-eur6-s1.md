## 3. Traps

Do not use (wrong or doubtful facts in the current notes):
- Do not use: Truman's Gallup approval as an exact figure; write "about 22 per cent" or "about a fifth", because sources vary slightly. Give his March 1952 announcement as "March 1952", not a day.
- Do not use: "Brown v. Board used the commerce power" or similar mixing; the Civil Rights Act 1964 used the commerce power, Brown rested on the Fourteenth Amendment (equal protection).
- Do not use: Poindexter and North as "convicted" (their convictions were later overturned or vacated); say "prosecuted".
- Do not use: "Eisenhower kept most New Deal programmes" without saying "accepted"; do not call him a New Dealer.
- Do not use: the Taft-Hartley override as proof that Truman had no power; he campaigned against the Congress that passed it and won in 1948.
- Do not use: Nixon's opening to China or the secret bombing of Cambodia as detailed facts in the assessment; they belong to the foreign policy notes, so mention only in a clause, if at all.
- Do not use: "Reagan's supporters credit him with ending the Cold War" as fact; put it as their view.
- Do not use any quotation not in section 2 (for example "The buck stops here" is Truman's famed desk sign but not needed).

Confusable people, terms and dates
- Fair Deal (Truman, 1949) vs New Deal (Roosevelt) vs New Frontier (Kennedy) vs Great Society (Johnson).
- Civil Rights Acts 1957, 1964 (and the Voting Rights Act 1965); do not merge them.
- Taft-Hartley (1947, veto overridden) vs the Twenty-Second Amendment (passed 1947, ratified 1951).
- The "do-nothing Congress" was the 80th Congress (1947–49), a 1948 campaign label.
- Dixiecrats (right, Thurmond) vs Progressives (left, Wallace) in 1948.
- Watergate burglary (June 1972) vs Nixon's resignation (August 1974) vs the pardon (September 1974).
- Iran hostage crisis (1979–81, Carter) vs Iran-Contra (1986, Reagan).

- National Military Establishment (1947) was renamed the Department of Defense (1949).

## 4. What exams ask

- Higher 100-mark essays on the Presidency (comparing or assessing presidents) recur: "Truman" alone (several years), "Truman or Johnson", the Presidency from Roosevelt to Reagan as a whole, "did presidents act for the good of America", and a Reagan success-and-failure question. Marks go to a stated judgement, balanced achievements vs limits, and dated evidence for each.
- Truman answers do best when they split domestic from foreign record, and then say how far a hostile Congress limited him: name the Taft-Hartley override, the blocked health insurance, and one success (desegregating the army, the 1948 win, the Housing Act).
- For Johnson, strong answers pair the Civil Rights and Voting Rights Acts, Medicare and the War on Poverty against the way Vietnam undermined the programme, with a date for each law.
- For Reagan, pair tax cuts, conservative revival and economic recovery after the recession against deficits, inequality criticism and Iran-Contra; name the Tower Commission.
- "Whose record is better" questions reward a decisive comparison on one criterion at a time (legislation passed, handling of Congress, leaving office with prestige), not two parallel biographies.
- Essays on the growth of presidential power are rewarded for naming the structures (Executive Office, National Security Council, CIA, Council of Economic Advisers), the stages of rise (Roosevelt to Nixon) and the checks that followed Watergate (the Court ruling, War Powers Resolution, congressional oversight).
- Contemporary vs later view: a point that Truman left office unpopular but rose in historians' rankings, and Johnson the reverse, earns assessment marks.
- Ordinary Level paragraphs on Truman (30 and 40 marks) look for: became president April 1945, the 21-point programme and strikes, the Fair Deal (1949), what Congress blocked, one achievement. Each correct dated statement earns marks, so give years.
- Federal-state tensions show up as the Little Rock crisis and the Dixiecrats: give who acted, what they did and what the President did in reply.
