## 1. Must cover (one bullet or block for every item; tick each off)

Background (assumed)
- [ ] A1 (assumed) Germany was unified by Prussia under Bismarck through three wars: against Denmark (1864), Austria (1866) and France (1870–71). Detail belongs to the unification notes.
- [ ] A2 (assumed) Before 1871 the balance of power rested on a weak, divided centre of Europe; the five Great Powers were Britain, France, Russia, Austria-Hungary and Prussia/Germany.
- [ ] A3 (assumed) The Eastern Question: the declining Ottoman Empire in the Balkans, with Russia and Austria-Hungary rivals for influence there, and Britain anxious to keep Russia away from the Straits and the eastern Mediterranean.

Key concept (defined as used in this topic; also in keyTerms)
- [ ] K1 Balance of power: no single state (or bloc) strong enough to dominate Europe; a united Germany upset the old balance, and Bismarck tried to manage the new one in Germany's favour.

The Second Reich: founding and constitution
- [ ] R1 Proclamation of the Empire at Versailles (January 1871); Wilhelm I as German Emperor.
- [ ] R2 A federation of 25 states dominated by Prussia; Alsace-Lorraine as an imperial territory (Reichsland).
- [ ] R3 The Kaiser: King of Prussia, commanded the army, appointed and dismissed the Chancellor, controlled foreign policy.
- [ ] R4 The Chancellor: responsible to the Kaiser, not to the Reichstag; Bismarck also Prussian Minister-President.
- [ ] R5 The Bundesrat: state governments' delegates; Prussia's votes enough to block constitutional change.
- [ ] R6 The Reichstag: universal male suffrage, secret ballot; could pass or reject laws and the budget but could not choose or remove the Chancellor.
- [ ] R7 The Treaty of Frankfurt (1871): indemnity and the loss of Alsace and part of Lorraine.
- [ ] R8 How the Reich changed the balance of power: a new Great Power at the centre of Europe; France weakened and hostile; the other powers adjusting.

Bismarck's domestic policy (only as needed for the domestic vs foreign judgement)
- [ ] D1 Bismarck's method: working with Reichstag parties in turn and identifying "enemies of the Reich" (Reichsfeinde); the National Liberals as his partners until 1878.
- [ ] D2 The Kulturkampf: one pointer only (struggle with the Catholic Church from 1871, wound down in the late 1870s and 1880s; see the notes on church–state tensions). Needed only so the assessment can name it.
- [ ] D3 The Anti-Socialist Law (October 1878): the two assassination attempts as pretext; what it banned; socialists could still stand for the Reichstag; renewed until 1890.
- [ ] D4 Its failure: the SPD vote kept growing (1890 result).
- [ ] D5 The 1879 tariff: protection for industry and agriculture ("rye and iron"); the National Liberals split; Bismarck turned to the Conservatives and the Centre.
- [ ] D6 State social insurance: sickness (1883), accident (1884), old age and invalidity (1889); aim of winning workers from socialism; "carrot and stick"; limits.
- [ ] D7 The army and the Reichstag: the 1887 army bill (Septennate), dissolution and the Kartell victory.
- [ ] D8 National unity measures: the mark (1873) and the Reichsbank (1875/76), as domestic achievements.

Bismarck's foreign-policy aims
- [ ] F1 Germany a "satiated" (saturated) power wanting no more European territory.
- [ ] F2 French revanche over Alsace-Lorraine; Bismarck assumed France was irreconcilable.
- [ ] F3 Core aim: keep France isolated and prevent a hostile coalition, above all a two-front war (his "nightmare of coalitions"); the Kissingen Dictation (1877).
- [ ] F4 Keep Russia and Austria-Hungary from war with each other over the Balkans, and keep Britain friendly; divert rivalries to the edges of Europe.

Franco-German tension
- [ ] FG1 The War-in-Sight crisis (1875): cause, the British and Russian warnings, the lesson Bismarck drew; historians' debate (bluff or real threat).
- [ ] FG2 Boulanger (1886–87) and the Schnaebelé affair (1887).
- [ ] FG3 Encouraging French colonial expansion (Tunisia 1881) and brief colonial cooperation with France (1884–85) to divert France from Alsace-Lorraine.

The Three Emperors' League and the Eastern crisis
- [ ] L1 The Three Emperors' League (1873): Germany, Austria-Hungary, Russia; a conservative monarchical understanding, not a binding alliance.
- [ ] L2 The Balkan crisis (1875–78) and the Russo-Turkish War (1877–78); Bismarck's view that the Balkans were not worth German blood.
- [ ] L3 The Treaty of San Stefano (March 1878): a large Bulgaria; British and Austrian opposition.
- [ ] L4 The Congress of Berlin (June–July 1878): Bismarck as "honest broker"; terms (Bulgaria divided, Serbia, Montenegro and Romania independent, Bosnia-Herzegovina to Austria-Hungary to occupy, Cyprus to Britain, Russia's gains).
- [ ] L5 Results: war avoided, but Russian resentment of Germany; the League lapsed; the Tsar's angry letter (1879).
- [ ] L6 The second Three Emperors' League (June 1881, renewed 1884): neutrality terms; its collapse in 1887 over Bulgaria.

The alliance system
- [ ] AL1 The Dual Alliance (October 1879): terms; secret and defensive; why Bismarck chose Austria-Hungary; Wilhelm I's reluctance.
- [ ] AL2 The Dual Alliance as the permanent core of German policy, lasting to 1918, and its danger (a commitment in the Balkans).
- [ ] AL3 The Triple Alliance (May 1882): why Italy joined (Tunisia); terms.
- [ ] AL4 The Triple Alliance's weaknesses: Italy would not fight Britain; Italy's quarrel with Austria-Hungary over Trentino and Trieste; Italy's neutrality in 1914 (brief).
- [ ] AL5 The Bulgarian crisis (1885–87) and the end of the League.
- [ ] AL6 The Reinsurance Treaty (June 1887): terms, secret protocol on Bulgaria and the Straits, three years; hidden from Austria-Hungary; did it contradict the Dual Alliance?
- [ ] AL7 The Lombardverbot (November 1887) and Russia's turn to French loans.
- [ ] AL8 The Mediterranean Agreements (1887): Britain, Italy, Austria-Hungary, encouraged by Bismarck; Britain linked loosely to his system.
- [ ] AL9 "Try to be one of three" in a Europe of five Great Powers: the logic of the system.

Colonies and Anglo-German relations
- [ ] C1 Bismarck's earlier reluctance about colonies (cost, no fleet, friction with Britain).
- [ ] C2 The colonies of 1884–85: South West Africa, Togoland, the Cameroons, German East Africa, the Pacific.
- [ ] C3 Explanations for the change of mind: pressure from merchants and the colonial movement; elections (1884); diplomatic leverage against Britain and a gesture to France; the theories of Taylor (quarrel with Britain before the liberal Crown Prince succeeded) and Wehler (social imperialism).
- [ ] C4 Chartered companies preferred to state rule.
- [ ] C5 Friction with Gladstone's Britain; the Berlin Conference (1884–85) as Bismarck's (pointer only; detail belongs to the New Imperialism notes).
- [ ] C6 Better relations with Salisbury from 1885–87; the "map of Africa lies in Europe" remark (1888).
- [ ] C7 Dynastic and political tension: Crown Princess Victoria, Queen Victoria's daughter, and Bismarck's fear of liberal influence.

The fall of Bismarck, 1888–1890
- [ ] B1 The year of the three emperors (1888): Wilhelm I's death, Frederick III's 99 days, Wilhelm II's accession.
- [ ] B2 Clashes with Wilhelm II: workers and the Anti-Socialist Law; the February 1890 election; Bismarck's talk of a coup; the Cabinet Order of 1852; the meeting with Windthorst.
- [ ] B3 Resignation (March 1890) and Caprivi; "Dropping the Pilot".
- [ ] B4 Immediate consequences (pointer only): the Reinsurance Treaty not renewed and the Heligoland–Zanzibar Treaty (1890); Russia moved towards France (detail in the notes on the structures of European diplomacy).

Key personality: Otto von Bismarck, part 2 (one block, every point)
- [ ] P1 Who he was, briefly: Junker from Schönhausen, born 1815; Prussian Minister-President from 1862; made unification possible (detail in the unification notes).
- [ ] P2 What he did as Chancellor 1871–90, with dates: constitution, domestic campaigns, alliances, Congress of Berlin, colonies. A direct participant who shaped events.
- [ ] P3 Shaped by events: the War-in-Sight lesson, Russian resentment after 1878, the Bulgarian crisis, and finally a new Kaiser who ended his career.
- [ ] P4 His beliefs: a conservative monarchist defending Prussian power and the Junker order, using nationalism and the Reichstag as tools; realpolitik in diplomacy.
- [ ] P5 Contemporaries: the "Iron Chancellor" admired by nationalists and conservatives; hated by Catholics, socialists and many liberals; respected abroad as a master diplomat.
- [ ] P6 Retirement: Friedrichsruh, criticism of Wilhelm II's governments in the press, memoirs, death in 1898; the Bismarck cult and monuments.
- [ ] P7 Historians: favourable (diplomat of peace after 1871: Taylor; Gall's "white revolutionary") vs critical (Wehler: Bonapartism and negative integration; a weak parliamentary tradition; a system only he could work).

Assessment (paired judgement points, final block)
- [ ] S1 Foreign success: no war for Germany 1871–90; no two-front war; France isolated.
- [ ] S2 Foreign success: the Congress of Berlin prevented a general war in 1878; Germany at the centre of European diplomacy; colonies gained without war.
- [ ] S3 Foreign limit: the system was complex, secret and partly contradictory, and depended on Bismarck personally.
- [ ] S4 Foreign limit: the Dual Alliance tied Germany to Austria-Hungary in the Balkans; treatment of Russia (1878, the Lombardverbot) prepared the Franco-Russian link; French hostility never ended.
- [ ] S5 Domestic success: national unity, currency, Reichsbank, the first national social insurance scheme.
- [ ] S6 Domestic limit: the Kulturkampf and the Anti-Socialist Law failed; the Centre and the SPD grew; a weak Reichstag and an unreformed constitution left to his successors.
- [ ] S7 Overall and contemporary vs later views: foreign record stronger than domestic (the usual judgement); contemporaries saw a great statesman, later historians stress the flaws he left behind.

## 3. Traps

Do not use (wrong or doubtful facts in the current notes):
- Do not use: Prussia had "about two thirds of the territory and population", because its share of the population was nearer three-fifths. Use "about two-thirds of the territory and about three-fifths of the population".
- Do not use: Bismarck called himself an "honest broker" at the Congress of Berlin, as if he said it there; he used the phrase in a Reichstag speech in February 1878, before the Congress. Say he promised to act as an "honest broker".
- Do not use: "Contemporaries spoke of his fear of coalitions" as the source of the phrase; the "nightmare of coalitions" was Bismarck's own phrase.
- Do not use: after his dismissal Bismarck "published his memoirs" as if in his lifetime; Thoughts and Recollections appeared in 1898 just after his death (third volume 1921).
- Do not use: the Congress of Berlin itself "gave" Cyprus to Britain without qualification; Britain gained it by a separate convention with Turkey in June 1878. Say Britain gained Cyprus at the time of the Congress.
- Do not use: Lothar Gall's "white revolutionary" glossed as one "who changed Europe from above"; gloss it as a conservative who used revolutionary methods (accepting nationalism and universal suffrage) to preserve the monarchy and the old elites.
- Do not use: "A.J.P. Taylor and others praised his skill" as an unqualified admirer; Taylor said his policy after 1871 was peaceful but saw him as an opportunist. Use the wording in section 2.
- Do not use: "Anglo-German tension was also dynastic" as a main cause of Anglo-German tension; present the Crown Princess point only as Bismarck's distrust of liberal influence, and the Taylor theory on colonies as one interpretation.

Doubtful or misquoted quotations
- The Balkans quotation is "Pomeranian musketeer" (1876); "Pomeranian grenadier" is a common misquotation. Keep it inside quotation marks only in the checked form.
- "Try to be one of three" and the Kissingen Dictation: give as reported thought, not in quotation marks.
- "Blood and iron" (1862) and "Dropping the Pilot" are fine but belong mainly to other periods/notes; do not attribute "blood and iron" to the Chancellorship.
- Do not invent a Bismarck quotation on France, Britain or Gladstone.

Confusable people, terms and dates
- Wilhelm I (Kaiser 1871–88) vs Wilhelm II (1888–1918); Frederick III reigned only in 1888. Tsar Alexander II (to 1881, assassinated) vs Alexander III (1881–94, Tsar for the 1881 League, the Bulgarian crisis and the Reinsurance Treaty). Alexander of Battenberg (Prince of Bulgaria) is not a Tsar.
- First Three Emperors' League (1873, an understanding) vs second (1881, a secret treaty). Neither is the Holy Alliance of 1815.
- Dual Alliance (1879, Germany–Austria-Hungary) vs Triple Alliance (1882, adds Italy) vs Triple Entente (Britain, France, Russia, 1907, belongs to later notes) vs the Franco-Russian Dual Alliance (1894).
- Congress of Berlin (1878, Balkans) vs Berlin Conference (1884–85, Africa).
- Treaty of San Stefano (March 1878, Russia–Turkey) vs Treaty of Berlin (July 1878).
- Treaty of Frankfurt (1871) vs Frankfurt Parliament (1848).
- Bundesrat (state governments, not elected) vs Reichstag (elected).
- Anti-Socialist Law (1878–90) vs the May Laws of 1873 (Kulturkampf) vs Russia's May Laws of 1882 (anti-Semitism). Only the first belongs here.
- Mediterranean Agreements (1887, Britain, Italy, Austria-Hungary; Germany not a signatory) vs the Reinsurance Treaty (1887, Germany and Russia only).
- Heligoland–Zanzibar (1890) came after Bismarck's fall; it is Caprivi's, not his.
- Bismarck was Chancellor of the Reich and also (separately) Prussian Minister-President; do not merge the offices.

## 4. What exams ask

- Higher 100-mark essay, the commonest form: Bismarck's foreign and domestic policies compared, i.e. was he more successful or competent abroad than at home (asked many times, 2008–2025). Marks go to a judgement stated in the opening sentence and sustained to the end (the usual verdict: abroad more successful than at home), with dated points on both sides. Answers that cover only foreign policy, or only narrate, lose marks.
- Higher essay comparing Bismarck with Wilhelm II as handlers of German affairs or foreign policy, or how German foreign policy changed from Bismarck to Wilhelm II and affected the balance of power. Students need Bismarck's aims (satiated power, isolate France, avoid two fronts) set against the post-1890 changes; the lapse of the Reinsurance Treaty in 1890 is the hinge point.
- Higher essay on German foreign policy and the balance of power: define balance of power, explain how unification upset it, and show how each treaty (1873, 1879, 1881, 1882, 1887) kept France isolated. Linking each treaty to the aim earns more than listing it.
- Higher essay on tensions in the Reich: domestic "enemies of the Reich" (Catholics, socialists) belong here as well as foreign tension; Wehler's negative integration is a strong analytical point.
- Ordinary 40-mark paragraph on Bismarck's foreign policy or on Bismarck as Chancellor: each accurate, relevant, dated statement earns marks (satiated power; Three Emperors' League 1873; Congress of Berlin 1878, honest broker; Dual Alliance 1879; Triple Alliance 1882; Reinsurance Treaty 1887; no war, no two-front war; dismissal 1890).
- Ordinary 40-mark paragraph on his domestic record: successes (currency, Reichsbank, insurance laws with dates) and failures (Kulturkampf, Anti-Socialist Law, SPD growth).
- Ordinary document questions (DBQ style) on Bismarck and France or Bismarck on colonies: students must recognise that France wanted Alsace-Lorraine back, that Bismarck backed French colonies to ease French hostility (which did not work), and that he was reluctant about German colonies (no fleet, cost, lack of skilled officials). Reading the source carefully and adding one fact from outside earns full marks.
- Cause and result questions: why the Dual Alliance was made (Russian resentment after 1878); why Italy joined (Tunisia); why the Reinsurance Treaty was needed (collapse of the League over Bulgaria); why Bismarck fell (clash with Wilhelm II over workers, the constitution and control of ministers).
- Judgement points examiners reward: achievements vs limits as paired points (peace kept vs a fragile, contradictory system; unity vs failed campaigns against Catholics and socialists); contemporary view (Iron Chancellor, "Dropping the Pilot") vs later historians (Taylor, Gall, Wehler), naming at least one historian with their view.
