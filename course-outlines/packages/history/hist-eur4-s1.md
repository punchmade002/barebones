## 1. Must cover (one bullet or block for every item; tick each off)

Background (assumed)
- [ ] A1 (assumed) The wartime Grand Alliance: the USA, Britain and the Soviet Union fought Hitler together from 1941 although they distrusted each other (Western intervention against the Bolsheviks after 1917, the Nazi–Soviet Pact of 1939).
- [ ] A2 (assumed) The ideological divide: Western capitalist democracy (free elections, several parties, private enterprise) against Soviet communism (one-party rule, state ownership, central planning).
- [ ] A3 (assumed) The Soviet Union's huge war losses and the German invasions through Poland, which explain Stalin's demand for security in the west.

Key concepts and essential terms (each defined as used in this topic; also in keyTerms)
- [ ] K1 Cold War: the rivalry of the two blocs short of direct war between the superpowers; fought by propaganda, aid, alliances, arms and crises. Where the term came from (section 2).
- [ ] K2 Iron Curtain: Churchill's image (1946) of the line dividing Soviet-controlled Eastern Europe from the West; later the fortified border itself.
- [ ] K3 Sovietisation: the remaking of Eastern European states on the Soviet model (communist one-party rule, secret police, planned economy, collectivised farming, loyalty to Moscow), imposed with Red Army backing.
- [ ] K4 Satellite state: formally independent but controlled by the Soviet Union in politics, economy and defence.
- [ ] K5 Capitalism: private ownership and production for profit in a market economy; the system the USA defended.
- [ ] K6 Communism: as practised in the Soviet bloc, a single party controlling the state, with publicly owned, centrally planned industry.
- [ ] K7 Containment: the American policy of resisting the spread of Soviet power and communism (essential term, not an outline key concept).
- [ ] K8 Salami tactics: removing opponents one slice at a time (essential term; outline cover item).
- [ ] K9 Sphere of influence and buffer zone (essential terms for Soviet security aims).

Wartime tensions and the conferences of 1945
- [ ] W1 Wartime tensions: the second front argument, Katyn and the break with the London Poles, the Warsaw Rising (1944), and the Churchill–Stalin percentages agreement (October 1944).
- [ ] W2 Yalta (February 1945): leaders; zones for Germany and Berlin; Poland's government and frontier, with free elections promised; the Declaration on Liberated Europe; Soviet entry into the war against Japan; the United Nations.
- [ ] W3 New leaders: Roosevelt's death and Truman's harder line; Churchill replaced by Attlee during Potsdam.
- [ ] W4 Potsdam (July–August 1945): the four Ds; Germany to be treated as one economic unit; the reparations compromise; the Oder–Neisse line and the expulsion of Germans; the Allied Control Council.
- [ ] W5 The atomic bomb: the US test and use in 1945 and the American monopoly until the Soviet test in 1949.
- [ ] W6 Soviet security aims: a buffer of friendly governments, the Red Army's presence across Eastern Europe in 1945, and how far these aims were defensive.

Occupied Germany and Berlin
- [ ] G1 The four occupation zones and who held each; how France's zone was created.
- [ ] G2 Berlin inside the Soviet zone, divided into four sectors, with Western access only by agreed routes.
- [ ] G3 The zones drift apart, 1945–47: Soviet dismantling and reparations, the US halt to reparations deliveries (1946), Byrnes's Stuttgart speech (1946), the SED merger in the Soviet zone (April 1946), Bizonia (January 1947).

The open breach, 1946–1947
- [ ] B1 Stalin's February 1946 speech and Kennan's Long Telegram (February 1946); the Long Telegram's ideas became containment.
- [ ] B2 Churchill's Fulton speech (5 March 1946) and Stalin's reply in Pravda.
- [ ] B3 Britain's withdrawal from Greece and Turkey (February 1947) and the Greek Civil War (1946–49).
- [ ] B4 The Truman Doctrine (12 March 1947): aid to Greece and Turkey, its wider principle, Congress's approval.
- [ ] B5 The Marshall Plan: announcement (5 June 1947), aims (recovery, stopping communism's appeal, markets), the ERP (1948–52) and the OEEC; open to all Europe.
- [ ] B6 The Soviet response to Marshall aid: Molotov leaves the Paris talks (July 1947); Czechoslovakia and Poland forced to withdraw; the "Molotov Plan" bilateral trade deals.
- [ ] B7 The Cominform (September 1947) and Zhdanov's "two camps" doctrine; Cominform vs the dissolved Comintern.
- [ ] B8 Comecon (January 1949) as the bloc's economic answer to the Marshall Plan: one bullet only, detail belongs elsewhere.

Sovietisation of Eastern Europe
- [ ] S1 The stages: genuine coalition, bogus coalition, monolithic communist rule (Seton-Watson's model), and the timescale 1945–48.
- [ ] S2 The tools: Red Army presence, Soviet and local secret police, communist control of interior ministries and police, land reform to win peasants, rigged elections, arrests and show trials, forced mergers of socialist parties into communist ones.
- [ ] S3 Poland: the Lublin government; the Trial of the Sixteen (1945); rigged 1947 election; Mikołajczyk's flight (October 1947); the PZPR merger (December 1948); Bierut; Gomułka removed (1948).
- [ ] S4 Hungary: the free election of November 1945; Rákosi and salami tactics; Kovács arrested and Ferenc Nagy forced out (1947); the 1947 election; the 1948 party merger; Mindszenty; Rajk; the People's Republic (1949).
- [ ] S5 Romania: the Groza government imposed (March 1945); Maniu tried (1947); King Michael's abdication (30 December 1947).
- [ ] S6 Bulgaria: the Fatherland Front; the monarchy abolished (1946); Dimitrov; Petkov hanged (1947).
- [ ] S7 Czechoslovakia: the 1946 election; the February 1948 crisis and Gottwald's takeover; the death of Jan Masaryk; Beneš's resignation.
- [ ] S8 The Soviet zone of Germany: the SED, the dismantling of industry, the road to the GDR.
- [ ] S9 Yugoslavia and Albania: communists who came to power through their own resistance, not the Red Army.
- [ ] S10 The Tito split (expelled from the Cominform June 1948) and the purges of "Titoists" that followed (Rajk, Kostov, Gomułka; Slánský later).
- [ ] S11 Stalinisation after 1948: the Soviet model copied (planning, heavy industry, collectivisation started, the cult of Stalin, purges), making the states satellites.
- [ ] S12 Opposition to Sovietisation and why it failed (peasant parties, the Church, socialists, armed underground in Poland), with Yugoslavia the partial exception.

The division of Germany and the Berlin Blockade
- [ ] D1 Western moves to a West German state: the London Six-Power Conference (1948), the Soviet walkout from the Allied Control Council (March 1948).
- [ ] D2 The Western currency reform (20 June 1948) and why Moscow saw it as a threat.
- [ ] D3 The blockade (from June 1948): what was cut, Stalin's aims.
- [ ] D4 The airlift: Clay, Reuter, the three airfields, scale, end of the blockade (12 May 1949) and of the airlift (September 1949).
- [ ] D5 Results of the blockade: a Western propaganda victory; Germany's division confirmed; the push for NATO; Berlin a symbol and flashpoint.
- [ ] D6 The Federal Republic of Germany (23 May 1949): Basic Law, Bonn, Adenauer chancellor (September 1949).
- [ ] D7 The German Democratic Republic (7 October 1949): SED rule, Pieck, Grotewohl, Ulbricht; East Berlin as its capital.

Military alliances
- [ ] M1 The Brussels Treaty (March 1948).
- [ ] M2 NATO (4 April 1949): twelve founding members, Article 5, US troops tied to Europe; Ismay's attributed summary; Greece and Turkey join (1952).
- [ ] M3 West German rearmament and entry into NATO (May 1955).
- [ ] M4 The Warsaw Pact (14 May 1955): members, purpose, Soviet command and troops; Albania's later exit.

Assessment
- [ ] X1 Who caused the Cold War: orthodox (Stalin's expansion), revisionist (US economic expansion and the atomic monopoly), post-revisionist (mutual misperception), and the effect of the Soviet archives after 1991; paired points.
- [ ] X2 How successful the Soviet Union was in controlling Eastern Europe by 1949: achievements (eight communist states, Cominform, Comecon, purges) vs limits (Tito, West Berlin held, Marshall aid in the West, NATO).

## 3. Traps

Do not use (wrong or doubtful facts in the current notes):
- Do not use: "the Soviets wanted reparations from the Ruhr; the Western powers refused", because at Potsdam the Soviet Union was granted a share of equipment from the western zones; the dispute came later, when the US zone stopped deliveries (1946). Use the wording in section 2.
- Do not use: "the occupation zones had been agreed by the Allies in 1944" without qualification, because the 1944 plan was for three zones; France's zone was agreed at Yalta and carved from the American and British zones.
- Do not use: "the USA the south and centre-west", because it is muddled; say the USA held the south.
- Do not use: "Albania stopped taking part in 1961", because sources give 1961 or 1962; say "in the early 1960s" and formally left in 1968.
- Do not use: Kennan's Long Telegram as the source of the word "containment", because the term came in his "X" article of 1947; the telegram supplied the ideas.
- Do not use: Ernst Reuter as simply "the Berlin mayor" during the whole blockade, because the Soviets blocked his taking office in 1947; say he led West Berlin and became its mayor in December 1948.
- Do not use: Rajk as only "the Hungarian foreign minister" in a way that hides that he had been the interior minister who ran the police; give both posts.
- Do not use: the "Molotov Plan" as a formal aid programme like the Marshall Plan, because it was a label for bilateral trade agreements; give it no figure.
- Do not use: the Ismay quotation as "the Americans in, the Russians out, and the Germans down", because the usual attributed order is "to keep the Russians out, the Americans in, and the Germans down"; use section 2.
- Do not use: "British in Greece, Yugoslavia and Hungary shared" for the percentages agreement, because it reads as British predominance in Yugoslavia; Greece was British (90 per cent), Yugoslavia and Hungary 50/50.
- Do not use: any Soviet war-death figure from a past-paper source (one gives about seven million, which is far too low); use section 2.

Other errors and doubtful claims to avoid:
- Don't give casualty figures for the airlift, numbers of flights, or the number of Germans expelled: none is checked.
- Don't say Masaryk was murdered or that he killed himself: the cause is disputed.
- Don't say Czechoslovakia's communists seized power by armed coup: it was a constitutional crisis exploited with police, militia and mass pressure; Beneš accepted the new government.
- Don't call Hungary's 1945 election rigged: it was free, and the communists lost; the rigging came in 1947.
- Don't present Yalta as "dividing Europe": it agreed zones for Germany and promises on Poland; the division followed from the Red Army's presence and later events.
- Don't treat the percentages agreement as a formal treaty: it was an informal note between Churchill and Stalin.

## 4. What exams ask

- Sovietisation (Higher, 100 marks; also Ordinary 30 marks): how communist rule was imposed, 1945–48. Marks go for the method (stages, salami tactics, police and interior ministries, rigged elections, mergers, show trials) shown with a named country, a named victim and a date for each (Mikołajczyk, Kovács, Petkov, King Michael, February 1948). Cover at least four countries, not just Czechoslovakia.
- How successful opposition to Sovietisation was: a judgement question. Pair each opposition force with its fate, and name Yugoslavia as the only real exception (and why: no Red Army occupation, Tito's own partisans).
- How successful Soviet control of Eastern Europe was (often to 1992, so the answer also needs the later notes): for this period, achievements (one-party states, Cominform, Comecon, purges of Titoists, Warsaw Pact) against limits (Tito, failure in Berlin, the West's recovery and NATO).
- Origins and development of the Cold War: a cause → result chain from wartime distrust through Yalta and Potsdam, the bomb, Fulton, the Truman Doctrine and Marshall Plan, Cominform, Czechoslovakia and Berlin to NATO and the two German states. Top answers show how each step made the next worse and end with a judgement on blame (orthodox vs revisionist vs post-revisionist).
- Why Germany was divided and why Berlin became the focus of tension: the zones, the reparations dispute, the West's choice of recovery (Bizonia, currency), the Soviet response, and Berlin's position deep inside the Soviet zone. Explain cause, not just narrate.
- The Berlin Blockade (Ordinary 30 and 40 marks, very frequent): cause (currency reform, West German state plans), events (what was cut, the airlift, the airfields, Clay and Reuter), end (May 1949) and results (Western propaganda victory, NATO, two German states).
- Marshall Aid (Ordinary 30 marks, very frequent): who announced it, when and where, the amount (about), mostly grants, the ERP and OEEC, the political aim of containing communism, and why the Soviet bloc refused. Say what it achieved for the Cold War division as well as for recovery.
- Alliances and tensions: NATO's origins (Brussels Treaty, Berlin) and Article 5; the Warsaw Pact as the reply to West German entry into NATO in 1955. Give the dates in the right order.
- Ordinary Level source questions on Soviet security: picking out from a source why Stalin wanted friendly governments (invasion routes through Poland and Romania, war losses) and linking it to the buffer zone. Short answers on Berlin photographs or maps (the three airfields, the sectors).
