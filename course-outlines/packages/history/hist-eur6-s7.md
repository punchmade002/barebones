## 1. Must cover (one bullet or block for every item; tick each off)

Background (assumed)
- [ ] A1 (assumed) The postwar boom to 1968 (see the earlier notes on the boom): high growth, low inflation, US dominance of the world economy and the dollar at the centre of the Bretton Woods system.
- [ ] A2 (assumed) Johnson's Great Society and the escalation of the Vietnam War from 1965 (the war's course belongs elsewhere; here only its economic cost).
- [ ] A3 (assumed) Inflation means a general rise in prices; recession means falling output and rising unemployment; a deficit means spending above revenue; a trade deficit means imports above exports.

Key concepts (each defined as used in this topic; also in keyTerms)
- [ ] K1 Globalisation: growing integration of national economies through trade, investment and finance, so US industries faced Japanese and European rivals and the dollar and oil prices were set in a world market.
- [ ] K2 Corporate capitalism: an economy dominated by large corporations; here, the position of the big US firms (steel, cars) was challenged by foreign competition, and Reagan-era deregulation and takeovers changed how they operated.
- [ ] K3 Stagflation: high inflation together with stagnant growth and high unemployment.
- [ ] K4 Reaganomics and supply-side economics: tax cuts, less regulation, higher defence spending; the theory that lower tax rates raise work, investment and growth.
- [ ] K5 Other essential terms: rust belt, federal deficit, national debt, Bretton Woods system, Plaza Accord, Savings and Loan crisis.

Guns and butter, 1965–1968
- [ ] G1 Vietnam spending plus Great Society programmes with no tax rise: demand outran supply near full employment.
- [ ] G2 Inflation rising from the mid-1960s; over 4 per cent by 1968.
- [ ] G3 Delay and compromise over taxes; the June 1968 surtax; it did not stop inflation.
- [ ] G4 Federal deficits becoming persistent.
- [ ] G5 Balance of payments strain: dollars flowing abroad, foreign governments demanding gold.

The end of Bretton Woods and the oil shocks, 1971–1979
- [ ] B1 Nixon's measures of 15 August 1971 (gold convertibility suspended, wage and price freeze, import surcharge).
- [ ] B2 Smithsonian Agreement (December 1971) and floating rates by 1973.
- [ ] O1 The 1973 oil embargo (cause, effects: price rise, queues, rationing, lifted March 1974).
- [ ] O2 The recession of 1973–75; Ford's Whip Inflation Now; Carter's failure to stop inflation.
- [ ] O3 The 1979 shock after the Iranian Revolution; inflation about 13 per cent in 1980; stagflation.
- [ ] O4 Energy policy: 55 mph limit (1974), Project Independence (1973), Department of Energy (1977), National Energy Act (1978); continued import dependence.

The rust belt and international competition
- [ ] R1 Steel decline under Japanese and European imports; mill closures; Youngstown Sheet and Tube (1977) as symbol.
- [ ] R2 Japanese cars (Toyota, Honda): small, fuel-efficient after the oil shocks; Detroit's large cars; imports about a quarter of the market; Japan the top car producer in 1980.
- [ ] R3 Chrysler bailout (1979) and the 1981 voluntary export restraints.
- [ ] R4 Regional effects: rust belt cities (Detroit, Cleveland, Youngstown) lost jobs, people and tax revenue; Sun Belt, Silicon Valley and defence-related growth partly offset this.
- [ ] R5 Trade deficit from the 1970s; deficit with Japan; the Plaza Accord (September 1985).
- [ ] R6 Why competitiveness fell (judgement point): older plant, management and quality, high dollar in the early 1980s, higher wages and costs, global change.

Reaganomics, recession and recovery
- [ ] E1 Volcker and high interest rates (from August 1979) to break inflation.
- [ ] E2 The 1981–82 recession and unemployment above 10 per cent in late 1982; recovery from 1983.
- [ ] E3 Economic Recovery Tax Act (1981): income tax cuts; supply-side theory and Laffer.
- [ ] E4 Defence build-up and spending cuts on some social programmes (1981); TEFRA (1982) raising some taxes; Gramm-Rudman (1985); Tax Reform Act (1986).
- [ ] E5 Deregulation: Airline Deregulation Act (1978) and Staggers Rail Act (1980) begun under Carter; thrift deregulation in the early 1980s and the Savings and Loan crisis.
- [ ] E6 Results: growth and falling inflation from 1983; long expansion and job creation, mostly in services; deficits and uneven gains remain.

Deficits, debt and the 1987 crash
- [ ] D1 Large deficits every year under Reagan because tax cuts met rising defence spending.
- [ ] D2 National debt nearly tripled in the 1980s; interest payments a major budget item.
- [ ] D3 Foreign borrowing: strong dollar, inflow of capital, hurt exporters; net debtor nation in 1985.
- [ ] D4 Trade deficit at a peak in 1987; Plaza Accord (1985) and Louvre Accord (1987).
- [ ] D5 The stock market crash of 19 October 1987 and the Fed's response.

Wages, incomes and inequality
- [ ] W1 Stagnant real earnings from the early 1970s; families relying on more earners.
- [ ] W2 Widening gap between rich and poor in the 1980s; tax cuts favoured high earners.
- [ ] W3 Decline of manufacturing and unions; lower-paid service jobs; minimum wage frozen 1981–1990.
- [ ] W4 Poverty rate up from the early 1970s low to the early 1980s; Black unemployment about double white.

Assessment: success or decline? (final block, paired points)
- [ ] S1 Success: recovery after 1983, low inflation, jobs, leadership in computers, services and finance.
- [ ] S2 Limit: debt and deficits, trade deficit, dependence on foreign capital, loss of manufacturing jobs.
- [ ] S3 Two phases: boom to 1968, then stagflation and restructuring; a recovery that was uneven.
- [ ] S4 Historians' debate: decline from global change vs policy choices; Paul Kennedy's "imperial overstretch" vs optimists.
- [ ] S5 What limited growth 1968–1989: Vietnam and Great Society costs, end of Bretton Woods, oil shocks, foreign competition, deficits.

## 3. Traps

Do not use (wrong or doubtful facts in the current notes):
- Do not use: "The Vietnam War cost well over 100 billion dollars", because estimates vary by method; say only that it was very costly.
- Do not use: "the Louvre Accord was an attempt to bring the dollar down", because by 1987 it aimed to stop the dollar falling further. Only the Plaza Accord aimed to lower it.
- Do not use: "1971 was the first year since 1893 that the US imported more than it exported" as a firm claim; say the trade balance moved into deficit from 1971.
- Do not use: "the 1973 oil crisis ended Bretton Woods"; the dollar link to gold ended in August 1971, before the oil shock.
- Do not use: any exact federal deficit or defence-spending figure.
- Do not use: "Reagan's tax cuts caused the 1981–82 recession"; the recession followed Volcker's high interest rates, and the tax cuts came into effect as recovery began.

Confusable terms and dates
- Inflation (rising prices) vs recession (falling output) vs stagflation (both together).
- Deficit (one year's shortfall) vs national debt (accumulated borrowing); budget deficit vs trade deficit.
- First oil shock 1973 (embargo after the Yom Kippur War) vs second 1979 (Iranian Revolution).
- Nixon 1971 (gold, freeze) vs Smithsonian 1971 vs floating 1973; Plaza 1985 vs Louvre 1987.
- Carter began deregulation (1978, 1980); Volcker was appointed by Carter, not Reagan.
- Rust belt (north-east and Midwest industry) vs Sun Belt (South and West growth).

## 4. What exams ask

- Higher 100-mark essay, nearly every year: strengths and weaknesses, success or decline, or what limited growth, across 1945–1989. Marks go to a clear judgement that separates the boom to 1968 from the decline phase after it. For this subsection, name each cause with a date: Vietnam and Great Society costs, 1971 gold decision, 1973 and 1979 oil shocks, Japanese competition, 1981–82 recession, deficits.
