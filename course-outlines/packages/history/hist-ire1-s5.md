## 1. Must cover (one bullet or block for every item; tick each off)

Background (assumed)
- [ ] A1 (assumed) The Great Famine (1845–c.1850) killed and drove out about a quarter of the population; the cottier class was devastated (full treatment in the Famine notes).
- [ ] A2 (assumed) Before the Famine: dependence on the potato, subdivision of holdings, cottiers and labourers, landlords and rent; the Act of Union (1801).
- [ ] A3 (assumed) The Corn Laws were repealed in 1846, so grain prices fell relative to livestock prices.
- [ ] A4 (assumed) The Encumbered Estates Act (1849) as a Famine-era measure.

Key concepts (each defined as used in this topic; also in keyTerms)
- [ ] K1 Dowry: money or land a bride's family gave on marriage; after the Famine it decided who could marry and inherit.
- [ ] K2 Landlordism (post-Famine): a small landlord class owning almost all the land, tenants without legal security of tenure; it survived the Famine.

Post-Famine agriculture and landlordism
- [ ] G1 Population decline: 1841 to 1871.
- [ ] G2 Tillage down, pasture up: cattle, sheep and dairy rose in price and needed less labour.
- [ ] G3 Live cattle exported to Britain by rail and steamship.
- [ ] G4 Consolidation of holdings; the end of the cottier class; larger farms.
- [ ] G5 The stronger-farmer class as the winners: wealth, tenure, influence in parish and politics.
- [ ] G6 Labourers declining but poor; the small farmers of the west near subsistence.
- [ ] G7 Encumbered Estates Act (1849) and the Landed Estates Court (1858): new owners, some commercial.
- [ ] G8 Landlordism survived: ownership, no security of tenure until the Land Act of 1870 (limited protection; detail in the notes on Fenianism and Liberal reforms).
- [ ] G9 Evictions fell from the Famine peak but remained a threat; tenant right as a grievance.

Marriage, dowry and family life
- [ ] D1 What a dowry was and how it was used.
- [ ] D2 Single-heir inheritance of the farm.
- [ ] D3 Matchmaking; land and money before affection.
- [ ] D4 Late marriage and high rates of celibacy, strongest in the west.
- [ ] D5 Options for non-inheriting children: emigration, labour, priesthood or religious life, domestic service.
- [ ] D6 Effect on population: low birth rate, no recovery.

Emigration after 1850 (brief; the diaspora is covered elsewhere)
- [ ] E1 Scale: about a million left in the 1850s; high through the 1860s.
- [ ] E2 Destinations and ports.
- [ ] E3 Who left: young, unmarried, women about as many as men.
- [ ] E4 Chain migration and remittances.
- [ ] E5 Effects on those who stayed: relief for land, loss of young people, the idea of exile.

Education: primary
- [ ] P1 The national school system (1831), the Stanley letter and the Commissioners of National Education.
- [ ] P2 Aims: combined literary and moral education for all denominations, separate religious instruction.
- [ ] P3 State role: grants, textbooks, inspection, a teacher-training establishment in Dublin.
- [ ] P4 Church attitudes: Church of Ireland (Church Education Society 1839), Presbyterians, Catholic bishops divided (Murray, MacHale).
- [ ] P5 In practice denominational: clergy management and one-faith districts; Cullen pressed for more Catholic control after 1850.
- [ ] P6 The Christian Brothers: Edmund Rice, Waterford, 1802; free schools for poor Catholic boys outside the national system.
- [ ] P7 Effects: rising literacy; teaching through English and the decline of the Irish language.

Education: higher
- [ ] U1 The Queen's Colleges at Belfast, Cork and Galway: Act of 1845, opened 1849, Queen's University 1850; non-denominational.
- [ ] U2 "Godless colleges"; Rome's condemnation (1847) and the Synod of Thurles (1850); a divided hierarchy. (The Synod itself belongs to the culture and religion notes.)
- [ ] U3 The Catholic University (1854): Newman as rector, no state-recognised degrees, few students, Newman's resignation (1858).
- [ ] U4 Trinity College Dublin: Anglican, Catholics admitted to degrees but not fellowships.
- [ ] U5 Belfast's Queen's College as the most successful of the three.

Railways and transport
- [ ] R1 The Dublin and Kingstown Railway (December 1834): the first, about six miles, Westland Row to Kingstown.
- [ ] R2 Network growth: slow start, rapid growth in the 1840s and 1850s, core largely complete by about 1860.
- [ ] R3 Main lines: Cork (1849), Galway (1851), Belfast–Dublin link (1855), Killarney (1853).
- [ ] R4 The Irish gauge (5 feet 3 inches).
- [ ] R5 Economic effects: cheaper, faster movement of cattle, butter, grain and fish; ties to British markets through the ports.
- [ ] R6 Towns and markets: fairs shifted to railway towns; junction towns grew; some small ports lost trade.
- [ ] R7 Communications: newspapers, mail, ideas; national organisation of political movements.
- [ ] R8 Tourism and travel (Killarney; rail and steamer link to Britain easing travel and emigration).

Key personality: William Dargan (one block, every point)
- [ ] W1 Who he was: born near Carlow in 1799; trained as surveyor and engineer; worked under Thomas Telford.
- [ ] W2 What he did, with dates: Dublin and Kingstown contract (1834); further lines (Dublin and Drogheda, Ulster Railway); Ulster Canal; the Victoria Channel at Belfast.
- [ ] W3 The 1853 Dublin Industrial Exhibition: financed by him, Leinster Lawn, over a million visitors, Queen Victoria's visit, his losses.
- [ ] W4 How he shaped events and was shaped by them: the railway boom made him; the failure of some ventures and the fall from his horse (1866) ended him; death on 7 February 1867.
- [ ] W5 Participant: a contractor and promoter in the events described, who employed thousands.
- [ ] W6 Contemporaries: praised as a self-made Catholic businessman acting from public spirit; refused a baronetcy; the Dargan testimonial and the National Gallery (opened 1864).
- [ ] W7 Historians: a symbol of Irish enterprise; limits, because his work rested on railway investment and not on a wider Irish industrial base.

Belfast and industrial development
- [ ] B1 Linen: mechanised flax spinning from the 1820s and 1830s; Belfast and the Lagan valley.
- [ ] B2 The American Civil War and the cotton famine boosting linen.
- [ ] B3 Population growth, 1851 to 1871, and where the migrants came from.
- [ ] B4 Harbour works (the Victoria Channel) and engineering serving the mills.
- [ ] B5 Shipbuilding: Hickson's yard, Edward Harland (1858), Harland and Wolff (1861).
- [ ] B6 Why Belfast industrialised: skilled labour, linen capital, business networks, links with Britain, cheap imported coal and iron.
- [ ] B7 Why the south did not: competition from British factory goods; Dublin and Cork kept brewing, distilling and food processing.
- [ ] B8 Sectarian geography: Catholics about a third of the population, concentrated in unskilled work and certain districts (the Pound).
- [ ] B9 Sectarian riots in 1857 and 1864.

Assessment (paired judgement points, final block)
- [ ] S1 Achievement and limit: stronger farmers' prosperity and railway markets against falling population, continuing emigration and a poor west.
- [ ] S2 Achievement and limit: literacy spread through the national schools against the decline of Irish.
- [ ] S3 Achievement and limit: Belfast's industrialisation against its confinement to the north-east, with the rest of Ireland dependent on agriculture and exporting people.
- [ ] S4 Contemporary view (improvement under the Union) against later view (a more commercial but more unequal and emigrant society).

## 3. Traps

Do not use (wrong or doubtful facts in the current notes):
- Do not use: "only about 65 miles of railway were open in 1845", because the figure is not reliably attested. Say the network grew fast in the late 1840s and 1850s, and use "about 1,000 miles by the mid-1850s".
- Do not use: "by 1870 roughly a million children were on the school rolls", because the figure is doubtful. Say attendance and literacy rose steadily.
- Do not use: "farms above fifteen acres became far more common" as a statistic. Say holdings were consolidated and small holdings fell sharply, with no figure.
- Do not use: "a training college for teachers opened in Dublin" with a date; the date is unchecked. Say a teacher-training establishment in Dublin.
- Do not use: "the National Gallery was founded partly as his memorial" as stated. Say the Dargan testimonial helped fund the gallery, which opened in 1864.
- Do not use: "emigrants ... followed established routes to particular towns" as a specific example; name no towns.

## 4. What exams ask

- Railways (very common): effects on trade, farming, towns, newspapers and mobility; award marks for naming Dublin and Kingstown 1834, Dargan, and two or more specific effects, each backed by a fact. Better answers note the uneven effects (railway towns grew, small ports lost trade).
- Belfast (very common): why it differed from the rest of Ireland. Marks go to linen, the cotton famine, engineering, shipbuilding and Harland and Wolff, population growth, and the contrast with the south. Link causes to results rather than listing them.
- Dargan (short and long questions): career dates, projects, the 1853 Exhibition, and an evaluation of his significance. A good evaluation uses the contemporary and historians' views from W6 and W7.
- Education: the national school system's aims and the churches' reactions; the Queen's Colleges and the Catholic University; effects on literacy and the Irish language. Distinguish primary from higher education and give the date for each body.
- Emigration: stay at the level of scale, who left, chain migration and effects on those who stayed; do not drift into the diaspora.
- Post-Famine economy and society: consolidation, pasture, stronger farmers, the dowry and late marriage, and the survival of landlordism. Marks reward explaining why the dowry system produced late marriage and celibacy, not just stating it.
- Evaluation: "how far" questions need a paired judgement (benefit and limit), as in section 1 S1 to S4.
- Short "Dargan, Aikenhead, Carleton or Davis" style questions: a clear statement of who he was, two or three dated achievements and one view of his significance.
