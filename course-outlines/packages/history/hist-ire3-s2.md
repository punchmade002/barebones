## 1. Must cover (one bullet or block for every item; tick each off)

Background (assumed)
- [ ] A1 (assumed) The 1916 Rising and the May 1916 executions turned opinion towards the rebels; the survivors and the public used "Sinn Féin" as a label for the Rising and its supporters.
- [ ] A2 (assumed) Before 1916 the Irish Parliamentary Party (IPP) under Redmond dominated nationalist politics and sought Home Rule by constitutional means; the IPP lost ground after 1916.
- [ ] A3 (assumed) The Irish Volunteers (founded 1913) and the secret IRB were the physical-force organisations behind the Rising and later the IRA.
- [ ] A4 (assumed) Britain governed Ireland from Dublin Castle through the Lord Lieutenant (Viceroy), the Royal Irish Constabulary (RIC, an armed police force) and the Dublin Metropolitan Police (DMP).
- [ ] A5 (assumed) World War I ended in November 1918; Britain's need for men in 1918 caused the conscription crisis.

Key concepts (each defined as used in this topic; also in keyTerms)
- [ ] K1 Republic: a state with no monarch as head, which Sinn Féin demanded from 1917 and the Dáil declared in 1919.
- [ ] K2 Sovereignty: supreme authority over a state's own affairs; the Dáil claimed it in the Declaration of Independence.
- [ ] K3 IRB/IRA: the Irish Republican Brotherhood (secret society) and the Irish Republican Army (the Volunteers' fighting force, owing allegiance to the Dáil).
- [ ] K4 Abstention: Sinn Féin MPs refused to take Westminster seats and sat in the Dáil instead.
- [ ] K5 Dáil Éireann (and the Declaration of Independence), flying column, Black and Tans/Auxiliaries, Truce: other essential terms for keyTerms.

The rise of the second Sinn Féin, 1917
- [ ] R1 The original Sinn Féin: founded by Arthur Griffith (1905); self-reliance, abstention, a dual monarchy of Britain and Ireland; small before 1916.
- [ ] R2 The 1917 by-elections: Plunkett (North Roscommon), McGuinness (South Longford), de Valera (East Clare), Cosgrave (Kilkenny City); what each showed (separatists could win; prisoners as candidates).
- [ ] R3 Thomas Ashe's death on hunger strike and his funeral (September 1917) as a rallying point.
- [ ] R4 The Ard Fheis of October 1917: the republican constitution (a republic first, then the people choose the form of government), de Valera elected president, Griffith standing aside; de Valera also became president of the Volunteers.
- [ ] R5 Why Sinn Féin grew: it united ex-Volunteers, the IRB, Gaelic Leaguers, younger priests and a new generation behind the republican demand; the IPP seemed discredited.

The conscription crisis and the "German Plot", 1918
- [ ] C1 The Military Service Act (April 1918) extended conscription to Ireland after the German spring offensive.
- [ ] C2 Nationalist resistance: all-party conference, the bishops, the pledge, the one-day general strike.
- [ ] C3 The "German Plot" arrests of May 1918 (who, why, the pretext) and the new Viceroy.
- [ ] C4 Results: nationalist opinion united behind Sinn Féin; the older leaders were removed and the radicals strengthened; Redmond's death (March 1918) and by-election gains finished the IPP's hold.

The 1918 general election
- [ ] E1 The Representation of the People Act 1918 and the enlarged electorate.
- [ ] E2 Date, and the results: Sinn Féin, Unionists and IPP seats; Sinn Féin's share of votes and uncontested seats.
- [ ] E3 The geography of the result: Unionist strength in the north-east; the IPP's collapse (Devlin as the notable survivor).
- [ ] E4 Markievicz's election (St Patrick's, Dublin) as the first woman elected to the House of Commons, while in Holloway; she did not take her seat; many winners were in prison; de Valera won two seats.
- [ ] E5 The mandate: Sinn Féin's abstention policy and its promise to set up an Irish assembly; what the result did and did not show about the people's wishes.

The First Dáil and the counter-state, 1919–1921
- [ ] D1 The First Dáil, Mansion House, 21 January 1919; how many attended and why.
- [ ] D2 Its three documents: the Declaration of Independence, the Message to the Free Nations of the World and the Democratic Programme (content of each in one line).
- [ ] D3 De Valera's escape from Lincoln (February 1919) and election as Príomh Aire (April 1919); the ministry (Griffith, Collins, Brugha, Plunkett, Markievicz).
- [ ] D4 The Dáil Loan (the National Loan): who ran it and how much it raised.
- [ ] D5 The counter-state: Sinn Féin control of local councils (1920), the Dáil arbitration and parish courts, republican police.
- [ ] D6 The British ban on the Dáil (September 1919) and the ministers' life on the run.
- [ ] D7 The Volunteers' oath of allegiance to the Dáil (August 1920) and the use of the name IRA.

The IRA campaign, 1919–1921
- [ ] W1 Soloheadbeg (21 January 1919): what happened, who, and that the Dáil had not ordered it.
- [ ] W2 Attacks on and withdrawal from RIC barracks; the IRA's guerrilla method and small numbers.
- [ ] W3 Flying columns and the West Cork column (Tom Barry).
- [ ] W4 Collins's intelligence network: Director of Intelligence, spies in Dublin Castle and the police (Ned Broy), the Squad and the G Division detectives.
- [ ] W5 The deaths of Tomás Mac Curtain, Terence MacSwiney and Kevin Barry (1920) and their effect on opinion.
- [ ] W6 Bloody Sunday, 21 November 1920: the Squad's killings, then Croke Park.
- [ ] W7 Kilmichael (28 November 1920).
- [ ] W8 The burning of the Custom House (May 1921) as a heavy IRA loss.
- [ ] W9 The scale of the war: about 2,000 dead.

British counter-measures and the road to the Truce, 1920–1921
- [ ] B1 The Black and Tans: who they were, nickname, arrival and numbers.
- [ ] B2 The Auxiliary Division of the RIC (ADRIC): who, when, how it operated.
- [ ] B3 The Restoration of Order in Ireland Act (August 1920) and martial law (December 1920).
- [ ] B4 Reprisals: Balbriggan (September 1920) and the burning of Cork (December 1920).
- [ ] B5 British and international opinion turned against the reprisals; calls for talks.
- [ ] B6 The Government of Ireland Act (1920) and the May 1921 elections (brief; partition belongs to a neighbouring subsection).
- [ ] B7 George V's speech in Belfast (June 1921) and Lloyd George's invitation to de Valera (24 June).

- [ ] B9 Why the British negotiated (reputation and opinion, the IRA could not be crushed cheaply, dominions and America, cost).
- [ ] B10 De Valera in the United States, June 1919 to December 1920 (one point only; detail in the notes on the diaspora).

Key personality: Michael Collins (one block, every point)
- [ ] P1 Who he was: born near Clonakilty, Co. Cork (1890); London 1906–1916; joined the IRB there.
- [ ] P2 1916 and after: in the GPO as aide to Joseph Plunkett; Frongoch internment camp; rose in the Volunteers, IRB and Sinn Féin; Ashe's funeral.
- [ ] P3 What he did 1919–1921: Minister for Finance, the Dáil Loan, Director of Intelligence, the Squad; the most wanted man in Ireland.
- [ ] P4 Participant: he organised and took part in the war, and then negotiated the Treaty (point to the notes on the Treaty negotiations and Civil War; his death in 1922 is not covered here).
- [ ] P5 Contemporaries: republicans admired the man who won the war; British officials saw a ruthless organiser of killing, yet Churchill and others came to respect him.
- [ ] P6 Historians: Coogan (hero of the revolution) vs Hart (more critical on the killings).

Key personality: Éamon de Valera, part 1, 1917–1921
- [ ] Q1 Who he was: born New York (1882), raised in Bruree, Co. Limerick, maths teacher, joined the Volunteers (1913); commanded at Boland's Mill in 1916; sentence commuted.
- [ ] Q2 Released June 1917; East Clare by-election; president of Sinn Féin and the Volunteers (October 1917).
- [ ] Q3 German Plot arrest, Lincoln escape, Príomh Aire (1919); in America (June 1919 to December 1920); the quarrel with Devoy and Cohalan.
- [ ] Q4 Return and lead in the talks with Lloyd George (summer 1921); his later career belongs elsewhere.
- [ ] Q5 Contemporaries: supporters saw the leader who held the movement together; critics saw rigidity and distance from the fighting.
- [ ] Q6 Historians: Coogan and Ferriter (Judging Dev) on pragmatist vs ideologue.

Key personality: Countess Markievicz, part 2, 1917–1921
- [ ] M1 Released June 1917 under the amnesty; joined Sinn Féin's executive.
- [ ] M2 Arrested in the German Plot; elected in December 1918 from Holloway; released March 1919.
- [ ] M3 Minister for Labour, April 1919 to January 1922: the only woman in the cabinet.
- [ ] M4 Arrested again (1920), hard labour, released July 1921; limits of her ministry because of prison and hiding.
- [ ] M5 Contemporaries admired her courage but some colleagues distrusted her socialism and manner; historians stress her pioneering role for women in revolutionary politics.

Assessment: the Sinn Féin mandate and the War of Independence (final block)
- [ ] S1 Achievement: a democratic mandate in 1918 and a working counter-state.
- [ ] S2 Achievement: guerrilla pressure and Dáil publicity brought Britain to talks.
- [ ] S3 Limit: the IRA was small, short of arms and under strain by mid-1921.
- [ ] S4 Limit: the 1918 vote was a minority of the electorate's votes, many uncontested; it was a vote for a republic or against the IPP, not an approval of every IRA act; violence and intimidation of informers.
- [ ] S5 Contemporary vs later views (British government called the IRA a murder campaign before changing course; historians such as Hart on Cork).
- [ ] S6 Two sources of authority (Dáil and IRA) left the movement open to the split over the Treaty in 1922 (one point only).

## 3. Traps

Do not use (wrong or doubtful facts in the current notes):
- Do not use: "from then on they were known as the Irish Republican Army" for August 1920, because the name IRA was in use before and after that date. Say the oath of allegiance to the Dáil was taken from August 1920.
- Do not use: Seán Hegarty's Cork forces as an example of flying columns, because the claim is not certain. Use Tom Barry's West Cork column only.
- Do not use: Griffith "later Foreign Affairs" in the 1919 ministry, because the dates of ministry changes are unclear. Give Griffith as Home Affairs.
- Do not use: the "murder gang" label as a quotation from Lloyd George or the British government, because it is not firmly attested. Say the British government at first treated the IRA as a criminal conspiracy.
- Do not use: the IPP kept seats "thanks to an arrangement with Sinn Féin" as a specific claim, because the pact details are uncertain. Say only that Devlin held a Belfast seat.
- Do not use: any death toll for the Black and Tans or specific individual reprisal tolls, because they are not checked.
- Do not use: a quotation from Collins at Ashe's funeral; give it as reported action only.

## 4. What exams ask

- Higher 100-mark essay: the fortunes and development of Sinn Féin from a small party to the dominant force by 1918, its counter-state and war, and its position by the Truce. Marks go to a clear thesis (1917 and the conscription crisis as turning points), dated stages (1916, by-elections, October 1917, April 1918, December 1918, January 1919, 1920, July 1921) and a view on how much was owed to British mistakes versus Sinn Féin's own organisation.
- Higher essay on the use of physical force and constitutional methods in the drive for self-government: contrast the IPP's route with the Rising and the IRA campaign, and judge which worked, with evidence (1918 result, Bloody Sunday, reprisals, Truce).
- Higher essay on attempts to win self-government 1912–1921: place the second Sinn Féin and the IRA campaign as the third route after Home Rule and 1916.
- Ordinary Level 30-mark paragraph on the conscription crisis and the German Plot: what the Act was, who resisted, what happened in May 1918, why Sinn Féin gained. Each dated, correct statement earns marks.
- Ordinary Level 30-mark paragraph on the 1918 election: the franchise change, the date, seats won by Sinn Féin, Unionists and the IPP, and what followed (the First Dáil). The seat numbers and the changed electorate are rewarded.
- Ordinary Level 30-mark paragraph on the War of Independence: Soloheadbeg, Collins's intelligence work and the Squad, Bloody Sunday, plus the British side (Tans, Auxiliaries, reprisals).
- Ordinary Level 40-mark profile of Collins, de Valera or Markievicz: career dates, roles in 1917–1921 (Finance and Intelligence; Sinn Féin and Dáil leader; Minister for Labour and jail), and one contemporary or historian's view. Name the roles with dates.
- Short ordinary-level answers: the party with most seats in 1918; the first woman elected to the Commons; where the First Dáil met; what was set up in January 1919; what ended on 11 July 1921.
- Strong higher answers explain why Britain negotiated rather than only narrate the war, and separate military from political reasons.
- Contemporary vs later views earn credit when each is attached to a named group or historian (British government, nationalists; Coogan, Hart, Ferriter).
