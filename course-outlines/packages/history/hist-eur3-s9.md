## 1. Must cover (one bullet or block for every item; tick each off)

Background (assumed knowledge)
- [ ] X1 (assumed) How the war began: Hitler's demands on Poland, the Nazi-Soviet Pact (August 1939) with its secret protocol dividing eastern Europe, and the British and French guarantee to Poland. One or two bullets; Hitler's foreign policy belongs to the Nazi state notes.
- [ ] X2 (assumed) First World War technology as the starting point: tanks, military aircraft, submarines and gas were all used in 1914–18, but tanks were slow and unreliable and the war was mostly static trench warfare. One bullet.
- [ ] X3 (assumed) Japan's expansion before 1941: war with China from 1937. One clause, inside P3.

Blitzkrieg and the war in the west, 1939–1940
- [ ] B1 Blitzkrieg (key concept): concentrated armoured (Panzer) divisions, motorised infantry, close air support (the Stuka dive-bomber), radio communication, speed, breakthrough and encirclement; aim of a short war avoiding the trench stalemate of 1914–18. Note that it was a label used mainly by Western journalists, not a formal German doctrine.
- [ ] B2 Poland, September 1939: German invasion 1 September; Britain and France declare war 3 September; Soviet invasion from the east 17 September; Warsaw surrenders at the end of September; Poland partitioned.
- [ ] B3 The Phoney War, September 1939 to April/May 1940: no major Allied attack in the west.
- [ ] B4 Denmark and Norway (April–June 1940): Denmark falls at once; Allied landings in Norway fail; Germany gains naval bases and secures the iron-ore route from Sweden. The failure in Norway led to Chamberlain's fall (link to C3).
- [ ] B5 The fall of France: the attack of 10 May 1940 through the Netherlands, Belgium and the Ardennes, bypassing the Maginot Line; the "sickle cut" to the Channel; the Dunkirk evacuation; France's armistice in June 1940; Italy enters the war in June 1940. The French political collapse and Vichy belong to the France notes: one bullet only.
- [ ] B6 The Battle of Britain (July–October 1940): the Luftwaffe tries to win air superiority before an invasion (Operation Sea Lion); RAF Fighter Command, Spitfires and Hurricanes, radar; Sea Lion postponed; the switch to bombing cities (the Blitz from 7 September 1940). The Blitz's effect on civilians belongs to the Home Front notes: one clause.

The war becomes global, 1941–1942
- [ ] P1 The Mediterranean and Balkans: Italian failures in Greece and North Africa (1940–41) bring German intervention in the Balkans (April 1941) and North Africa (Rommel).
- [ ] P2 Operation Barbarossa (22 June 1941): a huge front from the Baltic to the Black Sea; rapid early advances; Leningrad besieged; halted before Moscow by winter and the Soviet counter-attack of December 1941. Outline only: the Soviet side is in the notes on Stalin.
- [ ] P3 Pearl Harbor (7 December 1941): Japan's war with China and move into French Indochina; the American oil embargo; the attack brings the USA in; Germany and Italy declare war on the USA (11 December 1941).
- [ ] P4 Japan's conquests (Hong Kong, Malaya, Singapore February 1942, the Dutch East Indies, the Philippines, Burma) and their halt at Midway (June 1942). The Pacific war is background: two or three bullets at most.

Turning points and Allied victory, 1942–1945
- [ ] V1 El Alamein (October–November 1942) and the Allied landings in Morocco and Algeria (November 1942); the Axis expelled from North Africa (May 1943).
- [ ] V2 Stalingrad (ended early February 1943, surrender of the German Sixth Army): the turning point in the east. Outline only.
- [ ] V3 Kursk (July 1943): the last major German offensive in the east; one of the largest tank battles in history.
- [ ] V4 Italy: Sicily (July 1943), Italy's surrender (September 1943), German occupation of the north and centre. Mussolini's fall belongs to the notes on Mussolini.
- [ ] V5 The Battle of the Atlantic (whole war; the turning point in spring 1943): links to T5.
- [ ] V6 D-Day (6 June 1944): Operation Overlord, the Normandy landings under Eisenhower; Paris liberated 25 August 1944.
- [ ] V7 The fall of Berlin: the Soviet offensive from April 1945; Hitler's suicide (30 April 1945); German surrender (7 May at Reims, 8 May in Berlin).
- [ ] V8 The end of the war with Japan: island-hopping; the atomic bombs; the Soviet declaration of war (8 August 1945); surrender announced 15 August and signed 2 September 1945.
- [ ] V9 Why the Allies won: far greater industrial production and manpower once the USA and USSR were in, Axis strategic mistakes (war on too many fronts, declaring war on the USA), Allied intelligence and technology. A frequent Ordinary Level document answer.

The Axis
- [ ] A1 The Pact of Steel (22 May 1939): German–Italian military alliance; Italy unready for war, entered June 1940.
- [ ] A2 The Tripartite Pact (27 September 1940): Germany, Italy, Japan; spheres of leadership in Europe and East Asia; joined later by Hungary, Romania, Slovakia and Bulgaria; Finland fought alongside Germany against the USSR.
- [ ] A3 Weaknesses of the Axis: no joint strategy or command; Italy a drain on German resources; Japan did not attack the USSR, and Germany was not consulted over Pearl Harbor.

The Nazi-Soviet partnership, 1939–1941
- [ ] A4 The Nazi-Soviet Pact as a wartime alignment: the joint partition of Poland, Soviet supplies of raw materials to Germany, ended by Barbarossa. Two bullets at most; the making of the pact belongs to the Nazi state notes.

The Grand Alliance
- [ ] G1 Formation: Britain alone (with the Empire and Commonwealth) after June 1940; the USSR joins after Barbarossa; the USA after Pearl Harbor. "Grand Alliance" was Churchill's term.
- [ ] G2 Lend-Lease (March 1941): American war supplies to Britain, then to the USSR, without immediate payment; earlier the destroyers-for-bases deal (September 1940). Arctic convoys to the USSR.
- [ ] G3 The Atlantic Charter (August 1941, Placentia Bay, Newfoundland): Churchill and Roosevelt; no territorial gains, self-determination, restoration of self-government; the Declaration by United Nations (1 January 1942).
- [ ] G4 "Germany first": the Anglo-American decision to defeat Germany before Japan (Washington conference, December 1941 – January 1942).
- [ ] G5 Casablanca (January 1943): Roosevelt and Churchill; unconditional surrender.
- [ ] G6 Tehran (November–December 1943): the first meeting of the Big Three; agreement on the invasion of France in 1944; Polish frontiers discussed.
- [ ] G7 The percentages agreement (Moscow, October 1944): Churchill and Stalin on spheres of influence in the Balkans.
- [ ] G8 Yalta (February 1945): occupation zones for Germany (including a French zone); the Declaration on Liberated Europe promising free elections; Poland's frontiers and government; Soviet entry into the war against Japan; the United Nations.
- [ ] G9 Potsdam (July–August 1945): Truman (Roosevelt died April 1945), Stalin, Churchill then Attlee. One bullet; it ends the wartime alliance.
- [ ] G10 Tensions: the delay of the second front (Stalin's repeated demands; Dieppe 1942; the Mediterranean strategy); Poland (the Katyn revelation 1943, the Warsaw Uprising 1944, the Lublin government); mutual suspicion; Soviet control of eastern Europe foreshadowing the Cold War.

The technology of warfare between the wars
- [ ] I1 Armoured warfare theory: Fuller and Liddell Hart in Britain; Guderian's Achtung – Panzer! (1937); the first German Panzer divisions (1935); Britain and France spread tanks among infantry units.
- [ ] I2 Air power theory: Douhet and the belief that "the bomber will always get through"; the Spanish Civil War as a testing ground (the Condor Legion, Guernica 1937).
- [ ] I3 Fighter aircraft: Hurricane (first flight 1935), Spitfire (1936), Messerschmitt Bf 109.
- [ ] I4 Radar: Watson-Watt's demonstration (1935); Chain Home stations from 1936.
- [ ] I5 Codebreaking: Enigma broken by Polish mathematicians (from 1932) and their work passed to Britain and France in July 1939.
- [ ] I6 Jets and rockets: Whittle's jet engine patent (applied 1930); the first jet flight (Heinkel He 178, August 1939); von Braun's army rocket work (from the early 1930s; Peenemünde).
- [ ] I7 Sonar (ASDIC) and the aircraft carrier: developed after 1918; one bullet.

The technology of warfare, 1939–1945
- [ ] T1 Tanks and blitzkrieg: Panzer divisions with radios; the Soviet T-34; the American Sherman built in huge numbers; the anti-tank gun and the bazooka as responses (one clause).
- [ ] T2 Aircraft and strategic bombing: dive-bombers, fighters; the Luftwaffe's Blitz; RAF Bomber Command (Harris, area bombing at night) and the US Army Air Forces (daylight "precision" bombing, B-17); the thousand-bomber raid on Cologne (1942); Hamburg (1943) and Dresden (1945); long-range escort fighters (the Mustang) from 1944; the debate on morality and effectiveness.
- [ ] T3 Radar: Chain Home in the Battle of Britain; the cavity magnetron (1940) allowing small airborne and ship-borne sets; night fighters; finding U-boats; bombing aids.
- [ ] T4 Enigma and Ultra: Bletchley Park, Alan Turing and the bombe; Ultra intelligence in the Battle of the Atlantic and North Africa; Colossus (1944) for the Lorenz cipher.
- [ ] T5 Submarines and convoys: U-boat "wolf packs"; the convoy system, escort groups, long-range aircraft, radar and Ultra; the turning point in May 1943.
- [ ] T6 Amphibious warfare at D-Day: landing craft, Mulberry harbours, the PLUTO pipeline. One bullet.
- [ ] T7 Rockets: the V-1 flying bomb (from June 1944) and the V-2 ballistic rocket (from September 1944) against London and Antwerp; too late and too few to change the outcome; slave labour in their production.
- [ ] T8 Jet aircraft: the Messerschmitt Me 262 (in service 1944); the British Gloster Meteor (1944); too late to change the outcome.
- [ ] T9 The atomic bomb: the Manhattan Project (from 1942; Oppenheimer at Los Alamos); the Trinity test (16 July 1945); Hiroshima (6 August) and Nagasaki (9 August 1945); the start of the nuclear age.
- [ ] T10 Technology's overall impact: speed and mobility on land, the war in the air and at sea, intelligence, civilian targets; mass production mattered as much as invention. Judgement material for essays.

Key personality: Winston Churchill
- [ ] C1 Who he was: born 1874 at Blenheim Palace into an aristocratic family; soldier and war correspondent; MP from 1900 (Conservative, then Liberal from 1904, Conservative again in the 1920s).
- [ ] C2 Career before 1939: First Lord of the Admiralty (1911–15) and the Dardanelles/Gallipoli failure (1915); Chancellor of the Exchequer (1924–29) and the return to gold (1925) in one clause; the 1930s out of office ("wilderness years"), opposing Indian self-government and warning against Hitler and appeasement; condemned Munich.
- [ ] C3 Return to office and Prime Minister: First Lord of the Admiralty again (3 September 1939); the Norway debate; succeeded Chamberlain on 10 May 1940; coalition with Labour (Attlee, Bevin); also Minister of Defence.
- [ ] C4 1940: refusal to seek a negotiated peace; Dunkirk (Operation Dynamo); the speeches ("blood, toil, tears and sweat", "we shall fight on the beaches", "their finest hour", "the few"); radio broadcasts; symbol of resistance in the Battle of Britain and the Blitz.
- [ ] C5 Alliance leader: courting Roosevelt (destroyers-for-bases, Lend-Lease, the Atlantic Charter, Germany first); welcoming the USSR as an ally in June 1941 despite his anti-communism; the conferences (Washington, Casablanca, Quebec, Tehran, Moscow, Yalta, Potsdam); Britain the junior partner by 1944.
- [ ] C6 War strategy: the Mediterranean and North African strategy; support for strategic bombing; caution over a cross-Channel invasion; setbacks (Singapore and Tobruk 1942) and the no-confidence motion of July 1942, which he survived.
- [ ] C7 Defeat in July 1945: the Labour landslide; Attlee replaced him at Potsdam. Later: Prime Minister again 1951–55; the "iron curtain" speech (1946); Nobel Prize in Literature (1953); died 1965. One bullet.
- [ ] C8 Participant or witness: a participant who made the key decisions on British strategy and diplomacy.
- [ ] C9 How contemporaries saw him: admired as the voice of national resistance; distrusted before 1940 for his judgement (Gallipoli, gold, India, the abdication crisis); criticised by some generals (Alanbrooke's diaries) and Labour; rejected by voters in 1945, who trusted Labour more on post-war reform.
- [ ] C10 How historians judge him: praise for 1940 and alliance-building (e.g. Andrew Roberts); critics (e.g. John Charmley) argue the war cost Britain its empire and made it dependent on the USA; criticism of area bombing, of the Mediterranean strategy, and of his attitude to India and the empire, including the debate over his government's response to the Bengal famine (1943).

Assessment
- [ ] J1 Assessment block: Churchill's achievements vs limits; the Grand Alliance's success vs its divisions; how far technology decided the war vs production, manpower and strategy; contemporary vs later views.

## 3. Traps

Do not use (doubtful or wrong in the current notes):
- Do not use "Italy's defeats ... drawing forces away from Barbarossa" as a settled fact: whether the Balkan campaign delayed Barbarossa in a way that mattered is debated (the late spring and supply problems also delayed it). Say German intervention in the Balkans and North Africa stretched German resources.
- Do not use "the British Empire's position in Asia was lost in 1942": Britain lost Hong Kong, Malaya, Singapore and Burma but kept India. Name those losses instead.
- Do not use the current notes' "Historians ... criticising ... his attitude to colonial peoples, as in the Bengal famine of 1943" as if Churchill caused the famine: his responsibility is disputed. Use the C10 wording ("the debate over his government's response").
- Do not use "blitzkrieg ... was first used on a large scale against Poland" without qualification: the Polish campaign was largely a conventional advance with tanks in support; France in 1940 is the classic example. Say Poland showed the method and France perfected it, and that "blitzkrieg" was a press label (B1).
- Do not use "Norway was conquered by June despite a failed Anglo-French landing": Polish troops also took part. Say "Allied landings".
- Do not use "Frank Whittle patented the jet engine in 1930": he applied in 1930 and the patent was granted later. Use I6.
- Do not use "Contemporaries ... criticised ... his return to the gold standard in 1925" as a war-leadership point: keep it to one clause in C2/C9; the gold standard is told in the notes on inter-war Britain.
- Do not repeat facts: the current notes give Lend-Lease, the conferences and Dunkirk in more than one block. Each fact once: Lend-Lease in G2, conferences in G5–G9 (Churchill's block names them with a cross-reference), Dunkirk in B5.
- Do not use the current notes' bullet "Japan's attacks also took the Philippines, Malaya and Singapore" together with the separate list of conquests: one bullet (P4).

Quotations:
- Use only the quotations in section 2 (C2, C4, I2). Do not quote "We shall never surrender" as a separate speech: it is part of the 4 June 1940 speech. Do not invent or paraphrase other Churchill wit (the many anecdotes attributed to him are mostly unverified). Do not quote Stalin, Roosevelt or Hitler.
- "Iron curtain" is a post-war phrase (1946): one clause in C7 only.

Doubtful figures to avoid:
- No casualty totals for the war, for Pearl Harbor, the Blitz, Stalingrad or Kursk; no tank or aircraft numbers at Kursk; no production figures except the Sherman's "about 50,000".
- No exact death toll for Hiroshima or Nagasaki (sources range widely).
- Do not call Kursk "the largest tank battle in history" as a fact: say "one of the largest".
- Do not say radar was invented by Watson-Watt alone or first in Britain: several countries developed it in the 1930s; Britain's advantage was the integrated warning system (Chain Home linked to fighter control).
- Do not say Turing "broke Enigma" single-handed or that Colossus broke Enigma.

Confusables:
- Pact of Steel (May 1939, Germany–Italy) vs Tripartite Pact (September 1940, adds Japan) vs Rome–Berlin Axis (1936) vs Anti-Comintern Pact (1936) vs Nazi-Soviet Pact (August 1939).
- Tehran (1943, first Big Three meeting, second front) vs Yalta (February 1945, zones, Poland, UN) vs Potsdam (July 1945, Truman and Attlee) vs Casablanca (January 1943, no Stalin, unconditional surrender).
- The Atlantic Charter (August 1941, a statement of principles) vs Lend-Lease (March 1941, supplies) vs the Battle of the Atlantic (the naval war).
- Battle of Britain (air battle for air superiority, July–October 1940) vs the Blitz (bombing of cities, September 1940 – May 1941).
- V-1 (pilotless flying bomb, could be shot down) vs V-2 (ballistic rocket, no defence).
- Me 262 (jet) vs Bf 109 (piston-engined fighter); Hurricane vs Spitfire.
- Enigma (the machine and cipher) vs Ultra (the intelligence) vs Bletchley Park (the place) vs Colossus (computer for Lorenz).
- Area bombing (RAF, night) vs precision bombing (USAAF, day).
- First and Second Battles of El Alamein: the decisive one is the second (October–November 1942).
- Churchill's two spells as First Lord of the Admiralty (1911–15 and 1939–40).

## 4. What exams ask (topics only)

- Higher Level essay (100 marks) on the technology of warfare 1920–45: marks for organising by area (land, air, sea, intelligence, the atomic bomb) and covering both the inter-war developments and their wartime use, each with named machines, people and dates; the best answers judge impact (e.g. radar decisive in 1940, Ultra and convoys in the Atlantic, V-weapons too late) rather than list inventions.
- Higher Level essay on how technology affected the way the war was fought or its outcome: a clear judgement argued throughout, weighing technology against industrial production, manpower, strategy and alliances.
- Higher Level essay on wartime alliances (with or without the Home Front): the Axis and its weakness, the Grand Alliance from formation (Lend-Lease, Atlantic Charter) through the conferences, and its tensions (second front, Poland); marks for judging how far the alliances decided the outcome, not narrating battles.
- Higher Level essay on Churchill or Stalin as a war leader, or Churchill paired with Keynes and the Jarrow March: for Churchill, marks reward 1940 (refusal to negotiate, speeches, coalition), alliance diplomacy and strategy, with limits (Gallipoli and the 1930s reputation, setbacks in 1942, dependence on the USA, the 1945 defeat) and a final judgement.
- Higher Level essay on what a study of the Second World War teaches: alliances, intelligence and technology as decisive themes, with occupied Europe from the neighbouring notes.
- Ordinary Level short paragraph (30 or 40 marks) on Churchill: dated career facts (Prime Minister 10 May 1940, coalition, Minister of Defence, Dunkirk, the speeches with dates, the conferences, the 1945 defeat); each accurate dated fact earns marks, plus one or two judgements.
- Ordinary Level short paragraph on blitzkrieg and radar, or on radar and codebreaking: what each was, a named example, and its effect on the war.
- Ordinary Level short paragraph on the Axis and the Grand Alliance: the two pacts with dates, the Atlantic Charter, the Big Three conferences.
- Ordinary Level document (e.g. a cartoon of the Nazi-Soviet alliance): identify the leaders, explain why the alliance was surprising and artificial, say what ended it (Barbarossa, June 1941), and why Germany was finally defeated (Allied industrial production and manpower).
- Judgement material examiners reward: Churchill's achievements vs limits and contemporary vs later views; the alliance's success vs its divisions; technology's role vs other factors; the morality and effectiveness of strategic bombing and the atomic bomb.
- Precision: date the Pact of Steel and the Tripartite Pact; give the Big Three conferences in order with their main decisions; do not confuse the Battle of Britain with the Blitz.
