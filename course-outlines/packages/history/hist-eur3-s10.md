## 1. Must cover (one bullet or block for every item; tick each off)

Background (assumed knowledge)
- [ ] A1 (assumed) Total war: a war in which the whole population and economy are mobilised and civilians become targets; why the Second World War, more than the First, made the home front decisive. One or two bullets.
- [ ] A2 (assumed) The course of the war in outline as it touched civilians: war declared September 1939; the Phoney War; the fall of France (June 1940); the Battle of Britain and the Blitz; the occupation of most of Europe by 1942; the Allied bombing offensive; liberation 1944–45. One bullet; the military narrative belongs to the notes on the Second World War.
- [ ] A3 (assumed) The fear of bombing before 1939: governments expected air attack on cities to cause huge casualties and panic, which is why Britain planned evacuation, shelters and the blackout in advance. One bullet.
- [ ] A4 (assumed) Nazi racial ideology ranked peoples, so occupation in the east was far harsher than in the west; this shaped rationing, labour and resistance. One bullet; the detail belongs to the notes on anti-Semitism and the Holocaust.

Britain: evacuation
- [ ] E1 Operation Pied Piper: the government's evacuation scheme from the big cities, starting 1 September 1939: schoolchildren with their teachers, mothers with young children, expectant mothers and the disabled; labels, gas masks, trains; billeting in private homes, which householders could be required to accept.
- [ ] E2 Scale: about 1.5 million people in the first days, most of them children.
- [ ] E3 Reception: mixed; some hosts and evacuees formed lasting bonds, others met homesickness, culture clash and hostility; middle-class hosts shocked by lice, poor clothing and poor health among poor urban children.
- [ ] E4 The drift back: when the expected bombing did not come during the Phoney War, most evacuees had gone home by early 1940.
- [ ] E5 Later waves: further evacuation in 1940 after the fall of France and when the Blitz began, and again in 1944 against the flying bombs.
- [ ] E6 Overseas evacuation: the government scheme to send children to the Dominions and the USA ended after the sinking of the City of Benares (September 1940).
- [ ] E7 Significance: evacuation showed the comfortable classes the poverty of the inner cities and strengthened the case for social reform after the war (link to S1–S3).

Britain: rationing and food
- [ ] R1 Why ration: Britain imported much of its food, and U-boats sank merchant ships; rationing shared scarce food fairly and kept prices down. Ration books and registering with a shopkeeper.
- [ ] R2 The order of rationing: bacon, butter and sugar (January 1940); meat (March 1940); tea, margarine and cooking fats (1940); clothing (June 1941); the points system for tinned and dried goods (December 1941); sweets (1942). Petrol from September 1939.
- [ ] R3 The Ministry of Food under Lord Woolton (from 1940): controlled supply and prices, publicity and recipes, the National Loaf (1942), extra milk and vitamins (orange juice, cod liver oil) for children and mothers, British Restaurants.
- [ ] R4 Utility goods: standard designs of clothing and furniture at controlled prices; "Make Do and Mend".
- [ ] R5 Dig for Victory and the Women's Land Army: gardens, allotments and parks turned over to vegetables; more land ploughed; women on the farms.
- [ ] R6 Effects: most people accepted rationing as fair; the diet of the poorest improved and children's health held up or improved; but shortages, queues and a black market (the "spiv").
- [ ] R7 Rationing lasted beyond the war (bread rationed 1946–48) and ended in 1954. One bullet.

Britain: the Blitz and civil defence
- [ ] B1 The blackout from September 1939, enforced by ARP wardens; road deaths rose sharply in the first winter.
- [ ] B2 Shelters: Anderson shelters (garden shelters, free to poorer households), Morrison shelters (indoor, from 1941), public shelters and the London Underground, which people took over despite official reluctance.
- [ ] B3 The Blitz: from 7 September 1940 (London's docks and East End); London bombed on 57 consecutive nights; raids continued to May 1941.
- [ ] B4 Other cities: Coventry (14 November 1940), and Liverpool, Birmingham, Glasgow and Clydebank, Bristol, Plymouth, Hull and Belfast (April–May 1941).
- [ ] B5 Casualties and damage: civilian deaths in the Blitz and in the whole war (section 2); homes destroyed; the homeless and rest centres.
- [ ] B6 Civil defence: ARP wardens, the Auxiliary Fire Service, fire-watchers, ambulance and rescue workers, many of them part-time volunteers; the Women's Voluntary Service.
- [ ] B7 The Home Guard: formed as the Local Defence Volunteers (May 1940), renamed July 1940, older men and those in reserved jobs, to resist invasion.
- [ ] B8 Morale and information: the Ministry of Information, posters and slogans, censorship, BBC news, Churchill's broadcasts, comedy such as ITMA, cinema and newsreels; Mass Observation reported on morale. One block; radio and cinema in general belong to the notes on popular culture.
- [ ] B9 The flying bombs: V-1s from June 1944 and V-2 rockets from September 1944 on London and the south-east.
- [ ] B10 The "Blitz spirit": the idea of a united, cheerful people under the bombs; contemporary propaganda vs historians who show fear, looting, class anger (the East End's suffering) and complaints alongside real endurance.

Britain: work, women and conscription
- [ ] W1 Mobilising labour: Ernest Bevin, Minister of Labour from 1940, with power to direct workers; reserved occupations; conscription of men from September 1939; the Bevin Boys sent to the mines from December 1943.
- [ ] W2 Women's war work: factories (munitions, aircraft), transport, farms, offices; the women's auxiliary services (ATS, WAAF, WRNS) and nursing; women in anti-aircraft batteries but not in front-line combat.
- [ ] W3 Conscription of women: the National Service (No. 2) Act (December 1941) made unmarried women aged 20 to 30 liable for call-up to the auxiliary services, civil defence or industry; later extended; married women with young children exempt.
- [ ] W4 Limits of change: women usually paid less than men for the same work; nurseries limited; most expected or were expected to leave paid work after the war, but attitudes had begun to change.
- [ ] W5 Northern Ireland: part of the UK Home Front (Belfast's shipyards and aircraft works, the 1941 Blitz), but no conscription there. One bullet.

Britain: the Beveridge Report and the road to the welfare state
- [ ] S1 The Beveridge Report (December 1942), by Sir William Beveridge: the five giants (Want, Disease, Ignorance, Squalor, Idleness); a single national insurance scheme "from the cradle to the grave" in return for weekly contributions, family allowances, a national health service, and the aim of full employment.
- [ ] S2 Reception: huge public interest and sales; Churchill's coalition cautious; Labour enthusiastic.
- [ ] S3 Results: the Education Act 1944 (Butler); the Family Allowances Act 1945; Labour's victory in 1945; the National Insurance Act 1946; the National Health Service from 1948. The war made state planning and "fair shares" acceptable.

Germany's Home Front
- [ ] G1 Rationing from the start of the war (August 1939): rations relatively adequate until the later war years because Germany took food from occupied Europe; ration scales graded by work and by race (Jews and eastern workers received far less); cuts from 1942 and severe shortage in 1944–45.
- [ ] G2 The economy and women: Nazi ideology kept women's call-up limited in 1939–42; the number of German women in paid work rose little in the war (it was already high in 1939); the regime preferred foreign labour.
- [ ] G3 Total war: after Stalingrad, Goebbels's total war speech (February 1943); registration of women for labour service; closure of non-essential businesses; Speer's armaments drive (one clause; Speer belongs to the notes on the Nazi state); the Volkssturm (October 1944).
- [ ] G4 Foreign labour: about seven to eight million foreign civilian workers and prisoners of war by 1944, from all over occupied Europe; western workers treated better than eastern ones; the Ostarbeiter from the USSR and Poles wore badges, lived in camps and suffered hunger, beatings and high death rates; concentration-camp prisoners used by firms.
- [ ] G5 Bombing: RAF area bombing (from 1942) and American daylight raids; the Hamburg firestorm (July 1943); Dresden (February 1945); deaths and homelessness; morale damaged but the regime did not collapse and production rose until 1944.
- [ ] G6 Evacuation in Germany: the Kinderlandverschickung, run with the Hitler Youth, from 1940; millions left bombed cities.
- [ ] G7 Control and terror at home: SD reports on public mood, harsher punishment of "defeatism", listening to foreign radio a crime; terror rose as defeat neared (the July 1944 plot belongs to the notes on the Nazi state: one clause).

Refugees and displaced people
- [ ] F1 Before the war (background): refugees from Nazi Germany, including the Kindertransport (1938–39) to Britain. One bullet; emigration and Évian belong to the notes on anti-Semitism and the Holocaust.
- [ ] F2 The flight of civilians in 1940: millions of Belgians, Dutch and French fled the German advance (the French "exodus", May–June 1940), blocking roads and being strafed.
- [ ] F3 Internment in Britain (1940): after the fall of France German, Austrian and Italian "enemy aliens", many of them Jewish refugees, were interned, mostly on the Isle of Man; some shipped overseas; the Arandora Star sunk (July 1940); most released by 1941.
- [ ] F4 Governments in exile in London (Polish, Norwegian, Dutch, Belgian, Czechoslovak) and the Free French, with exiles serving in the Allied forces.
- [ ] F5 Population movements in the east: Germans settled in annexed Polish lands and Poles expelled; Soviet evacuation of people and industry eastward in 1941 (one clause).
- [ ] F6 Displaced persons (DPs) in 1945: millions of former forced labourers, prisoners of war and camp survivors; UNRRA (1943) and the Allied armies ran DP camps and repatriation; many eastern Europeans and Jewish survivors would not or could not go home.
- [ ] F7 The flight and expulsion of Germans from eastern Europe, 1944–47: more than ten million people. One bullet.

Collaboration
- [ ] C1 Concept, collaboration (keyTerm): cooperation with the occupier, from state collaboration by governments and officials, through economic collaboration (firms, workers), to collaborationism (ideological support, joining German units); distinguish collaboration forced by circumstance from willing collaboration.
- [ ] C2 Why people collaborated: belief in Nazism or fascism, anti-communism, ambition and profit, the wish to protect one's country or job, fear, and the belief in 1940 that Germany had won.
- [ ] C3 Norway: Vidkun Quisling and his party Nasjonal Samling; his attempted seizure of power on the day of the invasion (9 April 1940); Minister-President from February 1942 under the real ruler, Reichskommissar Josef Terboven; little popular support; executed in 1945; "quisling" as a word for traitor.
- [ ] C4 The Netherlands: civil service and police kept working under Reichskommissar Seyss-Inquart; Anton Mussert's NSB; Dutch police helped round up Jews; about three-quarters of the Jews of the Netherlands were killed.
- [ ] C5 Belgium: King Leopold III surrendered (May 1940) and stayed; Léon Degrelle's Rexists and Flemish nationalists collaborated; Degrelle fought in the Waffen-SS.
- [ ] C6 Denmark: a "model protectorate" in which the Danish government and parliament carried on until August 1943; collaboration by negotiation that spared Danes the worst until then.
- [ ] C7 Vichy France as the largest case of state collaboration: one or two bullets (Montoire, Laval, the deportations, the Milice); the detail belongs to the notes on France and Vichy.
- [ ] C8 Volunteers for German forces: Waffen-SS units recruited in western Europe (Norwegian, Dutch, Belgian, French volunteers); auxiliaries in the east (one clause; their role in the Holocaust belongs to those notes).
- [ ] C9 Punishment after liberation: trials and executions (Quisling, Laval, Mussert), purges and summary revenge. One bullet.

Resistance
- [ ] X1 Concept, resistance (keyTerm): from passive and symbolic acts (go-slows, jokes, refusing to cooperate) to underground newspapers, intelligence, escape lines for airmen, hiding Jews, strikes, sabotage and armed partisan war.
- [ ] X2 Outside help: Britain's Special Operations Executive (SOE, 1940) and later American agents supplied arms, radios and agents; the BBC carried messages; the resistance supplied intelligence.
- [ ] X3 France: the Free French and the internal Resistance, the maquis, sabotage before D-Day, reprisals (Oradour). One or two bullets with a cross-reference to the notes on France and Vichy.
- [ ] X4 Poland: the Polish Underground State and the Home Army (Armia Krajowa, 1942), linked to the government in exile; underground schools and courts; the Warsaw Uprising (1 August–2 October 1944), the Red Army's halt across the Vistula, the destruction of Warsaw.
- [ ] X5 Yugoslavia: Tito's Communist partisans from 1941 against Germans, Italians and the Croatian Ustaša; rivalry and civil war with Mihailović's royalist Chetniks; British support switched to Tito in 1943; the partisans liberated most of the country, with the Red Army taking Belgrade (October 1944); Tito's post-war Communist state.
- [ ] X6 Germany: the White Rose (Munich students, leaflets, 1942–43; Hans and Sophie Scholl executed February 1943); other opposition (Communist groups, youth gangs such as the Edelweiss Pirates, the churches, the July 1944 plot) as cross-references; the difficulty of resistance in a police state among one's own people.
- [ ] X7 Other notable acts: Norway (the Vemork heavy-water sabotage, February 1943; teachers' refusal to join a Nazi union); the Netherlands (the February Strike of 1941 against the round-up of Jews in Amsterdam); Czechoslovakia (the assassination of Heydrich, 1942, and the reprisal at Lidice); Denmark (the rescue of most Danish Jews to Sweden, October 1943); Soviet partisans behind German lines; Italian partisans 1943–45.
- [ ] X8 The cost: reprisals and hostage shootings, the destruction of villages, torture and execution of captured resisters.
- [ ] X9 Significance: military value (intelligence, sabotage, tying down German troops, especially in Yugoslavia and the USSR) vs limits (small numbers until 1943–44, heavy losses); political value (national self-respect; resistance movements formed post-war governments and the Communists gained prestige).

Civilians in occupied France (the France side of a Britain-and-France essay)
- [ ] P1 Occupation and division: the two zones; German requisitioning, curfews, identity checks and hostage-taking; the whole country occupied from November 1942.
- [ ] P2 Shortage: rationing by age and work category, queues, substitutes, the black market, food trips to the countryside; prices rose and city dwellers went hungry.
- [ ] P3 Absent men: well over a million prisoners of war in Germany for years; women ran farms, shops and families; the STO (February 1943) sent young men to work in Germany or into the maquis.
- [ ] P4 Persecution of Jews and deportations (one bullet, cross-reference to the notes on France and Vichy).
- [ ] P5 Allied bombing and the battles of 1944 killed many French civilians.
- [ ] P6 Attitudes: most accepted Pétain in 1940; attentisme ("wait and see") was the commonest response; opinion turned against Vichy and the Germans from 1942–43; only minorities actively resisted or collaborated.
- [ ] P7 Results for French women: women gained the vote in 1944 and first voted in 1945. One bullet.

Comparison and judgement
- [ ] K1 Comparing Britain and France: Britain was bombed but never occupied, kept democracy and a free (if censored) press, and the war united society and led to reform; France was defeated, occupied, divided by collaboration and resistance, and emerged with bitter memories.
- [ ] K2 Comparing Britain and Germany: both rationed, evacuated and were bombed; Britain mobilised its own people, women included, more fully, while Germany relied on forced foreign labour and the exploitation of occupied Europe.
- [ ] K3 Memory and historians: the British "people's war" and Blitz spirit (Angus Calder's challenge); the post-war myth of national resistance in France, the Netherlands and elsewhere, revised since the 1970s to show widespread accommodation; resistance and collaboration as two ends of a range in which most people lay in between.
- [ ] K4 "Assessment: civilians in the Second World War" block: paired points (endurance and solidarity vs fear, class tension and the black market; achievements of the British Home Front vs limits for women; the moral record of resistance vs the scale of collaboration and attentisme; contemporary vs later views).

## 3. Traps

Do not use (doubtful or wrong in the current notes):
- Do not use "Evacuation was repeated in the summer of 1940, when more children were sent away after the Blitz began", because the Blitz began in September, not summer; say there were further waves in 1940 (after the fall of France and when the Blitz began) and in 1944 (E5).
- Do not use "Anderson shelters, issued free to households", because they were free only to households below an income limit; others paid (B2).
- Do not use "The Blitz of 1940 and 1941 killed about 40,000 civilians in Britain, over half of them in London"; use "over 40,000, about half of them in London" (B5).
- Do not use "The National Service (No. 2) Act ... required single women aged 20 to 30 to register for war work", because the Act made them liable to conscription (call-up); registration of women for employment was a separate, earlier 1941 step. Use W3.
- Do not use "the government published ration allowances in the press to support confidence", because it is unchecked and adds nothing; say most people accepted rationing as fair (R6).
- Do not use "furniture [rationed] from 1943" as written; say Utility furniture from 1943 (R4), with no claim about a furniture ration.
- Do not use "by 1943 women made up a large share of the industrial workforce" with any figure; no figure is checked. Say women's war work grew greatly.
- Do not use "the regime was reluctant to ask German women to work in factories in large numbers before 1942" and "women's employment rose less than in Britain" without the qualification that many German women already worked in 1939 (G2). Historians (Richard Overy among them) stress this; do not repeat the older claim that German women hardly worked.
- Do not use "By 1944 about seven million foreign workers" as one figure; give "about seven to eight million" (G4).
- Do not use "Families coped with the absence of around one and a half million prisoners of war"; use "well over a million prisoners of war" (P3), to match the notes on France and Vichy.
- Do not use "Britain interned about 27,000 ... some were sent to the Isle of Man or overseas" with "some" for the Isle of Man: most internees were held on the Isle of Man; some were shipped to Canada and Australia (F3).
- Do not use "they gained control of much of the country by 1945" alone for the partisans; add that the Red Army helped take Belgrade in October 1944 (X5).
- Do not use "The Polish Home Army ... was the largest underground army in occupied Europe" as a flat fact; it is usually so described, but say "one of the largest" or "usually described as the largest".
- Do not use "research since the 1970s showing a more complex and divided record" without a name; tie it to Paxton (1972) for France and say "historians" for elsewhere (K3).
- Do not use "Mass Observation surveys" as a government body: it was an independent organisation whose reports the Ministry of Information used (B8).
- Do not use "Rationing ... meat was not freed from control until 1954" as the only post-war point; say all rationing ended in 1954, meat last (R7).

Doubtful claims to avoid:
- Do not use "Keep Calm and Carry On": the poster was printed in 1939 but hardly ever displayed; it became famous only after 2000. Use "Careless Talk Costs Lives".
- Do not quote Churchill's "collar the lot" or "set Europe ablaze"; both are reported second-hand. Describe internment and SOE without them.
- Do not say Britain was the first country to conscript women; it is commonly claimed but not checked here.
- Do not give a total number of resisters for any country, a number of collaborators punished, or a number of DPs.
- Do not claim the Blitz broke or did not affect morale in absolute terms: say morale bent but did not break, with fear and anger recorded alongside endurance (B10).
- Do not claim that bombing of Germany "failed" or "won the war"; say it caused huge civilian loss and damage, did not break morale or the regime, and its effect on production is debated (the strategic case belongs to the notes on the Second World War).
- Do not say the Danish king wore the yellow star; it is a legend.
- Do not say Beveridge wrote the NHS Act or that the report itself created the welfare state: it was a plan; the Labour government's Acts of 1945–48 (and the coalition's Education Act 1944) carried it out.
- Do not give exact day-and-month dates beyond those in the table.

## 4. What exams ask (topics only)

- Higher Level essay (100 marks), the impact of the war on civilians in Britain and/or France (2008): examiners reward specific, dated experience on each side, not a general narrative. Britain: evacuation (Pied Piper, scale, reception, drift back), rationing (first items and date, Woolton, fairness, health), the Blitz (dates, cities, shelters, casualties), women's work and conscription, and the Beveridge Report and welfare state as the long-term result. France: occupation and zones, shortage and the black market, prisoners of war, STO, persecution and deportation of Jews, reprisals, collaboration and resistance, attentisme. Top marks need a comparative judgement (bombed but free and united vs defeated, occupied and divided) and short-term vs long-term effects.
- Higher Level essays on what was learned from the Second World War (2009, 2020): the Home Front and occupied Europe are offered alongside alliances and technology. Marks reward a clear line of argument per topic, e.g. "civilians could sustain a total war" (British Home Front) or "occupied Europe showed the best and worst of human behaviour" (collaboration vs resistance), each backed by named examples (Quisling, Vichy, the Home Army, Tito, the White Rose, the Danish rescue).
- Ordinary Level 30-mark paragraphs on the British Home Front (2015, 2020): evacuation and rationing are the core, with the Blitz, the Ministry of Food and women's work. Each accurate dated fact scores: 1 September 1939, about 1.5 million, January 1940 (bacon, butter, sugar), Woolton, the National Loaf, 7 September 1940, Dig for Victory, the Land Army.
- Causes and results: why rationing and evacuation were needed (U-boats and imports; fear of bombing), and their results (fair shares, better health for the poor; social awareness leading to reform). Cause-and-result pairs earn more than description.
- Achievements vs limits: the British Home Front's success (morale held, production rose, reform followed) vs its limits (class tensions, black market, unequal pay for women, the myth of the Blitz spirit). Resistance's achievements (intelligence, sabotage, the Danish rescue, Yugoslav liberation, post-war legitimacy) vs its limits (minority, heavy reprisals, Warsaw's destruction).
- Collaboration questions reward definitions with examples from more than one country (Norway, the Netherlands, Belgium, Denmark, France) and the motives (ideology, anti-communism, self-interest, fear, belief that Germany had won), plus the judgement that most people neither collaborated nor resisted.
- Germany's Home Front, usually within broader essays: marks for the shift from limited mobilisation to total war after Stalingrad, the dependence on forced foreign labour (and its brutality), the bombing of cities and evacuation, and how the regime kept control. A Britain–Germany contrast (own people vs forced labour; women) earns judgement marks.
- Refugees: marks for named movements with dates (the 1940 exodus, internment and the Arandora Star, governments in exile in London, DPs and UNRRA in 1945, the expulsions of Germans), not a general statement that "many people fled".
