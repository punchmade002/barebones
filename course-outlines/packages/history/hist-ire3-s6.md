## 1. Must cover (one bullet or block for every item; tick each off)

Background (assumed)
- [ ] A1 (assumed) The Treaty (December 1921) gave the Free State dominion status: the Crown, an oath of allegiance, a Governor-General and British use of three Treaty ports; the Civil War followed (see the notes on the Treaty and the Civil War).
- [ ] A2 (assumed) Cumann na nGaedheal under W.T. Cosgrave governed 1922–32; de Valera led the anti-Treaty side, left Sinn Féin and founded Fianna Fáil, which entered the Dáil in 1927.
- [ ] A3 (assumed) The land annuities: repayments by tenant farmers for land bought under the Land Acts.

Key concepts (also in keyTerms)
- [ ] K1 Sovereignty: full independent authority over the state's own affairs.
- [ ] K2 Republic: a state without a monarch as head; the word was avoided in 1937 and used in 1948–49.
- [ ] K3 Dominion status: the Free State's Treaty status within the Commonwealth, gradually dismantled.
- [ ] K4 Allegiance: loyalty to the Crown, expressed in the oath.
- [ ] K5 Other essential terms: Statute of Westminster, External Relations Act, Economic War, land annuities, Blueshirts, Bunreacht na hÉireann, Republic of Ireland Act.

Anglo-Irish relations under Cosgrave, 1922–1932
- [ ] C1 Approach: accepted the Treaty, used the Commonwealth to widen independence by agreement; League of Nations membership (1923) and registering the Treaty there (1924); Britain objected.
- [ ] C2 Boundary Commission crisis settled by the Tripartite Agreement (December 1925); border question set aside (detail belongs to the Cosgrave notes).
- [ ] C3 Imperial Conference of 1926 and the Balfour Declaration; Kevin O'Higgins as a leading Irish advocate.
- [ ] C4 Statute of Westminster (December 1931): legislative independence for the dominions.
- [ ] C5 Limits: oath, Governor-General and Treaty ports left in place; the annuities paid to Britain.

Fianna Fáil takes office
- [ ] F1 Fianna Fáil founded 1926; won the February 1932 election and took office on 9 March 1932; de Valera also Minister for External Affairs.
- [ ] F2 Strengthened by the January 1933 snap election (a majority of its own).

Dismantling the Treaty, 1932–1936
- [ ] D1 The Governor-General: McNeill removed (autumn 1932), Domhnall Ua Buachalla appointed (November 1932) in a deliberately diminished role.
- [ ] D2 Constitution (Removal of Oath) Act, May 1933.
- [ ] D3 Constitution (Amendment No. 22) Act 1933: Privy Council appeal ended.
- [ ] D4 Seanad abolished, 1936.
- [ ] D5 Abdication crisis, December 1936: Amendment No. 27 Act (11 December) removed the Crown and Governor-General from the Constitution; External Relations Act (12 December) kept a role for the King in diplomatic appointments and international agreements.
- [ ] D6 Why Britain did not stop it: the Statute of Westminster, no wish for confrontation; Britain did not accept the Free State as outside the Commonwealth.

The Economic War, 1932–1938
- [ ] E1 Cause: de Valera withheld the annuities (about £3 million a year) from mid-1932, arguing they were owed to the Irish state, not Britain; Britain held that the 1920s agreements bound Ireland.
- [ ] E2 Measures: British special duties on Irish cattle, beef, butter and other exports from July 1932; Irish duties on British goods (coal, cement, iron and steel); self-sufficiency policy.
- [ ] E3 Coal–Cattle Pact (1935): a partial easing.
- [ ] E4 Political effect: Fianna Fáil presented it as the price of sovereignty and kept support; Cosgrave's party attacked it as damaging. (Farm economy and protectionism: neighbouring notes.)

The Blueshirts and challenges to the government, 1932–1936
- [ ] B1 Army Comrades Association (February 1932): ex-officers protecting Cumann na nGaedheal meetings from IRA disruption.
- [ ] B2 February 1933: de Valera dismissed Eoin O'Duffy as Garda Commissioner; O'Duffy took over the ACA (July 1933); blue shirt and salute; renamed the National Guard.
- [ ] B3 Planned march on Dublin prohibited; National Guard banned (August 1933).
- [ ] B4 Fine Gael formed (September 1933) from Cumann na nGaedheal, the Centre Party and the Blueshirts; O'Duffy forced out in 1934; Cosgrave leader.
- [ ] B5 IRA banned June 1936 (after the killing of Vice-Admiral Somerville).
- [ ] B6 O'Duffy's Irish Brigade to Spain, 1936–37 (about 700 men).

Bunreacht na hÉireann, 1937
- [ ] N1 Drafting: de Valera with civil servants John Hearne and Maurice Moynihan; Catholic clerical advice.
- [ ] N2 Plebiscite 1 July 1937 (about 56 to 44 per cent); in force 29 December 1937.
- [ ] N3 Name and status: Article 4 (Éire, or Ireland) and Article 5 (sovereign, independent, democratic state); the word republic not used.
- [ ] N4 Articles 2 and 3: the whole island as national territory; laws limited to 26 counties until reunification.
- [ ] N5 The President (seven-year term) and the Taoiseach.
- [ ] N6 Article 41: the family, the woman's life within the home, no divorce.
- [ ] N7 Article 44: the special position of the Catholic Church; other churches and the Jewish congregations named; removed 1972.
- [ ] N8 Reactions: unionists, women's groups and some nationalists critical; Douglas Hyde first President (inaugurated 25 June 1938).

The Anglo-Irish Agreements, 25 April 1938
- [ ] G1 Defence: Treaty ports at Berehaven, Spike Island (Cobh) and Lough Swilly handed over (July 1938).
- [ ] G2 Finance: annuities and other claims settled for £10 million.
- [ ] G3 Trade: special duties ended on both sides.
- [ ] G4 British motives (Chamberlain: better relations as war neared); Churchill's opposition; partition raised by de Valera but not moved.
- [ ] G5 Consequence: the ports made neutrality in 1939 possible (pointer to the Second World War notes; no detail here).

From Free State to Republic, 1948–1949
- [ ] R1 February 1948 election; Costello Taoiseach on 18 February at the head of an inter-party government (Fine Gael, Labour, Clann na Poblachta, Clann na Talmhan and others); Seán MacBride at External Affairs.
- [ ] R2 MacBride's wish to repeal the External Relations Act; Costello's announcement in Canada (September 1948).
- [ ] R3 Republic of Ireland Act passed December 1948; in force Easter Monday, 18 April 1949; the External Relations Act repealed; the state left the Commonwealth.
- [ ] R4 Ireland Act 1949 (UK): Ireland not a foreign country for British purposes; Northern Ireland's status changes only with the consent of the Parliament of Northern Ireland.
- [ ] R5 Anti-partition campaign of 1949: little achieved; unionist position strengthened.

Key personality: Éamon de Valera, part 2 and overall evaluation (one block, every point)
- [ ] P1 Who he was in this period: leader of Fianna Fáil in government 1932–48, 1951–54, 1957–59; President 1959–73; died 1975.
- [ ] P2 What he did, 1932–48: oath, Governor-General, Privy Council, 1936 legislation, the 1937 constitution, the 1938 agreements, the ban on the IRA (1936), neutrality.
- [ ] P3 Shaped by events: the Statute of Westminster, the abdication, the Blueshirts, the approach of war.
- [ ] P4 Participant: he drafted and negotiated personally, as his own foreign minister.
- [ ] P5 Contemporaries: supporters saw him as completing independence; opponents (Cumann na nGaedheal, Blueshirts) as a dictator or as damaging the economy for symbols.
- [ ] P6 Historians: Lee (credit for consolidating democracy, criticism of the economy); McMahon (diplomacy with Britain); Coogan (critical); Ferriter (balanced).
- [ ] P7 Failures: partition unended, the Economic War, high emigration and unemployment, a conservative Catholic social vision, censorship.

Assessment (paired judgement points, final block)
- [ ] S1 Cosgrave strengths: international recognition, gains within the Commonwealth, no economic conflict.
- [ ] S2 Cosgrave limits: symbols and ports left, Boundary Commission failure.
- [ ] S3 De Valera strengths: symbols removed, ports and annuities settled, constitution, neutrality made possible.
- [ ] S4 De Valera limits: Economic War, partition, reliance on Cosgrave's groundwork.
- [ ] S5 Contemporary view: Fianna Fáil (real sovereignty) against Fine Gael (symbols at an economic price).
- [ ] S6 Later view: the 1949 Republic a formal act after de Valera had already made independence real; neither leader achieved partition's end.

## 3. Traps

Do not use (wrong or doubtful facts in the current notes):
- Do not use: "Britain accepted these changes without military or economic action", because Britain did act economically (the special duties of the Economic War). Say Britain took no military action and did not accept that the Free State had left the Commonwealth.
- Do not use: "advice from clergy including the Archbishop of Dublin", because the Archbishop's part in the drafting is not established; name no archbishop. Say Catholic clerical advice was taken.
- Do not use: Ua Buachalla "with no official role", because he was a former TD; say a Fianna Fáil figure given a minimal role.
- Do not use: the "financial arrangement of 1923–25" as a single dated agreement; say the annuities were paid under arrangements of the 1920s, which de Valera and Britain read differently.
- Do not use: "Cosgrave became leader in 1935" as a precise date; say after O'Duffy's resignation in 1934 and by 1935.
- Do not use: "banned in August 1933" for the whole Blueshirt movement; it was the National Guard that was banned, and the movement continued under other names.
- Do not use: a cause for Costello's Ottawa announcement (a slight at a Canadian reception, a newspaper cartoon); the causes are disputed.
- Do not use: "the Republic of 1949 ended the Treaty" or "Ireland became independent in 1949"; independence was largely achieved by 1938, 1949 was the formal break with the Crown and Commonwealth.
- Do not use: the oath as the Treaty's only symbol; the Governor-General, the ports and the Crown were also symbols.

Confusable people, terms and dates
- Oath of allegiance (the Treaty oath to the Free State and the King) vs the oath removed in 1933 (the oath of fidelity to the King).
- The two December 1936 Acts: Amendment No. 27 (11 December, removed the Crown from the Constitution) vs the External Relations Act (12 December, restored a limited role for the King).
- Free State Constitution (1922) vs Bunreacht na hÉireann (1937); Éire (name, 1937) vs Republic of Ireland (description, 1949).
- Blueshirts (the movement) vs Fine Gael (the party formed in 1933) vs the Army Comrades Association (its first name).
- The 1938 Agreements (ports, annuities, trade) vs the Coal–Cattle Pact (1935).
- Republic of Ireland Act (1948, Irish) vs Ireland Act (1949, British).
- Costello (Taoiseach) vs MacBride (Minister for External Affairs).
- Fianna Fáil (1926) vs Sinn Féin; de Valera as President of the Executive Council (to 1937) then Taoiseach.
- Land annuities vs the later Land Acts they paid for; do not call them a tax.

## 4. What exams ask

- Higher 100-mark essay on Anglo-Irish relations, usually 1923–1949, or under de Valera 1932–45: wants a chronology with dates in two or three phases (Cosgrave's cooperation; confrontation and dismantling, 1932–38; settlement in 1938 and the republic in 1948–49), with a judgement. A list of Acts with no explanation scores poorly.
- "Cosgrave or de Valera" comparison: marks for a clear verdict at the start, paired strengths and limits for each, and the point that de Valera built on Cosgrave's Commonwealth work (Balfour, Westminster). Say what each achieved, not only what each wanted.
- De Valera's successes and failures or overall assessment: successes (symbols removed, ports, constitution, neutrality), failures (partition, Economic War, social and economic record). Credit for judgement of the short term against the long term and for naming a historian with a view.
- 40-mark Ordinary Level paragraph on de Valera's dismantling of the Treaty: each correct dated step earns marks: the annuities, the Economic War, the oath (May 1933), the Privy Council, the 1936 Acts, the constitution, the 1938 ports. Dates in the right order and a link between cause and result (for example, the annuities to the Economic War) are the commonest marks.
- Cause-and-result questions: why the Economic War began and how it ended; why Britain gave up the ports in 1938; why the Republic was declared in 1948 and what the British replied.
- Constitution questions: what Articles 2–3, 41 and 44 said, and why critics objected; the President's role. Marks for stating what the article said and one reaction, not for the article number alone.
- Document-style short answers: who the writer was, what the 1938 agreement or the 1937 constitution contained, and what the republic changed.
