## 1. Must cover (one bullet or block for every item; tick each off)

Background (assumed)
- [ ] A1 (assumed) The Act of Union (1801) abolished the Irish parliament; Irish MPs sat at Westminster.
- [ ] A2 (assumed) O'Connell's Repeal campaign (1840s) and the Young Ireland rising (1848) failed.
- [ ] A3 (assumed) The Fenians (IRB) wanted an Irish republic by force; the 1867 rising failed and Fenian prisoners were tried.
- [ ] A4 (assumed) Gladstone's first ministry (from December 1868): disestablishment of the Church of Ireland (1869) and the 1870 Land Act, whose limits left tenants insecure.
- [ ] A5 (assumed) Irish MPs in the 1860s were mostly Liberals or Conservatives, not a separate Irish party; Westminster decided Irish affairs.
- [ ] A6 (assumed) Clan na Gael: the Irish-American Fenian organisation, source of money and support.

Key concepts (each defined as used in this topic; also in keyTerms)
- [ ] K1 Home Rule: an Irish parliament for domestic affairs within the United Kingdom and empire; not separation.
- [ ] K2 Democracy: government by elected representatives answerable to voters; here the secret ballot, a disciplined party and the widening of the voters' influence.
- [ ] K3 Political agitation: organised public pressure (meetings, newspapers, petitions, elections, parliamentary tactics) rather than violence.

Origins and Butt (1870–1879)
- [ ] B1 Why Home Rule emerged around 1870: failure of repeal, rebellion and Fenian conspiracy; the amnesty movement; disappointment with Gladstone's reforms; a constitutional alternative that might include Protestants.
- [ ] B2 Isaac Butt's background: Donegal-born Protestant, Dublin barrister, Trinity professor, former Conservative MP; his turn to nationalism through defending Young Irelanders and Fenians.
- [ ] B3 Butt's federal idea: an Irish parliament for domestic affairs, Westminster for empire, defence and foreign policy.
- [ ] B4 The Home Government Association (Dublin, May 1870): its mixed membership.
- [ ] B5 The Limerick by-election (1871).
- [ ] B6 The Ballot Act (1872): the secret ballot, reduced landlord pressure (and the Galway petition on clerical pressure).
- [ ] B7 The Home Rule League (November 1873): independent party pledged to act together.
- [ ] B8 The 1874 election: the first sizeable Home Rule party.
- [ ] B9 Why Butt failed: weak party discipline, mixed loyalties of MPs, heavy defeats of his motions, reliance on persuasion, little money or local organisation, rivals' impatience, ill health and debt.
- [ ] B10 Butt's death (1879) and the party's drift: leadership passes to the obstructionists.

Obstruction and Parnell's entry (1875–1881)
- [ ] O1 Obstruction defined; Joseph Biggar's role (Cavan, 1874) and background.
- [ ] O2 Parnell's election for Meath (1875) and growing role in obstruction from 1877.
- [ ] O3 Parnell's break with Butt's caution: his defence of the Manchester Martyrs and the Fenian respect it won.
- [ ] O4 Parnell replaces Butt as president of the Home Rule Confederation of Great Britain (August 1877).
- [ ] O5 The all-night sitting of January–February 1881 and the closure reform (1882): obstruction's lasting effect and why parliament condemned it.
- [ ] O6 John O'Connor Power and others who demanded a more aggressive policy.

The New Departure (1878–1879)
- [ ] N1 The agricultural depression from 1877 and the threat of evictions.
- [ ] N2 Michael Davitt: Fenian, convicted 1870, released December 1877; his 1878 American visit and meeting with John Devoy.
- [ ] N3 Devoy's offer (October 1878): Clan na Gael support in return for a more active programme (self-government, land, independent action by MPs).
- [ ] N4 Parnell's response: no formal acceptance, but a public alliance with Davitt; the New Departure as a link between Fenians, land agitation and constitutional politics.
- [ ] N5 Strains: IRB Supreme Council's opposition; Davitt (land nationalisation) vs Parnell (peasant proprietorship).

Parnell takes control (1879–1885)
- [ ] P1 The Land League of Mayo (Irishtown, 1879) and the Irish National Land League (October 1879): Parnell president, Davitt organiser.
- [ ] P2 The American tour of 1880 with Dillon: money and publicity.
- [ ] P3 The 1880 election: Parnell's three seats and choice of Cork City.
- [ ] P4 Chairman of the Irish Parliamentary Party (May 1880): the vote against William Shaw.
- [ ] P5 The press: United Ireland (1881), edited by William O'Brien.
- [ ] P6 Kilmainham: coercion, arrest (13 October 1881), the No Rent Manifesto, suppression of the Land League.
- [ ] P7 The Kilmainham "treaty" (April–May 1882): O'Shea as go-between, release 2 May, the Arrears Act; what each side gained; why it is called an understanding and not a treaty.
- [ ] P8 The Phoenix Park murders (6 May 1882): victims, the Invincibles, Parnell's reaction, the Prevention of Crimes Act, the hangings in 1883 after Carey's evidence.
- [ ] P9 The Irish National League (October 1882): aims, structure, parish branches, Parnell's personal control.
- [ ] P10 The party machine: the pledge and the constituency conventions (1884–85), clerical involvement, the end of the independent MP.
- [ ] P11 Funding from the Irish diaspora and local collections; the Parnell Tribute (1883) and the Vatican's disapproval.
- [ ] P12 Result by 1885: a mass movement, a disciplined party and the likelihood of holding the balance of power (the 1884 Reform Act and 1885 election belong to the next subsection).

Key personality: Charles Stewart Parnell, part 1 (one block, every point)
- [ ] C1 Who he was: Avondale, Co. Wicklow, 1846; Protestant landowning family; American mother; Cambridge; High Sheriff of Wicklow.
- [ ] C2 What he did, with dates: Meath 1875; obstruction; Confederation 1877; Land League 1879; chairman 1880; Kilmainham; National League 1882; the pledge.
- [ ] C3 Participant: he organised, led and negotiated (with Davitt, Devoy, Gladstone's government).
- [ ] C4 Shaped by events: the agricultural depression, Fenian and American pressure, coercion, imprisonment and the Phoenix Park murders.
- [ ] C5 His methods and words in this period: Westport (1879), Ennis (1880), Cork (1885, on the "march of a nation").
- [ ] C6 Contemporaries: supporters (admiration and devotion) vs critics (The Times; unionists; the Vatican on the Tribute; moderates in his party).
- [ ] C7 Historians: Lyons on his skill and the strains in his alliance; others on a pragmatic parliamentarian steering the Land War into politics.
- [ ] C8 Leave his later career (1886–91) and fall to the later notes; one pointer only.

Assessment: Butt and Parnell compared (final block, paired points)
- [ ] S1 Butt's achievements: founded a respectable constitutional national party; about 60 seats in 1874.
- [ ] S2 Butt's limits: no discipline, money or mass base; no concession won.
- [ ] S3 Parnell's achievements: discipline (pledge, conventions), press, American money, mass base through the land movement, balance-of-power position by 1885.
- [ ] S4 Parnell's limits: dependence on the land movement and Fenian allies he could not fully control; personal control rather than a lasting structure; no Home Rule yet.
- [ ] S5 Contrast in method: persuasion of English opinion vs disruption and organisation.
- [ ] S6 Contrast in social base: gentlemen and lawyers vs a parish-based mass movement.
- [ ] S7 Overall judgement: Butt prepared the ground and Parnell built the machine; Kilmainham read as sensible deal vs retreat.

## 3. Traps

Do not use (wrong or doubtful facts in the current notes):
- Do not use: the Manchester Martyrs remark dated 1877, because it was made in the Commons in 1876 (the exact date is not needed; give no date).
- Do not use: "Butt thought the land question a distraction and accepted the 1870 Act", because Butt wrote and spoke in favour of tenant right and the Three Fs; the contrast is that Parnell made land the engine of the national movement, not that Butt ignored it.
- Do not use: "most Catholic tenant farmers had no vote" as a cause of Butt's weakness, because the claim is doubtful; the sure point is that the electorate was small (widened only in 1884) and the League had no local organisation.
- Do not use: a vote count for Butt's defeated motions, and the Amnesty Association "leader" label for Butt; say only that his motions were heavily defeated and that he took part in the amnesty campaign.
- Do not use: "1877" for Parnell's active obstruction as the start of obstruction itself; Biggar began it in 1874–75 and Parnell joined in earnest from 1877.
- Do not use: the Kilmainham arrangement as "a treaty" without saying it was an unwritten understanding.

## 4. What exams ask

- Higher 100-mark essay, the commonest form: "Home Rule movement developed from Butt to Parnell, 1870–1886" or "Butt or Parnell as the more effective leader". Marks go to a clear thesis stated at the start (Parnell the more effective, because of the pledge, conventions, press and mass base) and a paired comparison with dated evidence for each leader.
- Strong answers give Butt credit (founding a constitutional, cross-class, respectable movement; 1874 result) as well as limits; one-sided dismissal of Butt scores lower.
- Development essays need chronology: 1870 Association, 1873 League, 1874 election, 1877 obstruction, 1878–79 New Departure, 1879 Land League, 1880 chairmanship, 1882 National League, 1884–85 pledge. Each dated step with its significance earns marks; a list without explanation does not.
- The strongest essays explain how Home Rule changed from respectable persuasion to organised agitation, and link the land movement to Home Rule (the New Departure).
- Ordinary Level 30-mark paragraph on Butt: background (Protestant barrister, Donegal, former Conservative), the 1870 Association, federal Home Rule, 1873 League, 1874 result, weaknesses. Each correct fact earns a mark.
- Ordinary Level 40-mark paragraph on Parnell: obstruction with Biggar from 1877, Confederation 1877, Land League 1879, American tour 1880, chairman 1880, Kilmainham, National League, pledge. Name at least three of these with dates.
- Documents-based question context (Home Rule documents, 2006 style): the notes must let a student place a speech, newspaper report or cartoon by naming organisations, dates and the views of each side; part (d) rewards Parnell's strengths and weaknesses with specific evidence (organisation, tactics, uniting Fenians, land and parliament; but dependence on alliances and his reliance on personal control).
- Short-answer topics: what obstruction was and why it worked; what the New Departure was and who made it; what happened at Kilmainham; what the pledge did; why the Phoenix Park murders harmed Parnell.
- Questions on successes and failures of Home Rule over a longer period use this material as the first stage; link forward in one sentence only.
