## 1. Must cover (one bullet or block for every item; tick each off)

Background
- [ ] B1 (assumed) Industrialisation created a large factory working class, concentrated in fast-growing towns, with long hours, low pay, child labour and slum housing, and no vote; this is the problem every movement in this subsection responded to (one bullet; the conditions themselves are in the notes on the changing economy and society).
- [ ] B2 (assumed) Britain before 1832 was ruled by a Parliament elected by a small, mostly propertied electorate, with "rotten" and "pocket" boroughs (tiny electorates controlled by a patron) while new industrial towns such as Manchester and Birmingham had no MPs of their own.
- [ ] B3 (assumed) Responses came in three kinds, which essays compare: practical self-help (trade unions, co-operatives), new theories (socialism, communism, anarchism), and political campaigns for the vote and for reform from above (Chartism, Peel).

Key concepts (each defined as used in this topic; also in keyTerms)
- [ ] K1 Socialism: common or public ownership of the means of production (land, factories, mines) so that wealth is shared; the word came into use in the 1820s–30s, before Marx.
- [ ] K2 Chartism: the British working-class movement (1838 to the 1850s) for the six points of the People's Charter.
- [ ] K3 Democracy: government by the people through elected representatives chosen by all (here, all adult men); the Chartists, the French Second Republic and Bismarck's North German Confederation each extended it.
- [ ] K4 Laissez-faire: the belief that the state should leave trade, prices, wages and hours to the free market; Peel's free trade applied it, while the Factory Acts showed its limits.
- [ ] K5 Essential terms: trade union; combination; New Model unionism; utopian socialism; communism; historical materialism; class struggle; bourgeoisie and proletariat; surplus value; dictatorship of the proletariat; anarchism; moral force and physical force; franchise / suffrage; free trade; Corn Laws.

Trade unions
- [ ] U1 The Combination Acts (1799, 1800) banned workers combining to raise wages or change conditions, out of fear of revolution after 1789.
- [ ] U2 Repeal in 1824, after a campaign by Francis Place and Joseph Hume; a wave of strikes followed, so the 1825 Act let unions bargain over wages and hours but restricted strike action (intimidation, "molestation").
- [ ] U3 The Tolpuddle Martyrs (1834): six Dorset farm labourers led by George Loveless, sentenced to seven years' transportation to Australia for swearing a secret oath; mass protest; pardoned in 1836.
- [ ] U4 The Grand National Consolidated Trades Union (1834): an attempt at one union for all trades, with Robert Owen its leading figure; it collapsed within months (lockouts, the Tolpuddle case, disputes, lack of funds). One block or bullet here; Owen's own part is in the New Lanark notes.
- [ ] U5 New Model unions: the Amalgamated Society of Engineers (1851): skilled men only, a high subscription, full-time officials, a central fund paying sick, unemployment and funeral benefits, and caution about strikes; copied by the carpenters (1860); their London leaders were known as "the Junta".
- [ ] U6 Limits: unions were mainly for skilled craftsmen; unskilled workers stayed largely unorganised until later in the century.
- [ ] U7 The Trades Union Congress first met in 1868 (Manchester); the Trade Union Act 1871 gave unions legal recognition and protected their funds, while the Criminal Law Amendment Act of the same year restricted picketing (one bullet; the end of the period).
- [ ] U8 The continent: in France the Le Chapelier Law (1791) banned combinations, Napoleon III granted the right to strike in 1864 and tolerated unions from 1868; in the German lands Lassalle founded the General German Workers' Association (1863) and Bebel and Liebknecht the Social Democratic Workers' Party at Eisenach (1869). One bullet each; exams on "Europe" reward going beyond Britain.

Socialism before Marx
- [ ] S1 Utopian socialists (background, one block): Henri de Saint-Simon (society run by scientists and industrialists for the benefit of "the poorest and most numerous class"); Charles Fourier (co-operative communities called phalanxes, in buildings called phalanstères); Robert Owen (New Lanark, New Harmony, co-operation; one clause only here).
- [ ] S2 Louis Blanc, The Organisation of Labour (1839): state-funded "social workshops" run by workers; his ideas lay behind the 1848 demand for the right to work (the National Workshops are in the notes on the State in France; one clause).
- [ ] S3 Pierre-Joseph Proudhon, What is Property? (1840): "Property is theft"; the first writer to call himself an anarchist; an influence on Bakunin.
- [ ] S4 The Communist League (1847, London, grew out of the League of the Just, a society of German exile workers) commissioned Marx and Engels to write its programme, the Communist Manifesto.
- [ ] S5 Why "utopian": Marx and Engels used the word for socialists who relied on model communities and appeals to the rich, and called their own theory "scientific socialism".

Key personality: Karl Marx
- [ ] M1 Who he was: born in Trier in the Prussian Rhineland in 1818, of a Jewish family that had converted to Protestant Christianity; studied law and then philosophy (Bonn, Berlin), influenced by Hegel.
- [ ] M2 Journalist and exile: editor of the Rheinische Zeitung, Cologne (1842), closed by the government in 1843; Paris (1843–45), where he met Engels (1844); expelled from Paris (1845); Brussels (1845–48).
- [ ] M3 Friedrich Engels: son of a German textile manufacturer, worked in the family firm's Manchester business, wrote on Manchester's workers (one clause), co-author, and financial supporter of Marx for decades.
- [ ] M4 The Communist Manifesto (February 1848, London): history as class struggle; the bourgeoisie (owners) and proletariat (wage workers); capitalism would produce its own gravediggers; the closing call to the workers of all countries to unite. Little immediate effect on the revolutions of 1848.
- [ ] M5 Participant in 1848–49: returned to Germany, edited the Neue Rheinische Zeitung in Cologne; expelled in May 1849; settled in London in 1849, where he lived until his death in 1883, often in poverty, studying in the British Museum reading room.
- [ ] M6 Historical materialism: the economic base (who owns the means of production) shapes politics, law, religion and ideas; each stage of history (feudalism, capitalism) is ended by class struggle; capitalism would be overthrown by a proletarian revolution, followed by a workers' state ("dictatorship of the proletariat") and then a classless communist society in which the state would wither away.
- [ ] M7 Das Kapital, volume 1 (1867): the theory of surplus value, that profit comes from the unpaid part of workers' labour; Engels edited volumes 2 and 3 after Marx's death.
- [ ] M8 The First International (International Workingmen's Association), London, 1864: Marx wrote its inaugural address and dominated its General Council; it linked British trade unionists with continental socialists.
- [ ] M9 Other writings, one clause each: The Eighteenth Brumaire of Louis Bonaparte (1852) on Louis-Napoleon's coup; The Civil War in France (1871), defending the Paris Commune.
- [ ] M10 How contemporaries saw him: little known to the wider public in the 1850s and 1860s; watched by Prussian police agents; famous and feared after he defended the Paris Commune in 1871; quarrelsome with rivals (Bakunin, Lassalle).
- [ ] M11 How historians judge him: the most influential socialist thinker; his ideas shaped later socialist parties and twentieth-century communist states; his prediction that revolution would come first in the most advanced industrial countries was not fulfilled, and workers' living standards rose; his analysis of class and economics influenced history, sociology and economics.
- [ ] M12 Comparison material, one bullet each: Marx vs Metternich (conflict and inevitable change vs order, hierarchy and preventing change; Metternich's own views are in the notes on his system); Marx vs Darwin (both offered a theory of change through struggle over time; Darwin's own work is in the notes on science); Marx vs Bakunin (M/BK block below).

Key personality: Mikhail Bakunin
- [ ] BK1 Who he was: born in 1814 into a Russian noble family; served as an army officer but resigned (1835); studied philosophy in Moscow and Berlin and became a revolutionary in the 1840s.
- [ ] BK2 1848–49, participant: the Slav Congress in Prague (June 1848); the Dresden rising (May 1849); arrested, sentenced to death in Saxony and again in Austria, each time commuted.
- [ ] BK3 Prison, exile and escape: handed to Russia (1851); held in the Peter and Paul Fortress and Schlüsselburg; exiled to Siberia (1857); escaped in 1861 by way of Japan and the United States to London.
- [ ] BK4 Ideas: anarchism, abolition of the state, organised religion and all coercive authority; revolution by spontaneous risings of peasants and the poor as well as workers, not only an industrial proletariat; secret revolutionary societies. "The passion for destruction is a creative passion."
- [ ] BK5 Split with Marx: Bakunin joined the International (1868); he argued that Marx's workers' state would become a new tyranny of a ruling minority, while Marx wanted workers' parties to win political power; Bakunin and his ally James Guillaume were expelled at the Hague Congress (1872); the General Council moved to New York and the International faded (dissolved 1876).
- [ ] BK6 Later life: Statism and Anarchy (1873); died in 1876 in Bern, Switzerland.
- [ ] BK7 Influence: strongest in Spain, Italy and the Swiss Jura; contemporaries saw him as a dangerous conspirator or a heroic rebel; historians see him as the founder of the anarchist movement, less influential than Marx on mass labour parties and a poor organiser.
- [ ] BK8 Marx vs Bakunin compared (one block or paired bullets): state vs no state; organised industrial workers vs all the oppressed including peasants; theory and patient organisation vs spontaneous insurrection; London theorist vs participant on the barricades.

Mass politics: the 1832 Reform Act and the franchise
- [ ] F1 The Reform Act 1832 (Earl Grey's Whig government): in the boroughs, men occupying property worth £10 a year got the vote; 56 small boroughs lost both their MPs and 30 lost one; seats went to industrial towns such as Manchester, Birmingham, Leeds and Sheffield; still only about one adult man in five in England and Wales could vote, and almost no workers.
- [ ] F2 Later British reform (one bullet): the Second Reform Act (1867) gave the vote to many urban working men; the secret ballot (1872); near-universal male suffrage only in 1918.
- [ ] F3 France: the Second Republic introduced universal male suffrage in March 1848, raising the electorate from fewer than 250,000 to about nine million; Louis-Napoleon kept it and used plebiscites (one clause; the notes on the State in France cover this).
- [ ] F4 The North German Confederation (1867): the Reichstag elected by universal male suffrage, kept by the German Empire in 1871; Bismarck hoped conservative rural voters would outvote the liberals.
- [ ] F5 The Anti-Corn Law League (1838, Manchester; Richard Cobden and John Bright): a middle-class pressure group with lecturers, pamphlets, a national fund and mass meetings, a model of how organised public opinion could change policy; it succeeded where Chartism failed.

Chartism
- [ ] C1 Causes: disappointment with the 1832 Act; anger at the Poor Law Amendment Act (1834) and its workhouses; the depression from 1837 and unemployment; the decline of handloom weavers; the failure of trade unionism after 1834.
- [ ] C2 The London Working Men's Association (1836, William Lovett, a cabinet-maker); the People's Charter (May 1838), drafted by Lovett with help from Francis Place.
- [ ] C3 The six points: universal male suffrage; the secret ballot; annual parliaments; equal electoral districts; abolition of the property qualification for MPs; payment of MPs.
- [ ] C4 Organisation: the National Convention (1839); the National Charter Association (1840), the first working-class political party in effect, with local branches and paid-up members; the Northern Star (O'Connor's newspaper).
- [ ] C5 The petitions: 1839 (about 1.3 million signatures), 1842 (over three million), 1848 (claimed about 5.7 million, counted at under two million, with many false names); each rejected by the Commons.
- [ ] C6 The Newport Rising (November 1839, south Wales): John Frost led armed men into Newport; troops fired and about twenty were killed; Frost and two others were sentenced to death, commuted to transportation for life.
- [ ] C7 The Plug Plot strikes (August 1842): strikers in Lancashire, Yorkshire and the Midlands pulled the plugs from factory boilers; mass arrests followed.
- [ ] C8 The Chartist Land Plan (National Land Company, 1845): members bought shares and drew lots for smallholdings on estates such as O'Connorville (Hertfordshire); it also aimed to give smallholders the county vote; a parliamentary inquiry found it legally and financially unsound and it was wound up (1851).
- [ ] C9 Kennington Common, 10 April 1848: a mass meeting to march the petition to Parliament; the government banned the procession, enrolled tens of thousands of special constables and brought troops under Wellington; O'Connor persuaded the crowd to disperse and the petition went in cabs; the forged signatures brought ridicule.
- [ ] C10 Moral force (Lovett: education, persuasion) vs physical force (George Julian Harney and others; O'Connor's speeches threatened force while he avoided armed risings): the division weakened the movement.
- [ ] C11 Why Chartism failed: divisions in leadership and methods; government repression (arrests, troops, the new railways and telegraph moving forces); Parliament's refusal; little middle-class support, which went to the Anti-Corn Law League; it rose in slumps and faded in good times, and the prosperity of the 1850s ended it; the 1848 fiasco; Ernest Jones kept it alive into the late 1850s.
- [ ] C12 Its achievement: the first national working-class political movement; five of the six points were later law (property qualification abolished 1858, secret ballot 1872, payment of MPs 1911, manhood suffrage 1918, more equal districts); only annual parliaments never came.

Key personality: Feargus O'Connor
- [ ] O1 Who he was: born in Co. Cork in 1796 into a Protestant landed family with a radical tradition (nephew of the United Irishman Arthur O'Connor); trained as a barrister.
- [ ] O2 Irish politics: elected MP for Co. Cork in 1832 as a supporter of O'Connell and Repeal; unseated in 1835 because he lacked the property qualification; quarrelled with O'Connell and moved into English radical politics.
- [ ] O3 The Northern Star, founded in Leeds in 1837: the most widely read radical paper in Britain, often read aloud in pubs and workplaces; it made him the movement's voice.
- [ ] O4 Leader and participant: toured the industrial North as a speaker; dominated the National Charter Association; jailed for seditious libel (York, 1840–41); involved in the 1839, 1842 and 1848 campaigns; presented the 1848 petition.
- [ ] O5 The Land Plan was his scheme (C8): his belief in returning workers to the land.
- [ ] O6 MP for Nottingham (1847), the only Chartist elected to Parliament.
- [ ] O7 Kennington Common (1848) and decline: after 1848 his authority waned; mental breakdown in 1852, confined in an asylum, died in 1855; his funeral drew huge crowds.
- [ ] O8 How contemporaries saw him: hero to many workers ("the Lion of Freedom"); a vain, reckless demagogue to Lovett, the moral-force men, middle-class reformers and the government.
- [ ] O9 How historians judge him: older accounts, drawing on his opponents, blamed him for Chartism's failure; Dorothy Thompson and James Epstein see him as a gifted speaker and organiser who held a national movement together and spoke for the rank and file.
- [ ] O10 O'Connor vs Marx compared (paired bullets): vote and land within the British state vs revolution and the end of capitalism; mass agitation vs theory and an international organisation; immediate failure for both in 1848, longer-term influence for both.

Key personality: Robert Peel
- [ ] P1 Who he was: born in 1788 near Bury, Lancashire, son of a rich cotton manufacturer (Sir Robert Peel, the elder); Harrow and Oxford; MP from 1809.
- [ ] P2 Early career, one bullet: Chief Secretary for Ireland (1812–18); chaired the committee that brought Britain back to the gold standard (1819).
- [ ] P3 Home Secretary (1822–27, 1828–30): reformed the criminal law, removing the death penalty for many offences, and the prisons.
- [ ] P4 The Metropolitan Police (1829): a professional, uniformed, unarmed force for London (except the City), with headquarters at Scotland Yard; police called "Peelers" and "Bobbies"; a model for forces across Britain.
- [ ] P5 Catholic emancipation (1829), with Wellington, after O'Connell's Clare election, to avoid conflict in Ireland; it angered many Tories (one bullet; it is mainly Irish history).
- [ ] P6 The Tamworth Manifesto (December 1834): accepted the 1832 Reform Act as final and promised careful reform of real abuses while preserving established institutions; seen as the founding statement of the Conservative Party.
- [ ] P7 Prime Minister 1834–35 and 1841–46.
- [ ] P8 Finance and free trade: income tax reintroduced in 1842 (7d in the pound on incomes over £150, meant to be temporary), which let him cut or abolish duties on hundreds of imports in the budgets of 1842 and 1845; trade and revenue grew.
- [ ] P9 The Bank Charter Act (1844): note issue gradually concentrated in the Bank of England, with notes beyond a fixed amount backed by gold.
- [ ] P10 Social measures of his government: the Mines Act (1842); the Factory Act (1844): children 8–13 limited to six and a half hours a day, women to twelve, dangerous machinery to be fenced; the Railway Act (1844, Gladstone at the Board of Trade) with cheap "parliamentary trains". Peel opposed the Ten Hours Bill, which passed in 1847 after his fall.
- [ ] P11 Repeal of the Corn Laws (1846): influenced by free-trade arguments and the Anti-Corn Law League and made urgent by the Irish potato blight (1845); passed with Whig votes while most Conservatives voted against; duties phased out to a nominal level by 1849; Peel was defeated on an Irish Coercion Bill the same night and resigned (June 1846).
- [ ] P12 Results: the Conservatives split into protectionists (Disraeli, Derby) and Peelites (including Gladstone), who later joined the Liberals; the Conservatives were long out of majority power.
- [ ] P13 Death in 1850 after a fall from his horse.
- [ ] P14 How contemporaries saw him: Disraeli and the protectionists called him a traitor to his party; the middle class and many workers praised cheap bread and sound finance.
- [ ] P15 How historians judge him: Norman Gash rates him one of the greatest statesmen of the century, an administrator who put the national interest above party; critics note that he twice split his party (1829, 1846) and resisted wider franchise and the Ten Hours Act.

Owen and Peel as reformers compared (Higher essay material; Owen's own story is the New Lanark case study)
- [ ] R1 Background: both sons of the textile industry (Owen a self-made Welsh mill manager; Peel the son of a cotton millionaire).
- [ ] R2 Method: Owen by example, model communities, education and co-operation outside Parliament; Peel by legislation and administration from inside government.
- [ ] R3 Aims: Owen to replace competition with co-operation and common ownership; Peel to preserve property, Church and Crown by making the state efficient and trade free.
- [ ] R4 Link: Owen's campaign helped bring about the 1819 Factory Act, introduced by Peel's father.
- [ ] R5 Reach: Peel's changes (police, tax, free trade, Bank Act, Factory and Mines Acts) affected the whole country and lasted; Owen's New Lanark worked but was rarely copied, New Harmony and the GNCTU failed, but his ideas inspired the co-operative movement (the Rochdale Pioneers, 1844) and factory reform.
- [ ] R6 A verdict: most answers judge Peel the more effective in practice and Owen the more original; marks come from evidence on both sides.

Assessment (end with this block, headed "Assessment: …")
- [ ] J1 Trade unions: legal after 1824 and recognised in 1871, with stable skilled unions after 1851; but repression (Tolpuddle), the GNCTU collapse and the exclusion of the unskilled.
- [ ] J2 Socialism: a new and lasting body of ideas and an international organisation (1864); but tiny support before 1871, divided (Marx vs Bakunin), and no revolution came.
- [ ] J3 Chartism: a mass movement that kept democracy on the agenda and whose points were mostly achieved later; but every petition failed and it was gone by the late 1850s.
- [ ] J4 Mass politics: manhood suffrage in France (1848) and the North German Confederation (1867), while Britain moved slowly (1832, 1867); the vote was widened more often from above than won from below.
- [ ] J5 Peel: lasting reform from above (police, finance, free trade, factory law); but no extension of the vote and a divided party.

## 3. Traps

Do not use (doubtful or wrong in the current notes):
- Do not use "the 1842 petition ... the third and largest": 1842 was the second of the three petitions (1839, 1842, 1848). It had the largest genuine number of signatures (C5).
- Do not use "Robert Owen's ... experiments at New Lanark and New Harmony ... showed that cooperative communities could be run successfully": New Harmony failed within a few years. New Lanark was a profitable mill village under a benevolent manager, not a co-operative community (S1, R5).
- Do not use "Utopian socialists such as Henri de Saint-Simon and Charles Fourier planned model communities": Fourier did; Saint-Simon wanted the whole of society run by experts and industrialists. Use the S1 wording.
- Do not use "most of the European governments kept him under surveillance" or "contemporaries in the 1850s and 1860s saw him as a dangerous exile": Marx was little known to the wider public until 1871. Say Prussian police agents watched the exiles, and that he became notorious after defending the Paris Commune (M10).
- Do not use "before Marx gave it a scientific basis" as fact: "scientific socialism" was Marx and Engels's own claim. Attribute it to them (S5).
- Do not use "the Reform Act of 1832 gave the vote to male householders in the boroughs who paid rent of £10 a year": the qualification was owning or occupying property with an annual value of £10 (F1).
- Do not use "Owen's reforms were confined to one community": he also campaigned for factory law, led the GNCTU and inspired co-operation. Say New Lanark was rarely copied (R5).
- Do not use "he left the army and became a revolutionary in the 1840s" as one event: Bakunin resigned his commission in 1835 and became a revolutionary in the 1840s (BK1).
- Do not use "lost the seat in 1835" without the reason: O'Connor was unseated because he lacked the property qualification (O2).
- Do not use "the GNCTU, founded in 1834 under Robert Owen's leadership" as if Owen founded it; say Owen was its leading figure (U4).
- Do not use "Chartism ... 1838–1858" as if the movement was strong throughout; it was a mass movement in 1838–48 and faded in the 1850s (C11).

Other errors and myths to avoid:
- Marx did not offer to dedicate Das Kapital to Darwin; that story is a myth. He admired On the Origin of Species and sent Darwin a copy of Das Kapital (second edition, 1873). Use only the comparison of ideas (M12).
- "Thesis, antithesis, synthesis" is not Marx's or Hegel's own formula. Do not use it.
- Bakunin was not the first anarchist: Proudhon first called himself one (1840). Do not attribute "Property is theft" to Bakunin or Marx (S3).
- Do not attribute the Catechism of a Revolutionary (1869) to Bakunin; its authorship is disputed. Leave it out.
- The Communist Manifesto did not cause the 1848 revolutions; it appeared just before them and had little immediate influence (M4).
- The 1844 Factory Act lowered the minimum age to 8 while cutting children's hours; do not call it a ban on child labour (P10).
- Peel did not introduce the Ten Hours Act; he opposed it (P10).
- Repeal of the Corn Laws did not end duties overnight; they fell to a nominal level by 1849 (P11).
- Peel founded the Metropolitan Police for London only, not a national police force; the City of London kept its own force (P4).
- No figures beyond section 2: no GNCTU membership, no union membership totals, no exact electorate numbers for 1832, no special-constable or Kennington crowd totals, no death toll beyond "about twenty" at Newport, no exact number of tariffs cut.
- Do not copy figures or phrases that appear only in past-paper documents (for example the details of the 2024 document on Peel's police).
- No quotations except those in section 2, each attributed as given there.

Confusables:
- Sir Robert Peel the elder (1819 Factory Act, Owen's campaign) vs Sir Robert Peel the Prime Minister (1844 Factory Act).
- Rheinische Zeitung (1842–43) vs Neue Rheinische Zeitung (1848–49), both Cologne.
- Communist League (1847, commissioned the Manifesto) vs the First International (1864) vs the Hague Congress (1872, the split).
- Marx's Manifesto (February 1848) vs Das Kapital (volume 1, 1867).
- Lovett (moral force, LWMA, drafted the Charter) vs O'Connor (Northern Star, Land Plan, physical-force rhetoric) vs Frost (Newport) vs Harney (physical force) vs Ernest Jones (1850s).
- The three petitions: 1839, 1842, 1848. Newport (1839) vs Plug Plot (1842) vs Kennington (1848).
- National Charter Association (1840, political organisation) vs National Land Company (1845, land scheme).
- Anti-Corn Law League (middle class, Cobden and Bright, succeeded 1846) vs Chartism (working class, failed).
- Combination Acts 1799–1800 (ban) vs repeal 1824 vs Act of 1825 (restrictions) vs Trade Union Act 1871 (recognition).
- GNCTU (1834, all trades, collapsed) vs ASE (1851, skilled engineers, lasted).
- Tamworth Manifesto (1834, Peel) vs the Communist Manifesto (1848, Marx and Engels).
- Lassalle's General German Workers' Association (1863) vs the Eisenach party (1869, Bebel and Liebknecht).
- Proudhon (anarchist ideas, 1840) vs Bakunin (anarchist movement, split with Marx).
- Universal male suffrage: France 1848, North German Confederation 1867, Britain only 1918.

Belongs to neighbouring subsections (one clause at most here):
- New Lanark, Owen's life and reforms, A New View of Society and the Institute: the New Lanark case study. Here Owen appears only as a utopian socialist, as the GNCTU's leading figure and in the Owen–Peel comparison.

## 4. What exams ask (topics only)

- Higher 100-mark essays on two or three of Marx, Bakunin and O'Connor (the most common personality pairing here): marks go for each person's aims, methods and achievements with dates, and an explicit comparison. Structure answers person by person or point by point, but make a judgement (for example who achieved more by 1871, or whose ideas lasted).
- Higher essays on Owen and Peel as reformers ("who was more effective", "compare their aims and methods"): the most frequent Higher question for this subsection. Credit goes for Peel's specific measures with dates (police 1829, Tamworth 1834, income tax 1842, Bank Charter and Factory Acts 1844, repeal 1846), Owen's methods and failures, the 1819 link, and a clear verdict supported on both sides.
- Higher essays on Owen, O'Connor and Peel together, or Owen, Marx and Bakunin together: students need short, dated summaries of each and the contrast of methods (example, agitation, theory, insurrection, legislation).
- Higher essays on trade unionism and socialism, or on whether they responded better to industrial change than mass politics: give union developments in order (1799–1800, 1824–25, 1834, 1851, 1868, 1871), socialism from utopians to Marx and Bakunin, then Chartism, 1832 and manhood suffrage abroad, and weigh results (achievements vs limits). Examples from France and the German lands raise an answer above a Britain-only one.
- Higher essays comparing Marx with Metternich (views of society, change and the state) or with Darwin (influence on ideas): supply Marx's ideas clearly (class struggle, historical materialism, inevitable revolution, workers' state) and say how each thinker's ideas were received.
- Ordinary Level 30- and 40-mark paragraphs on Marx (the most frequent single person): birth, Rheinische Zeitung, expulsions, London, Manifesto with Engels, historical materialism, Das Kapital, First International. Each SRP is one specific dated fact, so short, dated statements score best.
- Ordinary paragraphs on Bakunin (birth and noble family, Prague and Dresden, death sentences, prison and Siberia, escape, anarchism, Hague 1872), O'Connor (Cork, barrister, O'Connell, Northern Star, NCA, prison, Land Company, Nottingham, Kennington), Peel (police, Catholic emancipation, Tamworth, income tax, Factory Act, repeal), Chartism (causes, six points, petitions, Newport, failure) and early trade unions (Combination Acts, repeal, Tolpuddle, GNCTU, ASE).
- Ordinary Level document questions: on Marx and the Manifesto (where and when published, his background, his main work, what he predicted the workers would do, why industrialisation produced a working class) and on Peel's police (why he wanted an organised force, how it was organised, the Corn Laws as a later act). Students should link a document to its context: the factory working class for Marx, crime and the inadequate old watch system for the police.
- Assessment material examiners reward: paired achievements and limits for unions, socialism, Chartism and Peel; contemporary vs later views of O'Connor (demagogue vs organiser) and Peel (traitor vs great statesman); and the point that the vote was widened more from above (1832, 1867, Bismarck) than won by working-class pressure.
