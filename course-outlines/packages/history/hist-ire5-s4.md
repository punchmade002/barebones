## 1. Must cover (one bullet or block for every item; tick each off)

Background (assumed)
- [ ] A1 (assumed) Stormont and one-party Unionist rule since 1921; Westminster's sovereignty under the 1920 Act but its habit of not interfering in Northern Ireland's internal affairs.
- [ ] A2 (assumed) Catholic grievances (housing, jobs, local voting, policing) and the civil rights movement (NICRA, 1967; Derry, October 1968; Burntollet, January 1969); O'Neill's resignation (April 1969).
- [ ] A3 (assumed) The RUC and the B Specials (Ulster Special Constabulary): a mainly Protestant police and part-time reserve, distrusted by Catholics.
- [ ] A4 (assumed) The IRA's border campaign (1956–62) failed and the IRA was weak and divided in 1969; internment had been used against it in the 1950s.
- [ ] A5 (assumed) The Ireland Act (1949) guarantee: Northern Ireland stays in the UK unless its parliament (later its people) consents.

Key concepts (each defined as used in this topic; also in keyTerms)
- [ ] K1 Terrorism: violence against civilians and the state for political aims, used by both republican and loyalist paramilitaries.
- [ ] K2 Sectarianism: hostility between Protestants and Catholics based on religious identity and political allegiance.
- [ ] K3 Internment and Direct Rule (essential terms, not outline key concepts).

August 1969 and its aftermath
- [ ] R1 The Apprentice Boys' parade, 12 August 1969, and the Battle of the Bogside (12–14 August); barricades and "Free Derry".
- [ ] R2 British troops deployed in Derry (14 August), then Belfast; the start of the army's long role in internal security.
- [ ] R3 Belfast, 14–15 August 1969: rioting, Bombay Street burned, deaths (mostly Catholics), families fleeing, refugee centres.
- [ ] R4 The Downing Street Declaration of 19 August 1969 (consent guarantee and promised reform; not the 1993 declaration).
- [ ] R5 The Hunt Report (October 1969): an unarmed RUC under a police authority; the B Specials to go.
- [ ] R6 The B Specials stood down (April 1970) and the Ulster Defence Regiment formed (1970).

Chichester-Clark's government, 1969–71
- [ ] C1 Became Prime Minister 1 May 1969 (narrowly beat Faulkner); presided over August 1969 and the Hunt reforms.
- [ ] C2 Reforms: points system for housing, the Housing Executive (1971), local government reform; hardliners saw surrender, nationalists too little too late.
- [ ] C3 Resigned 20 March 1971 over security policy and London's response; Faulkner succeeded him on 23 March.

The Provisional IRA, the SDLP and Alliance
- [ ] P1 The IRA split (December 1969): Provisionals (Mac Stíofáin) v Officials (Goulding); why (the failure to defend Catholic areas in August 1969, abstentionism and the political/socialist direction).
- [ ] P2 Provisional aims (defend Catholic areas, drive out the British, united Ireland); Provisional Sinn Féin (January 1970).
- [ ] P3 The Falls curfew (3–5 July 1970): searches, civilian deaths, alienation of Catholics from the army.
- [ ] P4 The SDLP founded (21 August 1970): founders, constitutional nationalism.
- [ ] P5 The Alliance Party founded (April 1970): cross-community; Napier later leader.
- [ ] P6 The SDLP withdrew from Stormont (July 1971) after two men were shot dead by the army in Derry; Official IRA ceasefire (May 1972) left the Provisionals as the main republican force.

Loyalist paramilitaries
- [ ] L1 The UVF revived in 1966 and killed three people that year (a duplicate bullet in the current notes must appear only once).
- [ ] L2 The UDA formed in 1971 as an umbrella of vigilante groups; legal until 1992.
- [ ] L3 McGurk's Bar, 4 December 1971: 15 Catholics killed; police first blamed the IRA, the Police Ombudsman (2011) blamed the UVF.
- [ ] L4 Loyalist motives (defending the Union and Protestant areas) against the reality (most victims Catholic civilians); Craig's Ulster Vanguard (1972).

Key personality: Brian Faulkner, part 1 (one block, every point)
- [ ] F1 Who he was: born 1921, Unionist businessman and politician; Minister of Home Affairs (1959–63) and Minister of Commerce (1963–69); resigned from O'Neill's cabinet in 1969.
- [ ] F2 Prime Minister from 23 March 1971, beating William Craig in the party vote; Chichester-Clark's rival in 1969.
- [ ] F3 What he did: asked London to authorise internment (9 August 1971); offered opposition committee chairmanships; talked with Heath over control of security.
- [ ] F4 Shaped by events: violence, SDLP withdrawal, Bloody Sunday and London's demand for security powers ended his government; resigned 24 March 1972.
- [ ] F5 Participant or witness: his own memoirs (published 1978) from the inside; this part stops at March 1972 (Sunningdale and his later career belong to the next subsections).
- [ ] F6 Contemporaries: unionists praised firmness; nationalists saw internment as an attack on Catholics.
- [ ] F7 Historians: pragmatic, able, reforming, but unable to contain violence or satisfy either community.

Internment, Bloody Sunday, Direct Rule
- [ ] I1 Internment (9 August 1971, Operation Demetrius): purpose, who was arrested, bias (no loyalists until 1973), poor intelligence.
- [ ] I2 Interrogation in depth: the Hooded Men and the 1978 European Court ruling.
- [ ] I3 Ballymurphy (9–11 August 1971): civilian deaths, Father Mullan, the 2021 inquest.
- [ ] I4 Consequences of internment: violence rose, IRA recruitment rose, 1971 the first year deaths passed 100.
- [ ] B1 Bloody Sunday (30 January 1972): the NICRA march, Paratroopers, 13 killed, a 14th died later.
- [ ] B2 Reactions and inquiries: Widgery (19 April 1972) against Saville (June 2010) and Cameron's apology; the embassy burning in Dublin (2 February 1972).
- [ ] B3 Results: surge in IRA recruitment; breakdown of nationalist confidence in the army and the state.
- [ ] D1 Why Stormont fell: London demanded control of security; Faulkner's government refused and resigned (24 March 1972).
- [ ] D2 Direct Rule from 30 March 1972 under the Northern Ireland (Temporary Provisions) Act; Whitelaw as first Secretary of State; Stormont abolished in 1973.
- [ ] D3 Early Direct Rule measures: special category status (June 1972); the Whitelaw–Provisional IRA talks (7 July 1972).
- [ ] D4 Why Direct Rule lasted (unionists would not accept power sharing with nationalists, nationalists would not accept majority rule, London preferred to avoid the old system), apart from the 1974 executive.
- [ ] D5 Reactions: unionist anger (Vanguard rallies), nationalist welcome.
- [ ] M1 Bloody Friday (21 July 1972): about twenty bombs in Belfast, nine dead, about 130 injured.
- [ ] M2 Operation Motorman (31 July 1972): no-go areas in Belfast and Derry cleared; the IRA's campaign went on.
- [ ] M3 1972 as the deadliest year of the Troubles (nearly 500 deaths).

Explanation and assessment
- [ ] E1 Why the Troubles erupted: discrimination, partisan policing, civil rights confrontations, unionist fear, IRA revival, British misjudgement.
- [ ] E2 Why they lasted: IRA campaign, loyalist retaliation, army and internment alienating Catholics, no power-sharing institution, mutual hardening of attitudes.
- [ ] S1 Assessment, achievement or failure: Direct Rule ended an institution nationalists never accepted and opened the way to later settlements.
- [ ] S2 Assessment, failure: Stormont could not contain violence and relied on internment that alienated Catholics.
- [ ] S3 Contemporary views (unionists betrayed, nationalists relieved, London wanting a temporary arrangement) against later views (a settlement forced by violence, Stormont's decline partly its own policies).

## 3. Traps

Do not use (wrong or doubtful facts in the current notes):
- Do not use: "Faulkner demanded control of security from Westminster, but the British government refused", because it is the wrong way round. London demanded that security be transferred to Westminster, Faulkner's government refused, and Stormont was suspended.
- Do not use: "Loyalist paramilitaries killed hundreds of people between 1969 and 1993, the great majority of them civilians, mostly Catholics", because the figure is vague and the period wrong for this subsection. Say instead that most loyalist victims were Catholic civilians, with no figure.
- Do not use: a cumulative death toll "since 1969" for the end of 1972; use only the nearly 500 deaths in 1972.
- Do not use: "Chichester-Clark resigned after the British government refused to send more troops and reinforce the security policy he wanted" as it stands, because it overstates London's refusal. Say he resigned in March 1971 after London would not give him the security measures he wanted.
- Do not use: "unjustified and unjustifiable" as a quotation from the Saville Report, because it is David Cameron's wording in his statement of 15 June 2010. Attribute to Saville only the findings (1 PARA soldiers caused the deaths; no casualty posed a threat).
- Do not use: "the SDLP refused the offer of chairmanships and left Stormont" as one step, because it implies they quit over the offer. The offer came in June 1971; they withdrew in July 1971 after the Derry shootings.
- Do not use: the number "more than 12,000 soldiers" for Motorman; say "about 12,000".
- Do not use: "Direct Rule lasted throughout the period to 1993" without the exception of the 1974 executive; also the 1982–86 Assembly was only a partial devolution scheme and belongs to a later subsection.
- The UVF bullet in the current notes appears twice; write it once.

## 4. What exams ask

- Higher 100-mark essay on why Direct Rule was introduced and why it lasted, or how Stormont fell: marks go for a causal argument (loss of control of security, internment, Bloody Sunday, London's demand for security, Faulkner's refusal) and then explanation of why it lasted (no agreed power sharing, unionist and nationalist positions). A narrative of 1971–72 without cause and judgement scores poorly.
- Higher essay on how the Troubles erupted and lasted: combine long-term causes (discrimination, policing) with triggers (August 1969, the IRA split, internment, Bloody Sunday) and sustaining factors (paramilitaries, no political institution); a clear judgement on the most important cause is rewarded.
- Faulkner: Higher questions ask for strengths and weaknesses or his contribution; this subsection supplies his career to 1972, internment and the resignation, and each point needs a date and a result. Use paired success and failure points.
- Ordinary Level short answers: when and who introduced internment, what Faulkner asked London for, why Stormont lost control, the Provisional IRA split and its aims, Bloody Friday, the Falls curfew and Bloody Sunday; each correct dated statement earns marks.
- Ordinary Level 30-mark paragraph on a personality (Faulkner): who he was, offices held, how he became Prime Minister, what he did, why it failed, and how he ended; three or four dated facts.
- Document questions on 1971–72 events (internment, Direct Rule, no-go areas): place the document in time and say who wrote it and why; comparing how two witnesses describe the same event earns credit.
- Examiners reward naming both sides: republican and loyalist terrorism, and nationalist and unionist views of internment and Direct Rule.
- A common weakness is confusing Bloody Sunday and Bloody Friday or mixing the 1969 and 1993 Downing Street Declarations; a one-line identification with date and place avoids this.
