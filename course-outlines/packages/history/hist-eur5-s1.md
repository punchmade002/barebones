## 1. Must cover (one bullet or block for every item; tick each off)

Background (assumed)
- [ ] A1 (assumed) Britain ruled India as the largest colony; the Raj was direct Crown rule from 1858 (after the 1857 rising); the Viceroy headed the government; about 560 princely states stayed under British paramountcy.
- [ ] A2 (assumed) Before 1939 the Indian National Congress (1885) moved from petitioning to demanding self-rule; British reforms came in stages (1919, 1935).
- [ ] A3 (assumed) The Hindu majority and large Muslim minority (about a quarter of the population), concentrated in the north-west and east; worry about Muslim rights under majority rule.

Key concepts (each defined as used in this topic; also in keyTerms)
- [ ] K1 Colonial rule: control of a territory by an outside power, governed for the benefit of the imperial state, usually without the people's consent.
- [ ] K2 De-colonisation: the ending of European rule and the creation of independent states, mainly 1945 to the 1960s.
- [ ] K3 (British) Commonwealth: association of Britain and its former dominions and colonies; the 1949 change that let republics stay in.
- [ ] K4 Other essential terms: satyagraha, partition, dominion status, Raj, Quit India, Cabinet Mission, Muslim League, Indian National Congress.

World War II and Asia
- [ ] W1 Japanese conquests, December 1941 to 1942: Malaya, Hong Kong, Singapore, Burma, the Dutch East Indies; Indochina.
- [ ] W2 The fall of Singapore (15 February 1942), the scale of the surrender and its effect on British prestige.
- [ ] W3 Japanese propaganda ("Asia for the Asians", Co-Prosperity Sphere) and the encouragement of nationalists.
- [ ] W4 India's war: the Indian Army's expansion, the Viceroy declaring war without consulting Indians, Congress ministries resigning (1939).
- [ ] W5 The Cripps Mission (March 1942) and its rejection.
- [ ] W6 Quit India (August 1942): the resolution, arrests, unrest, repression.
- [ ] W7 Subhas Chandra Bose and the Indian National Army; the Red Fort trials (1945–46).
- [ ] W8 The Bengal famine of 1943 and its damage to British prestige.
- [ ] W9 Overall implication: European rule in Asia had been shown to be breakable; Britain exhausted.

The Indian National Congress and Gandhi to 1942
- [ ] G1 Gandhi's return (1915), Champaran (1917) and Kheda (1918): satyagraha in practice.
- [ ] G2 Amritsar massacre (13 April 1919) and its effect.
- [ ] G3 Non-Cooperation 1920–22 and its suspension after Chauri Chaura.
- [ ] G4 The Salt March, 12 March to 6 April 1930.
- [ ] G5 Government of India Act 1935 and the 1937 provincial elections: Congress in office.

The Muslim League and the Pakistan demand
- [ ] L1 The League's founding (1906) and Jinnah's leadership from 1934.
- [ ] L2 The 1937 elections and Congress rule: the League's complaint that Muslims were unsafe; Day of Deliverance (22 December 1939).
- [ ] L3 The Lahore Resolution (23 March 1940) and the two-nation argument; the name Pakistan.
- [ ] L4 The Simla Conference (1945) breaking down over Jinnah's demand.
- [ ] L5 The 1945–46 elections giving the League a mandate among Muslims.
- [ ] L6 Punjab and Bengal as the central problem (mixed populations).

Labour, the Cabinet Mission and the decision to leave
- [ ] D1 Labour's July 1945 landslide; Attlee as prime minister; commitment to self-rule.
- [ ] D2 Britain's economy: debts, the US loan, the sterling balances owed to India; unable to afford a large army in India.
- [ ] D3 The Royal Indian Navy mutiny (February 1946).
- [ ] D4 The Cabinet Mission (March–June 1946): its plan and why it failed.
- [ ] D5 The interim government (September 1946) and the League's joining it (October).
- [ ] D6 Direct Action Day (16 August 1946) and the Calcutta killings; spread of communal violence (Noakhali, Bihar, Punjab).
- [ ] D7 Attlee's announcement of 20 February 1947: June 1948 deadline; Wavell replaced by Mountbatten.
- [ ] D8 Mountbatten's arrival (22 March 1947), his judgement and speed.
- [ ] D9 The 3 June 1947 plan and the date brought forward to 15 August 1947.

Independence and partition
- [ ] I1 The Indian Independence Act (July 1947); two dominions; Jinnah as Pakistan's Governor-General.
- [ ] I2 Pakistan in two parts, separated by Indian territory.
- [ ] I3 The Radcliffe boundary commission; the line announced two days after independence.
- [ ] I4 Violence and migration in Punjab and Bengal; the numbers and the uncertainty of them.
- [ ] I5 The princely states; Hyderabad; Kashmir's accession (26 October 1947) and the first Indo-Pakistani war.
- [ ] I6 The administrative strain: dividing the army, civil service, assets in weeks.

Aftermath and post-independence relations
- [ ] X1 Gandhi's fasts and his assassination (30 January 1948) by Nathuram Godse.
- [ ] X2 Kashmir ceasefire (1 January 1949) and the line of control that followed.
- [ ] X3 Jinnah's death (September 1948).
- [ ] X4 The London Declaration (April 1949) and India's republic (26 January 1950) within the Commonwealth.
- [ ] X5 Nehru and non-alignment; Britain and India's continuing relations (trade, Commonwealth).

Importance of the withdrawal
- [ ] M1 For Britain: end of the Raj, loss of its largest and most valuable possession; reduced role in Asia; Commonwealth as the continuing link; example for other colonies (briefly: Burma and Ceylon 1948).
- [ ] M2 For India and Pakistan: independence and democracy, but partition, mass migration, killing and the Kashmir dispute.
- [ ] M3 Why Britain left: nationalist pressure, economic exhaustion, loss of confidence in the army and police, Labour's policy, communal deadlock (explanations as paired causes).
- [ ] M4 Difficulties of withdrawal: Congress–League deadlock, communal violence, the princely states, speed and the boundary, administrative division.

Key personality: Mohandas Gandhi (one block, every point)
- [ ] P1 Who he was: born 1869 at Porbandar, trained as a barrister in London, in South Africa 1893–1914.
- [ ] P2 What he did, with dates: South Africa; Champaran and Kheda; Non-Cooperation; Salt March; Round Table Conference (1931); Quit India; imprisonment; Noakhali and Calcutta; death.
- [ ] P3 His aims: swaraj, Hindu–Muslim unity, ending untouchability, non-violence, self-reliance (khadi, Hind Swaraj).
- [ ] P4 Shaped by events and shaping them: made Congress a mass movement; could not stop partition; was a participant, not the decision-maker, in 1947.
- [ ] P5 The Poona Pact (1932) and the clash with Ambedkar over separate electorates.
- [ ] P6 Contemporaries: revered as the Mahatma; British officials saw an obstacle; Jinnah saw a Hindu leader; Ambedkar a critic.
- [ ] P7 Historians: Judith Brown and others on mass mobilisation vs those who stress war, the INA, Labour and economics; limits on caste and partition.
- [ ] P8 His assassination and legacy.

Assessment (paired judgement points, final block)
- [ ] S1 Achievement: non-violent mass politics; Limit: no united India, no prevention of violence.
- [ ] S2 Achievement: Quit India showed British authority could not rest on force alone; Limit: partition was agreed by Congress and League leaders in June 1947, which Gandhi opposed.
- [ ] S3 Achievement: independence without a colonial war (contrast with France in Indochina and Algeria); Limit: haste and the rushed boundary left the population to bear the cost.
- [ ] S4 Contemporary vs later views of Gandhi and of Mountbatten's haste.

## 3. Traps

Do not use (wrong or doubtful facts in the current notes):
- Do not use: "The Dutch East Indies fell to Japan in early 1942, ending Dutch rule there until the Japanese surrender in August 1945" as written, because it is muddled and the Dutch East Indies belong to the Indonesia notes; if needed say only that Japan overran the Dutch East Indies in early 1942.
- Do not use: "Bose raised the Indian National Army in 1942–43", because the first INA was formed in 1942 under Mohan Singh; Bose took over in 1943.
- Do not use: "the British Indian Army the largest volunteer force in history", because this is a popular claim, not a checked fact; use "about 2.5 million volunteers".
- Do not use: "Hindus and Sikhs moved into India and Muslims into Pakistan" as a statement of equal flows to a single total without the range; use only "about 10 to 15 million".
- Do not use: a single casualty figure for partition, or the unsourced "hundreds killed at Amritsar" as a precise number.
- Do not use: Ghana's independence and other African independence dates in these notes, because they belong to the Africa notes. Burma and Ceylon (1948) are acceptable as brief examples.
- Do not use: the Bengal famine as caused by Churchill alone, or as a single-cause event; historians debate the causes.

Doubtful quotations: do not use any quotation not in section 2. "Do or die" and "a tryst with destiny" are the only ones allowed. Never put words in quotation marks for Jinnah, Attlee or Mountbatten.

Confusable people, terms and dates
- Cripps Mission (March 1942, wartime offer) vs Cabinet Mission (1946, which included Cripps).
- Quit India (August 1942, Congress) vs Non-Cooperation (1920–22) vs the Salt March (civil disobedience, 1930).
- Direct Action Day (16 August 1946, League call, Calcutta) vs Day of Deliverance (22 December 1939).
- Lahore Resolution (1940) never used the word Pakistan; Rahmat Ali coined the name in 1933.
- Wavell (Viceroy 1943–47) vs Mountbatten (Viceroy from March 1947, then Governor-General of India); Linlithgow (Viceroy 1936–43).

- Radcliffe Line (Punjab and Bengal) vs the later Kashmir ceasefire line.
- The Muslim League (1906) vs Congress (1885); the Indian National Army (Bose) vs the Indian Army (British Indian Army).
- Dominion status vs republic; the London Declaration (1949) allowed a republic in the Commonwealth.
- The Poona Pact (1932) was with Ambedkar over Dalit electorates, not about partition.
- Gandhi (Mahatma) is not related to Indira Gandhi; Nehru is not Indira's husband.

## 4. What exams ask

- Higher 100-mark essays on British withdrawal from India are the commonest form: causes of withdrawal, difficulties of withdrawal, consequences (for India, Pakistan and Britain) and its importance. Marks go to a clear opening judgement supported by dated, named facts and a balanced weighing of causes; a single narrative of events scores poorly.
- Causes: strong answers rank causes (Britain's exhaustion and debt, the INA trials and navy mutiny undermining the Indian Army, Congress pressure from Salt March to Quit India, Labour's commitment, communal deadlock and violence) and say which was decisive.
- Difficulties: Congress–League deadlock (Cabinet Mission, interim government), communal violence (Direct Action Day), the princely states, the speed and the boundary (Radcliffe's five weeks), division of the army and civil service. Link each difficulty to a date.
- Importance for Britain and for India as paired halves: for Britain the end of the Raj and the Commonwealth link; for India and Pakistan independence and partition, migration, killing and Kashmir. A comparison with Palestine (2014 style) needs both cases developed.
- Key personality essay on Gandhi (appears often, sometimes paired with Ho Chi Minh, Sukarno or Ben-Gurion): aims, methods (satyagraha), main campaigns with dates, achievements and limits, his place in the 1947 outcome. Credit goes to a sustained judgement about how effective he was, with the point that independence was not achieved by him alone and that he opposed partition. Naming a historian (Judith Brown) adds weight.
- The 2012 document-based question (difficulties of withdrawal and the Gandhi sources) tested: reading documents for the Congress–League positions, utility and bias of a visual vs a written source, and a cartoon of Jinnah and Gandhi. Students need the background to place documents: Congress and League positions in 1946–47, Gandhi's dress and public image.
- Ordinary Level 40-mark paragraphs: Gandhi (return in 1915, satyagraha, Salt March, Quit India); why Britain left (economy, Labour, INA, mutiny); partition (15 August 1947, Radcliffe, two Pakistans); Mountbatten's date. Each correct, dated point earns marks; short answers ask for one or two facts (Independence Day, partition led to violence).
- Frequently tested precise facts: Attlee's 20 February 1947 announcement, the 15 August 1947 independence date, Direct Action Day (16 August 1946), the Lahore Resolution (1940), Radcliffe's identity, Gandhi's death on 30 January 1948.
