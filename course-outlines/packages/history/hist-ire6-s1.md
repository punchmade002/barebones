## 1. Must cover (one bullet or block for every item; tick each off)

Background (assumed)
- [ ] A1 (assumed) Fianna Fáil under de Valera governed from 1932 to 1948 and had built a protected, self-sufficiency economy (tariffs, the Control of Manufactures Acts).
- [ ] A2 (assumed) The party system: Fianna Fáil against Fine Gael and Labour, with the Civil War still shaping loyalties; proportional representation made coalitions possible when no party had a majority.
- [ ] A3 (assumed) Ireland was neutral in the Second World War ("the Emergency") and came out of it poor, isolated and tied to the British market and to sterling.
- [ ] A4 (assumed) The Catholic Church's strong influence on social policy and the Constitution of 1937.

Key concept and essential terms (each defined as used in this topic; also in keyTerms)
- [ ] K1 Balance of payments: the record of a country's payments to and from the rest of the world; a deficit drained currency reserves and forced deflation.
- [ ] K2 Deflation: raising taxes and cutting spending or credit to reduce demand and imports, at the cost of jobs.
- [ ] K3 Inter-Party Government: the coalitions of 1948–51 and 1954–57.
- [ ] K4 Clann na Poblachta: Seán MacBride's republican party, founded 1946.
- [ ] K5 Mother and Child Scheme: Browne's free health scheme, defeated in 1951.
- [ ] K6 Marshall Aid: American recovery aid, 1948 onwards.
- [ ] K7 Industrial Development Authority (IDA): the 1949 agency promoting industry.
- [ ] K8 Republic of Ireland Act: in force 18 April 1949.
- [ ] K9 Wasted years: the description of the 1950s as a decade of missed opportunity.

The First Inter-Party Government, 1948–1951
- [ ] G1 The February 1948 election: Fianna Fáil largest party but without a majority after sixteen years; Clann na Poblachta's arrival and its small number of seats.
- [ ] G2 The coalition's parties (Fine Gael, Labour, National Labour, Clann na Poblachta, Clann na Talmhan, independents) and why it formed: to end Fianna Fáil rule, with no common economic programme.
- [ ] G3 Leaders: Mulcahy stood aside, Costello Taoiseach; Norton Tánaiste; MacBride External Affairs; Browne Health.
- [ ] G4 The Republic of Ireland Act: Costello's announcement in Canada (September 1948); the External Relations Act repealed; in force 18 April 1949; Commonwealth link ended.
- [ ] G5 Britain's reply, the Ireland Act 1949 (consent of Northern Ireland's parliament; Republic not a foreign country), briefly, with a pointer to the Anglo-Irish notes.
- [ ] G6 Ireland declined to join NATO (1949), citing partition.
- [ ] G7 The IDA, 1949: purpose and its weakness in the 1950s.
- [ ] G8 Marshall Aid: amount, grants and loans, where it was spent, and the criticism that it was a missed chance for industry.
- [ ] G9 Browne's anti-tuberculosis campaign: sanatoria, Hospitals Sweepstake money, BCG vaccination, falling TB deaths.

The Mother and Child Scheme crisis, 1950–1951
- [ ] M1 The scheme: free care for mothers and for children up to 16, no means test, under the Health Act 1947.
- [ ] M2 Opposition of the Irish Medical Association (doctors' private practice and independence).
- [ ] M3 Opposition of the Catholic hierarchy (state power over the family, the rights of parents, fear of state-provided sex education); the bishops' letters to Costello (late 1950 and April 1951).
- [ ] M4 The crisis in April 1951: MacBride's demand, Browne's resignation, publication of the correspondence.
- [ ] M5 The government accepts the hierarchy's decision; the fall of the government and the May 1951 election; Clann na Poblachta's collapse.
- [ ] M6 The replacement: the Health Act 1953 (means-tested, so many without free care).

Key personality: Archbishop John Charles McQuaid, part 1 (one block, every point)
- [ ] P1 Who he was: born Cootehill, Co. Cavan, 1895; Holy Ghost Father; president of Blackrock College; Archbishop of Dublin 1940–1972.
- [ ] P2 His influence in the state: close contact with de Valera and ministers; adviser on the 1937 Constitution's religious article.
- [ ] P3 What he did in 1950–51 (participant): advised the government and Browne, and argued that the scheme conflicted with Catholic social teaching; the formal hierarchy letters were the bishops' collective act, so do not make him sole author.
- [ ] P4 His principle: the family and the Church, not the state, should direct the care of mothers and children (subsidiarity-style argument).
- [ ] P5 How contemporaries saw him: defender of Catholic teaching (supporters) vs interfering prelate (Browne and his supporters).
- [ ] P6 How historians judge him: Whyte concluded that the government deferred to the hierarchy; later work using archives stresses Browne's weak political position and the doctors' opposition, while still treating the bishops' stance as decisive. Later life and Vatican II are for another subsection.

Fianna Fáil in office, 1951–1954
- [ ] F1 A minority government under de Valera: Lemass (Tánaiste, Industry and Commerce), MacEntee (Finance); dependence on independents.
- [ ] F2 MacEntee's 1952 budget: deflation, food subsidies cut, duties raised, to cut imports; unpopular.
- [ ] F3 The Underdeveloped Areas Act 1952: grants for industry in the poorer west.
- [ ] F4 The Health Act 1953.
- [ ] F5 Continued high emigration and unemployment; Fianna Fáil lost seats in the 1954 election.

The Second Inter-Party Government, 1954–1957
- [ ] S1 Costello Taoiseach again; Fine Gael, Labour and Clann na Talmhan, with Clann na Poblachta supporting from outside.
- [ ] S2 Gerard Sweetman, Minister for Finance: private enterprise, low spending.
- [ ] S3 The 1955–56 balance of payments crisis: causes (imports ahead of exports, falling reserves) and the deflationary response (taxes, credit restrictions, spending cuts) with its effect on jobs.
- [ ] S4 The Capital Investment Advisory Committee: an early step towards planning public investment.
- [ ] S5 The IRA Border Campaign (December 1956), Clann na Poblachta's withdrawal of support (January 1957) and the election.

Stagnation, unemployment and emigration in the 1950s
- [ ] E1 Population fell between the 1951 and 1961 censuses despite a high birth rate.
- [ ] E2 Net emigration about 400,000 over the decade, mostly young people to Britain; why Britain attracted them.
- [ ] E3 An economy dependent on small-scale agriculture and cattle exports to Britain.
- [ ] E4 Protection and the Control of Manufactures Acts discouraged foreign investment.
- [ ] E5 Slow growth while western Europe boomed; the widening gap.
- [ ] E6 The sterling link: Irish conditions followed British ones.
- [ ] E7 High unemployment throughout (described without figures).

Fianna Fáil's return and the "wasted years" debate
- [ ] R1 The March 1957 election: Fianna Fáil 78 seats and a majority; de Valera Taoiseach for the last time.
- [ ] R2 The new team: Lemass (Industry and Commerce, Tánaiste), James Ryan (Finance); Whitaker, Secretary of Finance from 1956, and the work towards Economic Development (pointer to the notes on economic planning).
- [ ] R3 View 1: critics (Lee, Garvin) say the leaders lacked ambition and preferred stability.
- [ ] R4 View 2: constraints (small economy, dependence on Britain and sterling, weak reserves).
- [ ] R5 View 3: groundwork was laid (IDA, Underdeveloped Areas Act, public investment) and planning tools only came late in the decade.

Assessment (paired judgement points, final block)
- [ ] X1 Achievements: the Republic of Ireland Act; IDA, Underdeveloped Areas Act and Marshall Aid as foundations; the TB campaign.
- [ ] X2 Limits: no long-term plan; repeated deflation worsened unemployment and emigration.
- [ ] X3 Limits: the Mother and Child affair showed the Church's power over social policy.
- [ ] X4 Contemporary view: voters punished each government in turn (1951, 1954, 1957).
- [ ] X5 Later view: slow growth and missed opportunity, with disagreement on policy error versus dependence on Britain; instability and economic failure were linked.

## 3. Traps

Do not use (wrong or doubtful facts in the current notes):
- Do not use: "nationalised the public transport companies into Córas Iompair Éireann in 1950", because CIÉ was created in 1945 and the Transport Act of 1950 made it state-owned; the point is also a side issue. Leave it out.
- Do not use: "Browne resigned on 11 April 1951" or any exact day, because sources differ (10 or 11 April). Say April 1951.
- Do not use: McQuaid as "the author of the argument" or as the writer of the bishops' letters, because the letters were the hierarchy's collective act (the primate, Cardinal D'Alton, headed it). Say he advised and was the most influential voice.
- Do not use: "unemployment" figures or growth-rate figures for the 1950s, because none are checked here.
- Do not use: Costello's reply to the bishops in quotation marks, because the wording is not checked.
- Do not use: that Clann na Poblachta withdrew "over the IRA Border Campaign" as the sole cause without saying it was about the government's handling of the North and its response to the campaign.

## 4. What exams ask

- Higher 100-mark essay, set often (2006, 2008, 2010, 2013, 2016, 2020, 2024): how effective were governments in 1949–59, or why were there so many changes of government. Marks go to a clear judgement at the start, sustained across the Inter-Party and Fianna Fáil governments, backed by dated points (1948, 1951, 1954, 1957) and by explaining that instability and economic failure were linked.
- Strong answers pair successes (republic, IDA, TB campaign, Marshall Aid) with failures (emigration, unemployment, deflation, no plan) and name the cause of each change of government rather than narrating.
- The "wasted years" question: define the term, give the critical view with a named historian, then the defence (constraints, groundwork), and decide.
- Balance of payments: explain what a deficit is and how deflation responded to it; naming MacEntee 1952 and Sweetman 1955–56 earns credit.
- Mother and Child Scheme, a regular short and long topic: what the scheme offered, who opposed it and why, how it ended, what it showed about Church power; a Health Act 1953 comparison is a frequent follow-up.
- Ordinary Level 30-mark paragraph on McQuaid (2006, 2007, 2009, 2012, 2014, 2022): career dates, his role in 1951, how he was viewed. Each correct, dated point earns marks.
- Higher personality essays (McQuaid with other figures): only this subsection's part of his career counts here; use specific events.
- Short factual questions: who led the governments, what the Republic of Ireland Act did, what the IDA was, why emigration was high.
