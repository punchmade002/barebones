## 1. Must cover (one bullet or block for every item; tick each off)

Background (assumed)
- [ ] A1 (assumed) The legacy of the French Revolution (1789): liberty, equality and the sovereignty of the people; the First Republic and the execution of Louis XVI (1793). It left France divided between those who accepted the Revolution and those (nobles, much of the Church) who rejected it, so every later regime was contested.
- [ ] A2 (assumed) Napoleon I's legacy: Emperor from 1804; the Code Napoléon, a centralised administration run by prefects, the Concordat with the Church; after his fall a "Napoleonic legend" of glory and order that his nephew later used.
- [ ] A3 (assumed) The Vienna settlement restored the Bourbons under the principle of legitimacy and left France a defeated power under Allied occupation until 1818. Only France's side belongs here.
- [ ] A4 (assumed) France was highly centralised and Paris dominated it, so a rising in Paris could overthrow a government; most French people were peasants, many owning small farms gained since the Revolution, and they were socially conservative.

Key concepts and essential terms (each defined as used in this topic; also in keyTerms)
- [ ] K1 Liberalism: constitutional government, an elected parliament, civil liberties (a free press) and limits on the ruler; in France it meant the Charter, then the July Monarchy, and a vote restricted to property owners.
- [ ] K2 Democracy: rule by the people through representatives elected by all (adult men in this period); in France, universal male suffrage from 1848.
- [ ] K3 Autocracy: rule by one person whose power is not limited by a parliament or constitution; the early Second Empire was authoritarian, with autocratic features, though it rested on plebiscites and kept an elected (weak) legislature.
- [ ] K4 The rival political groups: Ultras (extreme royalists), legitimists (supporters of the senior Bourbon line), Orleanists (supporters of Louis-Philippe), Bonapartists and republicans. Their rivalry is a main cause of instability.
- [ ] K5 Plebiscite and Bonapartism: a plebiscite is a yes/no vote of all voters on a single question; Bonapartism combined strong personal rule with universal male suffrage and plebiscites, order and prosperity.

The restored Bourbons, 1814–1830
- [ ] B1 The restoration: Louis XVIII returned in 1814; Napoleon's Hundred Days (1815) interrupted it; the second restoration followed Waterloo.
- [ ] B2 The Charter (1814): a constitution granted by the king; kept many Napoleonic institutions; two chambers; the king alone proposed laws and appointed ministers; civil equality and some liberties guaranteed.
- [ ] B3 The narrow franchise: tax qualifications for voting and for standing, so only a tiny wealthy minority voted.
- [ ] B4 The White Terror (1815) in the south, and the official purge that followed.
- [ ] B5 The Ultras gain ground: the murder of the Duc de Berry (1820), the Law of the Double Vote and tighter censorship; Villèle's ministry.
- [ ] B6 Charles X (from 1824): coronation at Reims, the law on sacrilege and the compensation for émigrés (1825), the disbanding of the Paris National Guard (1827); seen as a return to the old regime and to Church power.
- [ ] B7 Growing opposition: the 1827 elections went against the government; Martignac's brief moderate ministry; Polignac appointed (August 1829); the Address of the 221 (1830); the 1830 elections increased the opposition.
- [ ] B8 The capture of Algiers (1830): the start of the French conquest of Algeria, too late to save Charles X.
- [ ] B9 The July Ordinances (25 July 1830): what they did.
- [ ] B10 The July Revolution: the Three Glorious Days, Charles X's abdication and exile, Louis-Philippe made King of the French with the help of liberal deputies, Thiers and Lafayette.
- [ ] B11 Why the Restoration fell: it was identified with the nobles, the Church and foreign bayonets; Charles X broke the Charter; the narrow franchise; Paris rose.

The July Monarchy of Louis-Philippe, 1830–1848
- [ ] J1 Its character: "King of the French" (not "of France"), the tricolour restored, the revised Charter of 1830; a "bourgeois monarchy" (the "citizen king") of the wealthy middle class; the "juste milieu" (middle way).
- [ ] J2 The 1831 electoral law: lower tax qualification, electorate still under 1 per cent of the population.
- [ ] J3 Opposition and unrest: legitimists (the Duchesse de Berry's rising, 1832), republican risings in Paris (1832, 1834), the Lyon silk workers' revolts (1831, 1834), Bonapartist plots (Strasbourg 1836, Boulogne 1840), the Fieschi assassination attempt (1835) and the September Laws restricting the press.
- [ ] J4 Guizot, the dominant minister 1840–48: refused any widening of the vote; told the middle class to enrich themselves by work and saving; government by patronage and a docile chamber; scandals in 1847.
- [ ] J5 A regime without glory: a cautious, peaceful foreign policy seen as weak; Lamartine's "France is bored"; the return of Napoleon I's remains to Paris (1840) fed the Napoleonic legend.
- [ ] J6 The economic crisis of 1846–47: bad harvests and potato disease, high food prices, industrial depression and unemployment.
- [ ] J7 The banquet campaign (from July 1847) for electoral reform, and the ban on the Paris banquet (February 1848).
- [ ] J8 Why the July Monarchy fell: a narrow base, refusal to reform, the economic crisis, loss of the National Guard's loyalty, and a Paris rising.

The February Revolution and the Second Republic, 1848
- [ ] R1 The February Days (22–24 February 1848): crowds and barricades, Guizot dismissed, troops fire on a crowd, the National Guard sides with reform, Louis-Philippe abdicates and flees to England.
- [ ] R2 The Provisional Government and the proclamation of the Second Republic (24 February): Lamartine, Ledru-Rollin, Louis Blanc and the worker Albert.
- [ ] R3 Early reforms: universal male suffrage (March 1848), abolition of slavery in the colonies, a shorter working day in Paris, the "right to work", the Luxembourg Commission on labour.
- [ ] R4 The National Workshops: purpose, rapid growth, cost, and conservative hostility.
- [ ] R5 The provinces against Paris: the 45-centime tax angered peasants; the April 1848 elections to the Constituent Assembly returned a moderate and conservative majority.
- [ ] R6 The closure of the National Workshops and the June Days (23–26 June 1848); Cavaignac's repression; the Archbishop of Paris killed; deportations to Algeria.
- [ ] R7 Results of the June Days: workers alienated from the republic; property owners and peasants afraid of "the reds"; the move to the right.
- [ ] R8 The constitution of November 1848: a president elected by universal male suffrage for four years, not immediately re-eligible; a single-chamber assembly.
- [ ] R9 The presidential election of 10 December 1848: result and why Louis-Napoleon won (the name, peasant votes, conservative backing, a vague programme, other candidates discredited).

The presidency and the road to Empire, 1849–1852
- [ ] P1 The Roman expedition (1849): French troops crushed the Roman Republic and restored Pius IX, winning Catholic support.
- [ ] P2 The Legislative Assembly elections (May 1849): a large Party of Order majority (monarchists and conservatives), but a strong democratic-socialist left; Ledru-Rollin's failed protest (June 1849) and exile.
- [ ] P3 The conservative laws: the Falloux Law (March 1850) gave the Church a large role in education; the law of 31 May 1850 cut about three million voters. Louis-Napoleon later posed as the defender of universal suffrage against the Assembly.
- [ ] P4 The failed revision (July 1851): a majority voted to let him stand again, but not the three-quarters the constitution required.
- [ ] P5 The coup of 2 December 1851: the date's symbolism, Morny, arrests of deputies, universal suffrage restored, resistance in Paris and the provinces, mass arrests and transportation.
- [ ] P6 The plebiscite of December 1851 and the constitution of January 1852 (ten-year presidency, Senate, Council of State, a weak elected Corps législatif).
- [ ] P7 From president to emperor: the Bordeaux speech ("The Empire means peace"), the plebiscite of November 1852, the Second Empire proclaimed 2 December 1852.

Key personality: Napoleon III (one block, every point)
- [ ] N1 Who he was: born Paris 1808, son of Louis Bonaparte (King of Holland) and Hortense de Beauharnais, nephew of Napoleon I; grew up in exile after 1815; head of the Bonaparte claim after the death of Napoleon I's son (1832).
- [ ] N2 Before power: the failed coups at Strasbourg (1836) and Boulogne (1840); imprisonment at Ham, where he wrote The Extinction of Pauperism; escape (1846); life in London.
- [ ] N3 His ideas: order with universal male suffrage, economic modernisation, concern for the poor, national glory, and support for nationalities against the 1815 settlement.
- [ ] N4 Participant, shaping events: elected president (1848), made the coup (1851) and the Empire (1852), and personally directed policy at home and abroad until 1870; also shaped by events (the fear of the reds after June 1848 gave him his chance).
- [ ] N5 The end: ill health; war with Prussia; surrender at Sedan (2 September 1870); captivity in Germany; exile in England; death in 1873.
- [ ] N6 How contemporaries saw him: peasants, businessmen and Catholics (until 1859) supported him; republicans and liberals saw a usurper (Hugo's "Napoleon the Little", Marx's "farce"); foreign powers distrusted his ambitions.
- [ ] N7 How historians judge him: the older hostile view (a dictator and adventurer whose regime ended in disaster) vs later revisionists (Theodore Zeldin, Roger Price) who stress economic modernisation, social concern and the liberal reforms, while agreeing his foreign policy after 1860 failed.
- [ ] N8 Judgement for answers: more effective at home than abroad.

The authoritarian Empire, 1852–1860
- [ ] E1 How the system worked: ministers answerable only to the emperor; an appointed Senate; the Corps législatif could not propose laws; official candidates backed by the prefects; plebiscites as the link with the people.
- [ ] E2 Repression: press controls (the 1852 press decree's system of warnings and suspensions), exile of opponents, the Orsini bomb (1858) and the Law of General Security; only five republican deputies elected in 1857 ("the Five").
- [ ] E3 Economic growth: new banks (Crédit Mobilier and Crédit Foncier, 1852), a railway boom, the Paris Exhibitions of 1855 and 1867, the Suez Canal (opened 1869); Saint-Simonian ideas of state-led investment.
- [ ] E4 The rebuilding of Paris under Haussmann (from 1853): one bullet only, as part of domestic policy (prestige, health, traffic, control of riots).
- [ ] E5 Social policy: mutual aid societies, the right to strike (1864), Victor Duruy's expansion of primary schooling (minister 1863–69).
- [ ] E6 The court and dynasty: marriage to Eugénie de Montijo (1853), the birth of an heir (the Prince Imperial, 1856).

The liberal Empire, 1860–1870
- [ ] L1 Why he liberalised: Catholics angry at his Italian policy, manufacturers angry at free trade, a desire to secure the dynasty and broaden support.
- [ ] L2 The Cobden–Chevalier Treaty (1860): lower tariffs with Britain; good for consumers and modernisation, resented by protected industries.
- [ ] L3 The reforms: the 1860 decree (debate on the Address, debates published), the right to strike (1864), the right of interpellation (1867), the 1868 laws easing press controls and allowing public meetings; trade unions tolerated from 1868 but not legalised.
- [ ] L4 The growth of opposition: gains in the 1863 and 1869 elections; Thiers in opposition; Gambetta's Belleville programme (1869); republican strength in Paris and the big cities.
- [ ] L5 Ollivier's ministry (January 1870) and the plebiscite of May 1870: the liberal Empire approved by a large majority just months before its collapse.

Foreign policy
- [ ] F1 Aims: to undo the humiliation of 1815, win prestige and glory, support nationalities, gain territory, while claiming "the Empire means peace".
- [ ] F2 The Crimean War (1854–56) on Britain's side; Sebastopol; the Congress of Paris (1856) held in Paris showed France's restored standing.
- [ ] F3 Italy: Plombières (1858), the war of 1859 (Magenta, Solferino), the sudden armistice of Villafranca, Nice and Savoy (1860); the French garrison in Rome until 1870 angered Italian nationalists; the 1859 war angered French Catholics.
- [ ] F4 Overseas expansion: Algeria completed, Senegal, Cochinchina (Saigon 1859, cession 1862) and a protectorate over Cambodia (1863).
- [ ] F5 Poland (1863): sympathy for the rising but no action, which annoyed Russia and won nothing.
- [ ] F6 Mexico (1861–67): the debt intervention, Puebla, Maximilian as emperor (1864), US pressure, withdrawal, Maximilian's execution (1867): a costly humiliation.
- [ ] F7 Prussia: the Biarritz meeting with Bismarck (1865), French neutrality in 1866, the quick Prussian victory, failed demands for "compensation", the Luxembourg crisis (1867); army reform under Niel watered down.
- [ ] F8 The 1870 war from the French side: the Hohenzollern candidature, the French demand for guarantees, the Ems telegram, France declares war (19 July 1870), isolated and unprepared.
- [ ] F9 Defeat: Metz besieged; Sedan (1–2 September 1870), the emperor and his army captured.
- [ ] F10 Judgement: successes to 1860 (Crimea, Italy, Nice and Savoy), then failures (Poland, Mexico, 1866, 1870) that destroyed the regime.

Key personality: Victor Hugo, opponent and exile (one block, every point; his literary work belongs elsewhere)
- [ ] H1 Who he was: born 1802, son of a Napoleonic general; the leading French Romantic writer (one clause only); a royalist in the 1820s; made a peer of France by Louis-Philippe (1845).
- [ ] H2 1848–49: elected to the Constituent Assembly (June 1848) and the Legislative Assembly (May 1849); at first close to the conservatives; his family's newspaper L'Événement backed Louis-Napoleon for president.
- [ ] H3 The move to the left (1849–51): his speech on poverty (1849), his opposition to the Falloux Law and the law of 31 May 1850, his attack on Louis-Napoleon's ambition in the revision debate (July 1851), where he first used the phrase "Napoleon the Little".
- [ ] H4 The coup and exile: he tried to organise resistance to the coup, fled to Brussels (December 1851), was banished, then lived on Jersey (1852–55) and Guernsey (1855–70).
- [ ] H5 His weapons from exile: Napoléon le Petit (1852) and Les Châtiments (1853), smuggled into France.
- [ ] H6 He refused the amnesty of 1859 and stayed in exile as a symbol of republican resistance ("if only one remains, I shall be that one").
- [ ] H7 1870–71: returned to Paris on 5 September 1870; elected to the National Assembly (February 1871), resigned (March 1871); condemned both the Commune's killing of hostages and the government's reprisals; offered refuge to Communards in Brussels and was expelled from Belgium; later campaigned for their amnesty.
- [ ] H8 Participant, contemporaries and historians: an active participant in politics, not just a witness; admired by republicans as the conscience of resistance, mocked by opponents for his changes of side; his state funeral (1885) showed his status; historians see a sincere if changeable defender of liberty, democracy and the poor whose exile gave the republican cause a voice.

Sedan, the Third Republic and the Commune, 1870–1871
- [ ] T1 4 September 1870: the republic proclaimed at the Hôtel de Ville in Paris (Gambetta, Jules Favre, Jules Ferry); the Government of National Defence under General Trochu; the Empress fled.
- [ ] T2 The siege of Paris (from 19 September 1870), Gambetta's escape by balloon to raise armies in the provinces, hunger in Paris, the armistice (28 January 1871).
- [ ] T3 The National Assembly (elected February 1871): a monarchist, peace-seeking majority elected mainly by the countryside; Thiers head of the executive; it met at Bordeaux, then Versailles; the Treaty of Frankfurt (May 1871): Alsace and much of Lorraine lost and a large indemnity.
- [ ] T4 Why Paris rose: the humiliation of defeat and of the German victory parade, the end of the moratorium on rents and debts, the end of National Guard pay, a monarchist Assembly at Versailles, radical and socialist traditions.
- [ ] T5 The Commune (18 March to 28 May 1871): the Montmartre cannon, two generals killed, the government withdrew to Versailles; the Commune elected (26 March); its mix of Jacobins, Blanquists and socialists; its measures (separation of Church and State, end of night work in bakeries, the Vendôme Column pulled down).
- [ ] T6 The Bloody Week (21–28 May 1871): MacMahon's troops retook Paris; hostages including the Archbishop of Paris shot by Communards; public buildings burned; mass executions, arrests and transportation to New Caledonia.
- [ ] T7 Significance: a martyr myth for socialists (Marx's The Civil War in France), a lasting fear of revolution on the right, the workers' movement crushed for years; the republic survived because it had crushed the Commune and the monarchists were divided; constitutional laws of 1875 (one bullet, beyond the period).

Assessment and judgement
- [ ] X1 Why France was unstable (each with evidence): the divided legacy of 1789; rival dynasties and groups; narrow franchises excluding most men; the power of Paris and the Paris crowd; economic crises (1846–47); rulers who would not compromise (Charles X, Guizot); a constitution that pitted president against assembly (1848); regimes resting on one man; military defeat (1870).
- [ ] X2 How each regime ended: revolution (1830, 1848), coup (1851), military defeat (1870); and the town–country split (Paris radical, the provinces conservative, as in April 1848, December 1848 and February 1871).
- [ ] X3 Napoleon III at home, achievements vs limits: order, prosperity, modernisation, Paris, liberal reforms vs repression, a rubber-stamp legislature, a regime tied to one man.
- [ ] X4 Napoleon III abroad, achievements vs limits: Crimea, Italy, Nice and Savoy, colonies vs Mexico, Poland, 1866 and the disaster of 1870; overall, more successful at home than abroad.
- [ ] X5 Continuity beneath the changes: the centralised state, the Code, the prefects and the Concordat survived every regime; economic growth went on; politically unstable yet culturally productive (one bullet; detail belongs to the cultural notes).

## 3. Traps

Do not use (wrong or doubtful facts in the current notes):
- Do not use: universal male suffrage granted "on 2 March 1848", because the decree on universal suffrage was dated 5 March 1848; the 2 March decree cut the working day in Paris. Write "March 1848" or "5 March 1848". (The fact card dated 2 March is wrong; the notes must not repeat it.)
- Do not use: "Louis XVIII's government carried out a White Terror", because the killings of 1815 were mainly by royalist mobs in the south; the government's part was the official purge that followed (Ney's execution). Say royalist mobs carried out the White Terror and the government followed with purges.
- Do not use: "the elections of 1827 returned a liberal opposition majority", because the anti-Villèle majority combined liberals and discontented royalists. Say the 1827 elections went against the government and Villèle resigned.
- Do not use: Hugo "had become a republican by 1848", because in 1848 he was elected as a conservative and backed Louis-Napoleon; his move to the democratic left came in 1849–51. Say he turned against Louis-Napoleon and towards the democratic left in 1849–51.
- Do not imply Hugo was in Paris during the Commune: he was in Brussels in spring 1871, dealing with his son Charles's affairs after his death (March 1871). Keep "condemned both sides; offered refuge in Brussels".
- Do not use: electorate "about 250,000" in one place and "about 240,000" in another as if they conflict; give "about 240,000–250,000 by 1846–48" once.

Doubtful quotations: do not use
- Guizot's "Enrichissez-vous!" as a verbatim one-word quotation: the exact words are not well attested. Give the idea without quotation marks, or the attributed phrase "enrich yourselves" with "by work and saving".
- "Après moi le déluge" belongs to Louis XV, not to any ruler here. Do not use.
- Bismarck's sayings about Napoleon III ("a sphinx without a riddle", "a great unrecognised incapacity"): attribution not well attested. Do not use.
- Do not invent quotations for Louis-Philippe, Charles X, Thiers (beyond the T4 line) or Gambetta.

Confusable people, terms and dates
- Napoleon I (Emperor 1804–14/15, the uncle) vs Louis-Napoleon (President 1848–52) = Napoleon III (Emperor 1852–70, the nephew). There was no reigning Napoleon II; the title was given to Napoleon I's son, who never ruled.
- Louis XVIII (1814–24) vs Louis XVI (executed 1793) vs Louis-Philippe (1830–48, Orléans branch, not a Bourbon of the senior line).
- Three revolutions: July 1830 (Charles X), February 1848 (Louis-Philippe), 4 September 1870 (after Sedan; almost bloodless). The June Days (1848) and the Commune (1871) were risings crushed by republican governments, not revolutions that succeeded.
- Two "2 December" events: the coup (1851) and the proclamation of the Empire (1852); both chosen for the anniversary of 2 December 1804/1805.
- Three plebiscites under Louis-Napoleon: December 1851 (the coup), November 1852 (the Empire), May 1870 (the liberal Empire). Do not mix their figures.
- The Constituent Assembly (April 1848, wrote the constitution) vs the Legislative Assembly (May 1849, Party of Order) vs the Corps législatif (Second Empire) vs the National Assembly (February 1871).
- Cavaignac crushed the June Days (1848); MacMahon crushed the Commune (1871) and commanded at Sedan; Bazaine surrendered at Metz.
- Thiers: journalist in 1830, minister under Louis-Philippe, opponent of the Empire, head of government in 1871. Guizot: dominant 1840–48.
- Sedan (1–2 September 1870, the emperor captured) vs Metz (surrendered October 1870) vs the siege of Paris (September 1870 to January 1871).
- The Treaty of Paris (1856, Crimea) vs the Treaty of Frankfurt (1871) vs the Treaty of Turin (1860, Nice and Savoy).
- Liberalism (rights and a parliament, but a property franchise) is not democracy (the vote for all men). The July Monarchy was liberal but not democratic; the Second Empire was democratic in its franchise but not liberal until the 1860s.

## 4. What exams ask

- Higher 100-mark essay on why France changed regime so often, or why it moved from monarchy to republic to empire: marks go to taking each regime change in turn (1830, 1848, 1851–52, 1870) and explaining its causes with dated evidence, not narrating every event. Strong answers name the trigger each time (the July Ordinances, the banquet ban, the failed revision, Sedan) and the deeper cause (narrow franchise, refusal to reform, economic crisis, defeat).
- Higher essay on why France was politically unstable: a cause question. Build it on factors (the divided legacy of 1789, rival dynasties, narrow franchises, the power of Paris, economic crisis, the president–assembly conflict of 1848, one-man regimes, war), each with a concrete example. End with a judgement on which mattered most; the town–country split is an effective linking point.
- Higher essay on Napoleon III's internal and external policies, or on whether he was effective at home and abroad: examiners reward paired achievements vs limits on each side and a clear verdict (the usual one: more successful at home than abroad). Home: order, growth, banks, railways, Paris, social measures and liberal reforms vs repression and a weak legislature. Abroad: Crimea, Italy, Nice and Savoy and colonies vs Poland, Mexico, 1866 and 1870.
- Higher essay pairing Napoleon III with Haussmann: the Napoleon III half needs his rise, the authoritarian and liberal Empire, the economy and foreign policy, with a judgement; leave Haussmann's detail to the Haussmann notes.
- Higher essay agreeing or disagreeing that France was politically unstable but culturally productive: the political half comes from this subsection (five regimes, how each fell, why); state the agreement explicitly and give the cultural half from the cultural notes.
- Higher essays on Hugo (with Darwin or Haussmann): the political half needs his career from royalist peer to opponent of Napoleon III, the 1851 resistance, the exile, Napoléon le Petit and Les Châtiments, the refused amnesty and his return in 1870, with a judgement of his contribution. Literary detail alone does not cover the question.
- Ordinary Level 30/40-mark paragraphs on Napoleon III (the commonest from this subsection): each correct, relevant, dated statement earns marks. Cover birth and family, Strasbourg and Boulogne, Ham, the 1848 election and its result, the coup and plebiscite, the Empire, economic growth (Crédit Mobilier, railways), the liberal reforms, foreign policy (Crimea, Italy, Mexico), Sedan and exile.
- Ordinary Level paragraphs on Victor Hugo's opposition to Napoleon III: the 1848–51 Assembly career, the coup, exile on Jersey and Guernsey, his anti-Napoleon writings, refusal of the amnesty, return in 1870.
- Ordinary Level document questions: a Hugo passage attacking Napoleon III (comprehension of his view, the nickname "the Little", how the Empire ended); a short question on who replaced Charles X. Students need the regime sequence and dates exactly: Louis XVIII, Charles X, Louis-Philippe, Second Republic, Napoleon III, Third Republic.
- Contemporary vs later views are rewarded: Hugo and Marx as hostile contemporaries; revisionist historians (Zeldin, Price) as later, more favourable judges.
