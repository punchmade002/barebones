## 1. Must cover (one bullet or block for every item; tick each off)

Background (assumed)
- [ ] A1 (assumed) Germany's defeat in the First World War: the Kaiser abdicated and a republic was proclaimed in November 1918; the armistice of 11 November 1918 ended the fighting.
- [ ] A2 (assumed) Reparations: payments a defeated state makes to the victors for war damage.
- [ ] A3 (assumed) How the Weimar system worked: an elected President (head of state), a Chancellor (head of government) who needed the support of a Reichstag (parliament) majority, usually a coalition of several parties.
- [ ] A4 (assumed) The main parties, left to right: Communists (KPD), Social Democrats (SPD), the Catholic Centre Party, the liberal parties (including Stresemann's DVP), the nationalist DNVP, and the Nazis (NSDAP). The SPD, Centre and liberals were the parties that supported the republic.
- [ ] A5 (assumed) The Wall Street Crash (October 1929) began the worldwide Depression.
- [ ] A6 (assumed) Putsch: an attempt to seize power by force.

Key concepts (each defined as used in this topic; also in keyTerms)
- [ ] K1 Inflation, and hyperinflation as its extreme form (Germany, 1923).
- [ ] K2 The Depression: the slump after 1929, with falling output, bank failures and mass unemployment.
- [ ] K3 Democracy: government by elected representatives, with free elections and the rule of law; the Weimar Republic as Germany's first democracy and why it was fragile.

Foundations of the republic and its weaknesses
- [ ] W1 The National Assembly at Weimar (away from the unrest in Berlin) and the constitution of 31 July 1919: a federal republic; a President elected by the people for seven years; a Chancellor responsible to the Reichstag.
- [ ] W2 Proportional representation: many small parties, coalition governments, no stable majority.
- [ ] W3 Article 48: the President's emergency decree power, central after 1930.
- [ ] W4 Hostile elites: judges, civil servants, army officers and university teachers from the imperial era stayed in post.
- [ ] W5 The "stab in the back" myth (and the "November criminals" label for the politicians of 1918).
- [ ] W6 Presidents: Ebert (1919 to his death in 1925); Hindenburg, a former field marshal, from 1925.
- [ ] W7 Versailles resentment: the treaty of June 1919 (territorial losses, military limits, reparations, Article 231 war guilt).
- [ ] W8 The London Schedule of 1921: reparations set at 132 billion gold marks.

Early crises, 1919–1923
- [ ] C1 The Spartacist rising (January 1919) and the murders of Liebknecht and Luxemburg; put down with Freikorps help (brief background).
- [ ] C2 The Kapp Putsch (March 1920): Kapp and the Ehrhardt Freikorps brigade seized Berlin; the army refused to defend the government; it collapsed after four days when the unions called a general strike.
- [ ] C3 Right-wing political murders: Erzberger (1921), Rathenau (June 1922).
- [ ] C4 Biased courts: Gumbel's count of political murders, 1919–1922, and the far heavier sentences for left-wing killers.
- [ ] C5 The Ruhr occupation (January 1923) by France and Belgium after Germany fell behind on deliveries of timber and coal.
- [ ] C6 Passive resistance called by Chancellor Cuno and paid for by printing money.

Hyperinflation and currency reform, 1923–1924
- [ ] H1 Hyperinflation: the dollar rate by November 1923; prices rising by the hour; wages paid daily or twice daily; barter.
- [ ] H2 Losers: middle-class savers, pensioners, holders of war loans, people on fixed incomes; their lasting resentment of the republic.
- [ ] H3 Winners: debtors, speculators and industrialists such as Hugo Stinnes who borrowed to buy assets.
- [ ] H4 Stresemann as Chancellor (August 1923) called off passive resistance (September 1923).
- [ ] H5 The Rentenmark (15 November 1923), backed by a mortgage on land and industry; Schacht as currency commissioner then Reichsbank president limited its supply; replaced by the Reichsmark in 1924.
- [ ] H6 The Dawes Plan (1924): chaired by Charles Dawes; payments rising with capacity to pay; a loan of 800 million gold marks; the French left the Ruhr by 1925.
- [ ] H7 Recovery 1924–1929 dependent on American loans, withdrawn after the Wall Street Crash.

The early Nazi party and the Munich Putsch
- [ ] N1 Hitler joined the German Workers' Party (September 1919); renamed NSDAP with the 25-point programme (February 1920); Hitler leader with dictatorial powers (July 1921).
- [ ] N2 Early support: Bavarian ex-soldiers, anti-communist groups; the SA founded 1921.
- [ ] N3 The Munich (Beer Hall) Putsch, 8–9 November 1923: Hitler and Ludendorff, the Bürgerbräukeller, the planned march on Berlin; sixteen Nazis and four police killed.
- [ ] N4 The trial (early 1924) as a national platform; the minimum five-year sentence; about nine months served at Landsberg.
- [ ] N5 Mein Kampf: written in prison; race, Lebensraum, destroying Marxism and the Jews; first volume 1925.
- [ ] N6 The party refounded (February 1925) and the legal road to power through elections; 1928 result (12 seats, 2.6 per cent).
- [ ] N7 The 1929 campaign against the Young Plan with Hugenberg's DNVP and newspapers: national publicity and respectability.

Stresemann's stabilisation, 1924–1929
- [ ] S1 Foreign Minister 1923–1929: revising Versailles by negotiation (fulfilment), not force.
- [ ] S2 The Locarno Treaties (October 1925): Germany's western borders confirmed; League of Nations entry (1926).
- [ ] S3 Nobel Peace Prize 1926, shared with Briand.
- [ ] S4 The Young Plan (1929): chaired by Owen D. Young; total reduced; payments spread to 1988.
- [ ] S5 The welfare state: eight-hour day (1918), state-funded housing, the 1927 unemployment insurance act and its funding limit.
- [ ] S6 Stresemann's death (October 1929) and the loss of his leadership.
- [ ] S7 The limits of the "golden years": recovery built on American loans; agriculture and the middle classes did not share fully.

The Depression and the end of parliamentary government, 1929–1932
- [ ] D1 German unemployment: from about 1.9 million (1929 average) to about 6 million registered (early 1932); causes: American loans called in, exports fell.
- [ ] D2 The fall of the grand coalition (SPD, Centre, liberals) in March 1930 over unemployment insurance contributions.
- [ ] D3 Brüning (Chancellor from March 1930): rule by Article 48 decrees; cuts to wages, pensions and welfare; deflation that deepened the slump.
- [ ] D4 The banking crisis of 1931: Creditanstalt (May), Danat Bank (July).
- [ ] D5 Reparations ended: Brüning's aim; Hoover's moratorium (1931); the Lausanne Conference (1932).
- [ ] D6 Middle-class and farming groups, hit by falling prices, turned to radical parties.
- [ ] D7 The presidential cabinets: Brüning, Papen and Schleicher governed through the President, not a Reichstag majority.

The Nazi breakthrough, 1930–1932
- [ ] B1 September 1930 election: NSDAP 107 seats (18.3 per cent), second to the SPD; KPD 77 seats.
- [ ] B2 Propaganda: attacks on Versailles, the Weimar parties and communism; promises of work, bread and national revival.
- [ ] B3 Goebbels's methods (Gauleiter of Berlin from 1926; rallies, posters, Der Angriff, film) and Hitler's 1932 air tours. Brief: his full career belongs to the propaganda notes.
- [ ] B4 The SA: street violence against communists and Social Democrats, an image of strength, a home for unemployed young men (uniforms, meals, shelter).
- [ ] B5 Who voted Nazi: disproportionately Protestant, rural and small-town, middle-class and young; Catholic Centre voters and SPD/KPD industrial workers less receptive, though many workers did vote Nazi.
- [ ] B6 The 1932 presidential election: Hitler's citizenship (February 1932); both rounds' results; Hindenburg re-elected.
- [ ] B7 July 1932 election: 230 seats (about 37 per cent), largest party; Hitler refused any post below Chancellor; Hindenburg refused to appoint him (August).
- [ ] B8 November 1932 election: about two million votes and 34 seats lost (196 seats, about 33 per cent), still the largest party; the party short of money.

From Papen to Hitler, 1932–1933
- [ ] P1 Brüning's ban on the SA and SS (April 1932); his dismissal (May); Papen's lifting of the ban (June); Altona "Bloody Sunday" (July 1932).
- [ ] P2 The Prussian coup (July 1932): Papen removed the Social Democrat government of Prussia.
- [ ] P3 Schleicher (Chancellor 3 December 1932): the offer to Gregor Strasser; trade union approach and job creation; resignation 28 January 1933.
- [ ] P4 The Papen–Hitler meeting at Kurt von Schröder's house, Cologne (4 January 1933); Hitler appointed Chancellor on 30 January 1933 with only three Nazis in cabinet (Hitler, Frick, Göring), Papen Vice-Chancellor, Hugenberg in cabinet; the conservatives' belief that they could control him.

How Weimar governments dealt with economic and social problems
- [ ] G1 The Stinnes-Legien agreement (November 1918): eight-hour day and union recognition in return for employers' co-operation.
- [ ] G2 The Hauszinssteuer: a tax on house owners who had gained from inflation, funding municipal housing in Berlin and Frankfurt.
- [ ] G3 Ending hyperinflation (end of passive resistance, budget balanced, Rentenmark); savers never fully compensated.
- [ ] G4 The 1927 Unemployment Insurance Act: designed for about 800,000 claimants; overwhelmed when unemployment passed three million.
- [ ] G5 Brüning's 1930–31 emergency decrees: cuts in civil service pay, benefit and wages; tax rises; deflation.
- [ ] G6 The Voluntary Labour Service (1931), widened under Papen and Schleicher.
- [ ] G7 Papen's tax certificates (September 1932) and Schleicher's job-creation programme (December 1932): too late to take effect.
- [ ] G8 Social strain by 1932: families on reduced benefit or poor relief; the SA as refuge.

Hitler's rise, 1919–1933 (personality block for this period only)
- [ ] R1 Who he was: born 1889 at Braunau am Inn, Austria; served in the German army in the First World War; Iron Cross.
- [ ] R2 Army political informant in Munich in 1919; the party's most effective speaker.
- [ ] R3 Leader from 1921: rallies, a private army, charisma.
- [ ] R4 Participant who learned from failure: the legal road after 1923, with SA violence alongside.
- [ ] R5 Contemporary views: saviour (millions of voters), tool of big business (the left), a demagogue the conservatives thought they had hired and could control.
- [ ] R6 Historians: appointed legally, but why? Bullock (opportunism and will to power) vs Turner (backstairs intrigue among a few men around Hindenburg in January 1933).

Assessment: why the Weimar Republic failed (paired judgement points)
- [ ] J1 Structural weaknesses (PR, Article 48, no stable majority) vs the view that the constitution worked in the stable years.
- [ ] J2 Versailles and reparations as a lasting grievance vs Stresemann's success in revising them by negotiation.
- [ ] J3 Achievement: recovery 1924–1929 (inflation beaten, League entry) vs limit: dependence on American loans, exposed by the Crash.
- [ ] J4 The Depression as the decisive trigger (Nazis small in 1928, mass party by 1930) vs long-term causes.
- [ ] J5 Inevitability: Nazi support falling by late 1932; the appointment resulted from decisions by a small conservative group (Turner); contemporary left and liberal critics blamed the conservative elite, while the Nazis claimed to be the only force for order.

## 3. Traps (known errors and boundaries)

- Do not use: "with army support" for the Kapp Putsch (wording in the current factCards), because the army as a whole did not back Kapp: one general (Lüttwitz) joined him, while the army leadership refused to defend the government. Say "the army refused to defend the government".
- Do not use: the "eight-hour hour" wording in the current Stinnes-Legien factCard; it is a typo for "eight-hour day". Do not copy it into the notes (factCards themselves are not to be edited).
- Do not use: Stresemann's "dancing on a volcano" remark, because its wording and date are not well attested.
- Do not use: any figure for the number of Article 48 decrees or of Reichstag sittings per year, or Nazi membership figures, because none is checked here.
- Hitler was appointed, not elected, Chancellor. The Nazis never won a majority in a free election; their peak in a free election was about 37 per cent (July 1932). The March 1933 election belongs to the notes on the Nazi state.
- In 1930 the Nazis were the second largest party, not the largest; they became the largest in July 1932.
- Hindenburg was President, never Chancellor. Papen was Vice-Chancellor in Hitler's first cabinet.
- Stresemann was Chancellor only from August to November 1923; he was Foreign Minister from 1923 to 1929. Most of his achievements came as Foreign Minister.
- Rentenmark (November 1923, temporary) vs Reichsmark (1924, permanent).
- Dawes Plan (1924) rescheduled payments and brought loans but did not reduce the total; the Young Plan (1929) reduced the total and set payments to 1988.
- Locarno confirmed Germany's western borders only, not its eastern borders.
- Stresemann's Nobel Prize was 1926, shared with Briand (the 1925 prize went to Austen Chamberlain and Charles Dawes; do not name them in the notes).
- Hyperinflation (1923) and the Depression (1929–1932) are separate crises: the first destroyed savings through rising prices; the second brought mass unemployment and falling prices, made worse by Brüning's deflation. Do not say the Depression caused hyperinflation.
- Hitler's sentence was five years but he served about nine months; Landsberg, not a Munich prison.
- Gumbel's figures cover 1919–1922 only, not the whole Weimar period.
- The SA was founded in 1921; the SS ban in April 1932 was alongside the SA ban. The Night of the Long Knives (1934) is in the notes on the Nazi state.
- Key personality: the outline assigns Hitler to the notes on the Nazi state. Here keep his block to 1889–1933 only and say his rule from 1933 is in the notes on the Nazi state in peace.
- Neighbouring subjects. Leave these out or give one line with a cross-reference by subject:
  - The Reichstag fire, Enabling Act, Gleichschaltung, the Night of the Long Knives, Hitler as Führer, the Nazi economy and Hitler's foreign policy: the notes on the Nazi state in peace.
  - Goebbels's career after 1933, the Propaganda Ministry and the Nuremberg Rallies: the notes on Nazi propaganda.
  - The general conditions favouring fascism and its characteristics, and Mussolini: the notes on Mussolini's Italy.
  - Britain's Depression, Keynes and the Jarrow March: the notes on inter-war Britain and the Jarrow March.
  - Nazi anti-Semitism beyond Mein Kampf's outline ideas (boycott, Nuremberg Laws, Kristallnacht): the notes on anti-Semitism and the Holocaust.

  - Bolshevik Russia and the KPD's links to Moscow: the notes on communism in Russia.

## 4. What exams ask (topics only)

- Essay: how German governments dealt with economic and social problems (1920–1939; this subsection covers the Weimar part, to 1933). Reward goes to pairing each problem with a response and a verdict: hyperinflation ended by the Rentenmark (success), but savers ruined (limit); welfare state built (success), but unemployment insurance underfunded and collapsed (limit); Brüning's deflation and late job creation (failure). Conclude with an overall judgement ("mixed success").
- Essay: whether Britain or Germany had the greater inter-war economic and social problems. Students need the German side: defeat, reparations, the 1923 hyperinflation, dependence on American loans, unemployment reaching about 6 million, the political consequence (the collapse of democracy). Argue a clear position and support it with comparison.
- Essay: how Hitler and the Nazis won power (the first stage, 1920–1933, of questions on Hitler's route to absolute control). Credit goes to cause-and-result chains: Depression → unemployment → Nazi votes; propaganda and the SA; the 1930 and 1932 elections with seat figures; the intrigue of Papen, Schleicher and Hindenburg; appointment on 30 January 1933. Stress that he came to power legally, by appointment.
- Essay: why Germany (and Italy) embraced dictatorship, and the conditions that favoured fascism. German material: the "stab in the back" and Versailles resentment, a weak constitution, fear of communism (KPD gains), economic crisis twice (1923 and 1929–32), hostile elites.
- Short document questions (Ordinary Level) on everyday life in the 1923 hyperinflation: what wages could buy, being paid daily, prices changing before goods were bought. Students should link the details to their cause (printing money for passive resistance) and effect (ruined savings).
- Judgement throughout: achievements vs limits of Stresemann (recovery and diplomacy vs dependence on loans); whether the republic was doomed from the start or destroyed by the Depression and the choices of a few men. Paired points with dates and figures earn more than narrative.
