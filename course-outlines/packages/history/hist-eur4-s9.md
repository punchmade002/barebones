## 1. Must cover (one bullet or block for every item; tick each off)

Background (assumed)
- [ ] A1 (assumed) The Second World War left Europe devastated, with housing shortages and millions of men returning from service.
- [ ] A2 (assumed) Western Europe's post-war economic growth (see the notes on the Western economies, 1945–1973) is what paid for rising incomes, welfare states and consumer goods.
- [ ] A3 (assumed) Before 1945 most European women had few legal rights in marriage, were paid less than men, and in many countries had gained the vote only recently (France in 1944–45, Italy in 1945).

Key concepts
- [ ] K1 Feminism: argues for equal rights and opportunities for women and challenges the roles society assigns them (also in keyTerms).
- [ ] K2 Second-wave feminism: the movement of the 1960s and 1970s for equal pay, reproductive rights and an end to confinement in the home.
- [ ] K3 Consumer society, affluence, baby boom, consumer credit, equal pay, package holiday (essential terms; keyTerms).

Marriage, the family and the baby boom
- [ ] F1 The baby boom (about 1946 to the mid-1960s) and earlier, more common marriage.
- [ ] F2 The ideal of the nuclear family with a male breadwinner and a housewife-mother; promoted by churches and conservative parties; reinforced by cinema, magazines, television and advertising.
- [ ] F3 Housing shortages and public housing; family allowances as part of the welfare state.
- [ ] F4 Legal inequality inside marriage (the husband's legal powers): France, 1965 reform.

Women in the workforce
- [ ] W1 More married women in paid work from the 1950s, mainly in service and clerical jobs; low-paid, "women's" occupations; pay gap.
- [ ] W2 Equal pay: Article 119 of the Treaty of Rome (1957) and its weak early enforcement; the Defrenne case (1976); Britain's Equal Pay Act (1970, in force 1975) and Sex Discrimination Act (1975).

Feminism, contraception and abortion
- [ ] R1 The contraceptive pill (US 1960; widely used in Western Europe in the 1960s).
- [ ] R2 France: the Neuwirth Law (1967) on contraception; the Veil Law (17 January 1975) on abortion.
- [ ] R3 Abortion elsewhere: Britain (1967), Italy (1978).
- [ ] R4 The Catholic Church's opposition: Humanae Vitae (1968).
- [ ] R5 Second-wave feminism: Friedan (1963), the MLF in France (1970), the Manifesto of the 343 (1971), Choisir.

Divorce and family law
- [ ] D1 Britain: Divorce Reform Act (1969, in force 1971).
- [ ] D2 Italy: divorce law (1970) and the referendum of 12 May 1974.
- [ ] D3 West Germany: the 1977 reform (no-fault divorce, end of the "housewife marriage" clause).
- [ ] D4 Later and slower cases: Spain (1981), Ireland (1995).
- [ ] D5 Rising divorce rates, single-parent households; Italy's 1975 family-law reform (equality of spouses); the conservative reaction.

Affluence and the consumer society
- [ ] C1 Consumer durables (refrigerators, washing machines, televisions) and the car: Beetle, Fiat 500, 2CV, Mini.
- [ ] C2 Supermarkets and self-service; hire purchase and consumer credit.
- [ ] C3 Advertising and television as drivers of consumption.
- [ ] C4 Criticism of consumerism: Packard (1957), Galbraith (1958), Marcuse (1964), Debord and the Situationists (1967).

Leisure time and mass tourism
- [ ] L1 Shorter working hours and longer paid holidays.
- [ ] L2 Package holidays and charter flights to Mediterranean coasts; Club Méditerranée (1950).
- [ ] L3 Domestic leisure: car-based trips, camping, television, cinema, records, home improvement.

Differences between East and West
- [ ] E1 Eastern bloc: high female employment, state childcare, legal equality, but the "double burden" at home.
- [ ] E2 Examples: East Germany (maternity leave, nurseries, high divorce rate); Soviet Union (abortion legalised 1955; Tereshkova 1963).
- [ ] E3 Consumer goods scarcer in the East: waiting lists for cars (Trabant); travel restricted; the East's view of Western television and goods.
- [ ] E4 No independent feminist movement in the East; the Western movement did not exist there.

Key personality: Simone de Beauvoir (part 1 only; one block, every point)
- [ ] P1 Who she was: born Paris 1908; philosopher and writer; partner of Jean-Paul Sartre.
- [ ] P2 Career: passed the agrégation in philosophy (1929), taught for some years.
- [ ] P3 What she did: The Second Sex (1949) in two volumes; its argument (woman as the "Other"; woman made not born; history, law, religion and education, not biology; economic independence).
- [ ] P4 Participant or witness: lived through the Occupation and war; in the 1970s took part in the French feminist movement (MLF, Manifesto of the 343, Choisir).
- [ ] P5 Contemporaries: the book was attacked or mocked (Catholic Church Index 1956; some on the left and right), and read by younger women as a guide.
- [ ] P6 Historians: foundational text of second-wave feminism; criticisms (assumptions of a universal experience, class and race).
- [ ] P7 Her own words: "One is not born, but rather becomes, a woman."

Assessment (final block, paired points)
- [ ] S1 Achievements: legal and employment equality, contraception and abortion, easier divorce, more choice.
- [ ] S2 Limits: unequal domestic work, a persistent pay gap, low-paid jobs, uneven gains across countries and classes, in the East equality in law but not at home.
- [ ] S3 Consumerism: higher living standards and leisure vs conformity, credit and pressure to buy.
- [ ] S4 Contemporary vs later views: churches and conservatives defending the traditional family vs feminists; later historians see a turning point but stress uneven gains.

## 3. Traps

Do not use (wrong or doubtful facts in the current notes):
- Do not use: "In West Germany the law until 1977 allowed a husband to decide whether his wife could take a job", because the husband's right to forbid his wife's job was abolished in 1958; what lasted until 1977 was the clause that a wife could work only if it fitted her family duties. Use the wording in section 2 (D3).
- Do not use: Britain's Equal Pay Act 1970 as requiring pay "for work of equal value", because the original Act covered like work or work rated equivalent; "equal value" was added in 1983.
- Do not use: West Germany "reformed its abortion law in 1974–76" as a bare fact, because the 1974 law was struck down by the Constitutional Court in 1975 and replaced in 1976 by a narrower scheme. Leave West Germany out or say only that it reformed the law in the 1970s.
- Do not use: "family allowances extended in France in 1945–46", because the date is imprecise (allowances predate the war). Say only that welfare states paid family allowances.
- Do not use: "forty-hour week spreading in the 1960s" and "paid holidays extended to three or four weeks" across Western Europe generally, because the dates vary by country. Use the French dates in section 2 (L1).
- Do not use: de Beauvoir as "among the first women to teach philosophy in French lycées", because it cannot be checked. Use the agrégation fact.
- Do not use: "Sweden and Denmark had liberal divorce laws earlier", because it is vague and unchecked; drop it.
- Do not use: "Italy legalised divorce in 1970 ... a referendum upheld it" as a single event; two dates (1970 law, 1974 referendum) are given in section 2.
- Do not use: Volkswagen Beetle as a "symbol of the consumer society" without date; the Beetle's mass-market success came in the 1950s and 1960s.
- Do not use: any figure for women's employment percentages (not checked).
- Do not use: "Valentina Tereshkova" as evidence that Soviet women were equal; she is an example of achievement, and the double burden is the contrast.

## 4. What exams ask

- Higher 100-mark essay on marriage, the family and women, 1945–1992 (asked repeatedly, most recently in the early 2020s): how and why the position of women changed. Marks go to a stated judgement, organised by theme (work, law, reproduction, family), with dated laws and named people; mere lists of laws score less than explanations of why change came (growth in service jobs, the pill, feminism, the EEC's equal-pay rule).
- Higher question on affluence and the consumer society (also 2016-style): what changed in daily life (goods, cars, supermarkets, credit, holidays), why (growth, wages, credit, advertising, television), and who criticised it. Strong answers add limits (unequal access, scarcity in the East, critics such as Galbraith and Marcuse).
- Comparing East and West is rewarded: same legal equality and female employment, but different consumer goods and no free feminist movement in the East; the "double burden" is the key concept.
- Questions on de Beauvoir (usually paired with Solzhenitsyn or another figure): what The Second Sex argued, her 1949 date, her activism in the 1970s, how contemporaries and historians judged her. Include one sentence of argument ("made, not born") and one of impact; name a reaction (condemnation by the Church, praise by younger feminists).
- Ordinary Level short answers: de Beauvoir's book and its argument; the contraceptive pill and its effect; why more women worked; two ways life changed in the 1950s and 1960s (televisions, cars, holidays). Each correct, dated point earns marks.
- Ordinary Level 30/40-mark paragraphs: for women's work, give three or four changes with years (equal pay, the pill, abortion law, divorce law); for the consumer society, name items, the credit and supermarket changes and one critic.
- Tip for the writer: state each law with its country and year; examiners reward "France, 1975" more than "some countries legalised abortion".
