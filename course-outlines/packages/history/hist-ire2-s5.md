## 1. Must cover (one bullet or block for every item; tick each off)

Background (assumed)
- [ ] A1 (assumed) Gladstone's conversion to Home Rule (1885–86) and the First Home Rule Bill's defeat in 1886 (detail belongs to the Parnell and Home Rule notes).
- [ ] A2 (assumed) Gladstone's earlier reforms: disestablishment of the Church of Ireland (1869) and the Land Acts (1870, 1881), which alarmed many Protestants.
- [ ] A3 (assumed) Ulster's industrial north-east (Belfast linen, shipbuilding, engineering) differed economically from the agricultural south and west.
- [ ] A4 (assumed) The Parliament Act of 1911 removed the Lords' absolute veto, so a Home Rule Bill could become law within about two years.

Key concepts (each defined as used in this topic; also in keyTerms)
- [ ] K1 Militarism: the use of armed, drilled volunteer forces as a political tool (the UVF from 1913).
- [ ] K2 Democracy: rule by the elected majority; nationalists said unionist threats of force defied it, unionists said Ulster must consent to its own government.
- [ ] K3 Unionism: the movement to keep Ireland in the United Kingdom and oppose Home Rule (an essential term).

Who the unionists were
- [ ] W1 A coalition of Protestants of all classes in Ulster (landlords, industrialists, shopkeepers, Presbyterian farmers, Belfast workers).
- [ ] W2 Geography: Protestant majority in Ulster as a whole, concentrated in the east; the counties with a Protestant majority and those narrowly Catholic.
- [ ] W3 Southern unionists: a small minority (landlords, professionals, businessmen, Church of Ireland clergy), strongest in Dublin and parts of Leinster and Munster.
- [ ] W4 Presbyterians moved from the Liberals to unionism after 1886.

Why unionists opposed Home Rule
- [ ] R1 Religion: fear of Catholic Church influence in a Dublin parliament; "Home Rule is Rome Rule"; the Ne Temere decree as later support.
- [ ] R2 Economy: Belfast's industries depended on British and imperial markets and raw materials; fear of tariffs, higher taxes and an agricultural bias.
- [ ] R3 Identity and empire: Ulster Protestants saw themselves as British and imperial; Home Rule seen as a step to separation.
- [ ] R4 Fear of becoming a minority in a Catholic-majority state, and of nationalists' attitude to property after the Land War.
- [ ] R5 Liberal Unionist argument (Chamberlain, Hartington): Home Rule threatened the integrity of the United Kingdom, not only Ulster.

Organisation of unionism, 1885–1905
- [ ] O1 The Orange Order (founded 1795) revived in the 1880s and gave unionism a mass base of lodges.
- [ ] O2 The Irish Loyal and Patriotic Union (1885, southern unionists); the Ulster Loyalist Anti-Repeal Union (1886).
- [ ] O3 The Belfast riots of 1886 and Lord Randolph Churchill's "Orange card" (brief; detail belongs to the 1885–86 notes).
- [ ] O4 The Irish Unionist Alliance (1891) united the unionist bodies.
- [ ] O5 The Ulster Convention (June 1892) against the coming Second Home Rule Bill; the Lords' defeat of that Bill (1893).
- [ ] O6 The Ulster Unionist Council (1905): why it was formed (the 1904–05 devolution scare) and who it brought together.
- [ ] O7 The Ulster Women's Unionist Council (January 1911).

Key personality: Edward Carson (one block, every point)
- [ ] P1 Who he was: Dublin-born barrister, born 1854, educated at Trinity College; Solicitor General for Ireland (1892); MP for Dublin University from 1892; Solicitor General for England (1900–06).
- [ ] P2 Career highlights outside politics: the 1895 Wilde case as counsel for Queensberry.
- [ ] P3 What he did, with dates: leader of the Irish Unionist parliamentary party from 1910; chief campaigner 1911–14; Ulster Day 1912; Provisional Government plan 1913; Larne and the 1914 negotiations.
- [ ] P4 Shaped events: made the threat of resistance credible; shaped by Craig's organisation and Conservative backing; his Dublin and southern unionist background.
- [ ] P5 Participant: he led, spoke and signed first on Ulster Day.
- [ ] P6 Contemporaries: unionists revered him; nationalists and many Liberals saw a man ready to defy Parliament; Redmond's view.
- [ ] P7 Historians: bluff versus real intent; partition made inevitable; the position of southern unionists (cite Stewart, Jackson).
- [ ] P8 Later: resigned the leadership in 1921 and became a law lord; the 1921 partition settlement.

The Third Home Rule crisis, 1910–1912
- [ ] T1 Parliament Act (August 1911) and what it meant for the Bill.
- [ ] T2 James Craig as Carson's organiser; the Craigavon demonstration (September 1911).
- [ ] T3 Conservative alliance: Bonar Law's leadership and the Blenheim speech (27 July 1912).
- [ ] T4 The Third Home Rule Bill introduced (11 April 1912); the exclusion amendment defeated (June 1912).
- [ ] T5 Ulster Day, 28 September 1912: the Solemn League and Covenant, its wording, the signatories, the women's declaration.
- [ ] T6 The Bill's passage through Commons and Lords, 1913–14, under the Parliament Act.

The Ulster Volunteer Force and the Provisional Government, 1913
- [ ] U1 The UVF formed in January 1913 (commander, size, drilling).
- [ ] U2 The Ulster Unionist Council's Provisional Government plan (September 1913), loyalty to the Crown but not to a Dublin parliament.
- [ ] U3 The government did not prosecute; the nationalist response (Irish Volunteers, November 1913; detail belongs to the advanced nationalism notes).

1914: Curragh, Larne and exclusion
- [ ] Y1 Asquith's county option proposal (March 1914) and Carson's rejection.
- [ ] Y2 The Curragh incident (20 March 1914): what happened, the resignations, the effect on the government's options.
- [ ] Y3 The Larne gun-running (24–25 April 1914): organiser, ship, numbers, official inaction.
- [ ] Y4 Nationalist counter-gun-running at Howth and Kilcoole (brief).
- [ ] Y5 The Buckingham Palace Conference (21–24 July 1914): participants and failure over Tyrone and Fermanagh.
- [ ] Y6 Royal assent and suspension of the Home Rule Act (18 September 1914).
- [ ] Y7 The positions on exclusion: Carson wanted Ulster (or six counties) excluded permanently; the temporary county option was not enough for him; nationalists resisted partition.

Assessment (paired judgement points, final block)
- [ ] S1 Success: two Home Rule Bills defeated (1886, 1893); effective organisation; mass mobilisation.
- [ ] S2 Success: forced the government to accept exclusion in principle; Home Rule suspended in 1914.
- [ ] S3 Success: the UVF, Covenant and Conservative support showed Ulster could not be coerced; Carson and Craig decisive.
- [ ] S4 Limit: could not stop the Bill becoming law; southern unionists unprotected; unionism divided.
- [ ] S5 Limit: dependence on the Conservatives, who compromised later (1920–21).
- [ ] S6 Contemporary views (nationalists: arms threaten democracy; unionists: defence of rights) and historians' views (Stewart on closeness to civil war; Jackson on resilience and division).

## 3. Traps

- Do not use: "signed in their own blood or at the church door", because only a few anecdotal claims exist; "the total of 471,000 included many in Britain", because the 471,000 total is of the Ulster Day signatures and the claim is unsupported; "a British Covenant organised by the Union Defence League (about two million)", because the British Covenant (1914) was by a different body and the figure is a claim, not a verified count.
- Do not use: "the Catholic hierarchy's role in the Irish Party's candidate selection in 1885", because it is doubtful and not needed; "Protestants held a disproportionate share of Ireland's land, wealth and professions", because it is too general; "the Ulster Convention protested against the Second Home Rule Bill", because the Convention (June 1892) came before the Bill (1893).
- Do not use: "Carson lamented that unionism had been sacrificed in the creation of Northern Ireland" and any remembered quotation about being a "puppet", because the wording is disputed.
- Do not use: "Redmond rejected permanent exclusion, Carson rejected temporary exclusion", because the positions shifted: use Y7.
- Do not use: "the Curragh mutiny" as fact; call it the Curragh incident; the officers offered to accept dismissal. The term "mutiny" was applied by critics, but no mutiny occurred.
- Do not use: Orange Order lodge numbers, UVF numbers beyond "about 90,000 or more", Larne weapon counts beyond those in the table.
- Confusables: Carson (leader, Dublin-born) vs Craig (organiser, Belfast); Saunderson (leader to 1906) vs Long (1906–10) vs Carson (from 1910); Ulster Convention (1892) vs Ulster Day (1912); Irish Loyal and Patriotic Union (southern, 1885) vs Ulster Unionist Council (1905) vs Irish Unionist Alliance (1891); UVF (unionist, January 1913) vs Irish Volunteers (nationalist, November 1913); Larne (UVF) vs Howth and Kilcoole (nationalist); Asquith's March 1914 county option vs the Buckingham Palace Conference.
- Neighbouring subsections (do not narrate here): the First Home Rule Bill, the 1885 election and Parnell's career; Redmond, the Irish Volunteers, MacNeill and Howth in detail (Home Rule and advanced nationalism); the Land Acts and constructive unionism; Isabella Tod and women's suffrage; Dublin 1913 (working-class unionisation); Belfast's industrial growth and the shipyard expulsions; the First World War, the Easter Rising and partition after 1914 (a later chapter).
- Use "unionist" for the political movement; do not call all Protestants unionists without noting the southern minority.

## 4. What exams ask

- Why and how unionism developed and opposed Home Rule (recurring Higher Level essay): name the three reasons (religion, economy, identity and empire), then show development as organisation: Orange Order, ILPU/ULAU, Convention 1892, UUC 1905, Covenant 1912, UVF 1913. Marks go to linking cause to organisation, with dated examples.
- How effective unionism was in resisting Home Rule: pair achievements (two Bills defeated, exclusion conceded in principle, Home Rule suspended) with limits (the Bill became law, southern unionists left unprotected, reliance on the Conservatives). A balanced verdict, stated, earns top marks.
- Unionist achievements: be ready to list four or five, each with a fact.
- Edward Carson (Higher Level essay and ordinary-level short profile): a profile needs birth and background (Dublin, barrister), his 1910 leadership, the 1912 Covenant, and the 1913 Provisional Government plan. A longer answer needs a judgement of success and failure, contemporary views and historians' differing views.
- Carson or Redmond comparisons: use Carson's mobilisation of resistance and Redmond's Act on the statute book; compare methods (constitutional against threat of force) and results (delay versus partition).
- Source questions on Carson, the Covenant or Ulster resistance (documents-based): place a document by date and event; explain that the demand was Ulster's consent; spot the purpose and bias of unionist or nationalist sources.
- Short answers often ask "who, what, when": Craigavon, Blenheim speech, Ulster Day signing, UVF commander, Larne date. Learn each as a one-line fact.
- Common slips: confusing the Convention with Ulster Day; attributing Howth to unionists; saying Carson was an Ulsterman; claiming the UVF fought in 1914; claiming Home Rule was never passed (it passed and was suspended).
