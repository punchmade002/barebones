## 1. Must cover (one bullet or block for every item; tick each off)

Background (assumed)
- [ ] A1 (assumed) European powers partitioned Africa in the later nineteenth century (Berlin Conference, 1884–85), drawing borders that cut across peoples and joined rival ones; this is the root of later ethnic tension.
- [ ] A2 (assumed) The Second World War weakened Britain, France, Belgium's and Portugal's standing; African nationalism grew after 1945; Ghana (1957) led the wave, and about seventeen African states became independent in 1960.
- [ ] A3 (assumed) The Cold War: the USA and USSR competed for influence in new states; this shaped the Congo and Angola. (Katanga and Mobutu's career are covered in the separate case-study notes; ujamaa and aid in the economic notes.)

Key concepts (each defined as used here; also in keyTerms)
- [ ] K1 Colonial rule: control of a territory by an external power, governed for the benefit of the imperial state.
- [ ] K2 Tribalism: the colonial officials' term for ethnic loyalty; colonial policy often defined, fixed and exploited ethnic identities.
- [ ] K3 (British) Commonwealth: association of Britain and former colonies, many joining on independence.
- [ ] K4 Indirect rule (Lugard, Nigeria).
- [ ] K5 Federation (Nigeria's regions).
- [ ] K6 Secession (Katanga 1960, Biafra 1967).
- [ ] K7 Force Publique; K8 ONUC; K9 Estado Novo; K10 MPLA; K11 Carnation Revolution; K12 Zairianisation (each a keyTerm).

Nigeria
- [ ] N1 Amalgamation of Northern and Southern Nigeria in 1914 under Lugard; indirect rule through emirs in the north.
- [ ] N2 The three main groups (Hausa-Fulani north, Yoruba west, Igbo east) and religion (north mainly Muslim, south large Christian populations).
- [ ] N3 The 1954 federal constitution: three regions plus Lagos as federal territory; regional parliaments and revenue; regional parties on ethnic lines.
- [ ] N4 The 1959 federal elections: Northern People's Congress largest, coalition with the NCNC; Balewa prime minister.
- [ ] N5 Independence 1 October 1960: Commonwealth member, Balewa prime minister, Queen head of state, Azikiwe governor-general (from November 1960); republic 1 October 1963 with Azikiwe president; Mid-West region created 1963.
- [ ] N6 Oil: Oloibiri (Shell-BP, 1956), exports from 1958; oil lay in the Eastern Region.
- [ ] N7 Regional tension before 1966 (the Western Region crisis of the early 1960s, disputed elections), then the coup of 15 January 1966 (Nzeogwu; killing of the prime minister and regional leaders; seen as Igbo-dominated).
- [ ] N8 Ironsi's rule and the July 1966 counter-coup bringing Gowon to power.
- [ ] N9 The anti-Igbo massacres in the north in 1966 and the flight of Igbo to the east.
- [ ] N10 Aburi (January 1967), Gowon's twelve states (27 May 1967), Biafra's declaration (30 May 1967, Ojukwu), the blockade.
- [ ] N11 The Biafran War, July 1967 to 15 January 1970: famine, the death-toll range, the few states recognising Biafra, foreign backers of each side.
- [ ] N12 End of the war: Gowon's reconciliation policy and "no victor, no vanquished"; international sympathy, relief flights of Joint Church Aid including Irish missionaries.

The Belgian Congo
- [ ] C1 The Congo Free State under Leopold II from 1885; rubber abuses exposed by Casement and Morel; Belgian state takeover in 1908.
- [ ] C2 Belgian rule: paternalistic, little political role for Congolese, wealth in minerals, Union Minière in Katanga.
- [ ] C3 Unpreparedness: very few Congolese graduates; no Congolese officers in the Force Publique.
- [ ] C4 The road to independence: parties of the late 1950s (MNC under Lumumba), Léopoldville riots (January 1959), Baudouin's promise, the Brussels Round Table (January–February 1960) fixing 30 June 1960; the 1960 elections.
- [ ] C5 Independence on 30 June 1960: Kasa-Vubu head of state, Lumumba prime minister; the Baudouin and Lumumba speeches.
- [ ] C6 The Force Publique mutiny (5–6 July 1960), attacks on Europeans, Belgian troops return.
- [ ] C7 The secessions: Katanga (11 July 1960, Belgian backing), South Kasai (August 1960).
- [ ] C8 ONUC: authorised July 1960, about 20,000 troops at its height.
- [ ] C9 The dismissals of 5 September 1960; Mobutu's takeover on 14 September; Lumumba under UN guard.
- [ ] C10 Lumumba's flight, capture and murder in Katanga on 17 January 1961 with Belgian officers present; Belgium's later acceptance of moral responsibility.
- [ ] C11 Mobutu's coup of 24 November 1965; Zaire (1971); authenticité; Zairianisation (1973) and the copper-price collapse; the MPR one-party state; Western backing and the 1977–78 Shaba invasions; the April 1990 announcement; power until 1997.

Tanganyika and Tanzania
- [ ] T1 Background: German East Africa, British mandate, UN trust territory.
- [ ] T2 Nyerere and TANU (1954); 1960 election; independence 9 December 1961; republic December 1962.
- [ ] T3 Zanzibar: independence December 1963, revolution January 1964, Afro-Shirazi Party, Karume.
- [ ] T4 The union of 26 April 1964; Tanzania named October 1964; Zanzibar keeps its own government.
- [ ] T5 National unity: more than a hundred ethnic groups, Swahili as national language, no ethnic parties.
- [ ] T6 The Tanganyika Rifles mutiny (January 1964) and Royal Marines.
- [ ] T7 The break with Britain over Rhodesia (December 1965) while staying in the Commonwealth.

Angola
- [ ] G1 Portuguese rule: Salazar's Estado Novo, "overseas province", refusal to decolonise.
- [ ] G2 The war from 1961: Luanda attacks (4 February) and the northern rising (15 March); the war until 1974.
- [ ] G3 The three movements: MPLA (Neto, 1956), FNLA (Roberto), UNITA (Savimbi, 1966), with their regional and ethnic bases.
- [ ] G4 The Carnation Revolution (25 April 1974) and the decision to grant independence.
- [ ] G5 The Alvor Agreement (January 1975) and its collapse.
- [ ] G6 Independence on 11 November 1975 under the MPLA; civil war to 2002 with Cuban troops and Soviet aid for the MPLA and South African and American support for its opponents.
- [ ] G7 The settlers' departure (1975) and the resulting shortage of skills; oil (Cabinda) and diamonds as revenue.

Relations with former colonial powers (post-independence)
- [ ] R1 Britain and Nigeria: Commonwealth, Queen as head of state until 1963, Shell-BP, British arms to the federal side.
- [ ] R2 Belgium and the Congo: Union Minière interests, nationalisation 1966–67, settlement 1969, Belgian backing for Katanga and later support for Mobutu.
- [ ] R3 Portugal and Angola: late decolonisation, war, few trained administrators.
- [ ] R4 Tanzania and Britain: Commonwealth and aid, non-alignment, the Rhodesia break.
- [ ] R5 France: co-operation accords, the franc zone, military agreements ("Françafrique").

Tribalism and ethnic politics
- [ ] B1 Colonial rulers fixed ethnic identities (indirect rule, census categories, languages).
- [ ] B2 Nigeria: three regional, ethnic parties; officer corps divided on regional lines.
- [ ] B3 Congo: leaders with regional and ethnic bases (Lumumba east, Kasa-Vubu Kongo west, Lunda of Katanga).
- [ ] B4 Katanga's claim to a separate identity, rejected by the central government.
- [ ] B5 Tanzania's Swahili and nation-building as a contrast.

Assessment (paired judgement points, final block)
- [ ] S1 Opportunity: Africans controlled their own governments; Tanzania achieved unity without civil war.
- [ ] S2 Problem: the Congo's unpreparedness; mutiny and secession within weeks.
- [ ] S3 Opportunity: Nigeria's oil revenue after 1973 for reconstruction.
- [ ] S4 Problem: Nigerian federalism failing to hold ethnic groups together (1966, Biafra).
- [ ] S5 Problem: Angola's war because of different bases and foreign backers.
- [ ] S6 Contemporary vs later views: leaders saw an end to domination, colonial powers a new relationship of trade and aid; historians stress colonial borders, ethnic policy and the Cold War as constraints, but also African agency.
- [ ] S7 Overall judgement: outcomes differed by country (Tanzania stable, Nigeria and Angola war, Congo crisis), so a balanced answer compares them.

## 3. Traps

Do not use (wrong or doubtful facts in the current notes):
- Do not use: "Belgium's parliament concluded in 2002 that it bore a moral responsibility", because the parliamentary inquiry reported in 2001 and the government apologised in 2002. Say Belgium later accepted moral responsibility.
- Do not use: "copper, cobalt, tin and diamonds, much of it extracted in Katanga by the Union Minière", because the Union Minière mined copper and cobalt; diamonds came mainly from Kasai and were run by other companies. Say the wealth lay in minerals including copper, cobalt and diamonds, and that Union Minière ran Katanga's mines.
- Do not use: "the largest UN operation of its time", because it is doubtful. Say about 20,000 troops at its height.
- Do not use: any single Biafran death toll; the notes' range wording is acceptable only as "several hundred thousand to over a million".
- Do not use: Belgians "commanding all the ranks above sergeant" as a precise claim; say no Congolese officers.
- Do not use: "the Katangan exile invasions defeated by Western military help" without care; the 1977 and 1978 Shaba invasions were by Katangan exiles from Angola, repelled with Western help (name no other details).

## 4. What exams ask

- Higher 100-mark essay on problems of withdrawal: three states named (Britain/Nigeria, Belgium/Congo, Portugal/Angola) with a lack of trained leaders, ethnic and regional division, secession or civil war, and Cold War involvement. Marks go to a developed example per state with dates, so cover all three.
- Higher essay on opportunities and problems of independence: a paired structure (African control and national unity against instability, war and dependence), with at least one example each side, such as Tanzania's unity against Biafra or Angola.
- Higher essay on whether decolonisation was positive for the former colonies: balance gains (self-government, some social progress) against ethnic conflict and Cold War proxy wars; the judgement must be stated and sustained.
- Higher essay on whether the colonial powers helped or hindered: each power's actions after independence (British aid and arms, Belgian interests and intervention, Portuguese late exit, French networks); a verdict at the start earns marks.
- Cause-and-consequence questions: why Nigeria's federation broke down (ethnic parties, the 1966 coups, massacres, oil in the east) and why Biafra seceded; why the Congo's independence was chaotic (unpreparedness, mutiny, secession, Cold War); why Angola's war became a civil war.
- Ordinary Level short answers: who led a movement or country (Nyerere, Lumumba, Neto), when a country became independent, what a term means (tribalism, secession, Commonwealth); one correct, dated fact per mark.
- Ordinary Level 30 or 40-mark paragraphs: a leader (Nyerere, Lumumba, Gowon) in career order with dates; each correct, relevant, dated statement earns marks.
- Source questions (as in past Congo documents): students must place a document in context, using the unpreparedness, mutiny and Katanga points here, and spot bias (a document from one side or a colonial viewpoint).
- Do not describe Nigeria as the same as Biafra: examiners reward noting that the Biafran War was a secession and a famine crisis, with international recognition of Biafra limited to a few states.
