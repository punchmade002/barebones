## 1. Must cover (one bullet or block for every item; tick each off)

Background (assumed)
- [ ] A1 (assumed) Irish farming in the 1870s–80s: small tenant farms, the agricultural depression of 1877–79 and the land question, with land reform moving towards tenant ownership (see the notes on land agitation and reform).
- [ ] A2 (assumed) Irish farmers faced new competition from imports; Denmark had responded by organising farmer-owned co-operative dairies.
- [ ] A3 (assumed) Gombeen men: local shopkeepers and moneylenders who sold goods and lent money on credit at high prices and interest.
- [ ] A4 (assumed) The Industrial Revolution reached north-east Ulster (linen, shipbuilding) far more than the rest of Ireland; Belfast's trade was tied to Britain and the empire.
- [ ] A5 (assumed) Unionism and the Home Rule crisis: Belfast's Protestant majority opposed Home Rule (see the notes on unionism and the Ulster Question).

The co-operative movement
- [ ] C1 Horace Plunkett: background (Anglo-Irish landowner, son of Lord Dunsany; ranching in Wyoming; Unionist MP for South Dublin 1892–1900; worked with nationalists).
- [ ] C2 Problems of Irish farmers (poor marketing, dependence on gombeen men, competition from Danish butter) and the Danish co-operative model.
- [ ] C3 The first co-operative creamery, usually dated 1889 at Dromcollogher, Co. Limerick; how a creamery worked (pooled milk, mechanical separator, payment by quality) and its spread, strongest in Munster.
- [ ] C4 Helpers: Fr Tom Finlay SJ and George Russell (AE); the Irish Homestead.
- [ ] C5 The Irish Agricultural Organisation Society (1894): purpose, motto, methods.
- [ ] C6 Credit societies (Raiffeisen model) and other societies (supplies, eggs and poultry); the Irish Agricultural Wholesale Society (1897).
- [ ] C7 Scale by 1914 and regional pattern (strong in Munster, weak in Ulster and the west).
- [ ] C8 The Recess Committee (1895) and its 1896 report.
- [ ] C9 The Department of Agriculture and Technical Instruction (1899): functions, Plunkett as vice-president, the Chief Secretary as president; Plunkett's removal in 1907 and T.W. Russell.
- [ ] C10 Suspicion: nationalists (Plunkett as a unionist; the Irish Party), unionists/Ulster farmers, the Catholic clergy and Ireland in the New Century (1904), private creamery owners and shopkeepers.
- [ ] C11 Results: butter quality, incomes, self-help, local leaders; limits (poor western farmers helped more by the Congested Districts Board).

Industrial Belfast
- [ ] B1 Population growth: about 20,000 around 1800; passed Dublin city by the 1891 census (about 255,000); 1901 and 1911 figures.
- [ ] B2 Linen: Linenopolis, mills, the Civil War cotton shortage, power looms, a mainly female workforce.
- [ ] B3 The Belfast Ropeworks (1873).
- [ ] B4 Tobacco (Gallaher), engineering (Sirocco Works), distilling (Dunville).
- [ ] B5 Catholic migration into west Belfast (Falls Road); about a quarter of the city Catholic by 1911.

The shipyards
- [ ] S1 Edward Harland and Gustav Wolff: Queen's Island (1858), partnership (1861).
- [ ] S2 William Pirrie: Quebec-born, apprentice, partner 1874, chairman 1895, Lord Pirrie 1906.
- [ ] S3 The White Star Line and the Olympic-class liners (Olympic, Titanic, Britannic).
- [ ] S4 Titanic: launch (31 May 1911), maiden voyage and loss (15 April 1912), about 1,500 lives.
- [ ] S5 The Arrol Gantry (1908–09).
- [ ] S6 Workman Clark (1880): founders and role.
- [ ] S7 Scale of employment (Harland and Wolff about 15,000 by 1912; tens of thousands in both yards together).

Working conditions, sectarianism and unionism
- [ ] W1 Mill conditions (hours, heat, damp, flax dust, low wages; women and girls; half-timers).
- [ ] W2 Shipyard conditions (dangerous; casual labourers laid off between contracts).
- [ ] W3 Skilled jobs mainly Protestant (family and Orange Order links); Catholics mostly labourers.
- [ ] W4 Sectarian riots of 1857, 1864 and 1886 (1886 includes attacks on Catholic workers at the shipyards).
- [ ] W5 The July 1912 expulsions from the yards.
- [ ] W6 Larkin's 1907 strike: brief cross-religious unity.
- [ ] W7 Links to unionism: fear of tariffs and Catholic dominance; economic dependence on Britain and the empire.

Belfast and Dublin compared
- [ ] D1 Economies: export manufacturing (Belfast) vs administration, trade, brewing and distilling (Dublin); Guinness and Jacob's.
- [ ] D2 Casual labour and poverty in Dublin vs higher skilled wages in Belfast; poverty and disease persisted in both.
- [ ] D3 Politics: unionist and Protestant Belfast vs nationalist Dublin with a strong labour movement; segregated districts (Falls, Shankill).

Assessment (paired judgement points, final block)
- [ ] J1 Achievement: the co-operatives raised quality and incomes and encouraged self-help; Belfast became an industrial city of international importance.
- [ ] J2 Limit: co-operation hardly reached the poor west or Ulster, and drew political suspicion; Belfast's prosperity rested on cheap female labour, harsh conditions and sectarian divisions.
- [ ] J3 Contemporary vs later views: nationalists suspected Plunkett; unionists pointed to Belfast as proof of the Union's benefits; historians judge co-operation a success of self-help but less decisive than land reform.
