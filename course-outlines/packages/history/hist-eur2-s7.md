## 1. Must cover (one bullet or block for every item; tick each off)

Background (assumed)
- [ ] A1 (assumed) The first industrial revolution (coal, iron, textiles, steam power) began in Britain, which was still the leading industrial power in 1871.
- [ ] A2 (assumed) The Reich of 1871 in one bullet: a federation of states dominated by Prussia; the Reichstag elected by universal male suffrage, but the Chancellor answerable to the Kaiser, not the Reichstag; Prussia kept its own three-class voting system. Detail belongs to the Second Reich notes.
- [ ] A3 (assumed) The Zollverein (customs union) had removed most customs barriers between the German states (Austria excluded) long before 1871.
- [ ] A4 (assumed) Socialism and Marxism in one bullet: class struggle between owners and workers; the aim of common ownership of industry; the SPD as Germany's Marxist party.
- [ ] A5 (assumed) The Treaty of Frankfurt (1871): France ceded Alsace and part of Lorraine and paid an indemnity.

Why Germany industrialised fast after 1871
- [ ] C1 A growing population: labour and a large home market.
- [ ] C2 Coal and iron: the Ruhr coalfield as the heart of heavy industry, coal also in the Saar and Silesia, iron ore in annexed Lorraine.
- [ ] C3 Unification created a single market: one currency (the mark), a central bank (the Reichsbank), common commercial law, building on the Zollverein.
- [ ] C4 The French indemnity and the boom of the early 1870s (Gründerzeit); the crash of 1873 and the long depression that followed, which pushed firms towards cartels and the state towards tariffs.
- [ ] C5 Railways: rapid growth of the network after 1871; Prussian state ownership of most lines from 1879; railways linked the Ruhr to cities and ports and were themselves a huge market for coal and steel.
- [ ] C6 Banks: "universal banks" that lent long-term to industry and sat on company boards.
- [ ] C7 Education and research: technical universities (Technische Hochschulen) and technical schools trained engineers and chemists; firms set up research laboratories; close links between science and industry.
- [ ] C8 Cartels and big firms: what a cartel is; the coal syndicate and the steel association; several hundred cartels by the early 1900s; large combined firms (mines, ironworks and engineering under one owner).
- [ ] C9 State support: the protective tariff of 1879; the army, navy and state railways as large customers.
- [ ] C10 The advantage of a late start: German firms built new plant with the latest technology instead of patching up older works.

The scale of growth
- [ ] G1 Coal: output rose about six-fold 1871–1913, though Britain still mined more coal in 1913.
- [ ] G2 Steel: by 1913 Germany produced more than twice as much as Britain.
- [ ] G3 Germany the world's second industrial power after the USA by 1914; its share of world manufacturing roughly equal to or slightly above Britain's by 1913 (an estimate).
- [ ] G4 Trade and shipping: exports of machinery, chemicals and electrical goods; the Hamburg-America Line and North German Lloyd; Germany's merchant fleet second only to Britain's; British alarm ("Made in Germany").
- [ ] G5 Emigration: high in the 1880s, it fell steeply from the 1890s as industry created jobs at home.

The second industrial revolution
- [ ] S1 Definition (also in keyTerms): new industries based on steel, chemicals and electricity from about the 1880s, growing faster than coal and textiles, with Germany often the leader.
- [ ] S2 Steel: the basic (Thomas–Gilchrist) process made the phosphoric iron ore of Lorraine usable for steel; steel output soared in the Ruhr.
- [ ] S3 Chemicals: BASF, Bayer and Hoechst (all founded in the 1860s); Germany made most of the world's synthetic dyes; pharmaceuticals (Bayer's aspirin, 1899); Haber's ammonia synthesis scaled up by Bosch at BASF (fertilisers, later explosives).
- [ ] S4 Electricals: Werner von Siemens (Siemens & Halske, 1847; the dynamo; the electric railway and tram); Emil Rathenau and AEG; electric lighting, trams and power stations in cities from the 1880s–1890s; Siemens and AEG dominant by 1914.
- [ ] S5 Engines and the motor car: one pointer only (Benz, Daimler, Diesel; see the notes on the motor car).

Key personality: the Krupp family (one or two blocks covering every point)
- [ ] K1 Origins: Friedrich Krupp's cast steel works at Essen (1811); his son Alfred (1812–87) took over at 14 in 1826 when the firm was small and in debt.
- [ ] K2 Alfred's steel: the flawless cast steel ingot at the Great Exhibition, London (1851); seamless railway wheel tyres (the three interlocking rings became the Krupp trademark); early adoption of the Bessemer process; steel for railways.
- [ ] K3 Alfred's guns: breech-loading cast steel cannon, adopted by Prussia in the 1860s and effective in the Franco-Prussian War (1870–71); sales to many foreign governments; "the Cannon King".
- [ ] K4 Alfred's paternalism: workers' housing estates, sickness and pension funds, schools, shops, a hospital; in return he demanded loyalty and kept out unions and socialists (his 1877 appeal to workers to stay out of politics); the Villa Hügel (1873); about 20,000 workers by his death in 1887.
- [ ] K5 Friedrich Alfred Krupp (1854–1902), head 1887–1902: expansion into armour plate (the Gruson works, Magdeburg, 1893) and shipbuilding (the Germania yard at Kiel); a close friend of Wilhelm II; the fleet as a major customer (pointer to the naval notes for the Navy League).
- [ ] K6 Friedrich Alfred's death (November 1902): the SPD newspaper Vorwärts published allegations about his private life on Capri; he died a week later (officially a stroke; suicide suspected); Wilhelm II attended the funeral and attacked the SPD.
- [ ] K7 Bertha Krupp (1886–1957) inherited at 16; the firm became a joint-stock company, Fried. Krupp AG (1903), with Bertha holding almost all the shares.
- [ ] K8 Gustav von Bohlen und Halbach (1870–1950), a diplomat, married Bertha (1906); the Kaiser let him take the name Krupp von Bohlen und Halbach; he ran the firm, chairing its board from 1909.
- [ ] K9 Krupp in the First World War: the 42 cm "Big Bertha" howitzer against the Belgian forts (Liège, Namur, August 1914); guns, shells, armour and U-boats (at Kiel); a workforce of well over 100,000, many of them women.
- [ ] K10 The Kornwalzer scandal (1913): Karl Liebknecht revealed in the Reichstag that Krupp's Berlin agent had bribed War Ministry officials for secret information.
- [ ] K11 Shaped events vs shaped by them; participant: the Krupps armed Prussia and the Reich and profited from unification, the naval race and war; they also depended on state orders and on the Kaiser's favour. Contemporaries: a national symbol of German industrial and military strength to nationalists and the Kaiser; to socialists, exploiters who profited from armaments and controlled their workers.
- [ ] K12 Historians: the leading example of the link between heavy industry, the army and the state before 1914 (sometimes called a military–industrial complex), and the model of industrial paternalism; the firm's later role under the Nazis is outside this period (one clause at most).

Urbanisation and the working class
- [ ] U1 Internal migration: millions moved from the countryside, especially from the agrarian east (the "flight from the land"), to Berlin and the Ruhr; Polish-speaking migrants in the Ruhr.
- [ ] U2 Urban share: about a third of Germans in towns in 1871, about 60 per cent by 1910.
- [ ] U3 City growth: Berlin from under a million (1871) to about two million (1910); Essen and other Ruhr towns many times larger.
- [ ] U4 Living conditions: overcrowded tenements (Mietskasernen) in Berlin, poor sanitation and disease; public health and city services improved from the 1890s.
- [ ] U5 Working conditions: long hours, strict factory discipline, insecurity; women and children at work; the Labour Protection Law of 1891 (Sunday rest, limits on women's and children's work); real wages rose slowly from the 1890s.
- [ ] U6 Strikes: the great Ruhr miners' strike of 1889 (the Kaiser received a miners' delegation) and a larger one in 1905.
- [ ] U7 Divisions within the working class: Catholic workers often loyal to the Centre Party and Christian unions; Polish workers faced Germanisation pressure; skilled vs unskilled.

The SPD and the trade unions
- [ ] P1 Founding: the Gotha congress (1875) united the Lassalleans (General German Workers' Association, 1863) and the Eisenach Marxists of Bebel and Wilhelm Liebknecht (1869); the name SPD from 1890.
- [ ] P2 The Anti-Socialist Law (1878–90): organisations and press banned, but socialists could still stand for the Reichstag, and the vote rose (detail in the Second Reich notes).
- [ ] P3 The Erfurt Programme (1891): a Marxist analysis of capitalism plus immediate demands (democratic suffrage, the eight-hour day, social reforms).
- [ ] P4 Revisionism: Bernstein argued for gradual reform; the party condemned it (Dresden congress, 1903) but in practice acted as a reformist party (pointer: Luxemburg's attack belongs to the Second International notes).
- [ ] P5 Electoral growth: about 1.4 million votes in 1890; about 4.25 million votes and 110 seats in 1912, the largest party in the Reichstag.
- [ ] P6 Limits on SPD power: Chancellor not answerable to the Reichstag; the Bundesrat; constituencies unchanged since 1871 favouring the countryside; the Prussian three-class franchise; no SPD member in government before 1918.
- [ ] P7 Trade unions: the socialist Free Trade Unions about 2.5 million members by 1913, far bigger than the Catholic (Christian) and liberal unions; employers replied with associations, lockouts and blacklists, and the police and courts were used against strikes.
- [ ] P8 The socialist subculture: party newspapers, clubs, choirs, sports and education societies; critics called the SPD a "state within a state" and its members were treated as outsiders (Reichsfeinde).

Social tensions: Junkers, industrialists and workers
- [ ] T1 The Junkers: landowning nobility east of the Elbe who dominated the army officer corps, the Prussian civil service and the Prussian parliament (three-class voting).
- [ ] T2 The agricultural crisis: cheap American and Russian grain from the late 1870s; the tariff of 1879 and the "iron and rye" alliance of heavy industry and the Junkers.
- [ ] T3 Caprivi's trade treaties (1891–94) lowered grain duties; the Agrarian League (1893) campaigned against them; Bülow's tariff (1902) raised grain duties again.
- [ ] T4 Who lost from tariffs: dearer bread angered workers and consumers; exporting and finished-goods industries and the left opposed high duties ("bread usury" was an SPD theme).
- [ ] T5 Sammlungspolitik (from 1897): rallying landowners and industrialists behind the monarchy, the fleet and Weltpolitik against the SPD (one bullet; detail in the naval policy notes).
- [ ] T6 Industrialists vs Junkers: industrialists resented aristocratic privilege but many copied the nobility (titles, estates, reserve-officer commissions); Wehler's "feudalisation of the bourgeoisie" thesis vs Blackbourn and Eley's reply.
- [ ] T7 The middle classes and fear of socialism: support for nationalist pressure groups and the monarchy (pointer only).
- [ ] T8 Other tensions in the Reich, pointer only for essays on tensions in Germany: the Kulturkampf against Catholics (see the notes on church–state tensions); Poles and the Alsace-Lorraine population.

The state's response: welfare and repression
- [ ] W1 Bismarck's social insurance: sickness (1883), accident (1884), old age and invalidity (1889); aim of winning workers from the SPD; "carrot and stick" (one bullet; detail in the Second Reich notes).
- [ ] W2 Wilhelm II's "new course" (1890): the Anti-Socialist Law allowed to lapse; the Kaiser's labour decrees and the international labour conference in Berlin (1890); the Labour Protection Law (1891).
- [ ] W3 The turn back to repression: the Subversion Bill (1894–95) and the Penitentiary Bill (1899) against strikers both failed in the Reichstag; social insurance was consolidated in the Imperial Insurance Code (1911).
- [ ] W4 Company welfare: Krupp the best-known example, Siemens and others followed; employers wanted loyalty and no strikes.
- [ ] W5 Limits: welfare did not stop the SPD vote rising; no political reform followed economic change; the Prussian three-class franchise survived to 1918.

Assessment: industrial success and social cost (final block, paired points)
- [ ] X1 Achievement: Germany became continental Europe's leading industrial economy and led in steel, chemicals and electricals.
- [ ] X2 Limit: growth was uneven: agriculture and the east lagged; boom and slump (1873, the long depression).
- [ ] X3 Achievement: the first national social insurance system; rising real wages and falling emigration from the 1890s.
- [ ] X4 Limit: welfare was paternalist and paired with repression; the political system stayed closed to the largest party; class, religious and regional tensions were unresolved in 1914.
- [ ] X5 Impact abroad: industrial power fed militarism, the arms industry and the naval race, and trade rivalry with Britain.
- [ ] X6 Contemporary vs later views: nationalists saw proof of German greatness, socialists saw exploitation; historians debate a modern economy with an unreformed political system (Wehler) vs a normal modern society (Blackbourn and Eley).

## 3. Traps

Do not use (wrong or doubtful facts in the current notes):
- Do not use: socialists attacked the Krupps as "merchants of death", because that phrase dates from the 1930s arms-trade debates. Say socialists attacked the firm for profiting from armaments.
- Do not use: railways were "built mainly in the 1840s and 1850s", because the network roughly tripled after 1870; and do not say the state "kept rates low for industry", because it is doubtful. Use C5 as given.

- Do not use: the Reichstag "debated Krupp's profits and its bribery of officials in 1913–14" without naming the case; use K10 (1913, Liebknecht, the Kornwalzer affair).

## 4. What exams ask

- Ordinary 30- or 40-mark paragraph on the Krupp family (the commonest question on this subsection, asked many times): marks go to accurate, dated statements across the generations: the 1811 founding, Alfred taking over young, the 1851 ingot, railway tyres, breech-loading guns and Prussia's wars, the Cannon King, workers' housing and welfare as paternalism, Friedrich Alfred and armour/naval work, Bertha and Gustav, Big Bertha in 1914. Students who cover only Alfred lose marks; they need the family across the period.
- Higher 100-mark essay on the importance or impact of industrialisation in Germany 1871–1914: marks go to a clear judgement in the first sentence, then causes (resources, unification, railways, banks, education, cartels, tariffs) linked to results (scale of growth, new industries, urbanisation, working class, SPD, tensions, welfare). The best answers explain why each cause mattered rather than listing them, and show impact on society, politics and foreign policy, not just the economy.
- Higher essay on industry and its impact in Europe generally (often combining Germany with the motor car, science and technology, or Russia): students need German examples ready (steel, Siemens/AEG, chemical firms, Krupp) and the social impact (cities, class, socialism, welfare).
- Higher essay on what a study of Krupp, the motor car and science taught you: Krupp's role is the link between technology, industry, national prestige and war; give the Krupp guns and Big Bertha as evidence.
- Higher essay on tensions in Germany 1871–1914: social and economic tensions (Junkers vs industrialists vs workers, tariffs, the SPD) can be combined with the Kulturkampf or naval policy; examiners reward explaining who was in conflict with whom and why, and how the state responded (welfare, repression, Sammlungspolitik).
- Cause and result points that earn marks: why cartels spread (the 1873 crash and long depression); why the SPD grew despite repression and welfare (urban working class, unions, the subculture, repression creating martyrs); why the state introduced welfare (to weaken the SPD); why tariffs caused tension (dearer food for workers, profits for Junkers).
- Judgement points examiners reward, as paired points: achievement (economic power, social insurance first in the world, rising wages) vs limit (unresolved political exclusion of workers, paternalism, unevenness, militarism); Krupp as admired national symbol vs arms profiteer and controller of workers; Wehler's critical view vs Blackbourn and Eley, naming at least one historian with their view.
- Document questions (Ordinary DBQ) linked to the naval case study may mention developing the armour and armaments industries: students should connect this to Krupp as the main supplier.
