## 1. Must cover (one bullet or block for every item; tick each off)

Background (assumed knowledge)
- [ ] X1 (assumed) The German Confederation of 1815: a loose league of sovereign states under Austrian presidency, with a Diet at Frankfurt; Austria and Prussia the two great powers in it. One or two bullets only.
- [ ] X2 (assumed) The failure of 1848–49: the Frankfurt Parliament's Kleindeutsch crown refused by Frederick William IV; the lesson that unity would need Prussian power. One bullet only.
- [ ] X3 (assumed) The Punctation of Olmütz (1850) and the restored Confederation (1851): Prussia humiliated by Austria; Bismarck Prussia's delegate at the Diet from 1851. One bullet only.
- [ ] X4 (assumed) The Kleindeutsch (without Austria, under Prussia) vs Grossdeutsch (with Austria) choice, which 1866 settled. One clause.

Prussia's strength and Austria's weakness, 1850s–1863
- [ ] P1 The Zollverein: by the 1850s it included most German states but not Austria; a large common market under Prussian leadership, with shared tariff revenue.
- [ ] P2 Coal and iron of the Ruhr and Silesia; railways linking Prussia's industrial regions to Berlin and the Rhineland.
- [ ] P3 Krupp's works at Essen, expanding from the 1850s, making guns and railway materials: a symbol of Prussian industrial power.
- [ ] P4 Prussia's 1862 commercial treaty with France: a low-tariff course the Zollverein states followed and protectionist Austria could not match.
- [ ] P5 Austria's weakness: debt and weak finances; centralised, conservative government.
- [ ] P6 Austria's defeat in Italy, 1859 (Magenta, Solferino; Lombardy lost) exposed its military weakness. One bullet; the Italian side belongs to the Italy notes.
- [ ] P7 Austria had lost Russia's friendship by its stance in the Crimean War (1853–56).
- [ ] P8 The Frankfurt Princes' Congress (August 1863): Austria's plan to reform the Confederation failed when Prussia, on Bismarck's advice, refused to attend.

Army reform and the constitutional crisis, 1858–1862
- [ ] A1 William regent from 1858, king from 1861.
- [ ] A2 Roon's army reform: three-year service kept, regular army enlarged, citizen Landwehr reduced.
- [ ] A3 Albrecht von Roon (War Minister from 1859) and Helmuth von Moltke (chief of the general staff from 1857) as the men behind the reforms.
- [ ] A4 The liberal majority in the Landtag (the Progressive Party, founded 1861) refused the army budget; deadlock; the king considered abdicating.
- [ ] A5 Bismarck appointed Minister-President and Foreign Minister, September 1862, to break the deadlock.
- [ ] A6 The "blood and iron" speech to the Budget Committee, 30 September 1862.
- [ ] A7 The gap theory: taxes collected and the army reform carried out without an approved budget, 1862–66.

The Danish War, 1864
- [ ] D1 Schleswig and Holstein: ruled by the Danish king; Holstein German and in the Confederation; Schleswig mixed; Germans wanted links with Germany.
- [ ] D2 Christian IX's accession and the November Constitution (1863) binding Schleswig to Denmark, against the agreements confirmed by the London Protocol (1852).
- [ ] D3 Joint Austro-Prussian invasion (February 1864); Düppel and Als; Treaty of Vienna (October 1864): Schleswig, Holstein and Lauenburg handed to Prussia and Austria jointly.
- [ ] D4 The Convention of Gastein (August 1865): Austria administers Holstein, Prussia Schleswig; a ready-made source of dispute.
- [ ] D5 Why Bismarck acted with Austria: to keep the issue a matter for the two powers, not the Diet or the liberals, and to keep a later quarrel available.

The Austro-Prussian War and its results, 1866–1867
- [ ] W1 Diplomatic isolation of Austria: Russian goodwill after the Alvensleben Convention (1863, against the Polish rising); Napoleon III's neutrality after Biarritz (1865); the alliance with Italy (April 1866), Venetia promised.
- [ ] W2 The outbreak: Prussia occupies Holstein (June 1866); most German states side with Austria.
- [ ] W3 Why Prussia won: the breech-loading needle-gun, Moltke's planning, rapid railway mobilisation.
- [ ] W4 Königgrätz (Sadowa), 3 July 1866, the decisive battle; the war's short length (the Seven Weeks' War).
- [ ] W5 Moderation: Bismarck persuades William I to stop at the Nikolsburg preliminaries (July 1866): no march on Vienna, no Austrian territory taken, so Austria could be a later friend.
- [ ] W6 The Treaty of Prague (August 1866): Confederation dissolved, Austria excluded from German affairs, Venetia to Italy.
- [ ] W7 Prussian annexations: Hanover, Hesse-Kassel, Nassau, Frankfurt, Schleswig-Holstein; secret military alliances with the southern states.
- [ ] W8 The Indemnity Bill (1866): retrospective approval of rule without a budget; the liberals split; the National Liberal Party (1867) backs Bismarck for the sake of unity.

The North German Confederation, 1867
- [ ] N1 Formed 1867 by the states north of the River Main under Prussia.
- [ ] N2 Its constitution: King of Prussia as president with control of foreign policy and the armed forces; Bismarck Chancellor.
- [ ] N3 Reichstag elected by universal male suffrage; Bundesrat of state governments, dominated by Prussia.
- [ ] N4 The Customs Parliament (Zollparlament), 1868, with southern deputies, many of them opponents of Prussia: the south was not yet won.

The Franco-Prussian War, 1870–71
- [ ] F1 Background of French resentment: "revenge for Sadowa", and the Luxembourg crisis (1867) in which Napoleon III's attempt to acquire Luxembourg was blocked.
- [ ] F2 The Hohenzollern candidature: Prince Leopold of Hohenzollern-Sigmaringen offered the Spanish throne (1870); French fear of encirclement; withdrawn 12 July 1870.
- [ ] F3 France's demand for a guarantee; William I at Bad Ems refuses (13 July 1870); Bismarck edits the report and publishes a shortened version that reads as an insult.
- [ ] F4 France declares war (19 July 1870) and appears the aggressor; the southern states join Prussia under their treaties; France has no ally.
- [ ] F5 The campaign: Wörth and Spicheren (August 1870); Sedan (battle 1 September, surrender 2 September 1870), Napoleon III captured.
- [ ] F6 The fall of the Second Empire (4 September 1870); Gambetta and the Government of National Defence continue the war. One bullet; the French side belongs to the France notes.
- [ ] F7 Metz surrenders (October 1870); siege of Paris (September 1870–January 1871); armistice 28 January 1871.
- [ ] F8 Why Prussia won: railways, superior artillery, Moltke's planning, the southern states' troops, France's isolation.

The German Empire, 1871
- [ ] E1 Treaties with the southern states (November 1870); the letter from Ludwig II of Bavaria, drafted with Bismarck's help, inviting William to take the imperial title.
- [ ] E2 Proclamation of William I as German Emperor, Hall of Mirrors, Versailles, 18 January 1871.
- [ ] E3 Treaty of Frankfurt (May 1871): Alsace (except Belfort) and part of Lorraine including Metz to Germany; indemnity of five billion francs.
- [ ] E4 The 1871 constitution, based on that of the North German Confederation: federal state, emperor who was also King of Prussia, Reichstag, Bundesrat.
- [ ] E5 Limits: the Chancellor answered to the emperor, not the Reichstag; Prussia, with its own conservative franchise, dominated.

Key personality: Otto von Bismarck (to 1871)
- [ ] B1 Who he was: born 1815, a Junker from the Prussian province of Saxony; ultra-conservative in the United Diet (1847); in the Prussian Landtag from 1849.
- [ ] B2 Career before 1862: Prussian delegate at the Diet in Frankfurt (1851–59); ambassador at St Petersburg and Paris.
- [ ] B3 What he did 1862–71, with dates (the stages above), as a participant directing each one.
- [ ] B4 Offices: Minister-President 1862; Chancellor of the North German Confederation 1867; Imperial Chancellor 1871 (held until 1890: one clause only).
- [ ] B5 How contemporaries saw him: conservatives admired his success; many liberals saw an authoritarian who had broken the constitution, though most were won over by 1866–71.
- [ ] B6 How historians judge him: the Prussian school's master plan; A. J. P. Taylor's improvising opportunist; Lothar Gall's "white revolutionary".

Bismarck's methods and the plan-or-opportunism debate
- [ ] M1 Realpolitik: the term (Rochau, 1853) and its meaning.
- [ ] M2 Using liberal nationalism for Prussian ends while keeping the army and monarchy under royal control.
- [ ] M3 Isolating each enemy before war (W1, F4) and fighting short wars with limited aims.
- [ ] M4 Exploiting chances as they arose: the Danish succession, Gastein, the Hohenzollern candidature and Ems.
- [ ] M5 The debate: a long-term plan to unite Germany under Prussia, or flexible exploitation of opportunities towards a general aim of Prussian power. The usual balanced verdict: a clear aim, flexible methods.

Germany and Italy compared
- [ ] I1 Italy's dates: Kingdom of Italy 1861; Venetia 1866; Rome 1870.
- [ ] I2 Similarities: a strong kingdom (Piedmont, Prussia) leading; war plus diplomacy; Austria the common enemy; Italy gaining Venetia from Prussia's 1866 victory and Rome when the French garrison left in 1870.
- [ ] I3 Differences: Piedmont needed French armies, Prussia only foreign neutrality; Italy's unity partly from below (Garibaldi's volunteers, plebiscites), Germany's from above (army and princes); Germany emerged far stronger economically and militarily.
- [ ] I4 Unfinished business: Italy left Trentino and Trieste in Austria; Germany contained Poles, Danes and Alsatians and left out the Austrian Germans.

Assessment (end with this block, headed "Assessment: …")
- [ ] J1 Achievement: unity, the aim of 1848, achieved by 1871.
- [ ] J2 Limit: unity from above, by Prussian arms and the princes, not by a parliament.
- [ ] J3 Limit: Kleindeutsch, leaving Austria's Germans outside and including national minorities.
- [ ] J4 Limit: a Prussian-dominated semi-autocracy; a Reichstag elected by manhood suffrage but with no control over the Chancellor.
- [ ] J5 Contemporary vs later views: liberals' national triumph vs later critics' Prussian militarism and autocracy.

Key concepts and terms (keyTerms)
- [ ] T1 Nationalism, as used in German unification.
- [ ] T2 Autocracy, and the Prussian-German semi-autocracy.
- [ ] T3 Other terms: Realpolitik; blood and iron; gap theory; Zollverein; Landwehr; North German Confederation; Treaty of Prague; Convention of Gastein; Ems telegram; Hohenzollern candidature; Reichstag; Bundesrat; Indemnity Bill; Kleindeutsch.

## 3. Traps

Do not use (doubtful or wrong in the current notes):
- Do not use "the French army was defeated ... at Sedan on 2 September 1870": the battle was on 1 September and the surrender on 2 September. Use the dates in F5.
- Do not say Leopold was simply "a relative of the Prussian king" without qualification if it suggests a close one: he was from the Catholic Sigmaringen branch of the Hohenzollern family. Use the F2 wording.
- Do not say the London Protocol of 1852 "settled the succession to the duchies" on its own: it settled the succession to the Danish crown and confirmed Denmark's earlier promises about the duchies. Use the D2 wording.
- Do not say Königgrätz "ended the war" in a fact or date sense: it was decisive, but the war ended with the Nikolsburg preliminaries (July) and the Treaty of Prague (August 1866).
- Do not call the Ems telegram "his telegram to Bismarck" as if the king wrote it: it was a report sent on the king's instructions. Say "a report of the meeting telegraphed to Bismarck".
- Do not credit the phrase "white revolutionary" to Gall as his invention: it was used of Bismarck before him (and his biography's title uses it). Say Gall's biography called him "the white revolutionary".

Quotations not to use:
- Do not quote the "red rag to the Gallic bull", Moltke's "fanfare" remark at the Ems dinner, or any "steps of God" or "cloak of history" saying: they come from Bismarck's memoirs or later reports, and the exact wording is disputed. Describe the editing of the telegram without them.
- Do not quote Disraeli's "German revolution" speech, or any line calling 1871 a greater event than the French Revolution, in quotation marks.
- Use only "blood and iron" in quotation marks for the 1862 speech; paraphrase the rest.
- No casualty or prisoner figures for 1866 or 1870–71 (sources vary); none are in section 2.

Confusables:
- Frederick William IV (refused the crown 1849; incapacitated from 1857) vs his brother William (regent 1858, King William I 1861, German Emperor 1871). Wilhelm II (1888) is a different, later emperor.
- Two Treaties of Vienna: the Congress of Vienna (1815) and the Treaty of Vienna (October 1864) ending the Danish War.
- The Treaty of Prague (1866, Prussia–Austria) vs the Treaty of Frankfurt (1871, Germany–France); the Frankfurt Parliament (1848–49) and the Frankfurt Diet of the Confederation are different again.
- Königgrätz and Sadowa are the same battle (German and Czech-village names). Custoza 1866 is a different battle from Custoza 1848.
- Gastein (1865) divided administration only; the Treaty of Vienna (1864) had handed the duchies to Prussia and Austria jointly.
- Roon = War Minister (army organisation); Moltke = chief of the general staff (planning and command). Not the other way round.
- The North German Confederation (1867, north of the Main, Prussian-led) is not the German Confederation (1815–66, Austrian-presided), nor the Empire (1871).
- Kleindeutsch = without Austria (what happened); Grossdeutsch = with Austria.
- The title was "German Emperor", not "Emperor of Germany"; William was proclaimed at Versailles, in France, not in Berlin.
- The Reichstag (elected, manhood suffrage) vs the Bundesrat (state governments) vs the Prussian Landtag (three-class franchise).
- Realpolitik was coined by Rochau, not by Bismarck.
- The Alvensleben Convention (1863, Russia) vs Biarritz (1865, France) vs the Italian alliance (1866): three separate diplomatic steps.

## 4. What exams ask (topics only)

- Higher 100-mark essay: how and why the Germanic lands moved from Confederation to Empire. The question spans 1815–1871, so open with a sentence or two on the 1815 Confederation and 1848–50, then spend most of the answer on 1862–71. Marks go for a clear thesis stated at the start (Prussia's economic and military power plus Bismarck's diplomacy) and for each stage tied to a specific event and date: the Zollverein, the army reform, 1864, 1866, 1867, 1870–71, 18 January 1871. Narrative without explanation of why each step succeeded earns little.
- Higher comparison essays, Italy and Germany: which moved further towards unity, or how far their paths were similar. Examiners reward a judgement (Germany achieved greater unity and power) argued through paired similarities and differences (I2–I4), not two separate stories.
- Higher personality comparisons: Mazzini vs Bismarck, which contributed more to unity. For the Bismarck half, give concrete achievements (each war, each treaty, the 1867 and 1871 constitutions) and then a judgement on his contribution against the role of Prussian economic and military strength built before him.
- Plan or opportunism: examiners reward knowing both views with a named historian (Taylor's opportunist, the Prussian school's master plan, Gall) and a verdict of your own supported by events, such as Ems as opportunism and the isolation of Austria as design.
- Ordinary Level 30- and 40-mark paragraphs on Bismarck: appointment in September 1862 and the army-budget deadlock; "blood and iron"; ruling without a budget; the Danish War with Austria; Gastein as the cause of dispute; Königgrätz on 3 July 1866; the North German Confederation; Ems and the Franco-Prussian War; Versailles. Each SRP is one dated, specific fact, so give many short accurate facts in order.
- Ordinary Level short answers: what Bismarck wanted (to unite the German states under Prussian leadership) and one method he used.
- Cause and result: why Prussia beat Austria (needle-gun, railways, Moltke, isolation of Austria) and France (planning, artillery, southern allies, French isolation); results of each war (who gained what, which treaty).
- Achievements vs limits of the 1871 settlement: unity achieved, but from above, Kleindeutsch, Prussian-dominated, with a weak Reichstag and minorities inside. The Assessment block should give these as paired points.
- Contemporary vs later views of Bismarck: conservatives' admiration and liberals' hostility then reconciliation, vs historians' debate.
