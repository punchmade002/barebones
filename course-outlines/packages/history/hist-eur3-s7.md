## 1. Must cover (one bullet or block for every item; tick each off)

Background (assumed knowledge)
- [ ] A1 (assumed) Britain's economy before 1914: built on exports from the staple industries; the First World War cost it export markets (to the USA, Japan and others), left large war debts and ended its lead. One or two bullets.
- [ ] A2 (assumed) What the gold standard is: a currency fixed to gold, so the exchange rate is fixed; a high rate makes exports dearer and imports cheaper. One bullet.
- [ ] A3 (assumed) The governments of the period: Lloyd George's coalition (to 1922); Conservative governments under Baldwin (1923, 1924–29); minority Labour governments under MacDonald (1924, 1929–31); the National Government (1931–40) under MacDonald, then Baldwin, then Neville Chamberlain. One bullet.
- [ ] A4 (assumed) The Wall Street Crash (October 1929) and the world depression that followed: collapsing world trade, prices and investment. One bullet; the concept itself is C1.

The post-war boom and slump, 1919–1922
- [ ] B1 The boom of 1919–20: demand released after the war, rising prices, speculation; Lloyd George's promise of "homes fit for heroes".
- [ ] B2 The slump from late 1920: prices and exports collapsed; unemployment over two million by mid-1921, and above one million for the rest of the inter-war years.
- [ ] B3 The Geddes Axe (1922): a committee under Sir Eric Geddes recommended deep cuts in public spending (education, housing, the armed forces), which deepened the slump.
- [ ] B4 Unemployment insurance: the 1911 scheme for a few trades, extended in 1920 to most manual workers; benefit ("the dole") ran for limited periods, so long-term unemployed people fell back on extra payments or Poor Law relief; the "genuinely seeking work" test of the 1920s.
- [ ] B5 Coal in 1921: wartime government control ended, owners cut wages, a miners' lock-out; "Black Friday", when the railwaymen and transport workers of the Triple Alliance did not strike in support; the miners defeated.

Decline of the staple industries and regional unemployment
- [ ] S1 Why the staple industries declined: lost export markets, foreign competition, old equipment and methods, many small firms, falling world demand (oil and electricity replacing coal), and the high pound after 1925.
- [ ] S2 Coal: lost exports, worst hit coalfields in South Wales, Durham and Northumberland, Lanarkshire.
- [ ] S3 Cotton: Lancashire lost markets in India and the Far East to Indian and Japanese mills; long decline and mill closures.
- [ ] S4 Shipbuilding, iron and steel: yards on the Clyde, Tyne and Wear short of orders after the post-war boom and again after 1929; "rationalisation" by National Shipbuilders' Security (1930), which bought and closed yards (detail of Jarrow in the Jarrow March notes).
- [ ] S5 The regional pattern: the "distressed" or "depressed areas" (South Wales, the north-east, Lancashire, west Cumberland, central Scotland) vs the prosperous south-east and Midlands; regional rates from section 2.
- [ ] S6 "Two Britains" / "two nations": Priestley's contrast of old industrial England with the new England of by-passes, factories and suburbs.
- [ ] S7 Why the unemployed did not simply move: cost of moving and housing, ties of family and community, older skilled men unsuited to new work; the Industrial Transference Board (1928) helped some move.

The return to gold and the General Strike, 1925–1926
- [ ] G1 The return to gold (April 1925) by the Chancellor, Winston Churchill, at the pre-war rate of 4.86 dollars to the pound.
- [ ] G2 Effects: an overvalued pound made exports dearer and pushed employers to cut wages; Keynes's attack in The Economic Consequences of Mr Churchill (1925).
- [ ] G3 The coal crisis: "Red Friday" (July 1925), when the government paid a temporary subsidy to the industry; the Samuel Commission (report March 1926) recommended wage cuts; the miners' slogan "Not a penny off the pay, not a minute on the day".
- [ ] G4 The General Strike, 4–12 May 1926: called by the TUC in support of the locked-out miners; transport, printing, iron and steel, power and other workers out; the government's preparation (the Organisation for the Maintenance of Supplies, volunteers, troops guarding convoys); the British Gazette (government, edited by Churchill) vs the British Worker (TUC); the BBC under the government's influence; little violence.
- [ ] G5 End and results: the TUC called it off after nine days without guarantees; the miners stayed out until late 1926 and returned on lower wages and longer hours; union membership fell; the Trade Disputes and Trade Unions Act (1927) made general and sympathetic strikes illegal and required members to "contract in" to the political levy; it showed constitutional, not revolutionary, labour politics.

The crisis of 1929–1931
- [ ] K1 The second Labour government (1929–31), a minority government; Philip Snowden as Chancellor holding to balanced budgets and free trade (the "Treasury view" that state spending only crowds out private investment).
- [ ] K2 The Depression's effect on Britain: exports fell, unemployment rose from just over one million to about two and a half million by the end of 1930, and the insurance fund ran into debt.
- [ ] K3 Oswald Mosley's memorandum (1930), proposing public works and protection, rejected; he resigned (and later founded the New Party and the British Union of Fascists, see D1).
- [ ] K4 The May Report (July 1931): forecast a budget deficit of about £120 million and recommended large spending cuts, including a 20 per cent cut in unemployment benefit; a run on the pound followed.
- [ ] K5 The Labour Cabinet split over a 10 per cent benefit cut; MacDonald formed a National Government with Conservatives and Liberals (24 August 1931); Labour expelled him and went into opposition.
- [ ] K6 The emergency budget (September 1931): 10 per cent benefit cut, public-sector pay cuts, higher taxes; the Invergordon mutiny (15–16 September 1931) against naval pay cuts.
- [ ] K7 Leaving the gold standard (21 September 1931): the pound fell by about a quarter, making exports cheaper and allowing low interest rates.
- [ ] K8 The general election of October 1931: a landslide for the National Government; Labour cut to about 50 seats.

Recovery policies of the National Government, 1931–1939
- [ ] R1 Protection: the Import Duties Act (1932), a general 10 per cent tariff on most imports (Empire goods exempt), ending free trade.
- [ ] R2 Imperial preference: the Ottawa Agreements (1932) with the Dominions; effects on trade with the Empire vs the USA and Europe.
- [ ] R3 Cheap money: Bank Rate at 2 per cent from June 1932; the conversion of War Loan (1932) to a lower interest rate; cheap mortgages fed the housing boom.
- [ ] R4 Help for agriculture: the Wheat Act (1932) subsidy and marketing boards such as the Milk Marketing Board (1933).
- [ ] R5 Neville Chamberlain as Chancellor (from November 1931): balanced budgets and no large public-works programme; the 1931 cuts restored in 1934–35.
- [ ] R6 Rearmament from the mid-1930s, accelerating after 1936: orders for steel, shipbuilding, engineering and aircraft that helped some depressed areas more than any regional policy.

The unemployed: the means test, the NUWM and hunger marches
- [ ] U1 The Poor Law reformed: the Local Government Act (1929) abolished the Boards of Guardians and passed relief to council Public Assistance Committees (from 1930).
- [ ] U2 The household means test (November 1931): after 26 weeks of insurance benefit, "transitional payments" depended on an inspection of the whole household's income and savings by the Public Assistance Committees.
- [ ] U3 Why the means test was hated: inspectors in the home, earnings of sons and daughters and savings counted, furniture to be sold, families split up as young earners left home; humiliation and stigma.
- [ ] U4 Councils that applied it leniently (e.g. County Durham, Rotherham) had their committees replaced by government commissioners.
- [ ] U5 The Unemployment Act (1934): insurance benefit restored and run separately; the national Unemployment Assistance Board for the long-term unemployed; its new lower scales (January 1935) caused mass protest, above all in South Wales, and were suspended by the "Standstill" Act (1935).
- [ ] U6 The National Unemployed Workers' Movement (NUWM), founded 1921, Communist-led under Wal Hannington: protests, help with claims, resistance to evictions; distrusted by the Labour Party and TUC.
- [ ] U7 Hunger marches to London (national marches 1922, 1929, 1930, 1932, 1934, 1936): aims, police and government hostility, public reaction; the 1932 march's clashes in London.
- [ ] U8 The Jarrow March (October 1936) as the best-known protest: one bullet and a cross-reference to the Jarrow March notes.

Special Areas, housing and the new industries
- [ ] H1 The Special Areas (Development and Improvement) Act (1934): South Wales, the north-east (Tyneside and Durham), west Cumberland and central Scotland; two commissioners; small budget (£2 million at first); power to aid but not to compel industry.
- [ ] H2 The Special Areas (Amendment) Act (1937): incentives (rent, rates and tax help) for firms; trading estates such as Team Valley (Gateshead) and Treforest (South Wales); results small before rearmament.
- [ ] H3 Council housing acts: Addison (1919), Chamberlain (1923), Wheatley (1924), Greenwood (1930, slum clearance), the 1935 Act on overcrowding.
- [ ] H4 The private housing boom of the 1930s: cheap money, building societies, suburban semi-detached houses, mostly in the south and Midlands; building as a source of jobs.
- [ ] H5 New industries: motor cars (Morris at Cowley, Austin at Longbridge, Ford at Dagenham), electrical goods, chemicals (ICI, 1926), rayon, aircraft; the Central Electricity Board (1926) and the National Grid let factories locate anywhere, so they went near London and the Midlands markets.
- [ ] H6 Rising living standards for those in work: falling prices, real wages up, shorter hours, consumer goods (radios, vacuum cleaners, cars), holidays, cinema and radio (detail in the notes on Anglo-American popular culture).

Social conditions in the depressed areas
- [ ] L1 Effects of long-term unemployment: poverty, poor diet and health, bad and overcrowded housing, high infant mortality, loss of self-respect; women's burden in managing on the dole.
- [ ] L2 Contemporary investigations and writers: J.B. Priestley, English Journey (1934); Walter Greenwood, Love on the Dole (1933); John Boyd Orr, Food, Health and Income (1936); George Orwell, The Road to Wigan Pier (1937); the Pilgrim Trust, Men Without Work (1938).
- [ ] L3 The scale: registered unemployment peaked at about three million (over a fifth of insured workers) in 1932–33, fell with recovery, but stayed above one million to 1939.

Democracy in Britain
- [ ] D1 Democracy survived: extremists won little support. Mosley's New Party (1931) and British Union of Fascists (1932); the Battle of Cable Street (4 October 1936); the Public Order Act (1936) banned political uniforms; the small Communist Party (one MP from 1935). Reasons: a long parliamentary tradition, Labour and the TUC committed to constitutional methods, the National Government's broad support, the dole (however mean), and real prosperity in much of the country.
- [ ] D2 Comparison with Germany: Britain's problems were long-term decline in particular regions and industries; no hyperinflation, no collapse of democracy (detail in the notes on Weimar Germany). One or two bullets.

Key personality: John Maynard Keynes (one block, headed "Key personality: J.M. Keynes")
- [ ] P1 Who he was: born in Cambridge (1883); Cambridge economist, Fellow of King's College; part of the Bloomsbury group; married the ballerina Lydia Lopokova (1925).
- [ ] P2 First World War and Versailles: Treasury official from 1915; chief Treasury representative at the Paris Peace Conference; resigned (June 1919) over reparations; The Economic Consequences of the Peace (1919) called them unpayable and damaging to Europe; a participant who became a critic.
- [ ] P3 Critic of orthodoxy in the 1920s: A Tract on Monetary Reform (1923); The Economic Consequences of Mr Churchill (1925) against the return to gold.
- [ ] P4 Public works and the Liberal plan (1929): he backed Lloyd George's pledge to cut unemployment through public works, and with Hubert Henderson wrote Can Lloyd George Do It? (1929); member of the Macmillan Committee on Finance and Industry and the Economic Advisory Council (1930); A Treatise on Money (1930).
- [ ] P5 The General Theory of Employment, Interest and Money (1936): unemployment comes from too little total demand; economies can stay stuck in a slump; government should borrow and spend (public works) in a slump, and the multiplier spreads the effect; a direct challenge to the "Treasury view" and balanced budgets.
- [ ] P6 His wider reach in the 1930s: his open letter to President Roosevelt (December 1933); Harold Macmillan's The Middle Way (1938) and other planners drew on his ideas.
- [ ] P7 Second World War: How to Pay for the War (1940) proposed compulsory saving to curb inflation; adviser in the Treasury from 1940; his approach shaped the 1941 budget; made a peer (Baron Keynes, 1942).
- [ ] P8 Bretton Woods (July 1944): led the British delegation; the conference set up the International Monetary Fund and the World Bank; his own plan for an international clearing union was largely set aside for the American plan.
- [ ] P9 The American loan (1945) and death (April 1946).
- [ ] P10 How contemporaries saw him: brilliant and persuasive but resisted by the Treasury, the Bank of England, Snowden and Chamberlain; welcomed by Lloyd George, Mosley and some in Labour; his ideas had little effect on 1930s policy.
- [ ] P11 How historians judge him: his ideas shaped the 1944 Employment Policy White Paper and the post-1945 "Keynesian" consensus of managed demand and full employment; criticised later (inflation of the 1970s); debate over whether Keynesian spending could have cured unemployment in 1930s Britain, with its dependence on trade and the scale needed.

Key concepts and terms (keyTerms)
- [ ] C1 The Depression, as it affected Britain (falling exports and prices, mass unemployment concentrated in the staple-industry regions; milder than in Germany or the USA).
- [ ] C2 Protectionism, as practised by Britain (the 1932 tariff and imperial preference replacing free trade).
- [ ] C3 Democracy, as shown by Britain (parliamentary government survived the crisis; extremists marginal).
- [ ] C4 Other terms: staple industries; distressed/depressed areas; Special Areas; gold standard; the "Treasury view"; dole; means test; transitional payments; Public Assistance Committee; Unemployment Assistance Board; Geddes Axe; Black Friday; Red Friday; General Strike; TUC; National Government; May Report; Import Duties Act; imperial preference; Ottawa Agreements; cheap money; NUWM; hunger march; rationalisation; multiplier; Keynesianism.

Assessment (end with this block, headed "Assessment: …")
- [ ] J1 Achievements vs limits of government policy: recovery after 1932, housing, new industries, rising real wages, democracy preserved vs unemployment above a million throughout, the depressed areas neglected, the means test, the meagre Special Areas budget, and recovery owed more to cheap money, private building and rearmament than to planned regional policy.
- [ ] J2 Contemporary vs later views: the "hungry thirties" or "devil's decade" image of the left and the writers of the time vs historians since the 1970s who stress the prosperity of the employed majority (e.g. John Stevenson and Chris Cook, The Slump, 1977), while agreeing the depressed areas suffered badly.
- [ ] J3 Britain vs Germany (an exam pairing): which faced greater problems and which dealt with them better; Britain's slower, regional decline vs Germany's hyperinflation and Depression collapse; Britain's democracy survived.
- [ ] J4 Keynes vs other responses (an exam pairing with the Jarrow March and Churchill): Keynes changed economic thinking in the long run but not 1930s policy; the Jarrow March won sympathy but no immediate help.

## 3. Traps

Do not use (wrong or doubtful in the current notes):
- Do not use "In 1929 he co-wrote with Hubert Henderson the Liberal Party pamphlet We Can Conquer Unemployment", because We Can Conquer Unemployment was the Liberal programme issued under Lloyd George's name; Keynes supported it and with Henderson wrote the defence of it, Can Lloyd George Do It? (P4). The same mistake is in the current fact card; rewrite it.
- Do not use "The coal industry was returned to private ownership in 1921", because the mines were always privately owned; what ended (31 March 1921) was wartime government control.
- Do not use "Britain kept a system of imperial preference until the 1970s", because the date and form of its ending are unclear. Leave out any end date.
- Do not use "the National Hunger March of 1932 presented a petition with a million signatures to Parliament, which refused to receive it", because the petition's size and fate are reported inconsistently (by most accounts the police took it before it reached Parliament). Give no petition detail.
- Do not use "recommended cuts of 20 per cent in unemployment benefit and public sector pay", because the 20 per cent figure was for benefit only. Use K4.
- Do not use "about four million houses ... roughly two-thirds of them privately built", because the private share was nearer three-quarters. Use H4: about 1.1 million by councils, the rest private.
- Do not use "The Housing Act of 1935 extended subsidies for slum clearance", because that Act dealt with overcrowding. Use H3.
- Do not use "Unemployment insurance ... was extended in 1920 to cover most workers", because farm workers and domestic servants (and non-manual workers above an income limit) were left out. Say "most manual workers".
- Do not use "it was the only nationwide strike in British history" as worded. Say "Britain's only general strike".
- Do not use "Unemployment in the distressed areas could remain above 30 per cent in many towns": use the checked figures in S5.
- Do not use the current fact card's hunger-march years without checking them against U7, and do not use the notes' shorter list as if complete.

Doubtful claims to avoid:
- Do not say Keynes's ideas produced the 1930s recovery or were the basis of Roosevelt's New Deal. They had little effect on British policy before 1940, and the New Deal was not built on the General Theory.
- Do not say the General Strike was a revolutionary attempt, or that the government used the army against strikers in battles. It was largely peaceful.
- Do not give money figures for the Geddes cuts, Special Areas spending after 1934, rearmament or the dole; give no rates of benefit.
- Do not give exact real-wage, GDP or industrial-output growth figures; say "rose".
- Do not give unemployment figures for named towns other than "over half" for Merthyr Tydfil and Jarrow (Jarrow's exact figure belongs in the Jarrow March notes).
- Do not invent quotations from Keynes, Churchill, Baldwin, MacDonald or Orwell; only those marked "may be quoted" in section 2.

Confusables:
- The Economic Consequences of the Peace (1919, Versailles) vs The Economic Consequences of Mr Churchill (1925, gold).
- We Can Conquer Unemployment (Lloyd George's Liberal programme, 1929) vs Can Lloyd George Do It? (Keynes and Henderson, 1929).
- A Tract on Monetary Reform (1923) vs A Treatise on Money (1930) vs the General Theory (1936).
- Black Friday (15 April 1921, the Triple Alliance fails the miners) vs Red Friday (31 July 1925, the government pays a subsidy).
- The return to gold (1925, Churchill, Conservative) vs leaving gold (21 September 1931, National Government).
- The May Report (Sir George May, 1931) vs the Geddes Axe (Sir Eric Geddes, 1922) vs the Samuel Commission (coal, 1926) vs the Macmillan Committee (finance, 1929–31; Keynes a member) — and Harold Macmillan (The Middle Way, 1938) is a different man from Lord Macmillan who chaired the committee.
- Insurance benefit (contributory, time-limited) vs transitional payments / unemployment assistance (means-tested) vs Poor Law relief.
- Public Assistance Committees (local, 1930–) vs the Unemployment Assistance Board (national, 1934–).
- Distressed or depressed areas (general term) vs Special Areas (the four designated in 1934).
- The NUWM (Communist-led, many marches) vs the Jarrow March (council-organised, cross-party, kept apart from the NUWM).
- Churchill as Chancellor (1924–29, gold, the British Gazette) is in scope here; Churchill as war leader is not.
- Snowden (Labour Chancellor 1929–31) vs Chamberlain (National Government Chancellor 1931–37).

What belongs to neighbouring subsections (one clause or a cross-reference at most):
- The Jarrow March case study: Palmer's yard, National Shipbuilders' Security's closure of it, Ellen Wilkinson, the route, the petition, Baldwin's refusal, The Town That Was Murdered. One bullet here.
- Weimar Germany and the rise of Hitler: German inflation, the Depression in Germany and German governments' responses; use only as a brief comparison.
- The Second World War and Churchill: Churchill's career and war leadership. Keynes's wartime role stays here.

## 4. What exams ask (topics only)

- Higher Level essay (100 marks), the most frequent form: how Britain dealt with, or how successfully it dealt with, its inter-war economic and social problems. Marks reward naming the problems (staple-industry decline, regional unemployment, the 1931 financial crisis, industrial conflict, poverty in the depressed areas) and then judging each response: gold 1925 (harmful), the General Strike's defeat, the 1931 cuts and leaving gold, protection and Ottawa, cheap money, the means test, the Special Areas Act, housing, rearmament. A clear overall verdict ("partial success") repeated in each paragraph scores better than a narrative.
- Higher Level essay on Britain 1920–45 in peace and war: the inter-war half needs the same problems-and-responses structure; the wartime half belongs to the Home Front notes.
- Higher Level comparison: whether Britain or Germany had the greater problems, or dealt with them better. Marks reward explicit comparison in each paragraph (gradual regional decline vs hyperinflation and total collapse; democracy kept vs lost) rather than two separate accounts.
- Higher Level essay pairing Keynes with the Jarrow March (and, earlier, Churchill): what each contributed or achieved. For Keynes, marks reward his ideas (demand, public works, multiplier, against the Treasury view), his dated works, the fact that 1930s governments did not follow him, and his long-term influence (1944 White Paper, Bretton Woods, post-war consensus); end with a judgement weighing short-term failure against long-term success.
- Ordinary Level paragraph (30 or 40 marks) on Keynes: about six accurate dated facts (Versailles book 1919, gold pamphlet 1925, Liberal plan 1929, General Theory 1936, How to Pay for the War 1940, Bretton Woods 1944, death 1946) plus one sentence on why he mattered.
- Ordinary Level paragraph on unemployment or the depressed areas: where unemployment was (regions and industries), the peak of about three million, life on the dole and the means test, and contrast with the prosperous south-east.
- Ordinary Level and documents parts (6 and 10 marks) on the Depression and its effects: factories and shipyards closed after 1929, mass unemployment, the shame of unemployment, what the unemployed wanted from government (work, new industry). Answers need the cause-and-result link, not a list.
- Documents questions on inter-war Britain draw on contemporary writers and the "two Britains" contrast: the notes must make clear who Priestley, Orwell and the Pilgrim Trust were and when they wrote, so students can judge a source as primary and contemporary.
- Judgement material examiners reward: achievements (recovery after 1932, housing, new industries, rising real wages for the employed, democracy kept) vs limits (unemployment never below a million, depressed areas left behind, means test, tiny Special Areas budget, recovery owed to cheap money and rearmament); contemporary bleak views vs later revisionist views.
- Precision: date the return to gold and leaving it, the General Strike, the formation of the National Government, the Import Duties Act and the Special Areas Act; name the Chancellors responsible (Churchill, Snowden, Chamberlain).
