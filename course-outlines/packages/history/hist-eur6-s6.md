## 1. Must cover (one bullet or block for every item; tick each off)

Background (assumed)
- [ ] A1 (assumed) The Great Depression and the New Deal left the US with high unemployment in the 1930s; the Second World War ended the Depression.
- [ ] A2 (assumed) The US entered the war in December 1941 and was not bombed or invaded; Europe and Japan were devastated.
- [ ] A3 (assumed) The Cold War (containment, Truman Doctrine, NATO) is the political setting for aid, defence spending and overseas investment (detail belongs to the foreign policy notes).
- [ ] A4 (assumed) Keynesian idea: governments can manage demand through spending and taxation.

Key concepts (each defined as used in this topic; also in keyTerms)
- [ ] K1 Corporate capitalism: an economy dominated by large corporations run by salaried managers.
- [ ] K2 Globalisation: growing integration of national economies through trade, investment and multinationals.
- [ ] K3 Military-industrial complex: the alliance of armed forces, defence contractors and politicians with a shared interest in high defence spending.
- [ ] K4 Multinational corporation (essential term).
- [ ] K5 Bretton Woods system (essential term).
- [ ] K6 GI Bill (essential term).

The war as the base of the boom
- [ ] W1 Wartime production (aircraft, ships, tanks, munitions) ended the Depression; unemployment fell to very low levels.
- [ ] W2 The US the strongest industrial economy in 1945; rivals devastated.
- [ ] W3 Wartime savings and controls, then pent-up demand for cars, houses and appliances.
- [ ] W4 The GI Bill (1944): education, home and business loans, unemployment pay; mass housing market.
- [ ] W5 Government-built wartime factories sold cheaply to private firms after 1945.

International financing
- [ ] I1 Bretton Woods (July 1944): IMF and World Bank; dollar convertible to gold at a fixed price; other currencies pegged to the dollar.
- [ ] I2 The dollar as the reserve currency and the dollar gap in Europe.
- [ ] I3 The Marshall Plan, 1948–52: aid to Western Europe, largely spent on US goods; revived trading partners.
- [ ] I4 GATT (1947) and lower trade barriers.
- [ ] I5 Korean War and NSC-68: rearmament boosted demand.

Public investment
- [ ] P1 Federal-Aid Highway Act (1956) and the Interstate system; Highway Trust Fund; defence justification.
- [ ] P2 Defence spending through the 1950s and 1960s: aircraft, electronics, missiles.
- [ ] P3 Research funding: National Science Foundation (1950), universities, spin-offs (jets, radar, nuclear power, computers).
- [ ] P4 Sputnik (1957), NASA (1958), the space programme as an employer; National Defense Education Act (1958).

The military-industrial complex
- [ ] M1 Eisenhower's farewell address, 17 January 1961: the warning and its wording.
- [ ] M2 Major contractors and their dependence on government contracts.
- [ ] M3 Irony of the source (a general whose administration built up arms) and critics' use of it.
- [ ] M4 Effects: high-technology industry and regional growth (California, Silicon Valley); distortion of priorities.

The multinational corporation
- [ ] N1 Definition and the main examples: General Motors, IBM, Coca-Cola, ITT.

- [ ] N3 IBM World Trade Corporation (1949).
- [ ] N4 Coca-Cola's franchise system abroad.

- [ ] N10 The Fortune 500 (1955) and the concentration of the economy.

Key personality: the "Organization Man" (one block, every point)
- [ ] O1 Not one person: a type; defined as a salaried corporate manager or professional who moved with the company.

Assessment (paired judgement points, final block)
- [ ] S1 Achievements: rising living standards, a mass middle class, home ownership.

## 3. Traps

Do not use (wrong or doubtful facts in the current notes):
- Do not use: that the Taft-Hartley affidavit requirement lasted "until 1959", because the non-communist affidavit was replaced in 1959 (Landrum-Griffin Act) by a ban on communists holding union office, and the Supreme Court struck that down in 1965. Say only that Taft-Hartley required union leaders to sign non-communist affidavits.
- Do not use: that Eisenhower's speech "echoed" C. Wright Mills's The Power Elite, because that link is speculation. Mills's book may be mentioned only as an earlier critical study, if at all.
- Do not use: dollar figures for defence spending ("about 14 billion to about 50 billion"), because sources differ on which measure and fiscal year to use. Say roughly quadrupled.
- Do not use: "unemployment pay" or other GI Bill benefits without the caveat that black veterans were often denied the benefits in practice.
- Do not use: a percentage of IBM revenue earned abroad, or the claim that GM's overseas share was any set figure; none is checked.
- Do not use: "Coca-Cola's bottling networks spread ... from the 1950s" as a start date; the franchise system dates from earlier, with wide postwar expansion.
- Do not use: that Whyte coined "Organization Man" as a single real person. It names a type.

## 4. What exams ask

- Higher 100-mark essay, the commonest form: why the US economy boomed from 1945 to 1968 (or what its achievements were). Marks go to a stated list of causes (wartime strength, US dominance, public spending, international financing, consumer demand), ranked or linked, with dated examples (GI Bill 1944, Bretton Woods 1944, Marshall Plan 1948–52, Interstate 1956). A list without weighting scores lower.
- Pairing questions: multinationals and the military-industrial complex (and sometimes McCarthyism or the anti-war movement) as separate topics in one question: give each its own developed paragraph with named examples and a verdict on its impact.
- Boom vs later decline: essays that cover 1945–1989 expect the boom years to be treated as a distinct phase with its own causes; show the limits (dependence on defence spending and temporary dominance) to prepare the contrast.
- Military-industrial complex: who said it, when, what it meant, which companies, and a judgement of whether the warning was justified; naming the 17 January 1961 date earns credit.
- Multinationals: define, name General Motors, IBM, Coca-Cola and ITT with a distinct fact for each (IBM's World Trade Corporation 1949; Coca-Cola franchising; GM in Europe; ITT as conglomerate), explain why Western Europe and the Treaty of Rome mattered, and give European criticism (Servan-Schreiber).
- Ordinary Level short answer or 30-mark paragraph on multinationals: who they are, three or four named companies with one fact each, and one effect.
- Ordinary Level paragraph on the Organization Man: who Whyte was, the book and year, the social ethic and its three beliefs or conformity, suburbs, the type of worker. Each correct, relevant statement earns marks; the Organization Man must be described as a type, not a person.
- Cause and effect: why defence spending grew, why the Interstate system was built, how Bretton Woods and Marshall Aid helped US exports.
- Achievements vs limits and contemporary vs later views: rewarded as paired points; naming Galbraith or Harrington adds weight, as does recognising unequal sharing of the boom.
