# Package: hist-ire5-s6 — Direct Rule and the search for a settlement, 1974–1993

## 1. Must cover (one bullet or block for every item; tick each off)

Background (assumed)
- [ ] A1 (assumed) Direct Rule was first imposed in March 1972 when Stormont was suspended; the power-sharing executive of 1974 collapsed in May 1974 and Direct Rule returned (the Sunningdale case study covers the detail; one sentence here).
- [ ] A2 (assumed) The Provisional IRA, loyalist paramilitaries and the British army were already in conflict; the Troubles began in 1969.
- [ ] A3 (assumed) The consent principle (no change in Northern Ireland's status without majority consent) was already stated in 1973 and is the thread running to 1993.

Key concepts (defined as used in this topic; also in keyTerms)
- [ ] K1 Terrorism: violence against civilians and the state for political ends; used here of the IRA and loyalist paramilitaries, and the British response by security and politics.
- [ ] K2 Power sharing: unionists and nationalists sharing government; sought after 1974 but not restored by 1993.
- [ ] K3 Propaganda: messages used to shape opinion (hunger strike publicity, the broadcast ban, the IRA's and the governments' public statements).

Direct Rule, 1974–1976
- [ ] D1 Direct Rule after May 1974: Secretary of State and Northern Ireland Office govern; list of Secretaries of State (Rees to Mayhew).
- [ ] D2 The Constitutional Convention: elected 1 May 1975; unionist majority; recommended majority rule; rejected by Westminster; dissolved March 1976.
- [ ] D3 Ulsterisation: from 1976 the RUC and UDR take more of the security role from the army.
- [ ] D4 Criminalisation: special category status ended 1 March 1976; Diplock courts (1973); prisoners treated as ordinary criminals.
- [ ] D5 The Peace People (1976): cause (the Maguire children), founders, aims, Nobel Prize, decline.

The H-Blocks and the hunger strikes
- [ ] H1 The blanket protest (from 1976) and dirty protest (1978) at the Maze (Long Kesh) H-Blocks, for political status.
- [ ] H2 The 1980 hunger strike (ended 18 December 1980).
- [ ] H3 The 1981 hunger strike: Bobby Sands (start, election as MP, death); nine further deaths, ten in all; the end on 3 October 1981.
- [ ] H4 Thatcher's refusal ("crime is crime is crime") and the later concessions.
- [ ] H5 Effects: sympathy and recruitment for republicans, hardening of attitudes, and the turn to elections.

Sinn Féin's electoral strategy
- [ ] E1 Owen Carron's by-election win (August 1981); two prisoners elected to the Dáil (June 1981).
- [ ] E2 "The Armalite and the ballot box" (Danny Morrison, October 1981).
- [ ] E3 Five seats in the October 1982 Assembly election; abstention.
- [ ] E4 Adams wins West Belfast (June 1983); abstention from Westminster.

Key personality: Gerry Adams (one block, every point)
- [ ] G1 Who he was: born 1948, Belfast, republican family in West Belfast.
- [ ] G2 Career before 1983: interned in the early 1970s; the secret talks with Whitelaw (1972).
- [ ] G3 Sinn Féin president from November 1983 (to 2018); led the move into electoral politics.
- [ ] G4 Shaped events: Hume–Adams talks (1988, 1991–93); the broadcast ban (October 1988) as propaganda war.
- [ ] G5 Shaped by events: the hunger strikes, the 1984 shooting, loss of the Westminster seat in 1992.
- [ ] G6 Contemporaries: supporters and critics (unionists, many nationalists) disagreed over whether he was a peacemaker or the political wing of the IRA.
- [ ] G7 Historians: credited with leading republicans from armed struggle to negotiation; critics see the "Armalite and ballot box" strategy as a way to keep both; his denial of IRA membership.

The New Ireland Forum and the Brighton bomb
- [ ] F1 The Forum (1983–84): Dublin initiative; Fianna Fáil, Fine Gael, Labour and the SDLP; unionists did not attend; Sinn Féin excluded.
- [ ] F2 Report of 2 May 1984: three options (unitary state, federal/confederal state, joint authority); unitary state preferred.
- [ ] F3 Thatcher's rejection at a press conference, 19 November 1984 ("out, out, out"); significance.
- [ ] F4 The Brighton bomb (12 October 1984): the target, deaths, Thatcher's escape, Patrick Magee.

Key personality: Margaret Thatcher (one block, every point)
- [ ] T1 Who she was: Prime Minister May 1979 to November 1990; security-first approach.
- [ ] T2 What she did: held firm on the hunger strikes; rejected the Forum options; signed the Anglo-Irish Agreement.
- [ ] T3 Shaped by events: Mountbatten and Warrenpoint (1979), the hunger strikes, Brighton.
- [ ] T4 Participant: personal involvement with FitzGerald in 1984–85.
- [ ] T5 Contemporaries: unionists felt betrayed; nationalists and Dublin welcomed the Agreement; republicans saw intransigence.
- [ ] T6 Historians: differing views of her motives (security cooperation, undercutting Sinn Féin, relations with Dublin and Washington).

The Anglo-Irish Agreement, 15 November 1985
- [ ] AIA1 Signed at Hillsborough Castle by Thatcher and FitzGerald; why it was made (security, the Sinn Féin threat to the SDLP, the Forum).
- [ ] AIA2 Terms: Intergovernmental Conference, Maryfield secretariat, Irish government's consultative role, Article 1 (consent).
- [ ] AIA3 Unionist reaction: "Ulster says No", Belfast City Hall rally (23 November 1985), resignation of 15 MPs (January 1986), day of action (March 1986).
- [ ] AIA4 Nationalist, republican and Fianna Fáil reactions.
- [ ] AIA5 Significance: the biggest British policy shift since Sunningdale; it survived.

Key personality: James Molyneaux (one block, every point)
- [ ] M1 Who he was: born 1920; MP South Antrim 1970 and Lagan Valley 1983; Ulster Unionist Party leader 1979–95.
- [ ] M2 What he did: led mainstream unionist opposition to the Agreement, with Paisley's campaign; resignations of 1986.
- [ ] M3 Shaped events: integrationist policy; cultivated Conservative ministers; influence in the 1990s.
- [ ] M4 Contemporaries: valued as cautious by some, criticised as passive by others.
- [ ] M5 Historians: steady, quiet leader whose strategy of opposition did not stop the Agreement.

Key personality: John Hume, part 3 (one block, every point)
- [ ] U1 SDLP leader from 1979; MEP; his policy of dialogue and consent.
- [ ] U2 Supported the Agreement; the "three relationships" analysis.
- [ ] U3 The Hume–Adams dialogue (1988, 1991–93): purpose, criticism, outcome.
- [ ] U4 Took part in the Brooke–Mayhew talks.
- [ ] U5 Contemporary views (moderate vs criticised for talking to Sinn Féin) and historians' views (influence on the Downing Street Declaration).

Later steps
- [ ] L1 Enniskillen (8 November 1987): bomb at the Remembrance Day ceremony, deaths, Gordon Wilson, effects.
- [ ] L2 The broadcast ban (1988) as a propaganda measure (also under Adams).
- [ ] L3 Brooke's statement of November 1990 ("no selfish strategic or economic interest").
- [ ] L4 The Brooke initiative (1991) and Mayhew talks (1992): multi-party format, no agreement.
- [ ] L5 The Downing Street Declaration, 15 December 1993: Major and Reynolds; self-determination and consent; British "no selfish interest"; invitation to republicans; Irish Forum and Articles 2 and 3.
- [ ] L6 Reactions to the Declaration: nationalists and Dublin, unionists, Sinn Féin and the IRA (considered it; the ceasefire came in 1994, outside this subsection).

Assessment block
- [ ] S1 Achievement: Anglo-Irish Agreement gave Dublin a formal role, and the Declaration set out consent and self-determination as basis for later talks.
- [ ] S2 Limit: no devolved government restored by 1993; Direct Rule remained.
- [ ] S3 Limit: violence continued (Brighton, Enniskillen); dialogue did not end it in this period.
- [ ] S4 Contemporary views (unionists vs nationalists, republicans) and later view (framework for peace built step by step).

## 3. Traps

Do not use (wrong or doubtful facts in the current notes):
- Do not use: Thatcher "in her memoirs acknowledged that security cooperation did not improve as she had hoped", because the wording is unverified; say instead that historians differ over her motives and that the Agreement did not end violence.
- Do not use: "many historians and security sources identify him as a senior republican figure" stated as a fact about Adams; say that he has always denied IRA membership and that others have alleged it.
- Do not use: the keyTerm wording that Ulsterisation moved the security role "to the RUC" alone; the UDR was also involved.
- Do not use: that Enniskillen victims were all civilians; say "mostly elderly civilians".
- Do not use: figures for Peace People rally crowds, the Belfast City Hall rally, or vote counts, because sources vary.
- Do not use: Danny Lennon or the Maguire children's names and ages; the notes need only "three children".

## 4. What exams ask

- Higher essays on the moves towards a peaceful resolution between 1973 and 1993 (asked in 2008, 2012, 2025 style): list steps in order (Convention, Peace People, Forum, Anglo-Irish Agreement, Hume–Adams, Brooke–Mayhew, Declaration), and judge how far each moved things. A narrative with no judgement scores poorly; weigh the Agreement (permanent role for Dublin) against the Declaration (consent and self-determination).
- Comparing the Anglo-Irish Agreement and the Declaration (asked repeatedly): for each give date, signatories, terms and effect, then say which mattered more and why. Include both achievements and limits (unionist anger, continued violence).
- Short Ordinary-Level answers on the Agreement (asked often): date, place, signatories, Intergovernmental Conference, Maryfield. Learn these exact items.
- Short answers on the Declaration: date, Major and Reynolds, self-determination, consent of a majority, "no selfish strategic or economic interest".
- Key personality parts on Thatcher, Adams, Molyneaux and Hume: marks go to what the person did, with dates, and how contemporaries and historians judged them. Thatcher: hunger strikers, Forum rejection, Agreement. Adams: presidency, electoral strategy, Hume–Adams. Molyneaux: leader 1979–95, opposition to the Agreement. Hume: SDLP leader, dialogue, Declaration.
- Hunger strike and electoral strategy questions: the sequence from the end of special category status to Sands's death and election to Sinn Féin's seats. Explain why the strikes changed republican tactics.
- Document-style or "propaganda" questions: the broadcast ban, the use of the Peace People and hunger strike publicity; show who was trying to influence whom.
- Weak answers confuse Sunningdale (1973) with the Agreement (1985), or date the Brighton bomb and the Forum rejection to the wrong years.
