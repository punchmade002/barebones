## 1. Must cover (one bullet or block for every item; tick each off)

Background (assumed)
- [ ] A1 (assumed) Jim Crow: state and local laws enforcing racial segregation in the South; "separate but equal" (Plessy v. Ferguson, 1896).
- [ ] A2 (assumed) Brown v. Board of Education (May 1954): Supreme Court ruled school segregation unconstitutional; it raised black hopes and hardened white resistance.
- [ ] A3 (assumed) The NAACP: national organisation that fought segregation through the courts, with local branches.
- [ ] A4 (assumed) Black Americans in the South were largely excluded from voting; the black church was the main institution free of white control.

Key concept
- [ ] K1 Discrimination: unequal treatment of people because of their race, here enforced by law on Montgomery's buses (also in keyTerms).

Segregation on Montgomery's buses
- [ ] B1 The seating rules: front for white, rear for black passengers, the driver able to move the dividing line, black passengers made to give up seats and stand.
- [ ] B2 Other humiliations: paying at the front then re-entering by the rear door; drivers with police powers; insults.
- [ ] B3 Black riders were the large majority of passengers, so the rules hit most of the city's black working population (many were domestic workers and labourers).
- [ ] B4 Law behind it: city ordinance and Alabama state law.

Earlier protests and preparation
- [ ] E1 The Women's Political Council (WPC) under Jo Ann Robinson: letter to Mayor Gayle (May 1954) warning of a boycott.
- [ ] E2 Claudette Colvin (March 1955) and Mary Louise Smith (October 1955): arrested for refusing to give up seats.
- [ ] E3 Why Colvin was not made the test case (leaders wanted a respectable adult).
- [ ] E4 Earlier boycott elsewhere (Baton Rouge, 1953).

The arrest of Rosa Parks and the start of the boycott
- [ ] R1 Rosa Parks: 1 December 1955 arrest, driver, offence.
- [ ] R2 Parks' background: seamstress, NAACP branch secretary, Highlander Folk School; not a tired, accidental protester.
- [ ] R3 E. D. Nixon (NAACP branch president) bailed her out with the Durrs and backed using her case; lawyer Fred Gray.
- [ ] R4 Trial 5 December 1955: guilty and fined.
- [ ] R5 Robinson, the WPC and students ran off leaflets; ministers announced the boycott in church; one-day boycott on Monday 5 December 1955 almost completely observed.
- [ ] R6 The Holt Street Baptist Church mass meeting on the evening of 5 December voted to continue.
- [ ] R7 The three demands (courtesy, first-come first-served seating, black drivers on black routes) and why they grew into a demand for desegregation.

Organisation: the MIA, churches and car pools
- [ ] M1 The Montgomery Improvement Association formed 5 December 1955; King elected president.
- [ ] M2 Why King was chosen (young, new to the city, no enemies) and what he said at Holt Street (justice, non-violence, Christian love).
- [ ] M3 Other leaders: Ralph Abernathy, E. D. Nixon, Jo Ann Robinson.
- [ ] M4 The car pool (volunteer drivers, church dispatch points, station wagons bought by the churches) and walking.
- [ ] M5 Black churches as the organising base; outside support and donations.
- [ ] M6 Economic pressure on the bus company and city shops.
- [ ] M7 Rustin and Smiley advise on non-violence (February 1956).

White resistance and the legal challenge
- [ ] W1 Divide-and-rule tactics: false claims of a settlement; harassment of car-pool drivers and taxi fares.
- [ ] W2 King's arrest for speeding (26 January 1956).
- [ ] W3 Bombing of King's home (30 January 1956) and his calming of the crowd.
- [ ] W4 The February 1956 indictments of 89 leaders under a 1921 anti-boycott law; King's conviction in March 1956.
- [ ] W5 The White Citizens' Councils and the Ku Klux Klan: intimidation, sacking of black workers.
- [ ] W6 Browder v. Gayle: filed 1 February 1956 by Fred Gray for four women; federal court ruling 5 June 1956 (two to one); Supreme Court affirmed 13 November 1956.
- [ ] W7 The injunction against the car pools (13 November 1956).
- [ ] W8 Why Parks was not a plaintiff.

Ending the boycott and immediate results
- [ ] D1 The Supreme Court order served 20 December 1956; the MIA ended the boycott.
- [ ] D2 Buses desegregated 21 December 1956, after 381 days; King and Abernathy rode a bus; MIA rules on conduct.
- [ ] D3 Violence after the victory (snipers, bombed churches and homes, January 1957).
- [ ] D4 Founding of the Southern Christian Leadership Conference (SCLC), 1957.
- [ ] D5 What did not change (schools, housing, jobs, voting) and what happened to Parks (lost job, moved to Detroit, 1957).
- [ ] D6 Wider Alabama and Southern reaction in 1956: Graetz bombing, Autherine Lucy, the banning of the NAACP in Alabama, the Southern Manifesto.

Key personality: Martin Luther King Jr. (role in the boycott only; one block, every point)
- [ ] P1 Who he was: born 1929, Atlanta pastor's son, Boston University doctorate, pastor of Dexter Avenue Baptist Church from 1954.
- [ ] P2 What he did, as a direct participant: MIA president, Holt Street speech, arrests and trial, bomb, leading the car-pool and non-violent discipline, the first bus ride.
- [ ] P3 Shaped by events: chosen as a newcomer, made famous by the boycott; leader rather than originator; his thinking developed in Montgomery (Gandhi, Rustin and Smiley).
- [ ] P4 His writing: Stride Toward Freedom (1958).
- [ ] P5 Contemporaries: white officials called him an agitator; black supporters saw an inspiring leader.
- [ ] P6 Historians: Branch (King's growth into a leader); Morris and others (local networks existed before him).
- [ ] P7 Pointer only: his later career belongs to the notes on racial conflict and the civil rights movement.

Significance and assessment (final blocks)
- [ ] S1 Non-violence adopted as the movement's method; economic pressure shown to work.
- [ ] S2 Church and organisation as a model (MIA, then SCLC); a national leader produced; national and international publicity.
- [ ] S3 The role of women (Parks, Robinson, WPC) versus the male leaders' public recognition; Theoharis on Parks.
- [ ] S4 Who ended bus segregation: the courts (Browder) or the boycott? Both views.
- [ ] S5 Limits: only buses; violence followed; resistance hardened; real change came with the 1964 and 1965 laws.
- [ ] S6 Start of the civil rights movement or one phase of a longer struggle; contemporary versus later views.

## 3. Traps

Do not use (wrong or doubtful facts in the current notes):
- Do not use: "a black teenager was shot in the legs" after desegregation, because the incident is confused with the shooting of a black woman in December 1956 and a separate beating; say only that snipers fired at buses.
- Do not use: "several white men were arrested for the bombings, but they received little punishment" as a stated fact, because the outcomes of the trials are not certain to me; say only that the bombings followed.
- Do not use: that bus drivers "carried guns" as a general rule, because it is not certain; say drivers had police powers.
- Do not use: that E. D. Nixon "resented" King's rise, because it is not well attested.
- Do not use: "about twenty-four ministers" as a certain figure; say "many ministers".
- Do not use: "a boycott lasted 382 days" or "13 months" as the headline; the standard figure is 381 days.
- Do not use: the leaflet bullet's wording that the leaflets were produced the night of Parks' trial; they were run off on the night of her arrest (1 December).
- Do not use: that Parks' case, not Browder, went to the Supreme Court; her case stayed in the Alabama courts.
- Do not use: the keyTerm definition of "Discrimination" as a Montgomery event; define the term in general and then apply it to the buses.
- Do not use: Tallahassee (1956) as an established model without checking; leave it out.

Confusable people, terms and dates
- Rosa Parks (arrested 1 December 1955) vs Claudette Colvin (March 1955) vs Mary Louise Smith (October 1955). Only Colvin and Smith were plaintiffs in Browder v. Gayle.
- Arrest 1 December; trial and first boycott day 5 December; MIA founded the same day, 5 December; order served 20 December; buses desegregated 21 December.
- Browder v. Gayle (federal case on buses) vs Brown v. Board of Education (schools, 1954). Browder relied on Brown.
- The WPC (Robinson) vs the NAACP (Nixon, Parks) vs the MIA (King): three different organisations.
- The boycott was a one-day protest at first; it became a 381-day campaign after the Holt Street vote.
- King was the MIA's president but not the originator of the boycott; do not write that he began it.
- Montgomery bus boycott (1955–56) vs the "Montgomery to Selma" march (1965); do not mix them.

## 4. What exams ask

- Higher 100-mark essay (several years): why the boycott happened, how it was organised and run, and how it advanced civil rights, often with another case study in the same question. Marks go to causes (segregation, earlier protests, the WPC's readiness, Parks) separated from the trigger (the arrest), and to a clear judgement on importance.
- Document-based questions (2009 and 2021 style): part on how far it brought change, or its importance. Students must use the detail (dates, the ruling, the SCLC, what stayed segregated) to place a document and judge it; a balance of achievements and limits earns the top marks.
- Documents: students must say whether a source is primary or secondary, and what a city code, a speech by King or a letter from a lawyer such as Clifford Durr shows about intention, bias and usefulness. Know who wrote the documents and when.
- "How it was organised" answers need specifics: the MIA, the car pool with church dispatch points, the churches and ministers, the WPC leaflets, mass meetings; not just "they refused to ride the buses".
- King's role: elected MIA president on 5 December 1955, the Holt Street speech, non-violence, arrest, bomb; with a balanced view that he was a leader made by the boycott rather than its founder.
- Ordinary Level short answers: who, where and when (Montgomery, Alabama; 1 December 1955; 381 days), what the Women's Political Council and the MIA did, King's position, and what the car pool was. One correct dated fact earns a mark.
- Ordinary Level 40-mark paragraph: Parks' arrest, the one-day boycott on 5 December, the MIA with King as president, the car pool, white resistance, the Supreme Court ruling, desegregation on 21 December 1956. Each correct dated point earns marks.
- Significance: examiners reward a view on "start of the movement or one phase of a longer struggle" and on courts versus protest as the cause of success.
