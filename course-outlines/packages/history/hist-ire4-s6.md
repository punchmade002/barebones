## 1. Must cover (one bullet or block for every item; tick each off)

Background (assumed)
- [ ] A1 (assumed) Mass Irish emigration after 1840 (especially the Famine) meant large Catholic communities in the USA, Britain, Canada and Australia, most of them poor.
- [ ] A2 (assumed) Emigrants faced prejudice as poor Catholics (detail belongs to the notes on anti-Irish sentiment), so church and club offered shelter and community.
- [ ] A3 (assumed) The Ulster Protestant tradition: Presbyterians, Anglicans and Methodists, loyal to the Crown; Protestant emigration from Ulster began long before the Famine.

Key concepts (each defined as used in this topic; also in keyTerms)
- [ ] K1 Assimilation: emigrants and their children adopting the culture of the new country; set against preservation of Irish identity.
- [ ] K2 Discrimination: unequal treatment of Irish Catholics (here as the setting that made church and GAA important; do not develop it).
- [ ] K3 "Ireland's spiritual empire": only a passing link here; the concept belongs to the missionaries subsection.

The Catholic Church abroad
- [ ] C1 Irish dominance of the American hierarchy: why (largest Catholic group, English-speaking); John Hughes (archbishop of New York 1850); John McCloskey (first American cardinal, 1875).
- [ ] C2 Parishes and Irish-dedicated churches; St Patrick's Cathedral, New York (dedicated 1879).
- [ ] C3 Schools: the Third Plenary Council of Baltimore (1884) and the parochial school system.
- [ ] C4 Religious orders and sisters (Sisters of Mercy, Christian Brothers): schools, hospitals, charity.
- [ ] C5 Catholic fraternal societies (Ancient Order of Hibernians, Knights of Columbus): insurance, funerals, social life.
- [ ] C6 The parish as social centre; the priest as adviser and leader.
- [ ] C7 Britain: Irish priests in Liverpool and Glasgow; hierarchy restored 1850; Education (Scotland) Act 1918; Brother Walfrid and Celtic F.C. (1887); nuns and the poor.
- [ ] C8 Australia: Irish-born clergy; Irish orders staffing schools; Catholic school system built after state aid ended.
- [ ] C9 Canada: Irish Catholics in Montreal, Toronto, Ontario; St Patrick's Basilica, Montreal; Bishop Michael Power (died 1847); Newfoundland.
- [ ] C10 The church as a vehicle of both Irishness and assimilation (loyalty to the new country as well as faith).

Key personality: Archbishop Daniel Mannix (one block, every point)
- [ ] P1 Who he was: born Charleville, Co. Cork, 1864; priest and academic; president of Maynooth College 1903–1912.
- [ ] P2 Career in Melbourne: coadjutor archbishop 1912; archbishop 1917; died 1963 aged 99.
- [ ] P3 Conscription: opposition to conscription in the 1916 and 1917 referendums (both defeated); conflict with Prime Minister Billy Hughes and imperial loyalists.
- [ ] P4 Irish independence: support after 1916; the 1920 removal from his ship at sea and ban on landing in Ireland.
- [ ] P5 Sectarian conflict in Victoria, including with the Orange Order and Protestant bodies.
- [ ] P6 Later career: support for B. A. Santamaria's anti-communist Catholic "Movement" and the Labor split of 1955.
- [ ] P7 Shaped events or was shaped by them (his stand grew from Irish-Australian Catholic grievance and the First World War).
- [ ] P8 Contemporaries: admired by Irish Australians and many Catholics; denounced as disloyal by imperial loyalists and Protestant organisations.
- [ ] P9 Historians: a major figure in Australian Catholic and political life; debate over how far his nationalism was an Irish-Australian response to anti-Catholic prejudice.

The GAA abroad
- [ ] G1 Founding: Hayes's Hotel, Thurles, 1 November 1884; Michael Cusack and Maurice Davin among the founders.
- [ ] G2 Aims: Gaelic football, hurling and athletics as a distinctly Irish sporting culture; link with nationalism (the ban on "foreign" games, with no date given).
- [ ] G3 The 1888 American tour: exhibition matches; lost money; some stayed in America.
- [ ] G4 Clubs in New York, Chicago, Boston and other cities; New York as the main North American centre.
- [ ] G5 Britain (a London board in the 1890s) and Australia: clubs as a regular social life for emigrants.
- [ ] G6 The 1947 All-Ireland football final at the Polo Grounds, New York (Cavan v Kerry).

Protestant churches and Ulster-Scots identity
- [ ] U1 Ulster Presbyterians in Pennsylvania and the Appalachians in the 18th century; Francis Makemie.
- [ ] U2 "Scotch-Irish": the term, and its use to distinguish them from Catholic Irish, especially after the Famine.
- [ ] U3 Presbyterian and Methodist congregations in the USA and Canada: links with Irish churches and ministers.
- [ ] U4 Methodism: Philip Embury and Barbara Heck, Palatines from Co. Limerick, New York in the 1760s.
- [ ] U5 Ulster-Scots identity: Protestant descent, loyalty to the Crown, a heritage separate from Catholic Ireland.
- [ ] U6 Rapid assimilation of many Protestants; Irishness faded faster than among Catholics.
- [ ] U7 Canada: strong Presbyterian and Ulster-Scots identity in Ontario; tie to the British connection.

The Orange Order abroad
- [ ] O1 Origins: Co. Armagh, 1795, after the Battle of the Diamond.
- [ ] O2 Canada: lodges with soldiers and settlers; Grand Orange Lodge of British America (1830).
- [ ] O3 Political power in Toronto and Ontario: Orange mayors; "Belfast of Canada"; John A. Macdonald (Orangeman) and Mackenzie Bowell (Grand Master, prime minister 1894–96).
- [ ] O4 The Twelfth: parades marking the Boyne; display and occasional violence.
- [ ] O5 Australia: lodges in New South Wales from the 1840s, spreading to Victoria; opposed Catholic influence and Home Rule; clashes with Catholic institutions.
- [ ] O6 New York riots, 1870 and 1871: sectarian rivalry carried to America.

Assessment (paired judgement points, final block)
- [ ] S1 Achievement: Church and GAA gave emigrants community, social life and a lasting identity; the Church was the stronger of the two.
- [ ] S2 Limit: children assimilated; the institutions reached only part of the emigrant population.
- [ ] S3 Contemporary views: nationalists praised church and GAA as guardians of Irishness; nativists and imperial loyalists thought them divisive.
- [ ] S4 Later views: they preserved identity and also helped Irish communities organise and gain power.
- [ ] S5 Protestant and Orange identity: political influence in Canada and Australia, but expressing loyalty to the British connection, not Irish nationalism.
- [ ] S6 Sectarian rivalry was exported with the emigrants (Catholic v Orange in Canada, Australia, Britain, the USA).

## 3. Traps

Do not use (wrong or doubtful facts in the current notes):
- Do not use: "a rule formalised in 1905" for the GAA ban on foreign games, because the ban dates from the 1880s and its rule history is complicated (the rule was widened in 1901 and 1905). Say only that the GAA banned members from playing or attending "foreign" games.
- Do not use: "Gaelic Park in the Bronx opening in 1926", because the date is not certain. Say New York was the main centre.
- Do not use: Mannix "took a leading part" in both referendums equally; the notes should say he opposed conscription in both and was most prominent in 1917.
- Do not use: Methodism "spread in Canada by Embury and Heck" as a firm claim; say only they helped found the first Methodist society in New York in the 1760s.
- Do not use: the Knights of Columbus as an Irish society; it was a Catholic society founded by an Irish-American priest, with a mixed membership.
- Do not use: Scotch-Irish as a term used only from the 19th century; it was in use earlier, but became more common to distinguish Protestants from Catholics after the Famine.

## 4. What exams ask

- Higher 100-mark essay, usually "how far did the Church and the GAA sustain Irishness" or "role of churches and Orange Order". Marks go to a stated judgement from the start, with both achievement and limit points (assimilation of the second generation; institutions reaching only part of the emigrant population) and a comparison of Church (stronger, reached nearly every Catholic) with GAA (narrower, more male and sporting).
- Strong answers compare Catholic and Protestant experience: Catholic identity was institutional (parish, school, society) and lasted; Ulster-Scots identity was about descent, Presbyterianism and loyalty to the Crown, and faded faster through assimilation.
- Orange Order questions reward its Canadian political power (Toronto mayors, Macdonald, Bowell), the Twelfth, and the Australian side (opposition to Catholic influence and Home Rule); an answer that only describes Canada loses marks on Australia.
- Mannix is a short-answer and paragraph favourite. Marks go for correct dated career points (Charleville 1864, Maynooth president 1903–12, coadjutor 1912, archbishop 1917), conscription referendums, the 1920 ship incident, and one assessment point (admired by Catholics, called disloyal by loyalists). Each dated, correct statement earns a mark.
- Ordinary Level paragraphs on the GAA: founding place, date and founders; purpose; clubs abroad; the 1888 tour (lost money, some stayed). On the Church: priests and bishops dominating the American hierarchy; Baltimore 1884 parish schools; societies; orders running schools and hospitals.
- Combined questions pair Mannix with figures from other subsections; the answer must say what is distinctive about each person, so state Mannix's role as churchman, conscription opponent and nationalist supporter.
- Short sources or one-line questions test: who dominated the American hierarchy, what the GAA promoted, when the Orange Order was founded, who the Scotch-Irish were. Give each as a one-sentence definition in the notes.
