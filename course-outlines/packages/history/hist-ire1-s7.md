Background (assumed)
- [ ] A1 (assumed) The Penal Laws had left the Catholic majority poor and the Church of Ireland as the established church; Catholic Emancipation (1829) removed the main political barrier and freed the Church to expand.
- [ ] A2 (assumed) The Great Famine (1845–c.1850): heavy death and emigration, falling most on the poor, west and Irish-speaking areas.
- [ ] A3 (assumed) The Queen's Colleges (1845, opened 1849): non-denominational ("mixed") university colleges at Belfast, Cork and Galway; the education question for Catholics.
- [ ] A4 (assumed) Young Ireland and The Nation (see the notes on O'Connell, repeal and Young Ireland for politics).

Key concepts (also in keyTerms)
- [ ] K1 Ultramontanism: belief in strong papal authority over national churches and bishops; Cullen's guiding principle.
- [ ] K2 Evangelicalism: Protestant revival stressing personal conversion, Bible reading and missionary work.
- [ ] K3 Nation: a community of shared history, culture and territory (Young Ireland's definition, open to Protestants and Catholics).
- [ ] K4 Sectarianism: hostility between religious communities (Catholic/Protestant), shaping politics and everyday life.
- [ ] K5 Other terms: devotional revolution, Romanisation, Second Reformation, souperism, Apostolic Delegate, Synod of Thurles, cultural nationalism.

Identities
- [ ] C1 Catholic identity and the devotional revolution: Larkin's term; discipline and uniformity from the 1840s.
- [ ] C2 Church building after Emancipation (parish churches, cathedrals, paid for by the faithful).
- [ ] C3 Clergy: Maynooth (1795); better educated, under closer episcopal control.
- [ ] C4 Religious orders and congregations (Christian Brothers, Presentation Sisters, Sisters of Mercy, Sisters of Charity): schools, hospitals, orphanages.
- [ ] C5 New piety: parish missions (Redemptorists, Vincentians, Jesuits), rosary, novenas, confraternities, benediction, devotion to the Sacred Heart and the Virgin Mary.
- [ ] C6 Mass attendance (Larkin's claim) and explanations: the Famine's effect on the poor and on the farmer class; clerical authority; debate whether the change was imposed from above or grew from below.
- [ ] C7 Catholic identity tied to nationality.
- [ ] D1 Protestant identity: evangelical revival among Church of Ireland, Presbyterians and Methodists.
- [ ] D2 The Second Reformation (1820s–1850s): Bible societies, schools, missions; Achill (Nangle); Irish Church Missions.
- [ ] D3 Souperism in the Famine and the lasting bitterness.
- [ ] D4 The Orange Order: founded 1795, dissolved 1836, revived 1840s; loyalty to Crown, Union and Protestant ascendancy.
- [ ] D5 Henry Cooke and the Presbyterian–Anglican alliance against O'Connell; the Ulster revival of 1859.
- [ ] D6 The Church of Ireland: established church until disestablishment (1871), serving a minority.
- [ ] N1 Young Ireland's idea of the nation; The Nation (1842) and its poems, ballads and history.

- [ ] N3 Decline of the Irish language: O'Connell's attitude, the 1851 census, regional pattern, the Famine, national schools in English, parents' choice.

Case study: the Synod of Thurles, 1850, and the Romanisation of the Catholic Church
- [ ] T1 Background: no national synod for about two centuries; diocesan independence; varied practice.
- [ ] T2 Roman aims: uniformity, an Irish policy, resistance to mixed education (papal rescript of 1847).
- [ ] T3 Divided bishops: MacHale (Tuam) against the colleges; Murray (Dublin) more accommodating.
- [ ] T4 Cullen's appointment (Armagh, then Apostolic Delegate) to carry out Roman policy.
- [ ] T5 The Synod: dates, place, who presided and attended.
- [ ] T6 Decrees: condemnation of the Queen's Colleges; ban on clergy taking posts; Catholic University ordered; uniform liturgy (Roman rite) and discipline; regulation of clergy and sacraments; bishops' authority over priests; discouraging priests' political agitation.
- [ ] T7 Opposition: MacHale, liberal clergy and lay Catholics; Rome's support and confirmation.
- [ ] T8 Outcomes: ultramontanism and Romanisation; centralised Church; dominant position of Cullen.
- [ ] T9 The Catholic University: Newman, 1854, limited success, no recognised degrees; Catholics kept going to Trinity and the Queen's Colleges; later campaign for state support.
- [ ] T10 Significance and views: contemporaries (Protestants and liberal Catholics vs supporters); historians (Larkin: start of the devotional revolution; others: change already under way).

## 3. Traps

Do not use (wrong or doubtful facts in the current notes):
- Do not use: Cullen "Archbishop of Armagh in 1850" as the appointment date, because the notes contradict themselves (1849 and 1850). He was appointed in December 1849; 1850 is the year he became Apostolic Delegate and held the Synod.
- Do not use: the Ordnance Survey "directed by Thomas Larcom", because the director was Thomas Colby; Larcom headed the Irish topographical work. Say "under Larcom's organisation".
- Do not use: Davis "edited the Library of Ireland series", because the series was Duffy's, begun just after Davis's death. Say Davis urged the study of history and language.
- Do not use: a precise Mass attendance percentage as settled fact, because historians dispute it; attribute to Larkin, with "about".
- Do not use: Aikenhead's order as "the first Irish order working with the poor" without hedging, because other orders (Presentation Sisters, Sisters of Mercy) also worked with the poor; say "an early", or "described as the first devoted to work outside the convent".
- Do not use: the date 1802 for the Christian Brothers as a founding of the order; say Edmund Rice began his school in Waterford in 1802.

## 4. What exams ask

- Higher 100-mark essay, the commonest form: Cullen's influence on political attitudes and religious practice. Marks go to separating religious practice (Synod, Romanisation, devotions, discipline, orders, missions) from political attitudes (distrust of Young Ireland and Fenians, priests out of politics, support for the Liberals), with dated examples and a judgement at the start.
- Paired-figure essays, for example Cullen with Aikenhead, or Carleton, Davis and Cullen. Marks go to a clear contribution for each (who, what, when), a contrast in how each shaped Irish society, and a comparison, not two separate summaries.
- Creative arts and/or science: select at least three named contributors, each with a work or achievement and a date (Moore, Mangan, Carleton, Petrie, O'Donovan, O'Curry; Hamilton, Rosse, Callan, Boole), and say what they showed about Irish culture or capacity despite poverty. A bare list earns little.
- Development of religious and cultural identities: cover both Catholic and Protestant identity and the Young Ireland nation, with sectarianism as a link; a one-sided Catholic answer is limited.
- Ordinary Level 30/40-mark paragraphs on a person: Cullen (Kildare, Rome, Armagh, Delegate, Thurles, Dublin 1852, cardinal 1866); Aikenhead (Cork 1787, Sisters of Charity 1815, St Vincent's 1834); Carleton (Prillisk 1794, Traits and Stories, other works); Davis (Mallow, The Nation, ballads, died 1845). Each correct dated fact earns marks; name works and places.
- Synod of Thurles short answers: when, where, who presided, why it was held (unify the Church, resist the Queen's Colleges), and two decrees. Strong answers add the Catholic University and give a result.
- Short questions: define ultramontanism, evangelicalism, nation or sectarianism in a sentence with an Irish example; explain "devotional revolution".
- Contemporary vs later views earn marks as pairs: Protestants and liberal Catholics against papal control, against supporters' renewal; Larkin against historians who see earlier change.
