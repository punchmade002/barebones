## 1. Must cover (one bullet or block for every item; tick each off)

Background (assumed)
- [ ] A1 (assumed) Northern Ireland was created in 1921; the Ulster Unionist Party governed at Stormont continuously, so the state was Protestant-unionist in character; Catholics (about one third of the population) were mostly nationalist.
- [ ] A2 (assumed) Discrimination as the Catholic minority experienced it: housing allocation and local jobs controlled by unionist councils, the restricted local-government franchise, gerrymandered boundaries (especially Derry), the Special Powers Act and the B Specials/RUC.
- [ ] A3 (assumed) Brookeborough's premiership (1943–63) left these grievances in place; the welfare state had produced a more educated Catholic middle class (see the notes on Brookeborough's Northern Ireland). Only a brief link back.

Key concepts (each defined as used in this topic; also in keyTerms)
- [ ] K1 Civil Rights: equal treatment of Catholics in voting, housing and jobs, sought by peaceful protest and appeals to British opinion.
- [ ] K2 Gerrymandering: drawing electoral boundaries to give one group an advantage; the movement's target in local government, above all in Derry.
- [ ] K3 Tolerance and intolerance: willingness or refusal to accept other beliefs and identities; the unionist/nationalist divide and Paisley's opposition to reform.
- [ ] K4 Bridge-building (O'Neill's policy; essential term).
- [ ] K5 One man, one vote (the franchise demand; essential term).

O'Neill's modernisation and bridge-building
- [ ] O1 O'Neill becomes Prime Minister (March 1963); previously Minister of Finance.
- [ ] O2 Economic planning: Matthew Report (1963), Wilson Plan (1965), the new city (Craigavon, Co. Armagh).
- [ ] O3 Symbolic gestures to Catholics (visits to Catholic schools and hospitals) and resentment from unionist hardliners.
- [ ] O4 The Lemass meetings: Lemass at Stormont (14 January 1965); O'Neill in Dublin (February 1965); the cabinet kept in the dark.
- [ ] O5 Reactions: Nationalist Party under McAteer accepts the role of official opposition (1965); Catholics welcome gestures but see no change in housing, jobs or voting.

Unionist critics
- [ ] U1 Paisley's background: born Armagh 1926; Free Presbyterian Church (1951); strict Protestant doctrine, anti-Catholic and anti-ecumenical.
- [ ] U2 Paisley's campaign: opposition to the Lemass meeting; "O'Neill must go" from 1966; Ulster Constitution Defence Committee and Ulster Protestant Volunteers (1966); jailed for three months (1966).
- [ ] U3 Hardliners in the party and cabinet: William Craig; the UVF murders of 1966 as the violent edge of loyalism.
- [ ] U4 The effect: O'Neill squeezed between reformers and hardliners.

Key personality: Ian Paisley (part 1, to 1969 only)
- [ ] PA1 Who he was: minister, born Armagh 1926, founder of the Free Presbyterian Church (1951).
- [ ] PA2 What he did to 1969: rallies, protests, opposition to Lemass, the "O'Neill must go" campaign, the 1966 jail term, the Armagh counter-demonstration of 30 November 1968, and the 1969 Bannside contest.
- [ ] PA3 Shaped events or was shaped by them: mobilised grassroots Protestant opinion and restricted O'Neill's room to reform; his stand grew with each concession.
- [ ] PA4 Participant: his own campaign and, through his followers, the Armagh blocking (30 November 1968) and the Bannside contest (February 1969).
- [ ] PA5 Contemporaries: seen by many unionists as defender of the Union and Protestant identity, by moderates as an embarrassment and by nationalists as a bigot.
- [ ] PA6 Historians: his campaign pulled unionism towards the harder line of the 1970s. Pointer only: his later career (DUP, religion and ecumenism, power sharing) is in later notes.

The Campaign for Social Justice and Dungannon
- [ ] M1 The McCluskeys: Dr Conn McCluskey and Patricia McCluskey; Dungannon Homeless Citizens League (1963).
- [ ] M2 Campaign for Social Justice founded January 1964: evidence of discrimination in housing and jobs (Tyrone, Fermanagh), published and sent to MPs at Westminster.
- [ ] M3 Result: lobbying of British MPs and the Campaign for Democracy in Ulster (1965).
- [ ] M4 Method and image: constitutional, documentary, moderate; unionists called it a republican front; the McCluskeys denied it.

Key personality: Conn and Patricia McCluskey (one block, every point)
- [ ] MC1 Who they were; what they did, with dates (1963 league, 1964 Campaign); participants.
- [ ] MC2 How they shaped events: provided evidence and a model, and were ahead of NICRA.
- [ ] MC3 Contemporary views (unionists vs nationalists/British MPs) and historians' view.

NICRA and its demands
- [ ] N1 NICRA founded January 1967: who joined (nationalists, republicans, socialists, trade unionists, some liberal Protestants).
- [ ] N2 Demands: one man, one vote in local elections; an end to gerrymandering; fair allocation of houses; laws against discrimination in public bodies; repeal of the Special Powers Act; disbanding of the B Specials.
- [ ] N3 The franchise: Westminster elections already universal; local elections limited to ratepayers and spouses, with a company vote.
- [ ] N4 Why the movement emerged now: the educated Catholic generation, the example of the American civil rights movement, Labour in power in Britain, O'Neill's raised hopes, the McCluskeys' evidence.
- [ ] N5 Caledon (June 1968): Austin Currie's squat in a council house given to a young single Protestant.
- [ ] N6 First march: Coalisland to Dungannon (24 August 1968).
- [ ] N7 Methods: peaceful marches and sit-ins; counter-demonstrations and bans; Armagh (30 November 1968).

Derry, 5 October 1968 and the response
- [ ] D1 Derry as flashpoint: Catholic majority, unionist control of the corporation (gerrymandering), housing, jobs and a sense of neglect.
- [ ] D2 The march: NICRA, banned by William Craig; the route and defiance; RUC batons; Gerry Fitt struck; television pictures.
- [ ] D3 Immediate results: riots in Derry; British and international attention; the Derry Citizens' Action Committee (Hume vice-chairman) to keep protests peaceful; the radicalisation of students.
- [ ] D4 British pressure: meeting of O'Neill with Wilson in November 1968.
- [ ] D5 The five-point package (22 November 1968): housing points system; ombudsman; development commission replacing Londonderry Corporation; Special Powers Act to be withdrawn when possible; end of the company vote. One man, one vote not conceded.
- [ ] D6 "Ulster at the crossroads" broadcast (9 December 1968): appeal for moderation; backing from many moderates; hardliners angry. Craig dismissed (December 1968).

People's Democracy, Burntollet and the crisis of 1969
- [ ] B1 People's Democracy formed (October 1968) at Queen's, Belfast: students, Devlin among them; its aims.
- [ ] B2 The march from Belfast to Derry (began 1 January 1969) and the ambush at Burntollet Bridge (4 January 1969) by loyalists including off-duty B Specials; RUC failure to protect it.
- [ ] B3 Derry that night: RUC in the Bogside; barricades and "Free Derry".
- [ ] B4 Effect: loss of Catholic confidence in the RUC; the movement more confrontational; the split in the movement (NICRA vs PD) in early 1969, briefly.
- [ ] B5 The February 1969 Stormont election (24 February): unionist split (pro- and anti-O'Neill unionists); O'Neill holds Bannside against Paisley by 1,414 votes.
- [ ] B6 Bombs at water and electricity installations (March–April 1969), blamed at the time on the IRA, later shown to be loyalist (UVF).
- [ ] B7 Mid Ulster by-election (17 April 1969): Devlin elected.
- [ ] B8 Party and cabinet crisis over one man, one vote: accepted in April 1969.
- [ ] B9 O'Neill resigns (28 April 1969); Chichester-Clark succeeds, beating Faulkner by one vote.
- [ ] B10 What followed, as a pointer only: the Troubles (see the notes on the outbreak of the Troubles).

Key personality: Terence O'Neill (one block, every point)
- [ ] ON1 Who he was: born London 1914, landed Antrim family (Shane's Castle), Eton, Stormont minister, Finance 1956–63, Prime Minister 1963.
- [ ] ON2 What he did, with dates: planning, Lemass meetings, five-point package, broadcast, resignation.
- [ ] ON3 Shaped by events: pressure from London, the movement and his own party; reforms step by step.
- [ ] ON4 Participant: as Prime Minister he decided and spoke; as witness to the crisis he later wrote and gave interviews (briefly).
- [ ] ON5 Contemporaries: hardliners saw betrayal; nationalists saw gestures but too slow; British opinion backed him.
- [ ] ON6 Historians: modernising planner and sincere but limited reformer; reform on his terms, from above; hampered by his party; a debate over failure or success.
- [ ] ON7 After 1969: life peer 1970.

Key personality: Bernadette Devlin (one block, every point)
- [ ] DE1 Who she was: born Cookstown 1947, psychology student at Queen's.
- [ ] DE2 What she did, with dates: People's Democracy 1968, the Belfast to Derry march 1969, Mid Ulster election April 1969 aged twenty-one, youngest woman MP at the time.
- [ ] DE3 Shaped events and was shaped: carried civil rights to Westminster; the movement shaped her.
- [ ] DE4 Participant: in the march and in the campaign; memoir *The Price of My Soul* (1969).
- [ ] DE5 Contemporaries: a symbol of youth and defiance to supporters, an agitator to unionists.
- [ ] DE6 Historians: a key figure who took the movement into Westminster politics.
- [ ] DE7 Later events briefly: incitement to riot (Battle of the Bogside, August 1969, jailed 1970); Maudling incident (31 January 1972); lost seat 1974; shot 1981.

Assessment (paired judgement points, final block)
- [ ] S1 O'Neill's successes: modernisation, bridge-building, reforms conceded under pressure.
- [ ] S2 O'Neill's limits: too slow for Catholics and the movement; too fast for his party; no tackling of discrimination until forced; lost control.
- [ ] S3 The movement's successes: put discrimination on the British agenda; secured reforms (points system, ombudsman, one man, one vote).
- [ ] S4 The movement's limits: provoked loyalist reaction, bans, violence; radicalised; its leaders divided.
- [ ] S5 Contemporary vs later views (unionists, nationalists, historians).
- [ ] S6 Overall: reform from above met resistance from below and a unionist backlash; the crisis led to the Troubles.

## 3. Traps

Do not use (wrong or doubtful facts in the current notes):
- Do not use: "the Wilson Plan led to the new city of Craigavon", because the new city came from the Matthew Report's proposals; the Wilson Plan was economic. Say the Matthew Report and the Wilson Plan both shaped planning, with Craigavon coming from the Matthew proposals.
- Do not use: "the first meeting between the heads of the two governments since partition" as flat fact, because earlier contacts existed in the early 1920s. Write "often described as".
- Do not use: Craig was dismissed "after he spoke in favour of a unilateral declaration of independence" as the stated cause, because the reasons are disputed; say he was dismissed in December 1968 after clashing with O'Neill over policy.
- Do not use: "reviewing the wider franchise" as part of the five points, because only the company vote was promised to end; the main demand, one man one vote, was not conceded.
- Do not use: "a few dozen marchers" for the PD march; use "about 40 at the start", and no figure for those attacked or injured.
- Do not use: the claim that O'Neill "did not see Catholic grievances as primarily political" as fact; give it as a historian's view.
- Do not use: "O'Neill's party accepted one man, one vote only in April 1969" without saying it was the parliamentary party, after the cabinet had debated it.
- The Wilson who met O'Neill (Harold Wilson, Prime Minister) is not Thomas Wilson, the economist of the Wilson Plan.

Doubtful quotations: do not use any quotation of O'Neill other than the broadcast title; do not quote Paisley or Devlin; the "Free Derry" slogan may be named as a slogan.

## 4. What exams ask

- Higher 100-mark essay, the commonest form: how effective or successful was O'Neill as Prime Minister (modernisation, bridge-building, reform), often by comparison with Brookeborough or Paisley. Marks go to a clear judgement stated early and sustained: balanced points on what he achieved (planning, Lemass meeting, five points, one man one vote) against what he failed to do (reform in time, hold his party, prevent violence), with dates.
- Essay on why the Civil Rights movement emerged and how far it succeeded: causes (discrimination in housing, jobs, voting; the educated Catholic generation; the McCluskeys; the American example; O'Neill's raised expectations) and results (reforms conceded, loyalist backlash, the slide to violence). Both halves matter.
- Essay on a pair or trio of personalities (Paisley and O'Neill; Devlin, Paisley and Heaney; Devlin and Hume): students need a sphere of influence and a clear verdict for each, not a biography. Each person needs a date and a deed.
- Ordinary Level personality paragraph (30 or 40 marks): O'Neill, the McCluskeys, Devlin, Paisley. Marks for who they were, specific dated actions, and one point on the effect. Each correct, relevant, dated statement earns marks, so list dates (for the McCluskeys: 1963, January 1964; for Devlin: birth year, People's Democracy, Burntollet, the April 1969 election).
- Documents or short answers (6 marks): identify the speaker or organisation (O'Neill as Unionist Party leader), and what a text shows about a viewpoint (for example NICRA's demands, the Lemass visit, a unionist objection).
- Better answers separate the aims of the movement from its results, and separate O'Neill's intentions from what he achieved; they use evidence from both sides (hardline unionists, nationalists) rather than telling the story.
- Common mistakes: saying the five points included one man one vote; saying Burntollet was a NICRA march; confusing the Derry march (5 October 1968) with Bloody Sunday; attributing Craigavon to the Wilson Plan alone.
