## 1. Must cover (one bullet or block for every item; tick each off)

Background (assumed)
- [ ] A1 (assumed) The Second World War left Western Europe devastated and dependent on the USA; France and Germany had fought three wars in seventy years (1870, 1914, 1939).
- [ ] A2 (assumed) The Cold War division of Europe: the Iron Curtain, the Soviet bloc in the East, NATO (1949) in the West; integration in this period was Western European only.
- [ ] A3 (assumed) Marshall Aid (1948–52) and the OEEC (1948) made the USA a sponsor of European cooperation: one bullet only, as background.
- [ ] A4 (assumed) The Federal Republic of Germany (West Germany) was founded in 1949; how to rebuild and rearm it safely was the "German question" behind the Schuman Plan and the EDC.

Key concepts and essential terms (each defined as used in this topic; also in keyTerms)
- [ ] K1 Common market: free movement of goods, services, capital and people inside the area, with a common external tariff; also the popular name for the EEC.
- [ ] K2 Customs union (essential term): no tariffs between members and a common tariff on outside goods; the first stage of the common market.
- [ ] K3 Federal Europe: a united Europe with a central, supranational government above national governments; the federalists' aim.
- [ ] K4 Supranational (essential term): institutions whose decisions bind member states (the High Authority, the Commission, the Court of Justice).
- [ ] K5 Intergovernmental (essential term): cooperation decided by national governments, each keeping a veto (the Council of Europe, EFTA, de Gaulle's Europe of states, Thatcher's view).
- [ ] K6 The Six (essential term): France, West Germany, Italy, Belgium, the Netherlands and Luxembourg.
- [ ] K7 Qualified majority voting (essential term): decisions in the Council of Ministers by a weighted majority rather than unanimity; extended by the Single European Act.
- [ ] K8 Single market (essential term): the completion of the common market by removing physical, technical and fiscal barriers (the 1992 programme).

Key personality: Jean Monnet (one block, headed "Key personality: Jean Monnet")
- [ ] P1 Who he was: born in Cognac (1888); the family brandy business; international finance; deputy secretary-general of the League of Nations (1919–23).
- [ ] P2 Second World War: Anglo-French supply coordination; the proposal for Franco-British union (June 1940), not taken up; work on supplies in Washington.
- [ ] P3 The Monnet Plan: head of the French planning commission from 1946; modernisation of French industry by state planning and investment, helped by Marshall Aid.
- [ ] P4 The Schuman Declaration (9 May 1950): Monnet drafted it, Schuman announced it; his method of uniting Europe sector by sector through "concrete achievements".
- [ ] P5 The ECSC: first president of the High Authority (1952–55); resigned to campaign for further integration.
- [ ] P6 The Action Committee for the United States of Europe (1955): pressed for Euratom, the EEC and later British entry.
- [ ] P7 Never elected to office: a planner and persuader who worked through politicians; called a "father of Europe"; died 1979.
- [ ] P8 Contemporaries: admired by federalists and by American leaders; opposed by de Gaulle and the Gaullists, who rejected supranational institutions.
- [ ] P9 Historians: the architect of the "Monnet method" (gradual, functional integration); debate over how much credit he deserves compared with the national governments that took the decisions.

Key personality: Jacques Delors (one block, headed "Key personality: Jacques Delors")
- [ ] P10 Who he was: born in Paris (1925); Banque de France and French planning; a socialist influenced by Catholic social thought; MEP (1979–81); French finance minister under Mitterrand (1981–84).
- [ ] P11 President of the European Commission, January 1985 – January 1995 (three terms, ten years).
- [ ] P12 The single market: the 1985 White Paper (about 300 measures) and the Single European Act (1986), with the target of the end of 1992.
- [ ] P13 The social dimension: the Social Charter (1989), which Britain refused to sign; his 1988 speech to the British TUC.
- [ ] P14 Economic and monetary union: the Delors Report (1989) and its three stages; a participant in the Maastricht negotiations (1991) and Treaty (1992).
- [ ] P15 Contemporaries: credited with relaunching the Community after the "Eurosclerosis" of the 1970s and early 1980s; Thatcher saw him as the leader of a drive to a European super-state; British tabloid hostility.
- [ ] P16 Historians: one of the most influential Commission presidents; the debate over whether his federal ambitions outran what member states and voters wanted (the Danish "no" of 1992).

Reasons for unity, 1945–1950
- [ ] R1 Peace: ending Franco-German rivalry; tying West Germany into the West.
- [ ] R2 The Soviet threat and the Cold War: unity as strength; American encouragement.
- [ ] R3 Economic recovery: larger markets, efficiency, the example of Benelux.
- [ ] R4 Ideas: federalists (Spinelli, the Ventotene Manifesto, 1941) and Christian Democrat leaders (Schuman, Adenauer, De Gasperi); the decline of European power in the world.
- [ ] R5 Churchill's Zurich speech (September 1946): "a kind of United States of Europe", with Britain outside as a friend and sponsor.

Early cooperation, 1948–1949
- [ ] E1 Benelux customs union (in force 1948).
- [ ] E2 The Hague Congress (May 1948): federalists vs unionists.
- [ ] E3 The Council of Europe (5 May 1949): ten founders, Strasbourg, a consultative assembly without law-making power; why federalists were disappointed (Spaak's resignation, 1951).
- [ ] E4 The European Convention on Human Rights (signed 1950, in force 1953) and the European Court of Human Rights: the Council of Europe's main achievement.

The Schuman Plan and the ECSC
- [ ] S1 The Schuman Declaration (9 May 1950): what it proposed and why coal and steel (war industries; the Ruhr and the Saar).
- [ ] S2 Responses: Adenauer's welcome (equality for West Germany, end of international control of the Ruhr); Italy and Benelux join; Britain (the Attlee government) refuses because of the supranational authority.
- [ ] S3 The Treaty of Paris (18 April 1951; in force July 1952): the Six and the four institutions (High Authority, Common Assembly, Council of Ministers, Court of Justice).
- [ ] S4 Results: a common market in coal and steel; the institutional model for the EEC; Franco-German reconciliation in practice.

The failure of the EDC
- [ ] D1 The Pleven Plan (October 1950): a European army so that West Germany could rearm without a national army; the Korean War as the trigger.
- [ ] D2 The EDC Treaty (May 1952) and the plan for a European Political Community.
- [ ] D3 Rejection by the French National Assembly (30 August 1954): Gaullists and communists, fear of German rearmament, loss of sovereignty over the army, Britain's refusal to join, Stalin's death easing the threat.
- [ ] D4 Results: West Germany rearmed through the Western European Union and NATO (1955); integration turned back to economics; defence stayed national and Atlantic.

The relaunch and the Treaties of Rome
- [ ] T1 Messina (June 1955) and the Spaak Report (1956).
- [ ] T2 The Treaties of Rome (25 March 1957; in force 1 January 1958): the EEC and Euratom.
- [ ] T3 The EEC's aims: customs union and common external tariff, the "four freedoms", common policies (agriculture, transport), "ever closer union" as the long-term aim.
- [ ] T4 Institutions: Commission, Council of Ministers, Assembly (Parliament), Court of Justice; Walter Hallstein first Commission president (1958–67); the Merger Treaty (in force 1967).
- [ ] T5 Euratom: civil nuclear cooperation; less important than the EEC.

Evolution of the EEC, 1958–1973
- [ ] V1 The Common Agricultural Policy (from 1962): guaranteed prices, protection, export subsidies; benefits to farmers, food security; costs (the largest share of the budget, surpluses).
- [ ] V2 The customs union completed (1 July 1968), ahead of schedule; rapid growth in trade among the Six.
- [ ] V3 EFTA (1960): the seven, a free-trade area without supranational institutions; Britain's alternative; its weakness.
- [ ] V4 Britain's (and Ireland's and Denmark's) first applications (1961) and de Gaulle's veto (14 January 1963), with his reasons.
- [ ] V5 The second application and second veto (1967).
- [ ] V6 The empty chair crisis (from June 1965) and the Luxembourg Compromise (January 1966).
- [ ] V7 De Gaulle's Europe of states versus Monnet's supranational Europe; the Franco-German (Élysée) Treaty of 1963.
- [ ] V8 After de Gaulle (resigned 1969): the Hague summit (December 1969): completion, deepening, enlargement.

Enlargement and evolution, 1973–1992
- [ ] N1 The first enlargement (1 January 1973): Britain, Ireland, Denmark; Norway's voters reject membership (1972); Ireland's 1972 referendum; Britain's 1975 referendum.
- [ ] N2 The Mediterranean enlargements: Greece (1981), Spain and Portugal (1986): support for new democracies after dictatorship; twelve members.
- [ ] N3 Regional policy: the European Regional Development Fund (1975) and later the structural funds; benefits to poorer regions, including Ireland.
- [ ] N4 Direct elections to the European Parliament (June 1979).
- [ ] N5 The European Monetary System and the Exchange Rate Mechanism (March 1979); Britain stays out until 1990; Ireland joins and breaks the link with sterling.
- [ ] N6 "Eurosclerosis": slow progress during the recession after the oil crisis (one bullet; the oil crisis itself belongs elsewhere).
- [ ] N7 The British budget question and the Fontainebleau settlement (June 1984) (one bullet; detail in the notes on Thatcher).
- [ ] N8 The Single European Act (signed February 1986; in force July 1987): the 1992 single market, more qualified majority voting, more power for Parliament, foreign-policy cooperation; Ireland's 1987 referendum.
- [ ] N9 German reunification (3 October 1990) brought the former East Germany into the Community (one bullet; reunification itself belongs elsewhere).
- [ ] N10 The Maastricht Treaty (7 February 1992): the European Union and its three pillars, EMU and a single currency by 1999 at the latest, European citizenship, British opt-outs; the Danish "no" (June 1992) and the Irish "yes" (June 1992).

Attitudes to unity
- [ ] U1 Federalists (Spinelli, Spaak, Monnet, Hallstein, Delors) vs intergovernmentalists (de Gaulle, Thatcher, and British governments generally).
- [ ] U2 Thatcher's attitude: support for the single market but opposition to federalism, the social dimension and a single currency; the Bruges speech (20 September 1988) (one bullet; detail in the notes on Thatcher).
- [ ] U3 Britain as the "awkward partner": late entry, the referendum, the budget, opt-outs.

Effects of unity (examined often)
- [ ] F1 Economic: growth of trade, larger markets and investment; the CAP's effect on farmers and food prices; regional funds.
- [ ] F2 Political: Franco-German reconciliation and lasting peace in Western Europe; democracy consolidated in Greece, Spain and Portugal; a stronger voice in world trade.
- [ ] F3 Social: free movement of workers; common rules on working conditions and equal pay (one phrase; women's rights belong elsewhere); new opportunities for countries such as Ireland.
- [ ] F4 Limits: Western Europe only; no common defence; national vetoes; the CAP's costs and surpluses; a "democratic deficit".

Assessment block
- [ ] X1 Assessment: how far Europe moved towards unity, 1945–1992 (achievements vs limits as paired points); federal Europe or a Europe of states; contemporary vs later views of Monnet and Delors.

## 3. Traps

Do not use (wrong or doubtful facts in the current notes):
- Do not use: the ECSC High Authority "could set prices and quotas ... without needing the approval of member governments", because production quotas and some price controls needed the Council of Ministers' assent in a declared crisis; say it could take binding decisions, raise a levy on production and fine firms.
- Do not use: the Luxembourg Compromise "gave each member state a veto" as a legal right, because it was an informal political agreement to disagree, not a treaty change; say that in practice it allowed a state to block decisions affecting very important national interests, so unanimity became the norm.
- Do not use: Ireland's applications "were halted by de Gaulle's vetoes", because de Gaulle vetoed Britain, not Ireland; Ireland's applications lapsed because Irish trade depended on the British market and Ireland would not join without Britain.
- Do not use: Delors's presidency was "the longest tenure in the Commission's history", because a later president (José Manuel Barroso, 2004–14) served as long; say he led the Commission for ten years and three terms.
- Do not use: "Konrad Adenauer welcomed the plan because it ... ended the international control of the Ruhr" as an immediate effect: the International Authority for the Ruhr was wound up when the ECSC began (1952); say it offered an end to it.
- Do not use: "the Messina Conference ... relaunched the project" with Monnet as its organiser: Messina was a meeting of the six foreign ministers, prepared by the Benelux governments (the Benelux memorandum) and chaired by Italy; Monnet worked through his Action Committee.

Other errors and doubtful claims to avoid:
- Don't put "Europe des patries" in quotation marks as de Gaulle's phrase: he denied using it. Say he wanted a Europe of states or nations.
- Don't quote Thatcher's "I want my money back": the wording is disputed. Say she demanded a refund of what she called Britain's own money.
- Don't give percentages for the CAP's share of the budget, for trade growth or for structural-fund transfers to Ireland: figures vary by year and source.
- Don't say the Treaties of Rome created the European Union or the single currency; the EU dates from Maastricht (1992, in force 1993) and the euro from 1999 (notes and coins 2002, outside this period).
- Don't say the Council of Europe is an EEC institution, or confuse it with the European Council (meetings of heads of government, formalised from 1974–75) or the Council of Ministers.
- Don't say Churchill wanted Britain to join a united Europe; at Zurich he saw Britain as a friend and sponsor outside it.
- Don't say the European Court of Human Rights is an EEC court; the EEC's court is the Court of Justice in Luxembourg.
- Don't say Britain joined the EEC in 1975; it joined in 1973 and confirmed membership by referendum in 1975.
- Don't say Spain or Portugal joined in 1981; Greece alone joined in 1981.
- Don't say Thatcher opposed the single market; she supported it and signed the Single European Act, but opposed what she saw as federalism, the social dimension and monetary union.
- Don't say the EDC was rejected by the French government; it was the National Assembly, after the French government itself had proposed the plan.

## 4. What exams ask

- Why the EEC was established and how it evolved (Higher 100, frequent): marks go for causes (peace between France and Germany, the Soviet threat, recovery, American encouragement, federalist ideas) linked to the ECSC → EDC failure → Messina → Rome chain, then evolution in phases (1958–69 building the customs union and CAP; de Gaulle's vetoes and the empty chair; 1969–73 relaunch and enlargement; 1970s–early 80s Eurosclerosis but EMS and direct elections; 1985–92 Delors, the SEA and Maastricht). Answers that show change as uneven (advances and setbacks) score higher than a list of treaties.
- How far Europe moved towards unity (Higher 100, in 1945–57 or 1945–73 or to 1992 forms): a "how far" answer needs a judgement in the opening line and paired points: economic unity went far (customs union, CAP, single market) but political and defence unity did not (EDC failure, national vetoes, NATO), and unity was confined to Western Europe; supranational in coal, steel and trade, intergovernmental in most else.
- Effects of unity (Higher 100, often paired with Marshall Aid or the oil crisis): separate economic effects (trade, markets, farming under the CAP, regional funds) from social and political effects (free movement, reconciliation, democracy in the south, Ireland's modernisation). Balance benefits with costs (CAP surpluses and food prices, loss of sovereignty, democratic deficit).
- Attitudes of Monnet, Thatcher, Delors and others to unity (Higher 100): contrast federal/supranational (Monnet, Delors) with intergovernmental (de Gaulle, Thatcher); show each person's actions, not just views (Monnet's Plan and High Authority; Delors's SEA, Social Charter and EMU plan; Thatcher's rebate, Bruges speech and opt-out stance). Judgement on who was most influential earns marks.
- Monnet or Delors as one of several personalities (Higher 100) and as a short Ordinary Level answer (30 or 40 marks, very frequent): give the career in order with dates (Monnet: League 1919–23, Plan 1946, Declaration 1950, High Authority 1952–55, Action Committee 1955; Delors: finance minister 1981–84, Commission 1985–95, SEA 1986, Delors Report 1989, Maastricht 1992) and end with his significance. One specific fact per point.
- Ordinary Level short questions: identifying Monnet as a founder of European unity; naming the treaty that created the EEC and its year; naming the Six or the 1973 entrants; identifying a hostile speech or press reaction to Europe by its British context. Short answers need one exact fact each.
- Assessment material examiners reward: achievements (peace between old enemies, a customs union and single market, growth from six to twelve, democracy anchored in the south) vs limits (no common defence, de Gaulle's vetoes and the empty chair, the CAP's costs, Britain's reluctance, Western Europe only); contemporary views (federal hopes vs fears of a super-state) vs later views (integration as compromises between national governments rather than the triumph of the Monnet method).
