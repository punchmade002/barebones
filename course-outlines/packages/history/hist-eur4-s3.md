## 1. Must cover (one bullet or block for every item; tick each off)

Background (assumed)
- [ ] A1 (assumed) Hungary had been a German ally in the Second World War and was occupied by the Red Army in 1944–45; Soviet troops stayed afterwards.
- [ ] A2 (assumed) The communist takeover of 1945–49 by salami tactics, ending in the one-party People's Republic (1949): one bullet only, as background.
- [ ] A3 (assumed) The Warsaw Pact (May 1955) bound Hungary militarily to the Soviet Union; Austria became neutral in 1955 and Soviet troops left it, which made neutrality look possible to Hungarians.
- [ ] A4 (assumed) Stalin's death (March 1953) and the uncertain collective leadership in Moscow that followed.

Key concepts and essential terms (each defined as used in this topic; also in keyTerms)
- [ ] K1 Satellite state: formally independent but controlled by the Soviet Union in politics, economy and defence; Hungary as the example.
- [ ] K2 Sovietisation: the remaking of Hungary on the Soviet model (one-party rule, secret police, planned heavy industry, collectivised farms, Soviet-style culture and loyalty to Moscow).
- [ ] K3 De-Stalinisation (essential term): the post-1953, and especially post-1956, retreat from Stalin's terror and cult of personality.
- [ ] K4 New Course (essential term): Nagy's programme of 1953–55.
- [ ] K5 AVH (essential term): the hated secret police; give its full name from section 2.
- [ ] K6 Workers' councils and revolutionary committees (essential terms).
- [ ] K7 Neutrality (essential term): what Nagy's declaration of 1 November meant and why it crossed Moscow's line.
- [ ] K8 Counter-revolution vs revolution (essential terms): the official Soviet and Kádár label against the Hungarian and Western one.
- [ ] K9 Goulash communism (essential term): one bullet only, as an outcome.

Key personality: Imre Nagy (one block, headed "Key personality: Imre Nagy")
- [ ] P1 Who he was: birth; communist; years in the Soviet Union; return with the Red Army.
- [ ] P2 Minister of agriculture and the land reform of 1945; later posts.
- [ ] P3 First premiership (1953–55): appointed under Soviet pressure after Stalin's death; the New Course; Rákosi kept the party leadership and worked against him.
- [ ] P4 His fall (1955): removed as prime minister and expelled from the party; became the focus of reform-minded communists and intellectuals; readmitted to the party in October 1956.
- [ ] P5 Participant in 1956: reappointed prime minister; slow at first to side with the insurgents; then accepted the revolution's demands (ceasefire, end of the AVH, multi-party government, neutrality).
- [ ] P6 His 4 November broadcast and refuge in the Yugoslav embassy.
- [ ] P7 Abduction, deportation to Romania, secret trial and execution (1958); unmarked grave.
- [ ] P8 How contemporaries saw him: a traitor and counter-revolutionary to Moscow and Kádár; a national hero to the insurgents and the West; some insurgents distrusted him as a communist.
- [ ] P9 How historians judge him: a reform communist carried along by events rather than their organiser; debate over his naivety about Soviet intentions; rehabilitation and reburial (1989).

Causes (background block)
- [ ] C1 Rákosi's Stalinist rule: one-party state, cult of personality, show trials and purges (Rajk), the AVH, the jailing of Cardinal Mindszenty, persecution of the churches.
- [ ] C2 Economic hardship: forced collectivisation, heavy industry favoured, falling living standards, reparations and economic obligations to the Soviet Union.
- [ ] C3 National resentment: Soviet troops, Soviet-style culture, compulsory Russian, Hungarian nationalism and memory of 1848.
- [ ] C4 The split in the party: Nagy's New Course against Rákosi, 1953–55, which showed that change was possible and divided the communists.
- [ ] C5 Khrushchev's secret speech (February 1956) and its effect in Hungary (one bullet; the speech itself belongs elsewhere).
- [ ] C6 Ferment of 1956: the Petőfi Circle and the Writers' Union; Rákosi's removal (July 1956) and Gerő's succession; the reburial of Rajk (6 October 1956).
- [ ] C7 The Polish October: Gomułka's return in October 1956 without Soviet invasion encouraged Hungarians (one bullet; Poland itself belongs elsewhere).

Events (blocks in date order)
- [ ] E1 22 October: the students' demands (the sixteen points), with their main contents.
- [ ] E2 23 October: the demonstration in solidarity with Poland (the Bem statue, then Parliament); Gerő's hostile radio speech; the Stalin statue pulled down; the AVH fires on the crowd at the radio building; fighting begins; first Soviet intervention in the early hours of 24 October.
- [ ] E3 24–28 October: Nagy reappointed (24 October); Kádár replaces Gerő as party leader (25 October); the shooting at Parliament (Kossuth) Square (25 October); street fighting (Corvin Passage, Kilián barracks, Maléter); revolutionary committees and workers' councils across the country; the ceasefire and Nagy's 28 October broadcast (a national democratic movement, the AVH to be dissolved, Soviet troops to leave Budapest).
- [ ] E4 29–31 October: Soviet troops leave Budapest; the Soviet government declaration of 30 October offering talks on withdrawal; the end of one-party rule and the coalition with non-communist parties (30 October); the Republic Square lynchings (30 October); Mindszenty freed; the Presidium's reversal (31 October).
- [ ] E5 1 November: new Soviet troops entering Hungary; Nagy declares neutrality and withdrawal from the Warsaw Pact and appeals to the UN; the communist party refounded under Kádár; Kádár disappears to the Soviet side.
- [ ] E6 2–3 November: Khrushchev consults the other communist leaders, including Tito (who agrees); withdrawal talks; Maléter arrested at the talks (night of 3–4 November).
- [ ] E7 4 November: Operation Whirlwind; Nagy's broadcast; Nagy to the Yugoslav embassy, Mindszenty to the US embassy; Kádár's rival government announced.
- [ ] E8 The fighting to mid-November (Csepel and other working-class districts held out longest); the workers' councils and general strike into December; the councils crushed.
- [ ] E9 22 November: Nagy leaves the embassy under a written promise of safe conduct and is seized by Soviet forces.

The West's response
- [ ] W1 The Suez crisis overlapping the uprising, with dates, and its effect on Western unity, the UN and Soviet calculations.
- [ ] W2 Radio Free Europe's broadcasts and the charge that it raised false hopes.
- [ ] W3 US policy: liberation rhetoric vs no military action; Dulles's signal that the satellites were not seen as potential military allies; Eisenhower's caution (nuclear risk; Hungary inside the Soviet sphere; the US election).
- [ ] W4 The UN: Soviet veto in the Security Council; General Assembly resolutions; observers refused entry; no action.
- [ ] W5 The refugees welcomed in the West, including Ireland; propaganda value; Western communist parties lose members.

Outcomes
- [ ] O1 Casualties (as ranges and "about"), arrests, imprisonments and executions under Kádár.
- [ ] O2 Refugees: about 200,000, many young and skilled.
- [ ] O3 Nagy's trial and execution with Maléter and others (16 June 1958).
- [ ] O4 Kádár's regime: repression to the early 1960s, then compromise ("whoever is not against us is with us"), the New Economic Mechanism (1968), goulash communism; Kádár in power until 1988.

Why it failed
- [ ] F1 Soviet determination: Hungary's exit from the Warsaw Pact and the end of one-party rule threatened the whole bloc; the domino fear.
- [ ] F2 Overwhelming Soviet military force against insurgents without heavy weapons, outside help or a single command.
- [ ] F3 The West would not act (Suez, nuclear risk, spheres of influence); the UN was powerless.
- [ ] F4 Divisions and weaknesses among the Hungarians: Nagy followed events rather than leading them; no united organisation; Kádár's defection gave the Soviets a Hungarian government to install.
- [ ] F5 Contrast with Poland: Gomułka kept communist rule and the Warsaw Pact, so Moscow tolerated him.

Significance (and Assessment block)
- [ ] S1 For the Soviet bloc: the limits of de-Stalinisation; Soviet control would be defended by force; a precedent for Czechoslovakia (1968) (one phrase).
- [ ] S2 For the Cold War: "liberation" exposed as empty; spheres of influence tacitly accepted; Soviet prestige damaged in the West and among the left; tension raised in the same weeks as Suez.
- [ ] S3 For Hungary: lasting memory; the 1989 reassessment (Pozsgay's "popular uprising", Nagy's reburial, 23 October as national day).
- [ ] X1 Assessment: revolution or counter-revolution, and how far a success or failure (achievements vs limits as paired points); differing contemporary and later views of Nagy and of the West's role.

## 3. Traps

Do not use (wrong or doubtful facts in the current notes):
- Do not use: Kádár's rival government "based at Szolnok", because its proclamation was broadcast from Soviet-controlled territory while claiming to be at Szolnok; say only that it was announced on 4 November with Soviet backing.
- Do not use: Nagy left the Yugoslav embassy "after a Soviet promise of safe passage", because the written guarantee came from Kádár's government; Soviet forces then seized him.
- Do not use: "the AVH: the Hungarian State Security Authority", because its name was the State Protection Authority (ÁVH).
- Do not use: "a large crowd gathered at the statue of Stalin" as the start of the demonstration, because the march began at the Bem statue and went to Parliament; part of the crowd then pulled down the Stalin statue.
- Do not use: "Soviet troops entered the city ... on the same night", because they entered in the early hours of 24 October; say that.
- Do not use: "the Communist Party was reduced to a minority role" in the 30 October coalition, because the inner cabinet still included Nagy, Kádár and other communists; say one-party rule was ended and non-communist parties joined.
- Do not use: Tito "disliked Kádár's methods", because it is unsupported; say Tito agreed that intervention was necessary, and that Yugoslavia later protested at Nagy's seizure from its embassy.
- Do not use: "fighting in Budapest continued until mid-November" without care: organised armed resistance ended by about 10–11 November; strikes went on into December.
- Do not use: "Nagy appealed to the West for help", because his formal appeal on 1 November was to the UN; say that.
- Do not use: "historians now treat him as ... the uprising's political leader" without qualification, because historians stress that he followed events rather than organising them.

Other errors and doubtful claims to avoid:
- Don't give the size of the Soviet invasion force (troop or tank numbers): figures vary widely. Say "overwhelming" or "a large force".
- Don't give casualty figures as exact numbers or give a figure for the wounded, for the Kossuth Square shooting or for the Republic Square lynchings.
- Don't quote Nagy's 4 November broadcast or Dulles in quotation marks; translations vary. Give the content.
- Don't use the line "Stalin's best Hungarian pupil" for Rákosi.
- Don't say Radio Free Europe was run by the US government openly or that the USA promised help: it was US-funded (covertly, through the CIA, at the time) and no government made a promise; the problem was the impression its broadcasts gave.
- Don't say the UN sent observers or a force: Hungary refused them entry and nothing was sent.
- Don't say Suez caused the Soviet decision; it was one factor that made it easier (it divided the West and distracted the UN).
- Don't say the uprising aimed to restore capitalism or the pre-war regime: most demands were for national independence, free elections and democratic socialism.

## 4. What exams ask

- Causes of the uprising (Higher 100 marks; Ordinary 40 marks, very frequent): marks go for a cause → trigger chain, not a list. Long-term (Rákosi's Stalinism, the ÁVH, show trials, collectivisation and falling living standards, Soviet domination and national feeling), medium-term (the New Course and Nagy's fall dividing the party) and triggers (the secret speech, Rákosi's removal, the Rajk reburial, the Polish October, the 22–23 October demands and the shooting at the radio station). Name people and dates for each.
- Why the uprising failed or was crushed (Higher 100; Ordinary 40): the best answers weigh the factors and say which was decisive (Soviet determination once Hungary left the Warsaw Pact and ended one-party rule), then support it with the West's inaction, Suez, the UN's impotence, the insurgents' lack of heavy weapons and leadership, and Kádár's defection. The contrast with Poland earns marks as a "why Hungary, not Poland" judgement.
- Nagy (Ordinary 30 marks; part of Higher answers): his career in order, his New Course, his fall, his role in 1956 with dates (24 October, 30 October, 1 November, 4 November), and his fate (embassy, abduction, secret trial, execution 1958). Higher answers judge him: reformer carried by events vs leader; naive vs principled.
- Berlin and/or Hungary and international tension (Higher 100): for Hungary, show how it raised tension (Soviet force, Western anger, refugee propaganda, damage to Soviet prestige) and how it also eased it in a sense (the West's tacit acceptance of the Soviet sphere). Paired effects score better than narrative.
- Turning points of the Cold War and how far Sovietisation succeeded (Higher 100): Hungary as the proof that Soviet control rested on force and that de-Stalinisation had limits; "success" (control restored) vs "failure" (loss of legitimacy, refugees, Western communist parties weakened).
- Ordinary Level source questions (photographs, broadcasts, eyewitness accounts): identifying the date (4 November) and place (Budapest); who Nagy was and what his broadcast reported; what a photograph of tanks or torn-up streets shows; why the Soviets acted (to keep Hungary in the bloc); what the insurgents lacked (heavy weapons) and hoped for (UN or Western help). Short answers need one specific fact each.
- Assessment material examiners reward: achievements (Soviet troops withdrawn from Budapest, multi-party government, neutrality, a few days of freedom) vs limits (crushed in days, thousands dead, 200,000 refugees, Nagy executed); contemporary views (counter-revolution vs freedom fight) vs later views (1989 reassessment).
