# Package: hist-ire2-s9 — Case study: the GAA to 1891

- **loId:** `hist-ire2-s9` · **chapterId:** `hist-ire2`
- **Syllabus:** Later Modern Ireland, Topic 2 (Movements for political and social reform, 1870–1914), Cultural movements: "the GAA". Case study: **the GAA to 1891**. A documents-based case study (a DBQ source in 2007 and 2026), so the notes must let a student place any document in context. Outline scope: the state of Irish sport before 1884 (athletics under English rules, the decline of hurling); Michael Cusack and Maurice Davin; Hayes' Hotel, Thurles (1 November 1884); the patrons Archbishop Croke, Parnell and Davitt, and Croke's letter; the first rules; the ban on "foreign" games and on RIC members; clubs based on parishes; IRB infiltration and the clerical reaction; Cusack's removal (1886); the 1887 Thurles convention split; the first All-Ireland championships; the American "invasion" tour (1888); the Parnell split and the collapse in membership by 1891; why the GAA survived. Key concepts: anglicisation/de-anglicisation; Irish Ireland. Document types: letters and speeches of support, reports of meetings and matches. Write in full Higher Level detail.

## 1. Must cover (one bullet or block for every item; tick each off)

Background (assumed)
- [ ] A1 (assumed) The Famine and later emigration weakened Irish rural society and the Irish language; English language and customs spread in towns and schools.
- [ ] A2 (assumed) The Land War (from 1879), the Land League and Home Rule agitation under Parnell made the early 1880s a period of intense national feeling.
- [ ] A3 (assumed) The IRB (Fenians) was a secret oath-bound society seeking an Irish republic by force; the Catholic Church was a powerful, generally constitutional, influence on nationalist Ireland.

Key concepts (each also in keyTerms)
- [ ] K1 Anglicisation: the replacement of Irish language, dress, games and customs by English ones.
- [ ] K2 De-anglicisation: the effort to reverse it by reviving Irish language, games and customs.
- [ ] K3 Irish Ireland: the cultural-nationalist idea of building the nation on native language, games and traditions (developed after 1891; here only its roots in the GAA).
- [ ] K4 Other terms: foreign games ban, parish club, Central Council, American Invasion, amateurism.

Irish sport before 1884
- [ ] B1 Organised athletics followed English amateur rules that excluded manual workers, artisans and most farmers; sport was dominated by gentry and middle class.
- [ ] B2 English games (rugby, cricket, later soccer) were played in schools, garrison towns and among the gentry; the Irish bodies founded for rugby (1879) and soccer (1880).
- [ ] B3 Hurling had declined since the Famine but survived in parts of the south and west, played to differing local rules; football had no national code.
- [ ] B4 Cusack's Metropolitan Hurling Club (1882) and the push for standard rules.
- [ ] B5 The wider cultural and political background (Gaelic Union 1880; Land War; Fenian revival).

Founding, 1884
- [ ] F1 Michael Cusack: background, career, character and later fame in literature.
- [ ] F2 Maurice Davin: background, reputation, president.
- [ ] F3 Cusack's article in United Ireland (October 1884) calling for a national body to revive native athletics.
- [ ] F4 The invitation and the meeting at Hayes' Hotel, Thurles, 1 November 1884: who attended and numbers.
- [ ] F5 Officers elected: Davin president; Cusack one of the secretaries.
- [ ] F6 The Association's aims: revive Irish games, open athletics to all classes, promote national pride and fitness; its full title.

Patrons and support
- [ ] P1 Archbishop Thomas Croke of Cashel: his position, his December 1884 letter accepting the role of patron, and its message against imitating English games.
- [ ] P2 Charles Stewart Parnell and Michael Davitt as patrons (their GAA role only; their full careers belong to other notes).
- [ ] P3 Davitt's view of the GAA (working people into the national movement; reviving games as a way to break from English influence).
- [ ] P4 Press support from United Ireland and its editor William O'Brien.
- [ ] P5 Effect of the patrons: respectability, clergy as club officers, links to the Home Rule and land movements.

Rules and organisation
- [ ] R1 Davin's first rules for hurling and Gaelic football (1885): national codes replacing local variations.
- [ ] R2 Open to all classes; English amateur definition rejected; GAA itself amateur.
- [ ] R3 The foreign games ban: what it was, why (de-anglicisation), what its early form covered.
- [ ] R4 The bar on RIC and British forces members.
- [ ] R5 Structure: clubs based on parishes, county committees, Central Council, annual convention at Thurles; the fit with Church organisation.

Growth, 1885–1888
- [ ] G1 Rapid spread, first in the south and west, then the midlands and beyond; hundreds of affiliated clubs within a few years.
- [ ] G2 Matches as local occasions: crowds, bands, parades of club men (and the problems that came with them, such as drinking).
- [ ] G3 The first All-Ireland championships (1887 championship, finals played in April 1888); first hurling and football finals.
- [ ] G4 Cusack's newspaper The Celtic Times (1887).

Crisis, 1886–1891
- [ ] C1 IRB infiltration: use of clubs and county boards; suspicion of the authorities (Dublin Castle, RIC reports).
- [ ] C2 Cusack's removal as secretary (1886) and his quarrels with other officers.
- [ ] C3 The 1887 Thurles convention: contest for control of the Central Council between IRB-linked delegates and moderates.
- [ ] C4 The clerical reaction: unease of Croke and other bishops, priests withdrawing, Church concern about drink and discipline.
- [ ] C5 Davin's resignation as president (1889).
- [ ] C6 The American "invasion" tour (1888): purpose, scale, financial loss, disruption of the 1888 championship, athletes staying in America.
- [ ] C7 The Parnell split (December 1890) dividing GAA members; Parnell's death (October 1891).
- [ ] C8 Collapse in clubs and membership by 1891; slow recovery in the 1890s.

Survival and assessment
- [ ] S1 Why the GAA survived: parish club roots, appeal of the games, Church and nationalist support, leaders who changed rather than split the body, native identity.
- [ ] S2 Documents and views: founders and patrons (supportive), authorities and unionists (hostile), partisan convention reports; reading a document's author, date and purpose.
- [ ] S3 Historians' views (de Búrca, Mandle) and how to use them.
- [ ] S4 Achievements: national mass organisation, standard rules, open to all classes, parish network.
- [ ] S5 Limits: IRB entanglement, Church quarrel, debts, Parnell split, near collapse by 1891.
- [ ] S6 Significance: a cultural-nationalist body that anticipated the Gaelic League (1893) and Irish Ireland; contemporary vs later views (paired).

## 3. Traps

Do not use (wrong or doubtful facts in the current notes):
- Do not use: "J.K. Bracken elected a secretary" at the founding, because accounts of the first officers disagree on the secretaries beyond Cusack. Say Cusack was one of the secretaries.
- Do not use: Cusack's Metropolitan Hurling Club "held a match in 1883 under agreed rules and used the Irish Times", because the match and the newspaper are not certain. Give only the club's founding in 1882.
- Do not use: Trinity College students playing hurling, because it is not needed and not firmly checked.
- Do not use: the foreign games ban as covering "playing or attending" from the start, because the early ban concerned playing foreign games and the sweeping bans on attending came later (c. 1901 onward); say "banned members from playing foreign games such as rugby, soccer, cricket and hockey" and give no rule number or year.
- Do not use: John Wyse Power as "an IRB man", because it is not established for 1884; call him a journalist.
- Do not use: Cusack removed "because he disliked the IRB" or "after quarrels with Davin and the IRB", because the causes (clashes with other officers and over administration and money) are disputed. State the date and that he quarrelled with fellow officers.
- Do not use: Davin resigned "because of the IRB" as a fact; say he resigned in 1889 amid the disputes and divisions of those years.
- Do not use: figures for clubs or membership, scores in the 1888 finals, or crowd counts, because sources differ.
- Do not use: "first All-Ireland championships in 1887" as if the finals were played in 1887; the 1887 championship was decided in 1888.

## 4. What exams ask

- Documents-based question (2007 and 2026, higher part (d), 40 marks): how the GAA dealt with its difficulties to 1891, or what political and other problems it faced. Marks go to a clear thesis, then three or four organised difficulties (IRB infiltration and Dublin Castle suspicion; Church unease; quarrels among leaders and the removal of Cusack and resignation of Davin; the American tour and debt; the Parnell split) each paired with how it was handled or survived (parish clubs, patrons, leadership change, popularity of the games), with dates.
- Part (c) style source questions: who wrote it, when, why, for whom. Founders and patrons (Cusack, Croke, Davitt, Parnell, O'Brien) support the GAA and write with a purpose; officials and the authorities are hostile; match and convention reports are partisan or eyewitness. Students must say what a source shows and what it cannot show; naming the author's role (for example Cusack as founder, so not neutral) earns the reliability mark.
- Comparison questions: documents from different dates give a favourable picture (crowds, discipline, bands, a prelate reviewing club men) or a worrying one (drink, bad blood, weak local clubs); a strong answer quotes the wording and links it to the wider picture.
- Section 2 ordinary-level 30 and 40-mark paragraphs: founding facts (who, where, when), the patrons, spread and rules, and difficulties; each accurate dated statement earns a mark; a bare "it was founded in Thurles" does not.
- Higher 100-mark essays on the GAA alone or against the Gaelic League, Hyde or the suffrage movement: to 1891 the GAA is the example of a mass organisation open to all classes that survived near collapse. Link to anglicisation and de-anglicisation; give a judgement (contribution to national identity vs entanglement in politics and controversy).
- Contribution and controversy: students need both columns (achievements and quarrels), with the IRB and Church as the central controversies.
