## 1. Must cover (one bullet or block for every item; tick each off)

Background (assumed)
- [ ] A1 (assumed) The Act of Union (1801) made Ireland part of the United Kingdom, governed from Westminster and Dublin Castle.
- [ ] A2 (assumed) The French Wars (to 1815) had raised demand and prices for Irish grain and cattle.
- [ ] A3 (assumed) Catholics were the great majority of the population; most landlords were Protestant (Church of Ireland).
- [ ] A4 (assumed) The 1838 Poor Law created workhouses and unions run by boards of guardians, paid for by local rates (detail belongs to the notes on O'Connell after 1829 and the Poor Law).

Key concepts (defined as used in this topic; also in keyTerms)
- [ ] K1 Landlordism: a small, mainly Protestant landlord class owning the land, letting it to tenants with little security.
- [ ] K2 Economic depression: the slump after 1815 (falling prices, rural industry in decline, harvest failures).
- [ ] K3 Laissez-faire: the belief that government should not interfere with trade and the market.
- [ ] K4 Famine: catastrophic food shortage causing mass death from hunger and disease; here, 1845–1850.

The countryside in 1815
- [ ] C1 Landlords: owned nearly all the land; many held land in several counties.
- [ ] C2 Absentees: lived away, used agents; rents left the locality; little improvement.
- [ ] C3 Middlemen: long leases from landlords, sublet in small lots at higher rents; ended by landlords after 1815.
- [ ] C4 Strong (large) farmers: more secure, employed labour, often grazing.
- [ ] C5 Smallholders: small farms, usually tenants-at-will, evictable at short notice.
- [ ] C6 Cottiers: cabin and potato patch, rent paid largely in labour.
- [ ] C7 Landless labourers and conacre: one-season ground for potatoes at high rents.
- [ ] C8 No legal security of tenure; tenants' improvements belonged to the landlord, which discouraged investment.

Population, subdivision and the potato
- [ ] P1 Rapid population growth to 1841 (early marriage, large families among the poor).
- [ ] P2 Subdivision of holdings among sons; very many tiny holdings.
- [ ] P3 Dependence on the potato: high yield on poor land, nutritious with milk, but could not be stored from year to year; the lumper.
- [ ] P4 About 3 million people almost wholly dependent on it; poor housing (one-roomed mud cabins, 1841 census).

Economic crisis, 1815–1845
- [ ] E1 Price collapse after Waterloo; rents slow to fall; arrears; indebted landlords.
- [ ] E2 Clearances: consolidation of farms, pasture replacing tillage, eviction of small tenants.
- [ ] E3 Decline of rural industry (spinning and weaving; cotton after the end of protective duties) outside the north-east.
- [ ] E4 Partial famines and fever before 1845.
- [ ] E5 Agrarian secret societies (Whiteboys, Rockites, Ribbonmen): violence against landlords, agents and incoming tenants.
- [ ] E6 The Devon Commission (1843–45): findings and the absence of reform.
- [ ] E7 Escape routes: seasonal migration to Britain; emigration.

The Famine
- [ ] F1 The blight: cause, first report (September 1845), how it destroyed the crop in the ground and in store.
- [ ] F2 1845: partial loss; few deaths.
- [ ] F3 1846: near-total loss; the terrible winter of 1846–47.
- [ ] F4 1847 ("Black '47"): peak mortality; healthy but tiny crop (seed shortage).
- [ ] F5 1848 and 1849: renewed failure; cholera in 1849.
- [ ] F6 Fever and disease as the main killers (typhus, relapsing fever, dysentery, scurvy, cholera); deaths of doctors, clergy and relief staff.
- [ ] F7 Regional variation: west and south-west worst, east and north-east least.
- [ ] F8 Mortality, emigration and population change, 1841–1851 (about figures only).

Government responses
- [ ] G1 Peel, 1845–46: secret purchase of Indian corn; sale at cost; Relief Commission and local committees; Board of Works schemes.
- [ ] G2 Repeal of the Corn Laws (June 1846) and Peel's fall.
- [ ] G3 Russell's Whig government (June 1846): laissez-faire; no price controls; depots only in the west.
- [ ] G4 Public works and the Labouchere Act (August 1846): costs on local taxes; low task wages; numbers employed; why they failed.
- [ ] G5 Soup kitchens (Temporary Relief Act, February 1847): numbers fed; why they were closed.
- [ ] G6 Poor Law Extension Act (June 1847): Irish property to support Irish poverty; outdoor relief; the Gregory (quarter-acre) clause.
- [ ] G7 Evictions and clearance after 1847; bankrupt unions; workhouses as centres of fever.
- [ ] G8 Rate-in-aid (1849): terms and the northern protest.
- [ ] G9 Food exports during the Famine, and the debate over them.

Key personality: Charles Trevelyan (one block, every point)
- [ ] T1 Who he was: born 1807, East India Company career, Assistant Secretary to the Treasury from 1840.
- [ ] T2 What he did, as a participant: ran Treasury relief from 1845 (maize, works, depots, soup kitchens); carried out Whig policy; insisted Irish property pay.
- [ ] T3 His beliefs: evangelical Protestant, laissez-faire, fear of dependence; the Famine as Providence; blame of landlords and the Irish.
- [ ] T4 Shaped by events: closed the soup kitchens; defended the policy in The Irish Crisis (1848).
- [ ] T5 Contemporaries: knighted 1848; condemned by nationalists and by some relief workers and officials.
- [ ] T6 Later career: Northcote–Trevelyan Report (1854); Governor of Madras (1859).
- [ ] T7 Historians: Woodham-Smith (villain); Haines (scapegoat, worked within ministers' orders); the balanced view (typical of the Whig government's ideology).

Assessment (paired judgement points, final block)
- [ ] S1 Success: Peel's measures kept deaths low in the first season; soup kitchens fed millions cheaply.
- [ ] S2 Limit: laissez-faire, no control of prices, works too little and too late.
- [ ] S3 Limit: Irish property made to pay; Poor Law and Gregory clause drove clearances.
- [ ] S4 Limit: relief withdrawn while hunger continued; exports.
- [ ] S5 Contemporary vs later views: Mitchel and nationalists; Trevelyan's self-defence; historians on genocide, ideology and parsimony.

## 3. Traps

Do not use (wrong or doubtful facts in the current notes):
- Do not use: "about 10,000 landlords", because the figure is not reliably sourced; say "a small class".
- Do not use: "nearly half of all holdings were under 5 acres by 1841", because the proportion depends on how holdings under 1 acre are counted; say "a very large number of tiny holdings".
- Do not use: "an adult labourer ate up to 12–14 lb a day", because the figure is an estimate that varies; say only "several pounds a day".
- Do not use: "late 1830s local failures" and "early 1830s" as precise years; give only "the 1830s".
- Do not use: "wages could not keep up with food prices, which more than doubled", because the rise is not precisely sourced; say prices rose sharply.
- Do not use: Trevelyan "declared the Famine over" (1847 or 1850), because the dates are not well fixed. Say he and ministers treated the crisis as over once the soup kitchens closed, and that distress continued.
- Do not use: "the government treated the Famine as over by 1850" as a fact without qualification; say distress in the west continued after 1850.
- Do not use: "costs shared between the Treasury and the county cess" for Peel's works as a precise rule; say costs fell largely on local taxes.
- Do not use: "twenty-two unions" in the rate-in-aid; say "bankrupt western unions".
- Do not use: Trevelyan's remarks about Providence in quotation marks; give them as reported belief.

## 4. What exams ask

- Higher 100-mark essay, very common: how effective government responses (and/or private responses) were, 1845–1850. Marks go to a stated judgement, kept up, with paired points: Peel's maize and committees (effective in 1845–46) vs Whig laissez-faire, low-paid works, closed soup kitchens, Poor Law burden and the Gregory clause. Name acts with dates.
- Higher essay on how the Famine began and why it lasted: blight, repeated crop failure, dependence on the potato, poor tenant security, seed shortage in 1847, withdrawal of relief.
- Higher essay on social and economic developments 1815–1850: population growth, subdivision, potato dependence, price collapse, decline of rural industry. Link causes to the vulnerability before 1845.
- Higher question on landlords' fortunes: absentees, debt, clearances, evictions after 1847; connect with the Famine.
- Charles Trevelyan (Higher compare with Nicholson, or Ordinary paragraph): his post and dates, what he controlled (maize, works, depots, soup kitchens), his beliefs (laissez-faire, Providence), and his later career. State his role in the Famine, and separate the man from the ministers.
- Ordinary Level: short answers on a document (blight, laissez-faire, Trevelyan's role, the effect on the potato crop); 30-mark paragraphs on the Poor Law or relief schemes.
- Strong answers give dates and named acts, state numbers with "about", and weigh contemporary views (Mitchel) against later historians.
