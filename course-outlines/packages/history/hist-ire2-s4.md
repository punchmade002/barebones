## 3. Traps

Do not use (wrong or doubtful facts in the current notes):
- Do not use: "about 28" as the exact number who stayed with Parnell, because sources give 26 to 28; use "a minority of about 26–28".
- Do not use: Parnell "won" £5,000 in damages, because the claim was settled out of court; say The Times paid £5,000 in settlement.
- Do not use: "about 200,000 followed his funeral", because the estimates vary widely and none is reliable.
- Do not use: Gladstone "told the Irish Party that he would not lead the Liberals if Parnell stayed", because the message passed through Morley and McCarthy in a confused way; say his letter to Morley made his position plain.
- Do not use: "the Irish Party had 82 seats in January and 84 in December 1910", because these are totals for all nationalist MPs, including independents outside Redmond's party; say the nationalist MPs numbered in the low 80s and held the balance.
- Do not use: Healy "excluded" at reunion as a flat statement, because the sources differ on the timing of his expulsion; leave it out.
- Do not use: "about 170,000 followed Redmond and 11,000 stayed", because estimates vary; use the range in section 2.
- Do not use: the date order that puts the bishops' statement (3 December) before the manifesto (29 November) as if it caused it; the manifesto came first and the bishops condemned him on 3 December.
- Do not use: Dolan as a "former Irish Party MP" without qualification; say he had left the Irish Party.

Confusable people, terms and dates
- Parnellites (minority, kept the National League) vs anti-Parnellites (majority, Irish National Federation).
- Redmond's Parnellite group (1891–1900) vs the reunited Irish Party (from 1900).
- Irish National League (Parnell, 1882) vs Irish National Federation (anti-Parnellites, 1891) vs United Irish League (O'Brien, 1898). Three similar names: keep them separate.
- Sinn Féin before 1916 (Griffith's dual-monarchy party) vs the republican Sinn Féin after 1917.
- Irish Volunteers (nationalist, 1913) vs Ulster Volunteers (unionist, 1913) vs National Volunteers (Redmond's majority, 1914).
- The Irish Council Bill (1907, devolution, rejected) vs the Home Rule Bills (1886, 1893, 1912).
- Parliament Act (1911) removed the veto and left a two-year delay; it did not itself grant Home Rule.
- William O'Brien (Plan of Campaign, UIL) vs Captain William O'Shea (divorce).
- The Special Commission (1888–90, Pigott) is not the Commission of Inquiry into later events.

## 4. What exams ask

- Higher 100-mark essay on Parnell: his achievements and failures, strengths and weaknesses, or how far he was a success as a leader. This subsection supplies the 1886–91 half of the answer. Marks go to a clear judgement stated at the start, then paired points with evidence: discipline and Liberal alliance against aloofness, dependence on the Liberals and the divorce; the Pigott triumph as the high point, the split as the fall. Name a historian (Lyons, Bew) to add weight.
- Higher essay on whether the Home Rule movement achieved its aims, 1870–1891 or to 1914: successes (party, land reform, Gladstone converted, Act of 1914) set against failures (the split, the Lords' veto in 1893, Ulster). Students lose marks by describing events without saying how far the aim was reached.
- Higher comparison of Redmond with Carson as leader: state which was more effective and why, using what each achieved by 1914 (an Act on the statute book against delay and armed threat) and each one's limits (Redmond on Ulster and the war; Carson on partition).
- Ordinary Level 30-mark profile of Redmond: birth and career dates, loyalty to Parnell, reunion in 1900, Land Conference, Parliament Act, Home Rule Act, Woodenbridge and the split. Each correct, dated statement earns marks; a bare list without dates scores less.
- Ordinary Level profile of the first Sinn Féin: Griffith's background, The United Irishman, The Resurrection of Hungary and its Hungarian model, the 1905 policy points (abstention, assembly, protection), and why it stayed small (North Leitrim). A common error is to describe the post-1916 party.
- Ordinary Level profile of the Irish Volunteers: what prompted them (the Ulster Volunteers and MacNeill's article), founding date and place, chairman, IRB influence, Howth, and the split in 1914 over Redmond's call to fight.
- Ordinary Level questions on why Parnell fell: expect the divorce, Gladstone's letter, the bishops and the split, with dates. Explain why each of Liberals and Church turned against him, not just that they did.
- Cause and result questions: why the Pigott forgeries failed (the misspelling, the cross-examination); why the 1893 Bill failed (the Lords); why the party was divided in the 1890s.
- Documents-based questions may use a cartoon or report about 1886, so students need the Parnell story's later events to give part (d) context: the Pigott affair and the split show Parnell's strengths and weaknesses.
- A reliable way to earn marks on any essay here: give each point a year and a named person, and end each paragraph with a one-line verdict.
