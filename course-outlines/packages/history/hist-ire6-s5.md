## 1. Must cover (one bullet or block for every item; tick each off)

Background (assumed)
- [ ] A1 (assumed) Partition: the Government of Ireland Act 1920 set up Northern Ireland with its own parliament at Stormont; the Irish Free State followed (1922). A unionist majority governed the North and nationalists were a large minority.
- [ ] A2 (assumed) The External Relations Act (1936) left the Crown with a role in Irish external affairs; the 1937 Constitution (Bunreacht na hÉireann) claimed the whole island in Articles 2 and 3.
- [ ] A3 (assumed) The First Inter-Party Government of 1948–51 (Costello, MacBride) took office in February 1948 and moved to declare a republic.
- [ ] A4 (assumed) Ireland was neutral in the Second World War and did not join NATO; Britain, Northern Ireland and the Republic were closely linked by trade, migration and sterling.

The Republic of Ireland Act and the Ireland Act, 1949
- [ ] R1 Costello's announcement (September 1948) and the Republic of Ireland Act, in force 18 April 1949: the External Relations Act repealed, Ireland leaves the Commonwealth.
- [ ] R2 The Ireland Act 1949: the Republic not a foreign country; Irish citizens in Britain not treated as aliens.
- [ ] R3 The Ireland Act's guarantee: Northern Ireland stays in the UK unless its parliament consents (a unionist veto).
- [ ] R4 Irish reaction: seen as a guarantee of partition; the Dublin government's protest.
- [ ] R5 The Common Travel Area continues after 1949.
- [ ] R6 Articles 2 and 3 used by unionists and the British government as proof that Dublin did not accept the North.

The anti-partition campaign and the 1950s
- [ ] P1 The 1948–51 government's anti-partition campaign: all main parties, speeches abroad (especially the United States), complaints about the treatment of nationalists.
- [ ] P2 Result: Britain and Stormont refused to discuss it; no practical gain.
- [ ] P3 The IRA's Border Campaign (December 1956 to 1962) and Irish internment of suspects at the Curragh (from 1957).
- [ ] P4 Little support in the Republic for the campaign.
- [ ] P5 The shift to a pragmatic, economic approach by the late 1950s.

Trade dependence on Britain
- [ ] T1 Share of exports to Britain (mainly live cattle and farm produce) and of imports from it.
- [ ] T2 The link between the Irish pound and sterling.
- [ ] T3 Why this tied Irish EEC policy to Britain's and why the 1965 Free Trade Agreement mattered.
- [ ] T4 Later fall in the British share after joint EEC entry (1973) and the European Monetary System (1979).

Lemass, O'Neill and the Free Trade Agreement, 1965
- [ ] L1 Lemass's visit to Stormont (14 January 1965) and O'Neill's visit to Dublin (9 February 1965).
- [ ] L2 First meetings of the two heads of government since partition; agenda of trade, tourism and electricity, not partition.
- [ ] L3 A change from 1950s rhetoric: a de facto acceptance of the North's existence.
- [ ] L4 Unionist hostility (Paisley) and mistrust in O'Neill's party.
- [ ] L5 The Anglo-Irish Free Trade Agreement (signed 14 December 1965, in operation from 1966).

Lynch and the Troubles, 1969–1973
- [ ] Y1 August 1969: riots in Derry and Belfast, Lynch's broadcast, no troops across the border, British army deployed.
- [ ] Y2 Hillery in Belfast and the approach to the United Nations (August 1969).
- [ ] Y3 The Arms Crisis of 1970 and what it showed about divisions in Fianna Fáil (brief; detail belongs elsewhere).
- [ ] Y4 Chequers, September 1971: Lynch, Heath and Faulkner; the "Irish dimension".
- [ ] Y5 Internment (9 August 1971), Irish protest and the case at the European Court of Human Rights.
- [ ] Y6 Bloody Sunday (30 January 1972): events, Irish response (ambassador recalled, day of mourning).
- [ ] Y7 The burning of the British embassy in Dublin (2 February 1972) and the British protest.
- [ ] Y8 Direct rule (March 1972) welcomed in Dublin.

Joint EEC entry and Sunningdale, 1973–1974
- [ ] S1 Joint accession (1 January 1973) and regular ministerial contact in Brussels.
- [ ] S2 The Cosgrave–Labour coalition (March 1973) with FitzGerald at Foreign Affairs.
- [ ] S3 Sunningdale (December 1973): participants, power-sharing executive, Council of Ireland.
- [ ] S4 The Irish statement on the status of Northern Ireland (consent of a majority).
- [ ] S5 Loyalist opposition and the Ulster Workers' Council strike (15 to 28 May 1974); collapse of the executive.
- [ ] S6 The Dublin and Monaghan bombings (17 May 1974).
- [ ] S7 The Ewart-Biggs assassination (July 1976) and the Irish government's emergency measures (mid-decade security strain).

Haughey, Thatcher and the Falklands, 1979–1982
- [ ] H1 August 1979: Mullaghmore (Mountbatten) and Warrenpoint; Lynch–Thatcher agreement on security cooperation (September 1979).
- [ ] H2 Thatcher became Prime Minister (May 1979); meetings with Haughey in London (May 1980) and Dublin (8 December 1980).
- [ ] H3 The Dublin summit's "totality of relationships" statement and the differing readings of it.
- [ ] H4 The 1981 hunger strikes (ten deaths; Bobby Sands) and the strain on relations.
- [ ] H5 FitzGerald and Thatcher set up the Anglo-Irish Intergovernmental Council (November 1981).
- [ ] H6 The Falklands War (1982): Ireland on the UN Security Council, initial support for EEC sanctions, withdrawal after the Belgrano sinking, call for a ceasefire.
- [ ] H7 Thatcher's anger and the lasting loss of trust with Haughey.
- [ ] H8 The mood of the time in Irish public opinion: neutrality against solidarity with Britain.

FitzGerald, the New Ireland Forum and the Anglo-Irish Agreement, 1983–1985
- [ ] F1 The New Ireland Forum: members, dates (May 1983 to May 1984), three options.
- [ ] F2 Thatcher's rejection of all three (November 1984) and the Forum's longer-term value (SDLP strengthened, nationalist consensus).
- [ ] F3 Negotiation between officials; signing at Hillsborough, 15 November 1985.
- [ ] F4 Terms: consent principle reaffirmed; Intergovernmental Conference; Maryfield secretariat; Irish consultative role (not joint authority).
- [ ] F5 Unionist reaction: rally, resignation of fifteen MPs (January 1986).
- [ ] F6 Fianna Fáil's opposition; O'Malley and the founding of the Progressive Democrats (December 1985).
- [ ] F7 Thatcher's motives: security cooperation and support for the SDLP against Sinn Féin.
- [ ] F8 Nationalist and unionist views of the agreement (contemporary).

State of relations by 1989
- [ ] E1 The Conference meets regularly; security and cross-border cooperation grows; unionist boycott.
- [ ] E2 Continuing friction: extradition, miscarriages of justice, army conduct.
- [ ] E3 The IRA campaign continues; the North remains divided.
- [ ] E4 Haughey leaves the agreement in place when he forms a coalition with the Progressive Democrats (July 1989).
- [ ] E5 Closer relations than 1949 (EEC, contacts, habit of meetings) but the basic dispute unresolved.

Assessment (paired judgement points, final block)
- [ ] J1 Achievement: from hostility in 1949 to regular contact and a formal consultative role in 1985.
- [ ] J2 Limit: Sunningdale collapsed in months; 1985 did not end the violence; the governments' aims differed.
- [ ] J3 Contemporary views: nationalists saw recognition; unionists saw betrayal.
- [ ] J4 Later view: a foundation for the Downing Street Declaration (1993) and the Good Friday Agreement (1998), though its immediate effect was limited.

## 3. Traps

Do not use (wrong or doubtful facts in the current notes):
- Do not use: "Desmond O'Malley, expelled from the party for supporting [the agreement]", because he was expelled in February 1985, before the agreement, over a family planning vote. Say he left Fianna Fáil's ranks in 1985 and founded the Progressive Democrats in December 1985, in opposition to Haughey's leadership.
- Do not use: "Lemass's approach implied that the North would not change without the consent of its people", because it overstates him. Say Lemass accepted the North's existence in practice and talked of cooperation; the Irish government's declaration on consent came in 1973 (Sunningdale) and 1985.
- Do not use: "Dublin protested that the Northern Ireland parliament was unrepresentative" as a fact about 1949, because it is vague and unchecked; say only that the Irish government saw the Ireland Act as a guarantee of partition.
- Do not use: "a request Britain blocked" for the United Nations in 1969; say Ireland raised the matter at the UN and nothing came of it.
- Do not use: "roughly three-quarters", "less than half" as exact; use "about 70 to 75 per cent" and "under half".
- Do not use: "the Irish government accepted that the status of Northern Ireland would not change without majority consent" as if it ended the constitutional claim; Articles 2 and 3 stayed until 1998.

Doubtful quotations: do not use any quotation not in section 2. Give Lynch's August 1969 words as reported speech.

## 4. What exams ask

- Higher 100-mark essay: Anglo-Irish relations 1949–1989 as a whole (bitterness at partition to regular cooperation); marks go to a clear thesis, stages with dates (1949, 1965, 1972–74, 1980–82, 1985), and a judgement on how far relations improved. A list of events without a conclusion scores badly.
- Higher essay on Haughey and/or FitzGerald and Anglo-Irish relations: Haughey's 1980 dialogue and its collapse over the Falklands against FitzGerald's Forum and the 1985 agreement; strong answers compare approach and results and name what each achieved and failed to achieve.
- Name the cause behind each shift: the Ireland Act (response to the declaration of a republic); trade dependence (the move to cooperation in 1965); violence in the North (strain after 1969); joint EEC entry (new contacts); the Falklands (rift); the Forum (the path to 1985).
- Ordinary-level paragraphs on the Ireland Act and the 1965 meetings: the two provisions of the Ireland Act, the consent guarantee, who met whom, where and when, and what they discussed; each correct dated statement earns marks.
- Ordinary-level paragraphs on Lynch's handling of the North: refusal to send troops, the Arms Crisis, reaction to internment and Bloody Sunday (ambassador recalled), Chequers. Facts must be tied to what Lynch did.
- Ordinary-level paragraphs on Haughey and FitzGerald: the Haughey–Thatcher summit and Falklands, and the Forum and agreement, with signing date and place.
- Sunningdale and Anglo-Irish Agreement: be able to state who took part, what was agreed, why each ran into opposition, and why one failed in months and the other survived.
- Achievements against limits and contemporary against later views earn credit as paired points; naming the unionist and nationalist reactions to 1985 is rewarded.
- Document questions on Anglo-Irish relations tend to be extracts from British or Irish officials on partition, Anglo-Irish trade or EEC accession; students need to know the context of 1949, 1965, 1973 and 1985 to place a source.
