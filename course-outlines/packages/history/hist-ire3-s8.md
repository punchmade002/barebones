## 1. Must cover (one bullet or block for every item; tick each off)

Background (assumed)
- [ ] A1 (assumed) De Valera and Fianna Fáil were in government from 1932; de Valera was also Minister for External Affairs.
- [ ] A2 (assumed) The 1938 Anglo-Irish agreements returned the Treaty ports (Cobh, Berehaven, Lough Swilly) to Irish control.
- [ ] A3 (assumed) Partition: Northern Ireland stayed in the UK and was at war from 3 September 1939 (its war is a separate subsection).
- [ ] A4 (assumed) The IRA was banned in 1936 and was hostile to the government; Civil War divisions were still felt.

Key concepts (also in keyTerms)
- [ ] K1 Neutrality: staying out of the war while remaining a sovereign state; here it was seen as proof of independence.
- [ ] K2 Conformity/censorship: state control of information that pressed the public to follow the official line.
- [ ] K3 The Emergency: the official name for the war years in the South.

Neutrality
- [ ] N1 Declaration: the Dáil's emergency resolution (2 September 1939); the policy of neutrality; Britain at war from 3 September.
- [ ] N2 Reasons: sovereignty; partition; the Treaty ports already returned; weak defences and fear of bombing; Civil War divisions; public and cross-party support (Dillon's dissent).
- [ ] N3 Neutrality in practice: benevolent towards Britain and the Allies, never formally broken.

State powers and censorship
- [ ] E1 The Emergency Powers Act (1939): ministers could make orders with legal force.
- [ ] E2 Censorship: Aiken's department; what was banned (troop movements, sinkings, weather, criticism of neutrality); letters, radio, books; the Censorship of Publications Board.
- [ ] E3 Conformity: effect on public debate and press freedom.

Defence and the IRA
- [ ] D1 The Defence Forces expanded; reserves added.
- [ ] D2 The Local Defence Force (June 1940) and the Local Security Force.
- [ ] D3 Contingency planning with Britain in case of a German invasion.
- [ ] D4 The IRA: S-Plan (1939), the Magazine Fort raid (December 1939), the Offences Against the State Acts (1939, 1940), internment at the Curragh, executions and hunger-strike deaths.

Economy and supplies
- [ ] S1 Lemass and the Department of Supplies (1939–45).
- [ ] S2 Rationing and shortages: tea, sugar, butter, flour, bread, fuel; petrol restrictions; gas limits and glimmer men.
- [ ] S3 Compulsory tillage and the wheat harvest.
- [ ] S4 Turf as a substitute for coal.
- [ ] S5 Irish Shipping Ltd (1941) and the merchant seamen.
- [ ] S6 The Wages Standstill Order and the Trade Union Act (1941): falling real incomes.
- [ ] S7 Agriculture (cattle exports, guaranteed prices) versus industry (raw material shortages).

Relations with the great powers
- [ ] R1 The June 1940 unity offer (Malcolm MacDonald) and de Valera's refusal.
- [ ] R2 Churchill, the ports and the battle of the Atlantic; British pressure and restrictions on supplies.
- [ ] R3 The USA: David Gray; US entry into the war; the American Note (February 1944) and its refusal; British travel restrictions.

Help to the Allies and the bombing
- [ ] H1 Intelligence co-operation (G2), the arrest of German agents (Görtz), internment of German servicemen.
- [ ] H2 The Donegal air corridor and release of Allied airmen.
- [ ] H3 The Blacksod weather report (June 1944) and D-Day.
- [ ] H4 Irish volunteers in the British forces, and the 1945 penalty on deserters.

- [ ] H6 Fire brigades sent to Belfast (April 1941).

End of the war
- [ ] W1 De Valera's condolence visit to Hempel on Hitler's death (2 May 1945) and the comparable visit on Roosevelt's death.

Society and assessment
- [ ] C1 Emigration and war work in Britain; remittances.
- [ ] C2 Society: unemployment, prices, hardship of the poor, little protest; first steps in social policy (Children's Allowances Act 1944; separate Department of Health 1947).

## 3. Traps

Do not use (wrong or doubtful facts in the current notes):
- Do not use: "from under 20,000 men to about 40,000 by 1941", because strengths vary by source and by what is counted (regular army, reserves, LDF). Say the army was greatly expanded.
- Do not use: "50,000 to 70,000" for those who served in the British forces, because estimates vary and depend on whether Northern Ireland is counted. Say tens of thousands.
- Do not use: de Valera rejected the 1940 offer "because the Northern government was not consulted", because this is not a well-established reason. Give his doubt that Britain could deliver unity and his distrust of the offer.
- Do not use: "the British considered occupying the ports in 1940–41" as a dated fact; say only that Churchill complained and that no seizure took place.
- Do not use: the date 3 September for the declaration of neutrality; the Dáil's emergency resolution was 2 September and the Act 3 September.
- Do not use: the Censorship of Publications Board "active since 1929" as the date of the Board; the Act was 1929.
- Do not use: "West Germany paid compensation" with any year or sum.
- Do not use: "Churchill's speech had shown how a great power might justify infringing a small state's rights" as a quotation; give de Valera's reply as reported thought only.

Confusable terms and dates
- Dáil emergency resolution (2 September) vs Emergency Powers Act (3 September 1939).
- Local Defence Force (volunteer army reserve) vs Local Security Force (civil defence with the Gardaí).
- Offences Against the State Act 1939 (powers against the IRA) vs the 1940 Amendment Act (internment).
- The 1940 unity offer (June–July 1940, British) vs the American Note (1944, American; the Axis diplomats).
- North Strand (Dublin, 1941, error) vs Belfast blitz (Northern Ireland, April and May 1941).
- Neutrality was formal but benevolent; do not describe Ireland as pro-German or as an Allied member.
- Ireland's "Emergency" vs the Emergency Powers Act: the Act's powers continued through the war years.

## 4. What exams ask

- Higher 100-mark essay, the commonest form: the impact of the war on both parts of Ireland, set North against South (several years). For the South, marks go to a clear judgement (neutrality kept independence but at a heavy cost), with political, economic and social effects each shown with a dated example (Emergency Powers Act, rationing, Lemass, American Note, Churchill's broadcast).
- Strong answers argue a thesis about neutrality: a success for sovereignty and survival, against a limit in austerity, censorship and isolation. Name a historian (Lee, Girvin) to add weight.
- Explaining why Ireland stayed neutral: sovereignty, partition, the ports, weak defences, Civil War divisions. Students should rank reasons or link them, not just list them.
- Showing neutrality was "benevolent" or "partial": intelligence, air corridor, weather report, Allied airmen. Linking this to the pressure from Churchill and the United States gives a balanced answer.
- Economy and society: rationing, shortages, tillage, turf, wage controls, emigration. Better answers use the Department of Supplies and the Wages Standstill Order as specific evidence, and note who suffered (the urban poor, wage earners) and who gained (farmers).
- Control of opinion: censorship and the Emergency Powers Act, and what they say about conformity; a good answer also notes the state's firm treatment of the IRA.
- Ordinary Level short answers and 30/40-mark paragraphs: reasons for neutrality, what censorship banned, what was rationed, who Lemass was and his department, the American Note and why de Valera refused, the Churchill-de Valera exchange. Each correct, dated statement earns marks, so notes should supply short, exact statements.
- Document questions: a source such as an extract from the radio replies or the Note needs context (what was asked, by whom, the date, de Valera's response).
