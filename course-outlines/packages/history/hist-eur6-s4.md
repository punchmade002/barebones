## 1. Must cover (one bullet or block for every item; tick each off)

Background (assumed)
- [ ] A1 (assumed) Cold War context: containment and the Truman Doctrine (1947); the Soviet atomic bomb (1949); the communist victory in China (October 1949); the Korean War (from June 1950). These fed fear of subversion at home.
- [ ] A2 (assumed) Congressional committees could summon witnesses; the Fifth Amendment; the Senate and the House as separate chambers.
- [ ] A3 (assumed) Segregation in the South (Jim Crow) and the Black freedom struggle; the Vietnam War's escalation under Johnson from 1965 (detail belongs to the Johnson and Vietnam case study).

Key concepts (each defined as used in this topic; also in keyTerms)
- [ ] K1 Public opinion: what ordinary Americans thought, measured by polls and press, which set limits on what presidents could do abroad.
- [ ] K2 Mass media: newspapers, radio and above all television, which carried McCarthy, Vietnam and the civil rights struggle to a national audience.
- [ ] K3 Liberalism: in US usage, support for civil liberties, civil rights and an active federal government; attacked by McCarthyism, and defended by civil rights and anti-war campaigners.
- [ ] K4 McCarthyism and Red Scare (essential terms): reckless accusation of communist subversion; the wider wave of fear.

The Red Scare before and beside McCarthy
- [ ] R1 Truman's loyalty programme (March 1947): loyalty boards and investigation of federal employees.
- [ ] R2 HUAC and Hollywood: the October 1947 hearings, the Hollywood Ten, contempt convictions, prison terms.
- [ ] R3 The Waldorf Statement (November 1947) and the Hollywood blacklist.
- [ ] R4 The Hiss case: Chambers's accusation (1948), perjury conviction (January 1950), Nixon's part.
- [ ] R5 The Rosenbergs: arrest (1950), trial, execution (June 1953); Klaus Fuchs as the link in the fear of atomic spies.
- [ ] R6 Legislation and prosecutions: the Smith Act trial and Dennis v. United States; the McCarran Internal Security Act (September 1950) and the veto override.
- [ ] R7 Effects on society: loyalty oaths, lost jobs, blacklists, fear of dissent; teachers, unionists, entertainers.

McCarthy: rise, methods and fall
- [ ] M1 The Wheeling speech (9 February 1950) and the context that made it a sensation.
- [ ] M2 The Tydings Committee (1950) and the defeat of Tydings.
- [ ] M3 Methods: guilt by association, unnamed lists and shifting numbers, personal smears; Herblock's coining of "McCarthyism" (1950).
- [ ] M4 Peak of power: Republican victory in 1952, chair of the Permanent Subcommittee on Investigations (1953); Voice of America and the overseas libraries (1953, Cohn and Schine).
- [ ] M5 Margaret Chase Smith's Declaration of Conscience (June 1950); McCarthy's attack on George Marshall (1951–52).
- [ ] M6 The turn: Murrow's See It Now (9 March 1954); the Army–McCarthy hearings (April–June 1954, televised); Welch's rebuke.
- [ ] M7 The censure vote (2 December 1954, 67 to 22) and McCarthy's collapse; death in 1957.
- [ ] M8 Eisenhower's attitude: reluctance to confront McCarthy publicly; Executive Order 10450 (April 1953).

Key personality: Joe McCarthy (one block, every point)
- [ ] P1 Who he was: born 1908, Grand Chute, Wisconsin; lawyer and circuit judge; Marine in the Second World War; Republican senator for Wisconsin from 1947 (elected 1946).
- [ ] P2 What he did, as a direct participant: the Wheeling speech, the investigations of 1953–54, the dates above.
- [ ] P3 Shaped by events: he exploited the Hiss conviction, the fall of China, the Soviet bomb, Fuchs and Korea; the Red Scare existed before him and he rode it.
- [ ] P4 His fall as shaped by events: television exposure, the army hearings, Senate colleagues, Eisenhower's refusal to help him.
- [ ] P5 Contemporaries: many Republicans and conservatives, and much of the Catholic and Midwest public, backed him; liberals, most of the press and many Senate colleagues denounced his methods.
- [ ] P6 Historians: Rovere (demagogue); Schrecker (McCarthyism a wider system he exploited); Evans (revisionist defence, using Venona); Venona proved real Soviet spying but did not vindicate McCarthy's methods or lists.
- [ ] P7 Death and legacy: died 2 May 1957; "McCarthyism" became a word for smear and witch-hunt.

McCarthyism and US foreign policy
- [ ] F1 The "loss of China" charge (1949–50) and the attack on the State Department.
- [ ] F2 The China experts: Lattimore, Davies, Service; purge and loss of Asian expertise.
- [ ] F3 Political cost of negotiating with communists: Democrats on the defensive, constraints in the Korean and Chinese crises.
- [ ] F4 Eisenhower's caution: Dulles's State Department, security checks, avoiding open conflict with McCarthy.
- [ ] F5 The damage to America's image abroad: Soviet and allied criticism.

The anti-war movement, 1965–1971
- [ ] W1 Origins: the Michigan teach-in (24 March 1965) and the spread of teach-ins.
- [ ] W2 SDS: founding, Port Huron Statement, the April 1965 march, later fragmentation.
- [ ] W3 The draft: deferments, class and race, draft-card burning, evasion to Canada, the first lottery (1 December 1969).
- [ ] W4 Martin Luther King's Riverside Church speech (4 April 1967) linking Vietnam to the poor and to violence.
- [ ] W5 Muhammad Ali's refusal of induction (28 April 1967) and its consequences.
- [ ] W6 The March on the Pentagon (21 October 1967) and Mailer's The Armies of the Night.
- [ ] W7 Chicago, August 1968: the Democratic Convention, police violence on television.
- [ ] W8 1969: the Moratorium (15 October), Nixon's "silent majority" speech (3 November), the November march in Washington.
- [ ] W9 Cambodia and Kent State (30 April and 4 May 1970); Jackson State (May 1970); campus strike.
- [ ] W10 The Pentagon Papers (13 June 1971) and the Supreme Court ruling (30 June 1971).
- [ ] W11 Effects on policy: Johnson's March 1968 decision (pointer only); pressure on Nixon to withdraw troops and to Vietnamise; the Gulf of Tonkin Resolution repealed (January 1971).

Race relations and the Cold War image of the USA
- [ ] N1 Truman and desegregation of the armed forces (Executive Order 9981, July 1948) and the Cold War motive.
- [ ] N2 Brown v. Board of Education (17 May 1954) and the Justice Department's Cold War argument in the case.
- [ ] N3 Little Rock (September 1957): federal troops, world press, Soviet use of the pictures.
- [ ] N4 Freedom Rides (1961) and Birmingham (1963) as international news.
- [ ] N5 Soviet and Chinese propaganda, and newly independent African and Asian states as the audience.
- [ ] N6 Black leaders and foreign policy: King and Malcolm X.
- [ ] N7 The Civil Rights Act (1964) and Voting Rights Act (1965); Cold War credibility as one reason among several.

Assessment (paired judgement points, final block)
- [ ] S1 McCarthyism: strongest constraint in the early 1950s; damaged civil liberties and Asian expertise; but the Red Scare predated and outlasted him, and some spying was real.
- [ ] S2 The anti-war movement: shaped public opinion, Johnson's decision of 1968 and the pace of Nixon's withdrawals; but it was divided, often unpopular, and the war continued for years.
- [ ] S3 Race relations: an embarrassment that pushed leaders towards reform; but reform was driven mainly by the movement itself and domestic politics.
- [ ] S4 Overall: domestic opinion limited and shaped policy, but strategic and Cold War considerations stayed the main drivers; contemporary vs later views.

| R1 | Executive Order 9835, March 1947: loyalty programme for federal employees, with loyalty review boards and an Attorney General's list of "subversive" organisations. |

Time-bar prevented a spy charge, so he was tried for perjury: first trial ended in a hung jury (1949); convicted January 1950; served about three and a half years. Richard Nixon, a California congressman on HUAC, pressed the case. Historians still debate Hiss's guilt, though most think he was guilty. |
| R5 | Julius and Ethel Rosenberg: arrested 1950, convicted 1951 of conspiracy to commit espionage (passing atomic secrets to the USSR), executed 19 June 1953 at Sing Sing. Klaus Fuchs, a German-born British physicist who had worked on the Manhattan Project, confessed in January 1950 and was arrested in February 1950; his confession led to the Rosenbergs' ring. |
| R6 | Smith Act (1940) trial of eleven Communist Party leaders, 1949; convictions upheld in Dennis v. United States (1951). McCarran Internal Security Act, September 1950: Communist organisations had to register; Truman vetoed it; Congress overrode the veto. |
| R7 | Loyalty oaths were widely required of public employees, including teachers. Specific numbers of people dismissed: do not give. |

| M3 | Herblock (Herbert Block, Washington Post cartoonist) coined "McCarthyism" in a cartoon of March 1950. Methods: guilt by association, accusations without evidence, unnamed lists, bullying witnesses. |

| M5 | Margaret Chase Smith, Republican senator from Maine, delivered her "Declaration of Conscience" in June 1950, criticising smear tactics without naming McCarthy. In 1951 McCarthy attacked General George Marshall; Eisenhower, campaigning in 1952, did not defend Marshall in McCarthy's presence in Wisconsin. |
| M6 | Edward R. Murrow's CBS programme See It Now, 9 March 1954, was built from McCarthy's own speeches and film. Joseph Welch, the army's counsel, said on 9 June 1954 (as McCarthy attacked a young lawyer in Welch's firm): "Have you no sense of decency, sir?" |

| M8 | Eisenhower signed Executive Order 10450 in April 1953, replacing Truman's loyalty standard with "security risk" criteria for federal employment; many dismissals followed. Eisenhower avoided naming McCarthy in public for most of 1953–54 and kept his criticism private or indirect. |

| F3 | John Foster Dulles was secretary of state 1953–59; his department, with security chief Scott McLeod, applied the new security standards. |

## 3. Traps

Do not use (wrong or doubtful facts in the current notes):
- Do not use: "the Moratorium demonstrations of 15 October and 15 November 1969", because only 15 October was the Moratorium; the November march was a separate national mobilisation.
- Do not use: Hiss's accuser Chambers "said he was a Soviet agent" (fact card), because Chambers said Hiss was a communist who passed documents to him; Chambers described himself as a courier.
- Do not use: "Kent State fuelled Cooper-Church", because the Cambodian incursion prompted that amendment; Kent State strengthened support for it at most.
- Do not use: "Eisenhower's private disapproval was not made clear until later", because the evidence for his private views is mixed; say only that he avoided public confrontation for most of 1953–54.
- Do not use: Eisenhower's State Department adopted security checks "that McCarthy had demanded", because the checks came from Executive Order 10450 and Dulles's own policy; say only that the climate pressed the administration.
- Do not use: the Civil Rights Act and Voting Rights Act "were framed in part as steps needed to maintain US credibility", because that is a historians' argument, not the stated purpose of either law.
- Do not use: Wheeling "the number he gave" with any figure (205, 57), because the speech text is disputed and the figures varied.
- Do not use: "McCarthy was elected in 1946" as "senator from 1946"; he took his seat in January 1947.
- Do not use: Tydings's wording as a quotation unless you attribute it to the committee.
- Doubtful quotations: do not use "Are you now or have you ever been a member of the Communist Party?" as McCarthy's; it was HUAC's standard question. Do not put any other McCarthy line in quotation marks.

Confusable people, terms and dates
- HUAC (House, 1938 onwards, Hollywood 1947) vs McCarthy's Senate Permanent Subcommittee on Investigations (1953). McCarthy was never on HUAC.
- Hollywood Ten (1947 hearings) vs the blacklist (Waldorf Statement) vs McCarthy's own investigations, which did not concern Hollywood.
- Hiss (State Department, perjury 1950) vs the Rosenbergs (atomic spies, executed 1953) vs Fuchs (British physicist) vs Chambers (accuser).
- Truman's loyalty programme (1947) vs Eisenhower's security programme (1953).
- The Tydings Committee (1950) vs the Army–McCarthy hearings (1954) vs the Watkins Committee (which recommended the censure, 1954).
- Smith Act (1940, used 1949) vs McCarran Act (1950) vs Taft-Hartley Act (1947, labour law, not in this subsection).
- Welch (army counsel) vs Murrow (broadcaster). Cohn vs Schine.
- Kent State (4 May 1970, Ohio, National Guard) vs Jackson State (15 May 1970, Mississippi) vs Chicago 1968.
- Pentagon march (1967, protest) vs Pentagon Papers (1971, leaked study).
- Muhammad Ali's draft refusal (April 1967) vs the 1971 Supreme Court ruling.
- Executive Order 9835 (Truman, 1947) vs 10450 (Eisenhower, 1953) vs 9981 (desegregating the army, 1948).

## 4. What exams ask

- Higher 100-mark essay, the commonest form: how McCarthyism and/or the anti-war movement affected US foreign policy or society, usually asked as a comparison ("different ways and at different times"). Marks go to a clear thesis and a contrast: McCarthyism narrowed choices on China and Asia in the early 1950s; the anti-war movement worked later, through public opinion, on Johnson in 1968 and Nixon after. Open with the judgement; give dated evidence for each side.
- Combined questions that pair McCarthyism with another topic (the military-industrial complex, the Moon landing, Graham, Mailer): allow one tight section of about a third of the essay for McCarthy, with the Wheeling speech, three methods, the 1954 hearings and the censure, then a judgement on his significance.
- Ordinary Level paragraph on McCarthy (30 or 40 marks): who he was (Republican senator for Wisconsin), the Wheeling speech with its date and claim, methods (guilt by association, unnamed lists, smears), the Army–McCarthy hearings, the censure, and one effect on foreign policy (the State Department and China). Each correct, dated statement earns marks, so keep to four or five dated points.
- Ordinary Level document questions on McCarthy ask for the author's view of him, why he was believed, and what he wanted; answers need his role, the Cold War fears he used and the way public opinion turned on him in 1954.
- Anti-war movement paragraphs: where it began (campuses, the 1965 teach-ins), who led (SDS), how the draft fed it, the key events in order (Pentagon 1967, Chicago 1968, Kent State 1970, Pentagon Papers 1971), and what it achieved. Examiners credit naming Ali's refusal and King's 1967 speech as separate strands.
- "Effect" questions reward a balanced paragraph on limits as well as achievements: protests did not end the war directly; many Americans (Nixon's "silent majority") disapproved of protesters.
- Race and the Cold War image appears as a support for a comparison essay; a single paragraph on Little Rock or Brown and the Soviet response earns credit when linked to foreign policy rather than just narrated.
- Strong answers separate the Red Scare as a wider system (HUAC, FBI, loyalty boards, both parties) from McCarthy as a person, and name a historian (Rovere, Schrecker) for each view.
- Cause-and-effect: why McCarthy rose (Hiss, Fuchs, China, Korea, fear) and why he fell (television, the army hearings, loss of Eisenhower's and Senate support).
