# Package: hist-ire5-s10 — Case study: the Apprentice Boys of Derry

- **loId:** `hist-ire5-s10` · **chapterId:** `hist-ire5`
- **Syllabus:** Later Modern Ireland, Topic 5 (Politics and society in Northern Ireland, 1949–1993), case study: the Apprentice Boys of Derry (I5 C-CS). Outline scope: the siege of Derry (shutting of the gates December 1688, Lundy, the relief 1689); the founding of Apprentice Boys clubs and the parent club; the Memorial Hall; the August (relief) and December (shutting/Lundy) parades and their rituals; the walls and the Bogside; meaning for unionists (siege mentality, "No Surrender") and for nationalists (triumphalism); the 12 August 1969 parade and the Battle of the Bogside; parades banned and restored in the 1970s; the organisation's relationship with Derry. Key concepts: cultural traditions, cultural identity, sectarianism, tolerance and intolerance. No key personality is assigned. Write in full Higher Level detail.

## 1. Must cover (tick each off)

Background (assumed)
- [ ] A1 (assumed) Derry (Londonderry) is a walled city on the River Foyle, planted by London companies in the early 1600s as a Protestant settlement; hence the formal name Londonderry.
- [ ] A2 (assumed) The Glorious Revolution: the Catholic James II was replaced by the Protestant William of Orange in 1688–89; James fought to regain his thrones from Ireland (Williamite-Jacobite war).
- [ ] A3 (assumed) Northern Ireland's two communities: unionist/Protestant and nationalist/Catholic; the civil rights movement of the late 1960s (links to neighbouring subsections).

Key concepts (also in keyTerms)
- [ ] K1 Cultural traditions: the distinct heritage, customs and public commemorations of each community.
- [ ] K2 Cultural identity: a community's sense of belonging through shared history, symbols and commemorations.
- [ ] K3 Sectarianism: hostility between Protestants and Catholics based on religious identity and political allegiance.
- [ ] K4 Tolerance and intolerance: acceptance or refusal of the other community's identity and public displays.
- [ ] K5 Siege mentality (essential term).
- [ ] K6 Triumphalism (essential term).

The siege of Derry, 1688–89
- [ ] S1 Shutting of the gates, December 1688: thirteen apprentice boys closed the gates against a regiment of James II's army.
- [ ] S2 Lundy: governor who favoured negotiating with James; distrusted, left the city in April 1689; his name became a Protestant byword for traitor.
- [ ] S3 The siege proper from April 1689, lasting 105 days; Walker and Baker as defence leaders.
- [ ] S4 Hunger and disease; the garrison eating animals.
- [ ] S5 The relief: Mountjoy broke the boom across the Foyle, 28 July 1689; the Jacobites withdrew at the end of July / start of August.
- [ ] S6 Symbols of the siege: the walls, the crimson flag, the cannon Roaring Meg.
- [ ] S7 Wider context: the Boyne, 1690.

Founding and organisation
- [ ] F1 The parent club founded 1814 in Derry to commemorate the siege; the thirteen apprentices as the symbol.
- [ ] F2 Members: Protestant tradesmen and businessmen; male Protestant membership; part of the network of loyal Protestant bodies.
- [ ] F3 Growth: further clubs and branches spread beyond Derry; a central governing body.
- [ ] F4 The Memorial Hall, Society Street, opened 1877.

Parades and rituals
- [ ] R1 The August parade (Relief of Derry): the largest event; route round the walls.
- [ ] R2 The December commemoration (Shutting of the Gates): service at St Columb's Cathedral and the burning of a Lundy effigy.
- [ ] R3 Rituals: bands, banners, crimson sashes and regalia, a cannon, crimson flags, wreaths, speeches.
- [ ] R4 The walls as the focus; the Bogside lying below them.

Meaning
- [ ] M1 Unionists: defence of Protestant civil and religious liberty; siege mentality; "No Surrender" as defiance.
- [ ] M2 Nationalists: parades as triumphalism, a celebration of conquest and ascendancy, passing a Catholic district.
- [ ] M3 Sectarianism, tolerance and intolerance applied to the parades.

Derry before and in 1969
- [ ] D1 Derry: Catholic majority in population, unionist control of the Corporation through gerrymandering; housing and jobs grievances.
- [ ] D2 The Bogside: Catholic, working class, below the walls.
- [ ] D3 The parade of 12 August 1969: clashes, the Battle of the Bogside (12–14 August), barricades, CS gas, "Free Derry".
- [ ] D4 British troops deployed on 14 August 1969; the link to the outbreak of the Troubles.
- [ ] D5 Two readings: nationalists saw provocation; the Apprentice Boys said it was a legal traditional march.

The 1970s and after
- [ ] E1 Parades restricted, rerouted or banned in some years in the early 1970s because of violence, then restored.
- [ ] E2 The IRA's destruction of the Walker Pillar on the walls (1973).
- [ ] E3 Population shift: Protestants moving from the west bank to the Waterside, deepening the sense of siege.
- [ ] E4 Later: negotiated parade routes and the organisation's relationship with Derry (local dialogue, security planning).

Assessment
- [ ] X1 Achievements: kept a commemorative tradition and shared identity for Protestant Derry; an example of heritage and cultural expression.
- [ ] X2 Limits: route past the Bogside made it a point of conflict and a symbol of division.
- [ ] X3 Contemporary views: unionists (heritage, loyalty) vs nationalists (triumphalism, provocation).
- [ ] X4 Later view: historians see a real commemoration and a political statement whose meaning changed with the conflict.

## 3. Traps

Do not use (in the current notes)
- "Siege lifted on 1 August 1689" as the single date: sources vary (relief ship 28 July, Jacobite withdrawal 31 July/1 August). Give "late July–early August".
- "Burning of the Lundy effigy dates from 1832": not reliably dated; say it is a long-standing tradition.
- "The No Surrender Club followed in 1824", "a General Committee was set up in 1861" and "Dublin Apprentice Boys Society formed December 1813": not reliably checked; leave out.
- "December commemoration now on the first Saturday" and "August on the second Saturday": not checked; say "a Saturday near" the traditional date.
- "The Bogside barricades gave rise to the name Free Derry": the slogan was painted in January 1969; the barricades of August 1969 made it a reality.
- "Membership grew through the nineteenth century" and "branches in Britain": unsupported detail; keep to "spread beyond Derry".
- "The Memorial Hall was opened in honour of the thirteen apprentices" as the stated purpose: treat as general ("a memorial and headquarters").
- Do not give siege casualty or food-price figures.
- Do not state which side started the 1969 stone-throwing; give both accounts.

Confusable people, terms and dates
- Shutting of the Gates (December 1688, apprentices) vs the Relief (July–August 1689, Mountjoy): the two parades mark different events.
- Walker (governor, siege hero; the pillar) vs Lundy (the traitor).
- Derry vs Londonderry: nationalists say Derry, unionists often Londonderry; use "Derry" and mention the dual name once.
- Apprentice Boys vs the Orange Order (a separate loyal order, Boyne anniversary 12 July): do not blur them.
- Siege of Derry (1688–89) vs the 1969 "Battle of the Bogside" vs Bloody Sunday (1972): three different events.

## 4. What exams ask

- Higher 100-mark essay: how far the Apprentice Boys contributed to cultural identity, or whether their activities were controversial or divisive, often paired with another topic. Marks go to a stated judgement with detail from both communities and dated events (1688–89, 1814, 1877, 1969, 1970s).
- Significance for unionists and nationalists: students must give each side's reading of the same parade (heritage and liberty vs triumphalism and provocation), using the key concepts by name.
- Document-based question (2011): compare what two documents show about the Apprentice Boys (uniform, ritual, religion, crowds); cite details from the document and add outside context such as the siege and the August route.
- Ordinary Level 40-mark paragraph: founding in 1814, who the members were, the August and December events with their rituals, the route by the Bogside, why nationalists objected. Each correct, dated point earns marks.
- Short answers (6 marks): the date of the shutting of the gates, who organised a parade, what an apprentice boy is.
- Cause and consequence: how the 12 August 1969 parade led to the Battle of the Bogside and army deployment.
- Judgement material: achievement (preserving a tradition) vs limit (a flashpoint), unionist vs nationalist views, contemporary vs later views.
