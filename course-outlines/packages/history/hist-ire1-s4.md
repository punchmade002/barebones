# Package: hist-ire1-s4 — Private responses to Famine and the Famine's impact

- **loId:** `hist-ire1-s4` · **chapterId:** `hist-ire1`
- **Syllabus:** Later Modern Ireland, Topic 1 (Ireland and the Union, 1815–1870), case study "private responses to Famine, 1845–1849"; emigration (Famine emigration). Outline scope: the Society of Friends (Central Relief Committee, soup kitchens, reports); the British Relief Association; other charities and international giving; the churches, including "souperism" and its legacy; landlord responses, from relief to clearances and assisted emigration; local committees; Asenath Nicholson's travels, relief work and writings; what private relief could and could not achieve and why it shrank after 1847; the impact of the Famine to 1870 (population fall, end of the cottier class, consolidation, Encumbered Estates Act and new landlords, shift to pasture, late marriage and dowry, emigration as a habit, decline of the Irish language, political memory). Key personality: Asenath Nicholson. Key concepts used: famine, landlordism, dowry (also laissez-faire and evangelicalism as background). Write in full Higher Level detail.

## 1. Must cover (one bullet or block for every item; tick each off)

Background (assumed)
- [ ] A1 (assumed) Potato blight from September 1845; dependence on the potato; the Famine's worst years (1846–47, "Black '47") and the west and south-west worst hit (detail in the notes on the Famine and government responses).
- [ ] A2 (assumed) Government relief in outline: Peel's 1845–46 measures, Whig laissez-faire, public works, soup kitchens (1847), the Poor Law Extension Act (1847) with the Gregory clause. Private relief is judged against this.
- [ ] A3 (assumed) Laissez-faire and the principle that Irish property should support Irish poverty, which left gaps that charity tried to fill.
- [ ] A4 (assumed) Landlords, cottiers and labourers; rents, arrears and the poor rate paid by landlords.

Key concepts and terms (each defined as used; also in keyTerms)
- [ ] K1 Famine: the Great Famine of 1845–1850, a catastrophe of mass death, disease and emigration.
- [ ] K2 Landlordism: the system of landlord ownership; after the Famine a target of lasting anger.
- [ ] K3 Dowry: money or stock a bride's family gave the groom's family under the post-Famine farm match.
- [ ] K4 Souperism; Central Relief Committee; British Relief Association; assisted emigration; Encumbered Estates Act; consolidation (essential terms).

Case study: private responses, 1845–1849
- [ ] C1 Why private relief mattered: government limits, so charity filled gaps in food, clothing, seed and medical care; who gave (Irish committees, landlords, churches, Quakers, British and overseas donors, emigrants).
- [ ] C2 Local relief committees under Peel (1845–46): landlords, clergy and merchants raised subscriptions, with government grants added; the poorest western districts raised least.
- [ ] C3 Publicity: reports and drawings from Skibbereen, Co. Cork (winter 1846–47), the Illustrated London News, and the appeals of 1847.
- [ ] C4 Society of Friends: formation of the Central Relief Committee (Dublin, 13 November 1846) and its leading members.
- [ ] C5 Friends: eyewitness tours and reports (Forster and others, winter 1846–47); their trusted reports.
- [ ] C6 Friends: soup kitchens in Cork and elsewhere; boilers, food and money to local committees; food from American Quakers and donors.
- [ ] C7 Friends: non-sectarian, no proselytising, careful accounts, hence trust; later withdrawal from mass feeding (1847) and turn to seed, fisheries and spinning schemes; the Transactions (1852).
- [ ] C8 British Relief Association: founding (1 January 1847), founders, aims, royal letter and Queen's gift, day of fast (March 1847), amount raised, co-operation with the government.
- [ ] C9 British Relief Association: Strzelecki and the feeding of schoolchildren in the poorest western unions, 1847–48.
- [ ] C10 International giving: American relief ships (Jamestown, Macedonian), emigrant remittances and donations, the Choctaw Nation, India, Australia, Canada, the Ottoman Sultan, Pope Pius IX's encyclical (March 1847).
- [ ] C11 Churches: Catholic priests and Church of Ireland clergy on committees, letters, collections, and deaths from fever; nursing orders (Sisters of Charity, Sisters of Mercy).
- [ ] C12 Souperism: the charge, its context (Nangle's Achill colony from 1834; Irish Church Missions, 1849), the fact that most Protestant relief was unconditional, and its bitter legacy.
- [ ] C13 Landlords who helped: rent cuts, food imports, soup kitchens, employment; some ruined or died of fever.
- [ ] C14 Landlords who cleared: debt, arrears, poor rates, the Gregory clause, the under-£4 rating rule; evictions in Clare, Mayo and Galway; Kilrush and Captain Kennedy; police returns from 1849.
- [ ] C15 Assisted emigration: Major Denis Mahon (Strokestown, 1847) and larger later schemes such as Lansdowne's at Kenmare.
- [ ] C16 Outcomes: lives saved, speed and reach, exposure of suffering.
- [ ] C17 Limits: small beside the government's spending; uneven; short-lived.
- [ ] C18 Why giving shrank after 1847: donor fatigue, the 1847 commercial crisis, belief the Famine was over, anger at the 1848 rising, and the Quakers' judgement that only the state could cope.

Famine emigration (syllabus item)
- [ ] E1 Scale and destinations: over a million left in 1846–1851 (the US, Britain, Canada, some Australia); the poorest went to Britain and Canada.
- [ ] E2 Conditions: crowded sailing ships and fever (the "coffin ships" label), quarantine at Grosse Île, Quebec in 1847; the death of many on the voyage.
- [ ] E3 Assisted versus unassisted emigration; remittances from the first emigrants paid for later passages (link to emigration as a habit; full treatment in the notes on emigration, 1815–1914).

Key personality: Asenath Nicholson (one block, every point)
- [ ] P1 Who she was: American teacher from Vermont, widowed, worked among the New York Irish poor; temperance and vegetarian diet.
- [ ] P2 First visit 1844: walked through almost every county, staying in cabins and reading the Bible to the people she met.
- [ ] P3 Ireland's Welcome to the Stranger (1847): pre-Famine poverty and dependence on the potato.
- [ ] P4 Relief work, returning in 1846: Dublin soup kitchen in the Liberties; travels in the west; funded by appeals in New York papers; independent of committees; left in 1848.
- [ ] P5 Participant and witness: gave relief and recorded what she saw at first hand.
- [ ] P6 Writings: Lights and Shades of Ireland (1850) and Annals of the Famine in Ireland (1851); she blamed the system, landlords and proselytisers.
- [ ] P7 Contemporaries saw her as eccentric; some suspicion as a Protestant Bible reader, while the poor welcomed her.
- [ ] P8 Historians value her as one of the most vivid and sympathetic eyewitnesses, a woman relief worker; death in 1855.

Impact of the Famine to 1870
- [ ] I1 Population fall: about 8.2 million (1841), 6.6 million (1851), 5.4 million (1871); death and emigration.
- [ ] I2 End of the cottier and labourer classes; end of the potato-dependent class.
- [ ] I3 Consolidation: fewer small holdings (1 to 5 acres), more farms above 15 acres; end of subdivision.
- [ ] I4 Encumbered Estates Act (1849): the court, scale of sales, mostly Irish buyers, new landlords who often cleared tenants.
- [ ] I5 Shift from tillage to pasture (cattle, sheep, less labour, British demand).
- [ ] I6 Late marriage, fewer marriages, the arranged match and the dowry; impartible inheritance, with the other children emigrating, entering the Church or marrying into other farms.
- [ ] I7 Emigration as a habit: remittances and prepaid tickets.
- [ ] I8 Decline of the Irish language: heartlands lost most people; census of 1851; English the language of advancement.
- [ ] I9 Political memory: lasting hatred of landlordism and British government at home and among Irish Americans; Fenianism and the later Land War.
- [ ] I10 A more devout farming class supporting a stronger Catholic Church (as far as it relates to the Famine's effects).

Assessment (paired judgement points, final block)
- [ ] S1 Achievement of private relief (speed, compassion, practical aid, Quaker non-sectarian reputation) vs limit (too small, uneven, shrinking after 1847).
- [ ] S2 Damage: souperism and clearances deepened suffering and division.
- [ ] S3 Impact during the Famine vs after it: death, eviction, flight; then a smaller, older, more commercial, more devout rural society.
- [ ] S4 Contemporary British views (opportunity to modernise) vs nationalist views (Mitchel); historians on continuity (changes that began before 1845 speeded up).

## 4. What exams ask

- Higher 100-mark essay, very common: how effective were government and private responses to the Famine (2007, 2012, 2015, 2018, 2019, 2021, 2022). Marks go to a stated judgement and paired points: private relief was quick and reached remote places, but it was small, uneven and shrank after 1847. A one-sided account of only the Quakers earns less; compare with the government and give dated examples.
- Higher essay on the Famine's impact during and after (2006, 2025): sort into impact during (death, disease, eviction, emigration) and after to 1870 (population, consolidation, pasture, late marriage and dowry, language). Explain cause and result, not just list.
- Trevelyan and/or Nicholson (2008, 2010, 2016, 2017, 2020, 2023): for Nicholson give her role as witness and as participant, with at least two dated books or actions; contrast her independent relief with the government's.
- Ordinary Level 40-mark paragraph on private relief: Quakers (Central Relief Committee, date, soup kitchens, non-sectarian) and the British Relief Association (date, amount, Strzelecki). Each correct dated statement earns marks.
- Ordinary Level 40-mark paragraph on Nicholson: American, first came 1844, walked the counties, the 1847 book, relief in 1846 onward, later books.
- Ordinary Level short answers: name the committee or association, a date, a donor country, or what "souperism" or the dowry meant.
- Marks are lost for treating "private relief" as only the Quakers, for forgetting landlords and churches, and for omitting why relief shrank after 1847.
- Examiners reward linking cause and effect: why the Encumbered Estates Act created new landlords, why consolidation led to late marriage and the dowry.
