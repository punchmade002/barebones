## 1. Must cover (one bullet or block for every item; tick each off)

Background: the Ottoman Empire and the Eastern Question
- [ ] O1 (assumed) The Ottoman Empire in 1815: ruled the Balkans, including Greece, Serbia and the Danubian principalities (Moldavia and Wallachia), under the Sultan in Constantinople; a Muslim state ruling a largely Orthodox Christian population.
- [ ] O2 (assumed) The millet system: religious communities (the Orthodox under their Patriarch) ran their own religious and civil affairs, but Christians had no political rights and paid extra taxes.
- [ ] O3 (assumed) Ottoman decline: weak central control, local governors and Janissaries acting independently; Sultan Mahmud II's attempts at reform, including destroying the Janissaries in 1826.
- [ ] O4 The Eastern Question defined: what would happen to the Ottoman lands as Ottoman power declined, and which Great Power would gain.
- [ ] O5 The Great Powers' interests: Russia (fellow Orthodox, access to the Straits, influence in the principalities); Austria (feared Russian expansion and nationalism among its own Slavs, wanted the Ottoman Empire kept intact); Britain (route to India, the Straits, kept Russia out of the Mediterranean); France (influence in the Levant, protector of Catholics in the Holy Places).
- [ ] O6 Why Balkan nationalism grew: religion (Orthodoxy) and language as identity; Ottoman misrule; merchants and the diaspora; the example of the French Revolution; the hope of Russian help.

Serbia, 1804–1867
- [ ] S1 The First Serbian Uprising (1804) under Karadjordje against the Janissary leaders (the dahije) who had seized power in the pashalik of Belgrade.
- [ ] S2 The rising crushed in 1813 when Russia, at war with Napoleon, withdrew support; Karadjordje fled to Austrian territory.
- [ ] S3 The Second Serbian Uprising (1815) under Miloš Obrenović: limited fighting, then negotiation with the Ottomans.
- [ ] S4 The road to autonomy: the Akkerman Convention (1826) and the Treaty of Adrianople (1829), Russian–Ottoman agreements that bound the Sultan to Serbian autonomy; the Sultan's decree of 1830 recognising Serbia as an autonomous principality with Miloš as hereditary prince.
- [ ] S5 Limits of autonomy: Ottoman suzerainty, an annual tribute, Ottoman garrisons in the fortresses.
- [ ] S6 Internal rivalry, Obrenović vs Karadjordjević: Karadjordje murdered in 1817 on Miloš's orders (most accounts); Miloš's autocratic rule; the 1835 constitution suspended within weeks under foreign pressure; the 1838 statute and council; Miloš's abdication (1839); Mihailo; Alexander Karadjordjević (1842–58); Miloš's return (1858–60); Mihailo's second reign and the departure of the Ottoman garrisons (1867); Mihailo's assassination (1868).
- [ ] S7 Garašanin's Načertanije (1844): a plan for a larger state under Serbian leadership; shows Serbian nationalism growing in ambition.
- [ ] S8 Serbia's independence came only in 1878 (one clause; outside the dates).

Greece: the War of Independence, 1821–1829
- [ ] G1 The Philiki Etaireia (Friendly Society): founded in Odessa in 1814 by Greek merchants; secret; planned a rising; hoped for Russian support.
- [ ] G2 Alexander Ypsilantis's invasion of Moldavia (March 1821): no Russian help, and Tsar Alexander I disowned it; defeated by the Ottomans in June 1821.
- [ ] G3 The rising in the Peloponnese (March 1821): spread to central Greece and the islands; massacres on both sides (the Greeks took Tripolitsa in 1821 and killed its Muslim inhabitants).
- [ ] G4 Ottoman reprisals: Patriarch Gregory V hanged in Constantinople (April 1821); the Chios massacre (1822).
- [ ] G5 The National Assembly at Epidaurus declared independence (January 1822) and gave Greece a provisional constitution.
- [ ] G6 Greek divisions: civil strife among the Greek leaders (1823–25) weakened the rising.
- [ ] G7 Philhellenism: European enthusiasm for Greece (classical education, Christian sympathy, romanticism); volunteers and money; Byron joined the cause and died at Missolonghi (April 1824); Delacroix's paintings of Chios and Missolonghi. One bullet on the paintings; romanticism belongs to the cultural notes.
- [ ] G8 Metternich's hostility: he saw the Greeks as rebels against a legitimate ruler; the Congress of Verona (1822) refused to receive a Greek delegation. One bullet; the Congress System belongs to the Metternich notes.
- [ ] G9 Egypt intervenes: Muhammad Ali sends Ibrahim Pasha to the Peloponnese (1825); Missolonghi falls (April 1826); the Greek cause near defeat.
- [ ] G10 Great Power intervention: Nicholas I's readiness to act; the St Petersburg Protocol (1826, Britain and Russia); the Treaty of London (July 1827, Britain, France and Russia) to impose an armistice and Greek autonomy.
- [ ] G11 Navarino (20 October 1827): the allied fleet destroyed the Ottoman–Egyptian fleet; Ibrahim's forces could no longer be supplied by sea; a French force then cleared the Peloponnese (1828).
- [ ] G12 The Russo-Turkish War (1828–29) and the Treaty of Adrianople (1829): the Sultan accepted the Powers' settlement on Greece; Russia gained at the mouths of the Danube and influence in the principalities.

Greece: the kingdom, 1828–1863
- [ ] K1 Kapodistrias, former joint foreign minister of Russia, governor of Greece from 1828; assassinated in October 1831 by members of the Mavromichalis family.
- [ ] K2 The London Protocol (February 1830): Greece an independent state under the guarantee of Britain, France and Russia.
- [ ] K3 The 1832 settlement: Otto, a Bavarian prince, chosen as king (1832); arrived in 1833; frontier from the Gulf of Arta to the Gulf of Volos, leaving most Greeks outside the kingdom.
- [ ] K4 Otto's rule: Bavarian regents and officials, autocratic government; capital moved from Nafplio to Athens (1834); the revolt of September 1843 forced a constitution (1844).
- [ ] K5 The Megali Idea ("Great Idea"): the aim of uniting all Greeks in one state, voiced by Kolettis in 1844; it stayed the goal of Greek nationalism.
- [ ] K6 Otto deposed (1862); the Powers chose a Danish prince, George I (1863); Britain handed over the Ionian Islands (1864).

The Crimean War and Romania (background, brief)
- [ ] C1 Causes: the dispute over the Holy Places between Catholic (French-backed) and Orthodox (Russian-backed) monks; Menshikov's mission (1853) demanding a Russian protectorate over the Sultan's Orthodox subjects; Russia occupied the principalities (July 1853); the Ottomans declared war (October 1853); Sinope (November 1853); Britain and France declared war (March 1854).
- [ ] C2 Course in one or two bullets: Austria's pressure made Russia leave the principalities (1854); the siege of Sevastopol (1854–55), which fell in September 1855.
- [ ] C3 The Treaty of Paris (March 1856): Ottoman integrity guaranteed by the Powers; Black Sea neutralised; Russia lost southern Bessarabia; the principalities under collective Great Power guarantee instead of Russian protection; the Hatt-i Humayun (February 1856) promising equality for non-Muslims.
- [ ] C4 Romania: Moldavia and Wallachia both elected Alexandru Ioan Cuza (1859); union recognised (1861–62); Cuza deposed (1866), replaced by Carol of Hohenzollern-Sigmaringen. Still under Ottoman suzerainty until 1878.
- [ ] C5 The Crimean defeat as the trigger for emancipation in Russia (links to R-items below).

How far Balkan nationalism threatened European stability
- [ ] B1 Threat: each rising invited Great Power intervention and rivalry (Russia vs Austria and Britain); the Greek revolt split the Congress System (Britain and Russia acting apart from Metternich).
- [ ] B2 Threat: Russian–Ottoman wars (1828–29, 1853) and the Crimean War, the only war between Great Powers in 1815–1854, began over the Eastern Question.
- [ ] B3 Limit of the threat: the Powers usually acted together to contain crises (the 1827 Treaty of London, the 1830 Protocol, the 1856 Treaty of Paris) and imposed small, guaranteed states with foreign kings.
- [ ] B4 Limit of the achievement: by 1871 only a small Greece was independent; Serbia and Romania were autonomous but tributary; most Balkan Christians (Bulgarians, most Greeks, Bosnians) still lived under Ottoman rule. The Eastern Question stayed open (the later Balkan crises are a later topic).

Tsarist autocracy, 1801–1855
- [ ] A1 Autocracy in Russia: the Tsar's unlimited power; no constitution or national parliament; the nobility as servants of the state; the Orthodox Church supporting the throne.
- [ ] A2 Alexander I (reigned 1801–25): early reforming promise (the Law on Free Cultivators 1803; Speransky's plans); a constitution for the Kingdom of Poland (1815); the Holy Alliance (1815); more reactionary after 1820; serfdom untouched.
- [ ] A3 The Decembrists: secret societies of officers influenced by western ideas after 1812–15 (the Northern Society in St Petersburg; Pestel's Southern Society); the succession confusion (Constantine renounced, Nicholas succeeded); the rising in Senate Square, St Petersburg, 14 December 1825; crushed the same day; five leaders, including Pestel and Ryleev, hanged (1826); over a hundred exiled to Siberia.
- [ ] A4 Nicholas I (reigned 1825–55): the Third Section (political police, 1826); censorship; Uvarov's doctrine of Orthodoxy, Autocracy and Nationality (1833); codification of the laws under Speransky; Kiselev's reform of the state peasants (from 1837); secret committees on serfdom, but no action.
- [ ] A5 Nicholas I as "gendarme of Europe": crushed the Polish rising (1830–31) and helped Austria crush the Hungarians (1849); the Petrashevsky circle arrested (1849), Dostoevsky among them.

Serfdom in Russia
- [ ] F1 What serfdom was: peasants bound to the land and to the authority of a noble owner; landlords controlled labour, movement and marriage, punished serfs, could sell or mortgage them; serfs could be sent into the army for long service.
- [ ] F2 Extent: about 22–23 million privately owned serfs by the late 1850s, about a third of the population; with the state peasants, most Russians were unfree; serfdom had gone from western Europe (Prussia from 1807, France 1789).
- [ ] F3 Obligations: barshchina (labour dues on the lord's land, usually about three days a week) and obrok (quitrent in money or kind), obrok more common in the less fertile north, barshchina in the black-earth south; household serfs (dvorovye) as domestic servants.
- [ ] F4 Economic effects: low productivity (strip farming, the commune, no incentive to invest), little demand for manufactures, labour tied to the land; serf labour in Urals ironworks; many noble estates mortgaged to the state.
- [ ] F5 Social effects: serf unrest (many local disturbances under Nicholas I); fear of a Pugachev-style revolt.
- [ ] F6 Arguments for abolition: moral (literature such as Turgenev's A Sportsman's Sketches, 1852); economic (free labour more productive); military (the Crimean defeat showed a backward army and economy, and a serf army could not have a large trained reserve).

Alexander II's emancipation, 1861
- [ ] E1 Alexander II (reigned 1855–81): the Crimean defeat; his speech to the Moscow nobility (March 1856): better to abolish serfdom from above than wait for it to abolish itself from below (paraphrase only).
- [ ] E2 Preparation: provincial noble committees (from 1857); the Editing Commissions (from 1859) under Rostovtsev, with Nikolai Milyutin.
- [ ] E3 The edict (manifesto) of 19 February 1861 (Old Style; 3 March New Style): personal freedom for about 22–23 million privately owned serfs; rights to marry, own property, trade and go to court.
- [ ] E4 Land terms: peasants received allotments, but landlords kept much of the land and often the best of it; in the black-earth provinces peasants often lost land they had farmed (the "cut-offs", otrezki); household serfs were freed without land.
- [ ] E5 Temporary obligation: until redemption began, peasants still owed dues (labour or obrok) to the lord for their allotment; a two-year transition before the new arrangements took effect.
- [ ] E6 Redemption payments: the state paid landlords most of the land's price in bonds; peasants repaid the state over 49 years with interest, plus a part paid to the landlord directly; many historians judge the land prices too high.
- [ ] E7 The mir (obshchina, village commune): received the land, redistributed strips, was collectively responsible for taxes and redemption payments; peasants needed its permission to leave.
- [ ] E8 Other reforms that followed: the zemstva (elected local councils, 1864); the judicial reform (1864, trial by jury, independent judges); town dumas (1870); military reform with shorter service (1874, one clause only, outside the dates).
- [ ] E9 Results for peasants: personal freedom and civil rights, but land hunger, debt and poverty; peasant disturbances in 1861 (Bezdna); disappointment with the terms; redemption payments ended only from 1907.
- [ ] E10 Results for the economy: slow agricultural improvement because the mir kept strip farming and tied peasants to villages; but a gradual move to wage labour and, later, a labour supply for industry; landlords' debts often deducted from their compensation; many nobles sold land in later decades.
- [ ] E11 Results for autocracy: emancipation came from above and kept the Tsar's power intact; no national parliament; radical and revolutionary opposition grew in the 1860s and 1870s (Alexander II was assassinated in 1881: one clause only).

Assessment (end with this block, headed "Assessment: …")
- [ ] J1 Balkan achievement: autonomous Serbia (1830), independent Greece (1830–32), united Romania (1859–62): the first national states carved from the Ottoman Empire.
- [ ] J2 Balkan limit: success depended on Great Power intervention and rivalry, not on the nationalists' own strength; small states with imposed rulers; most Balkan Christians still under Ottoman rule in 1871.
- [ ] J3 Stability: a real threat (Navarino, 1828–29, the Crimean War) vs containment by Great Power co-operation (London 1827 and 1830, Paris 1856).
- [ ] J4 Emancipation achievement: ended personal bondage for about a third of the population, peacefully and from above, opening the way to further reforms.
- [ ] J5 Emancipation limit: land loss, redemption debt, the mir and continuing poverty; autocracy unchanged; contemporaries' disappointment (peasants expected all the land free) vs later historians' view of a major but flawed reform.

## 3. Traps

Do not use (doubtful or wrong in the current notes):
- Do not use "Count Sergei Uvarov" for 1833: he became a count only in 1846. Say "Sergei Uvarov, the Minister of Education".
- Do not use "privately owned serfs made up more than a third of Russia's population": estimates are close to a third and vary with what is counted. Say "about a third" (F2).
- Do not use "Speransky's codification (1833)" as a single date: the Complete Collection appeared in 1830 and the Digest in 1832–33. Use the A4 wording.
- Do not use "the Treaty of London (1832) made Greece a monarchy ... who arrived as king in 1833" together with the fact card "became the first king of independent Greece in 1832" as if they disagree: he was chosen in 1832 and arrived in 1833. Give both, as in K3.
- Do not call Kapodistrias "the first head of the Greek state" without qualification: say "the first governor of independent Greece" (K1).
- Do not say Greece's 1830 borders were "set in the south": the 1830 Protocol drew a narrower line; the Arta–Volos frontier came in 1832. Use K3 only.
- Do not say the London Protocol "recognised Greece ... with a guarantee" and the Treaty of Adrianople "ended a Russo-Ottoman war" without the dates: use 1830 and 1829 as in the table.
- Do not say the Greek kingdom was "the first independent Balkan state within the Ottoman lands": say "the first state to win full independence from the Ottoman Empire" (Serbia was autonomous earlier but not independent).
- Do not say the Crimean War "began over the protection of Christians in the Holy Places and Russian claims" and then that Britain and France went to war in 1853: the Ottomans declared war in October 1853, Britain and France in March 1854 (C1).
- Do not give "Redemption payments continued until they were cancelled in 1907" as cancelled in 1907 alone: they were halved for 1906 and abolished from 1 January 1907 (E9).
- Do not give the emancipation date 19 February 1861 without noting it is Old Style if a western date is also mentioned; never give "3 March" and "19 February" as two different events.

- Alexander II's 1856 words: paraphrase only (E1). No full sentence in quotation marks.

The phrase "sick man of Europe" may be mentioned without quotation marks as a later nickname for the Ottoman Empire, attributed to no one.

## 4. What exams ask (topics only)

- Higher 100-mark essay on Balkan nationalism (how far it succeeded, how it developed, how far it threatened stability, how far its progress depended on the Great Powers). Marks go for a thesis stated at the start (partial success, dependent on Great Power interests) and then dated evidence for each part: Serbia (1804, 1815, 1830, 1867), Greece (1821, Navarino 1827, 1830, Otto 1832), Romania (1859–62), and the Crimean War and Treaty of Paris (1856). The best answers weigh each step with "because the Powers ...", rather than telling two separate national stories.
- Higher judgement on stability: argue both sides with examples (threat: Navarino, the 1828–29 war, the Crimean War; containment: the London treaties and the Treaty of Paris) and reach a verdict. Explaining each Power's motive (Russia, Austria, Britain, France) earns marks; a list of events without motives earns little.
- Higher essays on agriculture across Europe may take Russian emancipation as one example: its terms and why it held back farming (the mir, redemption debt).
- Ordinary Level 30-mark paragraph on serfdom in Russia (the most frequent part for this subsection): what a serf was, the extent (about a third of the population), barshchina and obrok, the landlord's powers, the economic effects, and then the 1861 edict (date, personal freedom, land, redemption payments over 49 years, the mir). Each SRP is one specific fact, so students should give many short, accurate, dated facts in order.
- Ordinary Level 30-mark paragraphs on Serbia and Greece: Karadjordje 1804; Miloš 1815; autonomy 1830; the Philiki Etaireia; the 1821 rising; Epidaurus 1822; Chios; Byron at Missolonghi 1824; Navarino 20 October 1827; the London Protocol 1830; Otto. A paragraph that mixes up the two countries' leaders loses marks.
- Ordinary Level paragraph on the Decembrists and autocracy: 14 December 1825, demands (a constitution, end of serfdom), suppression, Nicholas I's repression (the Third Section, censorship, Official Nationality).
- Ordinary Level 40-mark paragraph on Balkan nationalism as a threat to stability: Great Power intervention and rivalry, with Navarino and the Crimean War as examples.
- Ordinary Level document parts (6 marks) on the emancipation edict: the purpose of the edict (to free the serfs) and what it gave or required. Students should know the edict's main terms well enough to recognise them in a document.
- Cause and result: why the serfs were freed (Crimean defeat, economic backwardness, fear of revolt, moral pressure) and the results (achievements vs limits for peasants, economy and autocracy). The Assessment block should give these as paired points.
