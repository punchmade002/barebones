## 1. Must cover (one bullet or block for every item; tick each off)

Background (assumed)
- [ ] A1 (assumed) Protection from the 1930s: Fianna Fáil's tariffs and drive for self-sufficiency (the Economic War is in the notes on earlier periods; mention only as background).
- [ ] A2 (assumed) The Control of Manufactures Acts (1932, 1934): majority Irish ownership of new firms; discouraged foreign investment.
- [ ] A3 (assumed) The 1950s: stagnation, high emigration, balance of payments crises (1951–52, 1955–56) met by deflation (detail belongs to the notes on alternating governments).
- [ ] A4 (assumed) Dependence on Britain as the main market for exports, mostly agricultural.

Key concepts
- [ ] K1 Economic planning: multi-year targets and directed public investment and incentives (also keyTerm).
- [ ] K2 Free trade: removal of tariffs and barriers between countries (also keyTerm).
- [ ] K3 Protectionism (essential term): tariffs and controls to shield home industry.
- [ ] K4 Balance of payments (essential term, key concept of neighbouring subsections): the gap between what a country earns abroad and what it spends abroad.

Case study: the First Programme for Economic Expansion, 1958–1963
- [ ] C1 Why protection and drift ended: small home market, small inefficient firms, one customer (Britain), emigration, population decline, stop-go crises.
- [ ] C2 Fianna Fáil back in government (March 1957): Lemass at Industry and Commerce, Ryan at Finance.
- [ ] C3 Whitaker as Secretary of the Department of Finance (1956): the group of younger officials; the Department's change of attitude.
- [ ] C4 Economic Development (May 1958): what it argued (protection had failed; emigration a symptom; productive over social investment; welcome foreign capital; export-led growth).
- [ ] C5 The government's acceptance and the Programme (published November 1958; five years, 1959–63).
- [ ] C6 Aims: raise output and living standards, halt emigration, create jobs in export industry and farming; modest target of about 2 per cent annual growth, deliberately low.
- [ ] C7 Measures: ending the foreign-ownership rule (1958 Act); Export Profits Tax Relief; IDA grants and a larger IDA role, with better terms in the west; Shannon Free Airport Development Company (1959); An Foras Talúntais (1958) and farm productivity; the shift of public capital to productive investment.
- [ ] C8 Results against targets: growth about 4 per cent a year; exports and industrial production up; emigration down; population up in the 1966 census.
- [ ] C9 Limits: unemployment still serious, total employment barely rose, farm jobs fell, balance of payments fragile, wage claims and inflation by the mid-1960s.
- [ ] C10 External help: boom in Britain and Europe.
- [ ] C11 Significance: start of regular planning; Second and Third Programmes; contemporary praise and cross-party support.
- [ ] C12 Differing views: turning point vs continuity (Inter-Party measures such as Export Profits Tax Relief; world boom; modest targets); the left's criticisms (profits repatriated, uneven growth, the west).

Lemass as Taoiseach, 1959–1966
- [ ] L1 Lemass becomes Taoiseach (June 1959, when de Valera became President); leaves in November 1966 (Lynch succeeds).
- [ ] L2 Style: pragmatic, businesslike, impatient for change; contrast with de Valera.
- [ ] L3 Social partnership: closer links with unions and employers; national wage rounds.
- [ ] L4 Preparing industry for competition: Committee on Industrial Organisation; adaptation councils.
- [ ] L5 The Second Programme (1963): target about 4 per cent a year to 1970; abandoned in 1967; the Third Programme (1969–72).
- [ ] L6 Modernisation of image: television used to explain policy (detail is in the notes on RTÉ).
- [ ] L7 Ireland joined GATT in 1967, the year after Lemass left office, as the end point of the move towards lower tariffs (do not credit it to Lemass as Taoiseach).

The move to free trade
- [ ] T1 First EEC application, 31 July 1961, just before Britain's.
- [ ] T2 De Gaulle's veto, 14 January 1963 (detail belongs to the notes on the UN and the EEC).
- [ ] T3 Anglo-Irish Free Trade Agreement: signed 14 December 1965, in force 1 July 1966; tariffs removed over ten years; farm access kept.
- [ ] T4 Effects and arguments: threat to protected firms (textiles, footwear, consumer goods) vs cheaper inputs, larger markets, investment.
- [ ] T5 The 1965 meetings with O'Neill: Lemass at Stormont 14 January 1965; O'Neill in Dublin 9 February 1965 (wider Anglo-Irish significance belongs to the notes on Anglo-Irish relations; here, as part of Lemass's record).

Key personality: T.K. Whitaker (one block, every point)
- [ ] W1 Who he was: born 1916, Rostrevor, Co. Down; civil servant from 1934.
- [ ] W2 What he did, with dates: Secretary of Finance 1956–69; Economic Development (1958); the First Programme; later Governor of the Central Bank (1969–76), senator (1977–82), chair of the Constitution Review Group (1990s); died 2017 aged 100.
- [ ] W3 How he shaped events: a civil servant using the Department of Finance's authority to argue for investment; working with Lemass and Ryan.
- [ ] W4 Participant/witness: author of the report; his own account that he and colleagues worked without extra pay (2019 source).
- [ ] W5 Contemporaries: praised for clarity and courage; Finance had been the department that resisted spending.
- [ ] W6 Historians: architect of the new approach, but political will came from Lemass and de Valera; debate over whether planning alone caused the growth.

Key personality: Seán Lemass (one block, every point)
- [ ] M1 Who he was: born Dublin 1899; 1916 Rising; anti-Treaty in the Civil War; brother Noel killed 1923.
- [ ] M2 Career: founder of Fianna Fáil (1926); minister from 1932 (Industry and Commerce 1932–39, 1941–48, 1951–54, 1957–59; Supplies 1939–45); Taoiseach 1959–66.
- [ ] M3 What he did: architect of 1930s protection, then of its ending; backed the First Programme, applied to the EEC, signed the Free Trade Agreement.
- [ ] M4 How events shaped him: the failure of protection convinced a former protectionist.
- [ ] M5 Contemporaries: practical, impatient, got things done; some in his party mistrusted the break with protection.
- [ ] M6 Historians: modernizer who changed the tone; but left rural poverty, urban housing and partition unresolved.

Assessment (final block, paired points)
- [ ] S1 Whitaker supplied analysis, Lemass political leadership; the partnership explains the change.
- [ ] S2 Achievements: growth, emigration down, exports, confidence, planning habit.
- [ ] S3 Limits: employment, west and farming, balance of payments, wage inflation, abandoned Second Programme, partition and poverty.
- [ ] S4 Contemporary view (success, public confidence) vs later view (turning point but debate over policy vs boom).
- [ ] S5 Whitaker and Lemass compared: civil servant vs politician; analysis vs leadership; caution vs drive.

## 3. Traps

- Do not use: "Fianna Fáil regained an overall majority in the 1965 election" (the notes say this), because the party's position that year was a bare half of the seats and it relied on others to govern; say only that it did well in 1965 and stayed in office.
- Do not use: "Whitaker was more sceptical than Lemass about the Second Programme's targets" (the notes say this), because it is not established; say only that the Second Programme was abandoned in 1967.
- Do not use: "grants ... with the best terms in the poorer western areas" as a named act or amount; say only that incentives favoured less developed areas, with no figures.
- Do not use: "Economic Development was presented under the authority of the government", because it appeared as a Finance Department study that the government then adopted. Say the government accepted its analysis.
- Do not use a day or figure for the Programme's capital spending, exports or unemployment; none is checked.
- Confusable: Economic Development (the May 1958 report, an analysis) vs the Programme for Economic Expansion (the November 1958 white paper, the plan). The Programme is a government document, not a Whitaker report.
- Confusable: First Programme (1959–63, target about 2 per cent, achieved about 4) vs Second Programme (1963, target about 4, abandoned 1967). Do not swap the targets.
- Confusable: Export Profits Tax Relief began under the Inter-Party government (1956), not under the Programme.
- Confusable: Ireland joined GATT in 1967, after Lemass had retired; the Free Trade Agreement (1965–66) is with Britain only; the EEC application (1961) is separate and was blocked by de Gaulle's veto of Britain.
- Do not call Lemass Taoiseach in 1958 or Whitaker a minister; Whitaker was a civil servant.
- Neighbouring subsections (name by subject): the 1948–59 governments and the "wasted years" debate (alternating governments); detail of the EEC applications, referendums and entry (the UN and the EEC); the impact of membership and fisheries; the Lemass–O'Neill meetings in their Anglo-Irish context (Anglo-Irish relations); Telefís Éireann and Lemass's attitude to RTÉ (the RTÉ case study); education reform and free secondary schooling (changes in education, health and welfare); Lynch (the 1970s and 1980s).

## 4. What exams ask

- Higher 100-mark essay on Lemass as Taoiseach is the commonest form (about seven papers in the period). Marks go to a stated judgement, balanced achievements and limits with dates: planning and growth, social partnership, the EEC application, free trade, O'Neill meetings, set against the abandoned Second Programme, employment, wage claims and partition.
- Essay on Whitaker and Lemass together: say what each contributed (analysis vs political leadership) and show how they combined; compare them directly, not in two separate lists.
- Higher essay on the First Programme: its main features, why it was a break with protection, and how far it achieved its aims. Use the target (about 2 per cent) against the result (about 4), then the limits (employment, balance of payments) and the outside boom.
- Document question (the 2019 style): documents include a 1957 civil servant's private memorandum and an outside observer's account of Ireland in 1957. Expect: spot agreement between documents, say which gives more information, judge whether a source is useful (private, eyewitness, expert, outsider) and a longer part asking whether the Programme achieved its aims. Use only the document's contents plus the checked facts in Section 2.
- Ordinary Level 40-mark paragraph on Whitaker: his post and date, what Economic Development argued, the plan that followed, one result. Each accurate, dated statement earns marks; four to five solid points are needed.
- Ordinary Level 40-mark paragraph on Lemass: when he became Taoiseach and why, the Programme, one measure of change in the economy, one foreign-policy step, when he left. Short answers ask for identity ("Secretary of the Department of Finance") and a one-line contrast with de Valera.
- A choice between the First Programme and the EEC: be ready to argue which mattered more for Ireland's economy, with dates.
- Contemporary vs later views: reward comes from pairing praise at the time with the later debate over policy vs the world boom.
