- [ ] K2 Military-industrial complex: the alliance of the armed forces, defence contractors and politicians; Eisenhower's warning in his farewell address (January 1961); how it funded missiles, aerospace and computing.

Military technology

- [ ] M2 Delivery systems: the B-52 bomber (1955); ICBMs (Atlas, Minuteman); Polaris submarine-launched missiles and second-strike capability; the nuclear submarine Nautilus (1954).

- [ ] M8 The Pentagon as a funder of industry and regions (California, Texas, Washington State): the military-industrial complex at work.

The space race, 1957–1969
- [ ] R1 Sputnik 1 (4 October 1957), the American shock, Explorer 1 (January 1958), the failed Vanguard launch (December 1957).
- [ ] R2 American responses: NASA (1958), the National Defense Education Act (1958), ARPA (1958).

- [ ] R4 Soviet firsts: Gagarin (12 April 1961), Tereshkova (1963), Leonov's spacewalk (1965).

Case study: the Moon landing, 1969
- [ ] C1 Organisation: NASA, Webb as administrator, Marshall Space Flight Center (von Braun), Houston, Kennedy Space Center, contractors (North American Aviation, Grumman, Boeing, IBM), hundreds of thousands of workers, universities (MIT guidance computer).
- [ ] C2 Cost and funding: about 25 billion dollars; NASA's share of federal spending at its peak; critics of the cost (Etzioni, congressional critics, the argument about poverty).
- [ ] C3 The hardware: the Saturn V and the three-part Apollo craft (command module, service module, lunar module); the guidance computer and integrated circuits.
- [ ] C4 Setbacks and testing: the Apollo 1 fire (27 January 1967) and its redesign; Apollo 7 (Earth orbit test); Apollo 8 (first crewed Moon orbit, December 1968); Apollo 9 and 10 (lunar module tests in 1969).
- [ ] C5 Apollo 11: crew; launch (16 July 1969); lunar orbit (19 July); landing of Eagle in the Sea of Tranquillity (20 July); the descent problems (computer alarms, a boulder-strewn landing area, manual flying); first steps (Armstrong, then Aldrin); the activities on the surface; splashdown (24 July) and quarantine.
- [ ] C6 Armstrong's words, the plaque and the flag; no territorial claim (Outer Space Treaty 1967).
- [ ] C7 Worldwide television coverage and the size of the audience; Nixon's call.
- [ ] C8 Significance at home: national pride, fulfilment of Kennedy's pledge, tribute to American technology and organisation; but also the cost debate and the troubled society of 1969.
- [ ] C9 Significance abroad: victory in the space race, prestige and technological leadership in the Cold War; the Soviet reaction.
- [ ] C10 Later missions and declining interest: Apollo 12 to 17 (five further landings), the cancelled flights, budget cuts, Skylab, Apollo-Soyuz.
- [ ] C11 The Space Shuttle (first flight 1981): purpose; the Challenger disaster (28 January 1986): date, cause, victims, the Rogers Commission.

Information technology
- [ ] I1 ENIAC (1945–46) and its military purpose; UNIVAC (1951) as the first commercial computer.
- [ ] I2 The transistor (1947) replacing the vacuum tube.
- [ ] I3 IBM and the System/360 (1964); IBM's dominance.
- [ ] I4 The integrated circuit (Kilby 1958, Noyce 1959); Moore's observation (1965).
- [ ] I5 The microprocessor: Intel 4004 (1971).
- [ ] I6 ARPANET (first message 29 October 1969), set up by the Defense Department's research agency.
- [ ] I7 Personal computers: Apple II (1977), IBM PC (1981), Macintosh (1984), Microsoft's operating system.
- [ ] I8 Silicon Valley: its location, Stanford, firms (Hewlett-Packard, Fairchild, Intel), and the role of defence money and space programmes as customers.
- [ ] I9 Links between the three fields: defence money, the space race and computing feeding one another.

Satellites and spin-offs
- [ ] T1 TIROS 1 (1960) weather satellite; Telstar (launched 10 July 1962; first public live transatlantic television 23 July 1962); the Communications Satellite Act (1962).
- [ ] T2 Spin-offs (miniaturised electronics, materials) and the historians' debate over how large they were.

Assessment (final block, paired points)
- [ ] S1 Achievements: a triumph of engineering and organisation; Cold War victory; lasting technologies (satellites, computing).
- [ ] S2 Limits: enormous cost; driven by rivalry rather than science; interest and budgets fell once the race was won; the Challenger disaster exposed management failures.
- [ ] S3 Contemporary views (celebration; criticism on the left and in Congress) against later historians' views (Cold War motive, short burst of spending, lasting technological legacy).

## 3. Traps

- Do not use: 10 July 1962 as the date live television crossed the Atlantic, because that is Telstar's launch date; the first public live transatlantic broadcasts were on 23 July 1962.
- Do not use: "no human has been beyond low Earth orbit since" (Apollo 17), because it is a present-day claim that may be out of date; say instead that the last Apollo Moon landing was December 1972.
- Do not use: reconnaissance satellites let the US monitor the Soviet Union "without flying over Soviet territory", because satellites do pass over it; the point is that they replaced risky U-2 aircraft flights (Powers was shot down in 1960).
- Do not use: the Apollo programme ran "from 1967 to 1972" (the programme began in 1961; the first crewed flight was 1968; 1967 is the Apollo 1 fire), because it misdates the start.
- Do not use: the Soviet test of August 1953 as a "thermonuclear device" without qualification; say "the Soviet Union tested a hydrogen-bomb type device in 1953" or leave it as a Soviet test.
- Do not use: the Mission Control opening date "1963" or the Manned Spacecraft Center "opened in 1963", because the dates are uncertain; say only that flight control was in Houston.
- Do not use: Saturn V "burned fuel at 13 tonnes a second", "41 Polaris submarines in the late 1960s" without "about", and any audience figure of exactly 600 million, because figures are estimates.
- Do not use: "the mission came in the same year as the rise of the anti-war movement", because it is vague and the movement began earlier; if mentioned, say only that the nation was divided by Vietnam, urban unrest and protest.
- Do not use: the NASA budget peak as "4 to 5 per cent in 1965 and 1966" unless written as "about" and "mid-1960s".
- Armstrong's quotation: the "a" dispute. Do not claim Armstrong definitely said or definitely omitted it.
- Apollo 8 orbited the Moon; it did not land. Apollo 10 flew near the surface without landing. Apollo 11 was the first landing. Apollo 13 never landed.
- Collins stayed in orbit; he did not walk on the Moon. Armstrong was first; Aldrin second.
- Do not confuse Sputnik (1957, satellite) with Gagarin's Vostok (1961, first human) or Shepard's flight (suborbital, 1961) with Glenn's (orbital, 1962).
- Do not confuse the integrated circuit (1958–59) with the microprocessor (1971) or the transistor (1947) with the vacuum tube it replaced.
- Do not confuse Challenger (1986, Shuttle) with Apollo 1 (1967, fire on the ground) or Apollo 13 (1970, survived).

## 4. What exams ask

- Higher 100-mark essay on the Moon landing (the commonest form across recent years): how and why the US achieved it, or its significance. Marks go to a clear thesis, then three developed strands: the political commitment (Kennedy, 25 May 1961, Cold War motives), the organisation and resources (NASA, contractors, cost, testing from Mercury to Apollo 10) and the outcome (Apollo 11 detail). Significance should be split: national (pride, technology), international (Cold War victory, prestige) and limits (cost, declining interest after Apollo 17).
- Higher essay pairing the Moon landing with another event (Reagan's SDI, McCarthyism, information technology): state the way each was important, with at least four dated facts for each, and link them to the Cold War. The SDI half needs date, aim, critics and result.
- Higher essay on advances in military, space and information technology together: link them by the Cold War and defence funding (military-industrial complex) and give a dated example for each of the three fields; a judgement on which mattered most earns credit.
- Higher essay on the military-industrial complex: define it, cite Eisenhower's farewell warning, then show it funding missiles, aerospace and computing, and weigh benefits against costs.
- Ordinary Level 40-mark paragraphs on the Moon landing: Kennedy's pledge with date, Sputnik and Gagarin as causes, NASA and cost, a named mission (Apollo 8 or 11) with dates, the crew, the landing site and date, the television audience, and the Cold War significance. Each correct dated fact earns a mark; a list without explanation caps the score.
- Ordinary Level 30-mark questions: four to five facts on one stage, for example "launch, landing and return of Apollo 11" or "Kennedy's commitment and NASA's choices (lunar orbit rendezvous)".
- Ordinary Level 6-mark short answers on a document (a photograph, a press report or a speech extract): what it shows about the landing, who was involved, what year, why it mattered to the US.
- What gets marks: a date with every event, named people and places, the cost and scale, the link to the Soviet Union (Sputnik, Gagarin, N1 failures), and a short evaluation. What loses marks: describing the landing without explaining why the US wanted it, and treating the Moon landing as if it ended the space race at once.
- Information technology answers earn marks for a chain of dated advances (ENIAC 1946, transistor 1947, integrated circuit 1958–59, microprocessor 1971, ARPANET 1969, PC 1981) and for linking them to defence funding and Silicon Valley.
