## 1. Must cover (one bullet or block for every item; tick each off)

Background (assumed)
- [ ] A1 (assumed) The Act of Union (1800) put Irish MPs in the Westminster Parliament; the Catholic Emancipation Act (1829) let Catholics sit as MPs (detail belongs to the O'Connell notes).
- [ ] A2 (assumed) The Famine (1845–50) and mass emigration cut the number of small tenants and left bitterness; Young Ireland's failed rising of 1848 (Ballingarry) is the Fenian starting point.
- [ ] A3 (assumed) The Irish land system: tenants had no legal security, landlords could raise rents or evict; the Ulster custom let tenants sell their interest.
- [ ] A4 (assumed) The Church of Ireland was the state church although most people were Catholic; Cardinal Cullen led a disciplined, Rome-oriented Catholic Church after 1850.

Key concepts (each defined as used here; also in keyTerms)
- [ ] K1 Physical force republicanism: Irish independence as a republic to be won only by armed rebellion (the Fenian creed).
- [ ] K2 Sectarianism: hostility between Catholic and Protestant communities shaping politics (Orange marches, Dolly's Brae, the Titles Act reaction).
- [ ] K3 Fenianism (term): the movement of the IRB and Fenian Brotherhood.
- [ ] K4 Disestablishment (term): ending the Church of Ireland's status as state church.
- [ ] K5 Ulster custom (term): the tenant's right to sell his interest, legalised in 1870.

Electoral reform
- [ ] E1 The 1829 franchise: the Disenfranchisement Act abolished the forty-shilling freeholders and raised the county franchise to £10; the Irish county electorate fell sharply.
- [ ] E2 The 1832 Irish Reform Act: seats 100 to 105; £10 householder vote in boroughs; electorate still small beside Britain's.
- [ ] E3 Open voting until the Ballot Act (1872); landlord and clerical influence on tenants.
- [ ] E4 The Irish Franchise Act (1850): £12 rated occupiers in counties, £8 in boroughs.
- [ ] E5 Effect of the 1850 Act: the county electorate grew markedly, the borough electorate stayed small; the Famine and emigration had already thinned the small tenant class.
- [ ] E6 Link to politics: a larger farmer electorate helped the Tenant League candidates in 1852.

Sectarianism in politics
- [ ] S1 The Orange Order: founded 1795 (Armagh) after the Battle of the Diamond; dissolved 1836; revived in Ulster in the 1840s.
- [ ] S2 Dolly's Brae, 12 July 1849: Orange march from Tollymore, armed Ribbonmen, Catholics killed (number disputed), houses burned.
- [ ] S3 Aftermath: inquiry criticised Lord Roden and the Orangemen; Party Processions Act (1850), repealed 1872.
- [ ] S4 Restoration of the English Catholic hierarchy (1850) and Lord John Russell's "papal aggression" reaction.
- [ ] S5 The Ecclesiastical Titles Act (1851): banned territorial titles; never enforced; repealed 1871.
- [ ] S6 Irish Catholic reaction: opposition by Irish MPs and the Catholic Defence Association; it joined Catholic and tenant politics.
- [ ] S7 The Tenant League (1850): founders (Duffy, Lucas, Ulster Presbyterian ministers), the demands (fair rent, fixity of tenure, free sale).
- [ ] S8 Clerical support (MacHale, Lavelle) and Cullen's distrust of priests in politics.
- [ ] S9 The 1852 election and the Independent Irish Party: over 40 MPs pledged to independent opposition.
- [ ] S10 The Sadleir–Keogh betrayal (December 1852): office in Aberdeen's government, the pledge broken.
- [ ] S11 Collapse: Lucas's death (1855), Duffy's emigration (1855), Sadleir's suicide (1856), Keogh made a judge.
- [ ] S12 Result: disillusion with parliamentary methods, feeding Fenianism.

Fenianism: origins and organisation
- [ ] F1 The IRB: founded in Dublin, 17 March 1858, by James Stephens with Luby and others; oath-bound, secret, republican.
- [ ] F2 The Fenian Brotherhood in New York (O'Mahony, 1858); the name from the Fianna.
- [ ] F3 Roots: 1848, Famine bitterness, disillusion after 1852, emigrant exile.
- [ ] F4 Organisation: circles, Stephens as Chief Organiser of the Irish Republic.
- [ ] F5 Membership: artisans, clerks, shopkeepers, farmers' sons; recruitment of British army soldiers.
- [ ] F6 The MacManus funeral (1861): a show of strength and Cullen's refusal of the Pro-Cathedral.
- [ ] F7 The Irish People (1863): editors Luby, O'Leary, Kickham; Rossa as manager.
- [ ] F8 American dimension: Civil War veterans; money and men; the American organisation's divisions.
- [ ] F9 Church opposition: Cullen and most bishops condemned the secret society; priests denounced it.

Suppression, rising and actions abroad
- [ ] R1 The September 1865 raid and arrests (Luby, O'Leary, Rossa, Kickham); Stephens arrested November 1865.
- [ ] R2 Stephens's escape from Richmond Prison, November 1865.
- [ ] R3 Habeas corpus suspended, February 1866.
- [ ] R4 Stephens postponed the promised rising; deposed December 1866.
- [ ] R5 The rising of March 1867: bad weather, small risings in Dublin (Tallaght), Cork, Limerick, Tipperary; crushed.
- [ ] R6 Why it failed: informers, lack of arms and leaders.
- [ ] R7 The Manchester rescue (September 1867): Kelly and Deasy freed, Sergeant Brett killed.
- [ ] R8 The Manchester Martyrs (Allen, Larkin, O'Brien), hanged November 1867 and widely mourned.
- [ ] R9 Clerkenwell (December 1867): explosion, about a dozen killed, British opinion hardened; Barrett hanged 1868.
- [ ] R10 The Canadian raids (Ridgeway 1866; 1870): failed.
- [ ] R11 Irish and international dimensions: a transatlantic movement with action in Ireland, England and Canada.

Key personality: James Stephens (one block, every point)
- [ ] P1 Who: born Kilkenny 1825; 1848 at Ballingarry, escaped to Paris.
- [ ] P2 Deeds: returned 1856, travelled Ireland, founded the IRB 1858 as Chief Organiser; linked with O'Mahony; the Irish People; recruitment in the army.
- [ ] P3 Shaped by events: arrest and escape 1865; failure to rise in 1866; deposed; exile.
- [ ] P4 Later life: Paris; returned 1891; died Dublin 1901.
- [ ] P5 Contemporaries: gifted organiser, charismatic, but autocratic, secretive, over-promising.
- [ ] P6 Historians: built the first lasting revolutionary organisation; criticised for indecision and for failing to lead the rising.

Key personality: Charles Kickham (one block, every point)
- [ ] P7 Who: born Mullinahone, Tipperary, 1828; almost deaf and poor sight after a gunpowder accident.
- [ ] P8 Deeds: wrote for The Nation; editor of the Irish People; arrested 1865, fourteen years in 1866, released 1869 through ill health.
- [ ] P9 Writings: Sally Cavanagh (1869), Knocknagow (1873).
- [ ] P10 Later: leading figure on the IRB Supreme Council in the 1870s; died Blackrock 1882.
- [ ] P11 Faith and conflict: a practising Catholic who rejected clerical condemnation of the movement.
- [ ] P12 Contemporaries admired his courage and endurance; historians see him as the voice of rural, literary Fenianism.

Liberal reforms
- [ ] L1 Why: the Fenian scare (Manchester, Clerkenwell) made reform urgent; Gladstone became prime minister December 1868 with a mission to pacify Ireland.
- [ ] L2 The Irish Church Act (1869): disestablishment from 1 January 1871; endowments passed to a commission; clergy compensated.
- [ ] L3 The Irish Land Act (1870): Ulster custom made law, compensation for disturbance and for improvements.
- [ ] L4 The Bright clauses: loans to buy holdings, little used.
- [ ] L5 Limits of the Land Act: no real security against eviction for non-payment of rent; rents could rise.
- [ ] L6 Fenian prisoners released from 1869 and the amnesty movement; Butt's Home Government Association (1870) as the next constitutional step.

Assessment (paired judgement points, final block)
- [ ] J1 Fenian achievements: revived physical force republicanism; an oath-bound organisation across three countries; kept the republic idea alive.
- [ ] J2 Fenian limits: military failure in 1867, informers, divisions, Church condemnation, small support.
- [ ] J3 Contemporary views: British (conspiracy), nationalists (martyrs), Church (sinful secret society).
- [ ] J4 Later views: Fenianism prompted reform; the legacy reaches 1916.
- [ ] J5 Liberal achievements: disestablishment ended Protestant privilege; first legal recognition of tenants' claims.
- [ ] J6 Liberal limits: Land Act failed on eviction; neither measure touched tenant ownership or self-government.
- [ ] J7 Overall: Fenianism or Liberal reform: violence gave the push, reform could not remove the grievances.

## 4. What exams ask

- Higher 100-mark essay, the commonest form: how effective, successful or important Fenianism was, or its strengths and weaknesses and international dimension. Marks go to a clear judgement stated at the start, with balanced points: for success, an organisation across Ireland, Britain and America, the Irish People, martyrs and Clerkenwell prompting Gladstone's reforms, a legacy leading to 1916; for failure, the crushed 1867 rising, informers, Church condemnation, loss of leaders. Narrative without a judgement scores poorly.
- The international dimension needs concrete evidence: the Fenian Brotherhood in New York, Civil War veterans, money and men, the Manchester rescue, Clerkenwell, the Canadian raids. Say what each shows (transatlantic reach) and what it cost (lost sympathy in Britain after Clerkenwell).
- Personality essays on Stephens and/or Kickham, often as a comparison ("different contributions"): Stephens as organiser, Chief Organiser, the oath, circles, the Irish People, escape, deposition; Kickham as writer and editor, prison, novels (Sally Cavanagh, Knocknagow), Catholic faith under clerical condemnation. Each needs dates, what they did, how others saw them and how historians judge them. Do not mix up the two.
- "Fenianism or Liberal reforms": which changed Ireland more. Strong answers link them (the Fenian scare made reform urgent) and then judge: reform changed more in law (disestablishment, Land Act), Fenianism changed the mood and the tradition. Name limits of the Land Act (evictions for non-payment, rents).
- Ordinary Level 30-mark paragraphs: Gladstone's mission (December 1868), Irish Church Act 1869 (effective 1 January 1871), Land Act 1870 (Ulster custom, compensation); the Orange Order, Dolly's Brae (12 July 1849) and Ribbonmen; the IRB founding (17 March 1858, oath-bound), its members and recruitment; Kickham's life (Mullinahone 1828, accident, The Nation, Irish People, 1865 arrest, Sally Cavanagh). Each correct, dated fact earns marks.
- Ordinary Level 40-mark profiles on Stephens: birth, Ballingarry, return 1856, IRB 1858, Chief Organiser, Irish People, escape, deposition, death; and the 1867 rising causes of failure.
- Short answers (6 marks) on a document about Stephens ask who he was, what he led and what he did: Fenian leader, IRB, escaped from prison.
- Political-structures essay (2011 style): the 1850 franchise, Tenant League and Independent Irish Party, and the reforms of 1869–70 as changes within the Union; answers should show they changed the electorate and the Church but not the Union.
