## 1. Must cover (one bullet or block for every item; tick each off)

Background (assumed)
- [ ] A1 (assumed) The 1937 Constitution made Irish the national and first official language; the state's policy was revival through schools, the civil service and the Gaeltacht.
- [ ] A2 (assumed) The Catholic Church's position in the state: a Catholic ethos in schools, health and social policy; very high Mass attendance; the Mother and Child dispute of 1951 (detail belongs to another subsection).
- [ ] A3 (assumed) Ireland in the 1950s was isolated, with heavy emigration; from 1958 and the 1960s economic expansion, urbanisation and rising living standards opened the country to outside influences.

Key concepts (each defined as used in this topic; also in keyTerms)
- [ ] K1 Ecumenism: closer relations and dialogue between Christian churches, promoted by Vatican II.
- [ ] K2 Secularisation: the decline of religion's influence over public life, institutions and personal behaviour.
- [ ] K3 Censorship: official banning or restriction of books, films and other material judged indecent, obscene or otherwise unacceptable.
- [ ] K4 Pluralism: acceptance that a society contains different beliefs and traditions, none privileged by the state.

The Irish language
- [ ] L1 Official policy: compulsory Irish in schools, a pass in Irish required for the Leaving Certificate and for entry to the civil service.
- [ ] L2 The Gaeltacht in decline in the 1950s (emigration); Gaeltarra Éireann set up 1957 for industry in Irish-speaking areas.
- [ ] L3 Gael Linn (1953): records, the Amharc Éireann newsreel (from 1956), the film Mise Éire (1959).
- [ ] L4 The Commission on the Restoration of the Irish Language (report 1963) and the 1965 White Paper (aim kept, approach more flexible).
- [ ] L5 The Language Freedom Movement (1966): opposed compulsion; counter-campaigns.
- [ ] L6 End of the compulsory pass in Irish for the Leaving Certificate (1973; Fine Gael-Labour government; Richard Burke); civil service requirement also relaxed.
- [ ] L7 The Gaeltacht Civil Rights Movement (1969) and the pirate station Saor Raidió Chonamara (1970).
- [ ] L8 Raidió na Gaeltachta (2 April 1972).
- [ ] L9 Bord na Gaeilge (1975) and Údarás na Gaeltachta (1980).
- [ ] L10 Overall: symbolic status kept, but everyday use declined outside a shrinking Gaeltacht; compulsion left many pupils indifferent.

Key personality: Breandán Ó hEithir (one block, every point)
- [ ] O1 Who he was: born on Inis Mór (Aran Islands) in 1930, died 1990; writer, journalist and broadcaster, in Irish and English.
- [ ] O2 What he did: journalist and broadcaster with RTÉ (current affairs, culture, sport), newspaper column; novel Lig Sinn i gCathú (1976) and its translation (1978); Over the Bar (1984); The Begrudger's Guide to Irish Politics (1986).
- [ ] O3 Participant, not movement leader: he presented Irish as a living medium for modern subjects, not a duty.
- [ ] O4 Contemporaries: valued as a bridge between the Gaeltacht and the English-speaking world, a rare Irish-language writer with a wide readership.
- [ ] O5 Historians and later critics: one of the most accomplished bilingual writers and broadcasters of his generation (state in general terms).

Traditional music and cultural revival
- [ ] M1 Comhaltas Ceoltóirí Éireann (Mullingar, 1951) and the fleadh cheoil from 1952.
- [ ] M2 Seán Ó Riada: score for Mise Éire (1959); Ceoltóirí Chualann from 1961.
- [ ] M3 Ó Riada's Ceol an Aifrinn (1968): the music revival linked to the Vatican II liturgy.
- [ ] M4 The Chieftains (1962), Clancy Brothers, The Dubliners, Planxty: ballads and traditional music popular at home and abroad.
- [ ] M5 Effect: Irish culture gained a modern, commercial and international dimension; the rural/urban divide in culture narrowed.

Vatican II and the Irish church
- [ ] V1 The council: opened 11 October 1962 (John XXIII), closed 8 December 1965 (Paul VI); Irish bishops including McQuaid attended.
- [ ] V2 The vernacular Mass (first Masses in English in Ireland, 1965); priest facing the people.
- [ ] V3 Lay roles (readers, communion), parish councils and lay organisations.
- [ ] V4 Ecumenism: the Trinity ban lifted (1970); Ballymascanlon inter-church talks (1973).
- [ ] V5 Religious freedom as a right, weakening the claim to a special legal position for the Catholic Church.
- [ ] V6 Reaction: welcomed by many, unsettling for older Catholics; encouraged questioning of authority and individual conscience.

Key personality: Archbishop John Charles McQuaid (part 2: Vatican II and overall evaluation; one block, every point)
- [ ] Q1 Who he was: Archbishop of Dublin 1940–1972, the most powerful churchman in Ireland (the Mother and Child dispute of 1951 is covered in another subsection; mention only as the earlier peak of his influence).
- [ ] Q2 At Vatican II: attended; on return reassured Dubliners that the council would not disturb their faith.
- [ ] Q3 Implemented liturgical reform slowly; wary of ecumenism and of lay initiatives outside clerical control.
- [ ] Q4 Wider work: schools, social work, new churches and schools in the suburbs, the Catholic Social Service Conference; the McGahern case as an example of his reach.
- [ ] Q5 Retirement (January 1972), death (1973), successor Dermot Ryan.
- [ ] Q6 Contemporaries: admired for efficiency and devotion; criticised as authoritarian and for intervening in politics and private lives.
- [ ] Q7 Historians: Cooney's biography (1999) portrays a ruler of Catholic Ireland whose style came to seem outdated; others stress his building of schools and welfare services and argue the decline of church power came from social change as much as from his policies.

Secularisation, pluralism and practice
- [ ] S1 Practice stayed high into the 1970s (survey of 1973-74).
- [ ] S2 Humanae Vitae (1968) and the difficulty many Irish Catholics had with it.
- [ ] S3 The fall in vocations to the priesthood and religious life from the late 1960s.
- [ ] S4 The papal visit of September-October 1979 and the Phoenix Park Mass: great enthusiasm, but no halt to long-term change.
- [ ] S5 Secularisation advanced in the 1970s and 1980s (education, television, emigration, contact with Europe).
- [ ] S6 Pluralism debates and the referendums of 1983 (abortion) and 1986 (divorce): traditional views remained strong.

Censorship
- [ ] C1 The Censorship of Publications Act 1929 (board) and the 1946 Act (appeals board): thousands of books banned, including leading Irish writers.
- [ ] C2 John McGahern's The Dark (1965) and the loss of his teaching post.
- [ ] C3 The Censorship of Publications Act 1967 (Brian Lenihan): bans last twelve years; thousands of titles freed.
- [ ] C4 Film censorship more liberal in the 1960s and 70s; contraception under separate law until 1979 (see the notes on the status of women); broadcasting controlled through the minister's powers over RTÉ (see the notes on RTÉ).

The communications revolution
- [ ] R1 Transport: growth in car ownership, better roads, buses and air travel reduced rural isolation.
- [ ] R2 Telephones: poor service improved; Bord Telecom Éireann (1984).
- [ ] R3 Television: reached most homes in the 1960s and early 1970s; RTÉ 2 (1978); British channels in the east and north (detail of RTÉ belongs to another subsection).
- [ ] R4 Newspapers and magazines: the Irish Times under Douglas Gageby (from 1963); Magill (1977).
- [ ] R5 Radio: pirate stations in the 1970s and 80s; the Radio and Television Act 1988 licensed local independent radio.
- [ ] R6 Computers: business and government use from the 1970s; American electronics firms (Digital) built the base of an electronics industry.
- [ ] R7 Effect: wider ideas and lifestyles; end of the isolation on which traditional authority rested.

Assessment (paired judgement points, final block)
- [ ] J1 Achievements: Irish gained broadcasting and institutions; traditional music revived and went global; the church accepted liturgical reform and ecumenism; censorship eased.
- [ ] J2 Limits: Irish kept declining as a community language; compulsion bred indifference; church leadership under McQuaid was slow to adapt.
- [ ] J3 Contemporary views: traditionalists feared for Irish identity and Catholic values; reformers saw change as overdue.
- [ ] J4 Later views: the start of a more secular, pluralist society, with the church still strongly influential on law and public life into the 1980s.

## 4. What exams ask

- Higher 100-mark essays on Irish language and culture (the commonest form): how attitudes changed 1949 to 1989. Marks go to a clear thread from enforced duty (compulsory Irish, civil service) to flexibility and choice (1973), supported with dated evidence from Gael Linn, the LFM, Raidió na Gaeltachta and the institutions, plus the traditional music revival. Name at least four organisations or events with dates. A list of institutions without a judgement on whether the language actually recovered scores poorly.
- Higher essays pairing or ranking Vatican II with television, the EEC or education as a force for change: students need what Vatican II changed (vernacular Mass, lay roles, ecumenism, conscience and religious freedom), how Irish Catholics and McQuaid reacted, and a reasoned comparison. Link to secularisation with evidence both ways (high Mass attendance, falling vocations, Humanae Vitae, the 1979 visit, 1983 and 1986 referendums).
- Higher essays on McQuaid and Vatican II (a recurring pairing): contrast his authority and conservative style with the council's openness; state his reaction (reassurance, slow reform, wary of ecumenism) and a verdict using both contemporary and historian views. The part 1 material (Mother and Child) can add context but cannot be the whole answer.
- Higher essays on the communications revolution: argue that it ended isolation and weakened traditional authority. Needed: separate named media (transport, telephones, newspapers, radio, television, computers), each with an example and date, and an effect on attitudes. Better answers also note limits (British media, slow telephone growth, the church's continued influence).
- Ordinary Level 30- and 40-mark paragraphs on Ó hEithir: birthplace and year, journalist and broadcaster with RTÉ, work in Irish and English, three named books with dates, and his significance. Each correct, dated statement earns marks.
- Ordinary Level 30-mark paragraphs on Vatican II: dates of opening and closing, the Pope, the vernacular Mass and the first English Masses in Ireland (1965), priests facing the people, lay participation, ecumenism. Ordinary Level paragraphs on the language: the 1937 Constitution, Gael Linn, the 1963 Commission and 1965 White Paper, LFM 1966, end of the compulsory pass in 1973.
- Short-answer (6-mark) items usually test one fact: a person's role, a date, a body's purpose. State the key fact first and add one explanatory clause.
- Concepts such as censorship, ecumenism, pluralism and secularisation are best defined in one sentence and then illustrated (1967 Act; Ballymascanlon; the 1986 divorce vote; falling vocations).
