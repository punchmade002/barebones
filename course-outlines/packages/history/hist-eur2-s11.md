## 1. Must cover (one bullet or block for every item; tick each off)

Assumed knowledge
- [ ] A1 (assumed) Before germ theory most doctors blamed disease on "miasma" (bad air from filth); surgery before anaesthetics and antiseptics was fast, painful and often fatal from infection. One or two bullets, as the "before" picture for the medical changes.
- [ ] A2 (assumed) Before the car, road transport meant horses, carriages and, from the 1880s, the bicycle; railways carried long-distance traffic. The bicycle trade supplied skills, light frames, chain drive and tyres to the early car makers.
- [ ] A3 (assumed) An element is a basic chemical substance; an atom was then thought to be unchangeable. Radioactivity showed atoms could break down and give off energy. One bullet only, in plain words.

Key personality: Marie Curie (one block, headed "Key personality: Marie Curie")
- [ ] C1 Early life: born Maria Skłodowska in Warsaw, 1867, in Russian-ruled Poland; women could not attend university there; went to the Sorbonne in Paris in 1891 to study physics and mathematics.
- [ ] C2 Married Pierre Curie, 1895. After Becquerel's discovery (1896) of rays from uranium she took them as her doctoral research and coined the word "radioactivity".
- [ ] C3 Pitchblende was more radioactive than its uranium could explain, so it must hold new elements: polonium (July 1898, named after Poland) and radium (December 1898).
- [ ] C4 Years of processing tonnes of pitchblende in a poorly equipped shed to separate a small amount of radium salt (by 1902); pure radium metal isolated with André Debierne in 1910.
- [ ] C5 Nobel Prize for Physics 1903, shared with Pierre Curie and Becquerel: first woman to win a Nobel Prize.
- [ ] C6 Pierre's death in a street accident in Paris, April 1906; Marie took over his post and became the first woman professor at the Sorbonne.
- [ ] C7 Nobel Prize for Chemistry 1911 (polonium and radium): first person to win Nobel Prizes in two different sciences.
- [ ] C8 Hostility in 1911: the French Academy of Sciences refused her membership by a narrow vote; press attacks over her relationship with Paul Langevin, with xenophobic (anti-foreign) overtones.
- [ ] C9 First World War: mobile X-ray vehicles ("petites Curies") for the front, fixed radiological posts in hospitals, training of women operators, helped by her daughter Irène; she learned to drive to take the units out herself.
- [ ] C10 The Radium Institute in Paris (building completed 1914); later medical use of radium against cancer (radiotherapy).
- [ ] C11 Death, 1934, of aplastic anaemia, probably caused by long exposure to radiation; the dangers were not understood in her working life.
- [ ] C12 Contemporary and later views: admired as a great scientist and a national hero in both France and Poland, but resented by some as a woman and a foreigner; historians see her as a founder of the study of radioactivity (opening the way to atomic physics and radiotherapy) and a pioneer for women in science.

Medicine
- [ ] M1 Louis Pasteur: microbes cause fermentation and decay (1860s); pasteurisation; argued microbes cause disease (germ theory).
- [ ] M2 Pasteur's vaccines from weakened microbes: chicken cholera (about 1879), anthrax (1881), rabies (Joseph Meister, July 1885); the Pasteur Institute (1887–88).
- [ ] M3 Robert Koch: pure cultures and staining methods; anthrax bacterium (1876), tuberculosis bacillus (24 March 1882), cholera bacterium (1883); Nobel Prize 1905. Koch's methods let others identify the germs of many diseases.
- [ ] M4 Pasteur–Koch rivalry had a French–German national edge after the war of 1870–71: science as national prestige.
- [ ] M5 Behring and Kitasato: antitoxins against diphtheria and tetanus (1890); Behring the first Nobel Prize for Medicine (1901).
- [ ] M6 Paul Ehrlich: Salvarsan against syphilis (1909–10), the first modern chemotherapy ("magic bullet" idea).
- [ ] M7 Anaesthetics (background, before 1871): ether (1846), chloroform (1847), Queen Victoria's use of chloroform in childbirth (1853) made it acceptable.
- [ ] M8 Antiseptics: Joseph Lister used carbolic acid on wounds, dressings and instruments from 1865, published 1867, based on Pasteur's work; deaths from infection after surgery fell sharply.
- [ ] M9 Asepsis: from the 1880s, especially in Germany, surgeons prevented germs reaching the wound at all: steam sterilisation of instruments, gowns, and later rubber gloves. Antisepsis (kill germs) vs asepsis (keep germs out).
- [ ] M10 Public health: clean water, sewers, better food and housing cut deaths from cholera and typhoid in European cities; germ theory explained why these worked.
- [ ] M11 X-rays in medicine (link to X1 and C9): diagnosis of fractures and locating bullets and shrapnel; standard in military hospitals in the First World War.

Science, electricity and communications
- [ ] X1 Wilhelm Röntgen discovered X-rays at Würzburg, November 1895; the X-ray image of his wife's hand; rapid use by doctors; first Nobel Prize for Physics (1901).
- [ ] X2 The Nobel Prizes, first awarded 1901 under the will of Alfred Nobel (inventor of dynamite); early prizes dominated by German and French scientists.
- [ ] X3 Telephone: Alexander Graham Bell's patent (1876); exchanges and networks spread in cities in the 1880s–90s.
- [ ] X4 Electricity: Edison's phonograph (1877) and long-lasting light bulb (1879, with Joseph Swan working on the same problem in England); first public power stations (1882); electric lighting, trams and underground railways in cities.
- [ ] X5 Guglielmo Marconi: wireless telegraphy from 1895–96 (patented in Britain, 1896); Irish mother (Annie Jameson); transatlantic signal from Poldhu, Cornwall, received in Newfoundland, December 1901; transatlantic station at Clifden, Co. Galway (1907); shared Nobel Prize for Physics 1909.
- [ ] X6 Wireless at sea and in war: the Titanic's distress calls (April 1912); military communication in the First World War.
- [ ] X7 Rudolf Diesel's compression-ignition engine (patent applied for 1892).
- [ ] X8 Fritz Haber and Carl Bosch: synthesis of ammonia (1909–13), making artificial fertilisers and, in the war, explosives for Germany despite the blockade.
- [ ] X9 Albert Einstein: special relativity (1905) and general relativity (1915). One bullet.

Flight
- [ ] F1 Count Zeppelin's rigid airships (from 1900).
- [ ] F2 The Wright brothers: first controlled, powered, sustained flight of a heavier-than-air machine, Kitty Hawk, North Carolina, 17 December 1903.
- [ ] F3 Louis Blériot flew across the English Channel, 25 July 1909; flying displays and prizes in Europe.
- [ ] F4 Aircraft in the First World War: reconnaissance, artillery spotting, bombing, air combat; production rose from hundreds to tens of thousands; engines improved fast (one or two bullets; the fighting is in the notes on the conduct of the war).
- [ ] F5 Alcock and Brown: first non-stop transatlantic flight, Newfoundland to a bog near Clifden, Co. Galway, June 1919.

Key personality: Karl Benz (one block, headed "Key personality: Karl Benz")
- [ ] B1 Born 1844 near Karlsruhe, son of a locomotive driver who died when Karl was a small child; studied mechanical engineering at the Karlsruhe Polytechnic.
- [ ] B2 Early struggles: a first engineering business in Mannheim failed; built a working two-stroke gas engine (1879) to earn money.
- [ ] B3 Founded Benz & Cie in Mannheim, 1883, building stationary gas engines, which paid for his car work.
- [ ] B4 Designed the car as a whole, not an engine added to a carriage: a light three-wheeled vehicle with a single-cylinder four-stroke petrol engine at the rear, running in 1885.
- [ ] B5 Patent applied for 29 January 1886 (the "Benz Patent-Motorwagen"), granted November 1886; often called the birth certificate of the car.
- [ ] B6 Bertha Benz: his wife and business partner, whose money helped fund the firm; her 1888 drive (see CS5) gave publicity and showed faults he then fixed (e.g. a lower gear for hills).
- [ ] B7 Later success: the Benz Velo (1894), the first car built in series; Benz & Cie among the largest car makers in the world by about 1900; France was its biggest early market.
- [ ] B8 Benz was cautious about faster, more powerful designs and fell behind the Daimler company's Mercedes after 1901; he left day-to-day management in the early 1900s.
- [ ] B9 1926: Benz & Cie merged with the Daimler company to form Daimler-Benz (Mercedes-Benz cars). Benz died in 1929.
- [ ] B10 Views: honoured in Germany as the inventor of the car; historians credit him with the first practical car designed as a whole, while stressing that Daimler and Maybach's engine, French manufacturers and Ford's mass production were equally needed to make the car a success. Participant, not witness: he designed and built the machine himself.

Case study: the motor car (at least four blocks: background/causes; events in order; outcomes; significance and views)
- [ ] CS1 Background: earlier steam road vehicles and electric cars; why petrol won (light engine, range, quick refuelling).
- [ ] CS2 The internal combustion engine: Nikolaus Otto's four-stroke gas engine (1876) at the Deutz company; four strokes: intake, compression, power, exhaust.
- [ ] CS3 Gottlieb Daimler and Wilhelm Maybach (formerly at Deutz): a small, light, high-speed petrol engine (1880s); fitted to a wooden motorcycle (1885) and a four-wheeled carriage (1886); founded the Daimler company (Daimler-Motoren-Gesellschaft, 1890).
- [ ] CS4 Benz's Motorwagen and patent (1885–86): see B4 and B5; Benz and Daimler worked separately, about 100 km apart.
- [ ] CS5 Bertha Benz's drive, August 1888: with her sons Eugen and Richard, Mannheim to Pforzheim, about 100 km, without telling Karl; fuel (ligroin) bought at a pharmacy in Wiesloch; roadside repairs; proved the car practical and won publicity.
- [ ] CS6 France takes the lead: Panhard et Levassor and Peugeot built cars with Daimler-licensed engines (1890–91); the Paris–Rouen trial (1894) and later races; Louis Renault's first car (1898) and the Renault company (1899); France the biggest car producer in Europe before 1914.
- [ ] CS7 Tyres: John Boyd Dunlop, Scottish vet in Belfast, patented the pneumatic tyre (1888) for his son's tricycle; the Michelin brothers' detachable pneumatic tyre (1891).
- [ ] CS8 The Mercedes (1901): built by the Daimler company to Maybach's design, named after Emil Jellinek's daughter; lighter, more powerful, lower: the model for later cars. Daimler had died in 1900.
- [ ] CS9 Britain: the "red flag" laws held back motoring; the 1896 Locomotives on Highways Act raised the speed limit and was celebrated by the Emancipation Run, London to Brighton (November 1896); Rolls and Royce met (1904) and the Silver Ghost (1906–07) made Britain known for luxury cars.
- [ ] CS10 Ireland: the Gordon Bennett race, July 1903, in Co. Kildare, Queen's County (Laois) and Co. Carlow; the Motor Car Act 1903 (registration plates, driving licences, 20 mph limit) for Britain and Ireland; Ford's factory at Cork (1917).
- [ ] CS11 Early motoring as a luxury: handmade, expensive cars for the wealthy; advertisements aimed at the upper classes; motoring clubs; drivers often employed as chauffeurs.
- [ ] CS12 Hardships of early motoring (needed for documents questions): no filling stations (fuel bought in cans from chemists, shops or hotels), open cars with no roof or windscreen, dust from unsurfaced roads, poor oil, candle or acetylene lamps, frequent breakdowns and punctures, protective goggles and dust coats; hostility from farmers, carters, cyclists and pedestrians.
- [ ] CS13 Henry Ford: the Ford Motor Company, Detroit, 1903; aim of a cheap, simple car for ordinary people.
- [ ] CS14 The Model T, October 1908: simple, strong, easy to repair; price cut again and again as output rose, so far more people could buy one.
- [ ] CS15 The moving assembly line, Highland Park, Michigan, 1913: chassis moved past workers each doing one task; standard interchangeable parts; chassis assembly time cut from over 12 hours to about 90 minutes.
- [ ] CS16 The five-dollar day, January 1914: about double the usual wage for an eight-hour day, to cut very high staff turnover caused by dull, repetitive work; workers could become customers.
- [ ] CS17 Mass production spreads: by 1920 the USA had millions of cars and made most of the world's cars; Ford assembly at Trafford Park, Manchester (1911); European firms studied Ford's methods (later called "Fordism").
- [ ] CS18 Economic effects: demand for steel, rubber, glass, petrol and machine tools; jobs in factories, garages and repair; growth of oil and tyre firms.
- [ ] CS19 Social effects to 1920: road improvement and tarring, traffic laws, licences and speed limits; leisure touring; doctors, salesmen and country clergy among early users; but in Europe cars stayed rare and expensive by 1920.
- [ ] CS20 The motor car in the First World War: about 600 Paris taxis took troops to the Marne (September 1914); lorries along the "Voie Sacrée" (Sacred Way) supplied Verdun (1916); motor ambulances; the tank and armoured car; war production of engines and lorries.
- [ ] CS21 After the war: surplus army lorries and trained drivers fed bus services and road haulage, which began to compete with the railways in the 1920s (one clause; outside the topic dates).

Assessment block (required by the standard)
- [ ] J1 Assessment of science, technology and medicine: achievements (germ theory, vaccines, antiseptics and X-rays saved many lives; electricity, telephone and wireless changed communication; the car and aeroplane changed transport) vs limits (benefits reached the poor slowly; radiation dangers unknown; the same science made the war deadlier: aircraft, motor transport, chemistry, wireless); science as progress vs science as national prestige and as a weapon.
- [ ] J2 Assessment of the motor car: invention vs innovation (Germany invented it, France developed and raced it, the USA mass-produced it); importance by 1920 greater for industry, engineering and war than for everyday European life; contemporary view (exciting luxury, or dangerous nuisance) vs later view (its mass social effects came mostly after 1920).

## 3. Traps

- Do not use: "Joseph Lister, a Scottish surgeon", because Lister was English (born in Essex); he worked in Glasgow and Edinburgh. Say "a surgeon working in Glasgow".
- Do not use: "spray the operating theatre from 1865–67", because Lister's carbolic spray came later (from about 1870) and he later dropped it. Say he used carbolic acid on wounds, dressings and instruments from 1865.
- Do not use: "Benz and Daimler never met", because it is widely repeated but not well attested. Say only that they worked separately.
- Do not use: "the 1896 Act raised the limit to 12 mph", because the Act set 14 mph and regulations then set 12 mph; sources differ. Say only that it raised the speed limit and ended the red-flag rules.
- Do not use: "the price fell to under 300 dollars by the early 1920s", because the exact year and model vary between sources. Use the wording in section 2.
- Do not use: "about 106 km" as a single exact figure for Bertha Benz's drive; say "about 100 km".
- Do not use: "her notebooks are still radioactive" or other trivia not in section 2.
- Do not use: "Daimler and Maybach fitted their engine to a carriage in 1886" as the first car; it was a converted coach. Benz's 1885–86 vehicle is the first designed as a whole.
- Do not name Siegfried Marcus or other rival "first car" claimants, or Robert William Thomson's earlier tyre patent; they are disputed or off-syllabus.
- Do not quote any saying of Ford, Benz or Curie (including Ford's remark about colour); no quotation in this subsection is checked.
- Do not give Marie Curie's 1911 Nobel as a Physics prize, or the 1903 one as Chemistry: 1903 Physics (shared), 1911 Chemistry (alone).
- Confusables:
  - Henri Becquerel (discovered uranium rays, 1896) vs Röntgen (X-rays, 1895) vs Curie (named radioactivity, new elements).
  - Polonium (July 1898, named after Poland) vs radium (December 1898).

  - Wright brothers (aeroplane, 1903) vs Zeppelin (airship, 1900) vs Blériot (Channel, 1909) vs Alcock and Brown (Atlantic, 1919).

## 4. What exams ask (topics only)

- Higher Level essays on key developments in science, technology and medicine 1871–1920: organise by field (medicine, science, communications, transport) and for each give named people, dates and the effect on ordinary life; finish with judgement (which mattered most, and the link to war). A list of inventors without effects scores poorly.
- Higher Level essays on the motor car's importance or effects to 1920: strong answers distinguish invention (Benz, Daimler) from mass production (Ford) and judge that before 1920 the main effects in Europe were on industry, engineering and war, not everyday life; use the Marne taxis and Verdun lorries as concrete war evidence, and the Model T price fall and assembly-line times as evidence of cost cutting.
- Combination essays: the motor car with medicine; Curie with the motor car and Krupp; Curie with Leo XIII and another personality; Curie with women in the workforce and Rosa Luxemburg ("what did your study teach you"). Each part must stand on its own with dates, then link to a theme: science and national prestige, science and war, women in public life.
- Essays on industry and society (1870–1914 or 1871–1918) and on German industrialisation can draw on the petrol engine, Benz and Daimler as German industry, and the car's demand for steel, rubber and oil.
- Ordinary Level short paragraphs (the most frequent): Marie Curie's life and work (birth, Sorbonne, radioactivity, polonium and radium, two Nobels, war X-ray units, death from radiation); the invention of the car (Otto, Daimler and Maybach, Benz, Bertha Benz, Ford); medicine (Pasteur's vaccines, Koch's discoveries). Marks go to separate, accurate points with dates.
- Ordinary Level documents questions on early motoring: an advertisement or a motorist's account. Students read closely, then use background knowledge: who cars were sold to (the wealthy), the problems of early motoring (fuel, dust, open cars, lamps, breakdowns), who pioneered the petrol engine (Daimler, Benz). Short accurate answers taken from the document, then one point of outside knowledge.
- Explanations that earn marks: link germ theory to vaccines and to antiseptic and aseptic surgery; link X-rays to battlefield surgery through Curie's units; link the assembly line to lower prices and the five-dollar day to staff turnover; link the car to steel, rubber and oil industries and to road building.
- Curie judgement points: achievements (two Nobels, new elements, medical and wartime uses) vs obstacles (sex, foreign birth, the 1911 Academy vote and press attacks, the cost of radiation to her health).
