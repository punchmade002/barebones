## 1. Must cover (one bullet or block for every item; tick each off)

Background (assumed knowledge)
- [ ] X1 (assumed) The long roots of European anti-Semitism: Christian religious hostility, economic resentment, and from the late nineteenth century "scientific" racial theories; Russian pogroms, the Dreyfus Affair and the forged Protocols of the Elders of Zion as examples. One or two bullets with a cross-reference to the notes on Europe before 1914; do not retell them.
- [ ] X2 (assumed) Germany after 1918: the "stab in the back" myth, Jews blamed for defeat, Versailles, communism and hyperinflation; the Nazis came to power in January 1933. One bullet; the rise of Hitler is in the Weimar notes.
- [ ] X3 (assumed) The tools of the Nazi state: the SS (under Himmler), the Gestapo, concentration camps from 1933 (Dachau). One bullet; the Nazi state notes tell them in full.

Nazi racial ideology
- [ ] R1 Anti-Semitism (key concept): define it; the Nazi shift from religious to racial anti-Semitism, so that conversion or assimilation could not make a Jew "German".
- [ ] R2 Nazi racial ideology: history as a struggle between races; "Aryans" at the top, Jews portrayed as a racial enemy behind both capitalism and communism; Mein Kampf (1925).
- [ ] R3 Herrenvolk (key concept): the "master race" of Germanic people; Slavs as Untermenschen (subhumans); link to lebensraum in the east, which brought millions more Jews under German rule.
- [ ] R4 Propaganda against Jews: Der Stürmer (Julius Streicher); school teaching of race "science"; the films The Eternal Jew and Jud Süß (both 1940). One or two bullets; Goebbels's propaganda machine belongs to the propaganda notes.
- [ ] R5 German Jews in 1933: about 500,000, under one per cent of the population, largely integrated; many had served in the First World War.

Persecution in Germany, 1933–1939
- [ ] P1 The boycott of Jewish shops and businesses, 1 April 1933: lasted one day; mixed public response.
- [ ] P2 Exclusion laws from 1933: the civil service law (April 1933) dismissing Jewish officials; later exclusion from the law, medicine, journalism, culture and the universities. The book burning (May 1933) in one clause only.
- [ ] P3 The Nuremberg Laws (15 September 1935): the Reich Citizenship Law and the Law for the Protection of German Blood and Honour; Jews lost citizenship; marriage and sexual relations with "Germans" banned; a decree of November 1935 defined who counted as a Jew by grandparents. Announced at the Nuremberg rally of 1935 (one clause; the rallies are in another subsection).
- [ ] P4 "Aryanisation" of Jewish businesses and property (forced sales at low prices), accelerating in 1938; Austria after the Anschluss (March 1938): public humiliation and violence against the Jews of Vienna.
- [ ] P5 Kristallnacht causes: the expulsion of Polish Jews from Germany (October 1938); Herschel Grynszpan shoots Ernst vom Rath in Paris; Goebbels uses his death to launch a pogrom.
- [ ] P6 Kristallnacht events and results (9–10 November 1938): synagogues burned, shops and homes wrecked, Jews killed, about 30,000 men sent to Dachau, Buchenwald and Sachsenhausen; the collective fine of one billion Reichsmarks; Jews then excluded from economic life and German schools. A turning point from legal discrimination to open violence.
- [ ] P7 Emigration: about 280,000 Jews left Germany by September 1939; obstacles (loss of property, emigration taxes, visas, quotas abroad).
- [ ] P8 The Évian Conference (July 1938): called on Roosevelt's initiative; 32 countries; almost none would take more refugees (only the Dominican Republic made a substantial offer).
- [ ] P9 The Kindertransport (December 1938 to September 1939): about 10,000 children to Britain; their parents were not admitted.
- [ ] P10 Anti-Semitism beyond Germany before the war: Italy's racial laws (1938). One bullet; how the Church responded belongs to the church–state notes.

War, ghettos and mass shootings, 1939–1941
- [ ] W1 Poland, 1939: about 3 million Polish Jews came under German or Soviet rule; armbands with a Star of David in occupied Poland from late 1939; the Madagascar Plan (1940) to deport Jews overseas, abandoned.
- [ ] W2 Ghettos: Łódź (sealed 1940), Warsaw (sealed November 1940, about 400,000 people, the largest); Jewish Councils (Judenräte) forced to administer them.
- [ ] W3 Ghetto conditions: overcrowding, starvation rations, typhus, forced labour, high death rates before any deportation.
- [ ] W4 The Einsatzgruppen: SS and police mobile killing units (four groups) following the army into the Soviet Union from June 1941; mass shootings of Jews, Roma and communist officials, helped by German police battalions and local auxiliaries.
- [ ] W5 Babi Yar (29–30 September 1941): about 33,000 Jews shot near Kiev.
- [ ] W6 Germany 1941: the yellow star compulsory from September 1941; deportations of German Jews to the east from October 1941.

The "Final Solution"
- [ ] F1 The decision: mass murder grew in 1941; no written Hitler order survives; Göring's order to Heydrich (31 July 1941) to prepare a "total solution"; Himmler and Heydrich as organisers.
- [ ] F2 The Wannsee Conference (20 January 1942): chaired by Heydrich, minutes kept by Eichmann; senior officials coordinated the deportation of all European Jews; the list of about 11 million Jews to be included, even in neutral and unoccupied countries (Ireland was listed with 4,000). It coordinated a policy already under way; it did not start it.
- [ ] F3 The death camps, all in occupied Poland: Chełmno (gas vans, December 1941); the Operation Reinhard camps Bełżec, Sobibór and Treblinka (opened 1942); Majdanek; Auschwitz-Birkenau. The difference between a concentration camp and a death (extermination) camp.
- [ ] F4 Auschwitz: the main camp, Birkenau (gas chambers, Zyklon B) and Monowitz (labour for IG Farben); selection on the ramp; about 1.1 million murdered, about 1 million of them Jews.
- [ ] F5 Deportations from across Europe by rail, organised by Eichmann's office; the round-ups in France (one clause; the France notes tell the Vel' d'Hiv round-up); Hungary 1944: about 430,000 deported to Auschwitz in about eight weeks after the German occupation in March.
- [ ] F6 Death marches (late 1944–1945): prisoners driven west into Germany as the Soviets advanced; very many died.
- [ ] F7 Scale: about six million Jews murdered, about 3 million of them Polish Jews; national rates of loss (Poland and the Baltic states about 90 per cent, the Netherlands about 75 per cent, France about 25 per cent); Anne Frank as an individual victim. The term "Shoah".

Other victims of Nazi racial policy
- [ ] O1 People with disabilities: the sterilisation law (July 1933, about 400,000 sterilised); Aktion T4 (from 1939, about 70,000 murdered by August 1941); Bishop Galen's protest (one clause; the church–state notes tell it); T4 staff and gas techniques later used in the death camps.
- [ ] O2 Roma and Sinti: persecuted as racially inferior; estimates of the dead range from about 200,000 to 500,000.
- [ ] O3 Others: about 3 million of some 5.7 million Soviet prisoners of war died in German captivity; Polish elites and Slavs as forced labour; homosexual men (Paragraph 175); Jehovah's Witnesses; political opponents.

Resistance, collaboration, bystanders and rescuers
- [ ] J1 The Warsaw ghetto uprising (19 April to 16 May 1943): the Jewish Fighting Organisation (ŻOB) under Mordechai Anielewicz; the ghetto destroyed.
- [ ] J2 Revolts in the camps: Treblinka (August 1943), Sobibór (October 1943), and the Sonderkommando revolt at Auschwitz-Birkenau (October 1944).
- [ ] J3 Other Jewish resistance: partisans in the forests (the Bielski brothers in Belorussia); unarmed resistance: smuggling, schools, religious and cultural life, and Emanuel Ringelblum's secret archive in the Warsaw ghetto (Oneg Shabbat).
- [ ] J4 Collaborators: governments and police who helped (Vichy's anti-Jewish statute of October 1940 and French police round-ups; Dutch officials; local killers in Lithuania and elsewhere); the Judenräte's impossible position (W2) as a matter of debate, not collaboration.
- [ ] J5 Bystanders: indifference, fear and profit from Jewish property; the churches' limited public protest (one clause; the church–state notes tell it).
- [ ] J6 Rescuers: the Righteous Among the Nations (Yad Vashem); Oskar Schindler; Raoul Wallenberg in Budapest; the Danish rescue (October 1943).
- [ ] J7 The Allies: knowledge of mass murder by 1942; the Allied declaration of December 1942; no bombing of Auschwitz or its railway lines; immigration limits; the debate over whether more could have been done.

Liberation, trials and legacy
- [ ] L1 Liberation of the camps: Majdanek (July 1944) and Auschwitz (27 January 1945) by the Soviets; Buchenwald and Dachau by the Americans and Bergen-Belsen by the British (April 1945); the shock of the newsreels and photographs.
- [ ] L2 The International Military Tribunal at Nuremberg (November 1945 to October 1946): 22 leading Nazis tried; the new charge of crimes against humanity.
- [ ] L3 Later trials: doctors, SS and camp staff; Eichmann captured in Argentina (1960) and tried in Jerusalem (1961).
- [ ] L4 Legacy for Europe: the word "genocide" (Raphael Lemkin, 1944) and the UN Genocide Convention (1948); Jewish displaced persons after 1945; emigration to Palestine and the founding of Israel (1948). Two bullets at most; it lies just past the topic's dates.

Key terms
- [ ] K1 keyTerms: the three key concepts (anti-Semitism, the Holocaust, herrenvolk) plus Nuremberg Laws, Kristallnacht, Aryanisation, ghetto, Judenrat, Einsatzgruppen, Wannsee Conference, Final Solution, death camp, Kindertransport, Righteous Among the Nations.

Assessment
- [ ] A1 Assessment block: how anti-Semitism and the Holocaust affected Europe (the destruction of Jewish communities and culture; the complicity of states and societies vs rescue and resistance; the refugee crisis; the legal and moral legacy) as paired points.
- [ ] A2 Contemporary vs later views and the historians' debate: many contemporaries treated persecution as Germany's internal affair or did not believe the reports; historians debate intentionalist vs functionalist explanations, and why ordinary men became killers (Christopher Browning, Ordinary Men, 1992; Daniel Goldhagen, Hitler's Willing Executioners, 1996).

## 3. Traps

Do not use (doubtful or wrong in the current notes):
- Do not use "the quotas for Jewish refugees were not filled in many years": there were no quotas for Jews as such. American quotas were by country of birth, and the German–Austrian quota went unfilled through most of the 1930s and was filled only in 1939. Say immigration limits and visa rules kept most refugees out.
- Do not use "destroyed over 250 synagogues and damaged more than a thousand": the second figure is not settled. Use P6 only.
- Do not use "the boycott ... was poorly supported and was called off after a day" as stated: it was announced as a one-day action, and public response was mixed. Use P1.
- Do not use "Treblinka alone accounted for over 800,000 deaths" as a single figure: estimates vary. Use the range in section 2.
- Do not use "Hungarian Jews ... in under two months": use "in about eight weeks between May and July 1944".
- Do not use the current notes' "death marches ... in which tens of thousands died": the total is uncertain and probably much higher. Say very many died; give no figure.
- Do not use "Oskar Schindler ... saved about 1,200 people from deportation to Auschwitz": say he saved about 1,200 Jews by employing them in his factories.
- Do not use "the Einsatzgruppen ... carried out mass shootings of ... political commissars" as their main task: the army shot most captured commissars. Say Jews, Roma and communist officials.
- Do not use "the Wannsee Conference ... set out the plan" or the keyTerm "the plan ... set out at Wannsee": mass murder was already under way; Wannsee coordinated it across government. The phrase "Final Solution" was in use before the conference.
- Do not use the keyTerm and factCard wording that the Nuremberg Laws of 15 September "defined Jews by descent": the definition came in the decree of 14 November 1935 (P3).
- Do not say the Kristallnacht shooting and the pogrom were on the same day: vom Rath was shot on 7 November and died on 9 November.
- Do not repeat facts: the current notes give Évian, the Allied response and the six million figure in more than one block, and split the death camps across two blocks. Each fact once.

Confusables:
- Nuremberg Laws (1935) vs Nuremberg Rallies (annual party rallies, in the propaganda notes) vs Nuremberg trials (1945–46).
- Concentration camp (prison and labour camp, e.g. Dachau, Buchenwald, Bergen-Belsen, where many died of disease and starvation) vs death camp (built for killing: Chełmno, Bełżec, Sobibór, Treblinka; Auschwitz-Birkenau and Majdanek were both). Bergen-Belsen and Dachau were not death camps.
- Warsaw ghetto uprising (Jewish fighters, April–May 1943) vs the Warsaw Uprising (the Polish Home Army, August–October 1944). The second belongs to the war notes; do not mix them.
- Évian (1938, refugees) vs Wannsee (1942, deportation and murder).
- Himmler (head of the SS, overall in charge) vs Heydrich (organiser, chaired Wannsee) vs Eichmann (deportation logistics, minutes at Wannsee) vs Göring (signed the July 1941 order).
- Herrenvolk (master race) vs Volksgemeinschaft (national community, in the Nazi state notes) vs lebensraum (living space, in the foreign policy notes).
- Kindertransport (children to Britain, 1938–39) vs the Danish rescue (to Sweden, 1943).
- Einsatzgruppen (mobile killing squads in the east) vs Sonderkommando (Jewish prisoners forced to work in the crematoria) vs Judenrat (Jewish council).
- Babi Yar (Kiev, 1941) is a mass shooting, not a camp.
- Do not call the Judenräte collaborators: they were coerced and historians disagree about their choices.
- Stalin's Gulag and the Soviet purges are not part of the Holocaust.

## 4. What exams ask (topics only)

- Higher Level essay (100 marks) on how anti-Semitism and/or the Holocaust affected Europe, 1920–45: marks for a clear thesis that names the effects and argues them throughout, not a narrative of Nazi laws. Strong answers organise by effect: ideology made a long prejudice into state policy; persecution in Germany and Austria (laws, Kristallnacht); a refugee crisis that other states refused to solve (Évian, quotas); genocide across occupied Europe (ghettos, Einsatzgruppen, Wannsee, the death camps, the six million); complicity of collaborators and bystanders balanced by resistance and rescue; and the legacy (Nuremberg, crimes against humanity, genocide as a crime). Each paragraph needs named events, places and dates.
- Higher Level: "Europe", not only Germany. Bring in Italy's racial laws, Vichy France, the Netherlands, Hungary 1944, Denmark, Poland and the Baltic states, and the Allies. An answer confined to Germany limits its marks.
- Higher Level judgement: was the Holocaust planned from the start or did it develop step by step (intentionalist vs functionalist); how far ordinary Germans and other Europeans shared responsibility; whether the Allies could have done more. A short balanced paragraph on one of these earns overall-evaluation marks.
- Ordinary Level short paragraph (30 marks) on Nazi anti-Semitism or the Holocaust: a chronological run of dated facts earns most marks: Mein Kampf (1925), the boycott (1 April 1933), the Nuremberg Laws (15 September 1935), Kristallnacht (9–10 November 1938), ghettos (Warsaw), the Einsatzgruppen (from June 1941) and Babi Yar, Wannsee (20 January 1942), Auschwitz, the six million, liberation (27 January 1945).
- Ordinary Level short question (6 marks): what the Final Solution was: the Nazi plan to murder all the Jews of Europe.
- Precision examiners reward: date Kristallnacht and the Nuremberg Laws exactly; distinguish concentration and death camps; name at least two death camps; say who chaired Wannsee; give the six million as "about".
- Cause and result pairs that suit tips: vom Rath's death → Kristallnacht → mass arrests, fine and exclusion from the economy; invasion of Poland and Barbarossa → millions more Jews under German rule → ghettos and mass shootings → industrial killing in the camps.
