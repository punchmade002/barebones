## 1. Must cover (one bullet or block for every item; tick each off)

Background (assumed knowledge)
- [ ] X1 (assumed) The Roman Question: the Italian state took Rome in 1870 and ended the Papal States; the popes refused to recognise the Kingdom of Italy and called themselves "prisoners in the Vatican"; for decades Catholics were told not to take part in national politics. One bullet.
- [ ] X2 (assumed) Mussolini in power from 1922 and a one-party dictator by 1926; Fascism began anticlerical, but Mussolini dropped this to win Catholic and conservative support; the Vatican let the Catholic Popular Party (Popolari) go, and its leader, the priest Luigi Sturzo, went into exile. One bullet; the rise of Fascism is in the notes on Mussolini's Italy.
- [ ] X3 (assumed) Germany in 1933: Hitler Chancellor (30 January 1933), the Enabling Act passed with the votes of the Catholic Centre Party, Gleichschaltung (bringing every organisation into line). Germany was about two-thirds Protestant and one-third Catholic; Protestants were divided into 28 regional (Land) churches; Catholics remembered Bismarck's Kulturkampf of the 1870s. One or two bullets.
- [ ] X4 (assumed) The dictatorships' own propaganda machines (Goebbels's ministry, the People's Receiver, Riefenstahl; Mussolini's Istituto Luce) are told elsewhere: one clause and a cross-reference, used only for contrast with Anglo-American radio and cinema.

Mussolini and the Church
- [ ] M1 Motives on each side: Mussolini wanted the support of a Catholic nation, legitimacy at home and prestige abroad; Pope Pius XI (pope 1922–1939) wanted the Roman Question settled, Catholic life (schools, marriage, Catholic Action) protected, and an ally against communism.
- [ ] M2 The Lateran Treaties (11 February 1929), signed by Mussolini and Cardinal Pietro Gasparri: three parts, a treaty (Vatican City), a financial convention (compensation) and a concordat (the Church's place in Italy); ratified June 1929.
- [ ] M3 The terms: Vatican City an independent sovereign state of about 44 hectares; the papacy recognised the Kingdom of Italy with Rome as its capital; compensation of 1.75 billion lire (750 million in cash and 1 billion in state bonds) for the lost Papal States; Catholicism the sole religion of the state; religious teaching in state primary and secondary schools; church marriage valid in civil law; bishops to swear loyalty to the state; Catholic Action allowed if it stayed out of politics.
- [ ] M4 Results for Mussolini: Catholic backing in the plebiscite election of March 1929; prestige at home and abroad; Pius XI publicly praised him as a man Providence had sent (describe this; do not quote it). The settlement is often called Mussolini's most lasting achievement.
- [ ] M5 The Catholic Action dispute (1931): the regime saw Catholic youth and student groups as rivals to the Fascist youth movement; Fascist attacks on Catholic centres and the dissolution of Catholic youth groups (May 1931); Pius XI's encyclical Non abbiamo bisogno ("We have no need", 29 June 1931) condemned the regime's claim to monopolise the young; the compromise of September 1931 kept Catholic Action alive under the bishops but confined it to religious work.
- [ ] M6 The racial laws (1938): the Manifesto of the Race (14 July 1938) and laws of September–November 1938 against Jews, following the Axis with Germany; Pius XI objected above all to the ban on marriages between Italians and Jews (including Jews who had become Catholics) as a breach of the Concordat's marriage terms; his remark to Belgian pilgrims in September 1938 that Christians are spiritually Semites; the Church protested but did not break with the regime. Pius XI died in February 1939; his successor Pius XII (Eugenio Pacelli) was more cautious.
- [ ] M7 Judgement on Italy: both sides gained; the Church was the one large organisation in Italy that stayed outside Fascist control (a limit on Italian totalitarianism); the settlement outlived Fascism and was revised only in 1984.

Hitler and the churches
- [ ] H1 Nazi aims and tactics: the party programme (1920) promised "positive Christianity"; Hitler courted the churches in 1933 while planning to subordinate them; leading Nazis (Alfred Rosenberg, Martin Bormann, Himmler) were hostile to Christianity; Rosenberg's The Myth of the Twentieth Century (1930) was placed on the Church's Index of forbidden books (1934). The German bishops lifted their ban on Catholics joining the Nazi Party (28 March 1933).
- [ ] H2 The Reich Concordat (20 July 1933), signed by Cardinal Eugenio Pacelli (Vatican Secretary of State) and Vice-Chancellor Franz von Papen: the Church kept freedom of worship, its schools, religious teaching and its religious organisations; clergy were barred from party politics and bishops swore loyalty to the state. The Centre Party dissolved itself on 5 July 1933. Gains for Hitler: international respectability and an end to political Catholicism.
- [ ] H3 Breaking the Concordat: Erich Klausener, head of Catholic Action in Berlin, murdered in the Night of the Long Knives (30 June 1934); Catholic youth groups squeezed and then banned as the Hitler Youth became compulsory; Catholic schools replaced by state "community schools" by the end of the 1930s; the Catholic press curbed; show "morality trials" of priests and monks (1936–37); clergy sent to camps (a priests' block at Dachau). Popular resistance could win locally: the order removing crucifixes from schools in Oldenburg was withdrawn after protests (1936).
- [ ] H4 Reichskirche (key concept): the plan for a single, centralised Protestant Reich Church loyal to the Nazi state, replacing the 28 regional churches. Its backers, the German Christians (founded 1932), wanted a Christianity fitted to Nazi ideas: the "Aryan paragraph" barring pastors of Jewish descent, and talk of dropping the Old Testament. They won about two-thirds of the votes in the church elections of July 1933; Ludwig Müller, Hitler's adviser on church affairs, became Reich Bishop (September 1933).
- [ ] H5 Failure of the Reichskirche: a mass German Christian rally in Berlin's Sportpalast (November 1933) demanding the Old Testament be dropped drove moderates away; Müller never controlled the churches and was sidelined; the regime set up a Ministry for Church Affairs under Hanns Kerrl (1935) to impose control directly, with little more success. The Protestant churches split three ways: German Christians, the Confessing Church, and a cautious neutral majority.
- [ ] H6 The Confessing Church: grew from Niemöller's Pastors' Emergency League (September 1933, several thousand pastors); the Barmen Declaration (31 May 1934, mainly drafted by the Swiss theologian Karl Barth) said the Church answered to Christ, not to the state or a Führer. It defended the Church's independence and its teaching; it did not set out to overthrow the regime, and few of its members spoke up for Jews who were not Christians.
- [ ] H7 Martin Niemöller: U-boat commander in the First World War, pastor at Dahlem in Berlin; at first welcomed the Nazis; founded the Pastors' Emergency League and led the Confessing Church; arrested 1 July 1937, tried in 1938, then held in Sachsenhausen and Dachau as Hitler's personal prisoner until 1945.
- [ ] H8 Dietrich Bonhoeffer: Lutheran theologian of the Confessing Church; ran its secret seminary at Finkenwalde (from 1935) until the Gestapo closed it (1937); moved from church opposition to active resistance through contacts in military intelligence (the Abwehr) and the plotters against Hitler; arrested April 1943; executed at Flossenbürg concentration camp on 9 April 1945.
- [ ] H9 Mit brennender Sorge ("With burning concern", dated 14 March 1937): Pius XI's encyclical, written in German, drafted with Cardinal Michael von Faulhaber of Munich and Pacelli; condemned breaches of the Concordat and the worship of race and state; smuggled into Germany and read from Catholic pulpits on Palm Sunday, 21 March 1937; the Gestapo seized copies and closed the printers, and persecution of the clergy increased.
- [ ] H10 Clemens August von Galen, Bishop of Münster (the "Lion of Münster"): his sermons of July–August 1941 attacked Gestapo seizures of church property and the killing of disabled people (the T4 "euthanasia" programme); the programme was officially halted in August 1941 but killing continued in secret; the regime did not arrest him for fear of Catholic opinion in wartime. Cross-refer to the notes on the Holocaust for T4 itself.
- [ ] H11 Limits of church opposition: the churches defended their own institutions and teaching far more than the victims of the regime; there was little public protest at the persecution of the Jews (Kristallnacht passed without an official protest from either church); most clergy and churchgoers conformed and many backed the war. The debate over Pius XII: his Christmas broadcast of 1942 spoke of people killed because of their nationality or race without naming the Jews or Germany; critics such as John Cornwell (Hitler's Pope, 1999) call this silence complicity; defenders say public protest would have made things worse and point to Catholic help for Jews in hiding.
- [ ] H12 Long-term Nazi plans: Bormann, Himmler and Rosenberg intended to destroy the churches' influence after the war; the "German Faith Movement" and neo-pagan ideas had little popular following. One bullet.
- [ ] H13 Comparison: both dictators began with a concordat, both later clashed over youth and race; Mussolini needed the Church and kept a working partnership, while Hitler saw the churches as rivals for total loyalty and moved from courtship to persecution. The churches survived both regimes as the largest institutions outside party control.

Radio
- [ ] R1 Radio as the new mass medium: from the early 1920s; cheap sets brought news, music, sport, comedy and leaders' voices into the home; in Britain about 9 million licences by 1939.
- [ ] R2 The BBC and John Reith: the British Broadcasting Company (1922), with Reith as general manager; the British Broadcasting Corporation (1 January 1927) under royal charter with Reith as its first director-general (to 1938); a monopoly funded by the licence fee, with no advertising; Reith's public-service ideal to "inform, educate and entertain"; strict Sunday programming; the Empire Service (1932).
- [ ] R3 American networks: NBC (1926) and CBS (1927); commercial radio paid for by advertisers, who sponsored whole programmes; comedy, variety, dance bands, serials ("soap operas", named after their soap-company sponsors). Contrast with the BBC's public-service model is an exam point.
- [ ] R4 Roosevelt's fireside chats: from 12 March 1933 (the banking crisis); about 30 talks up to 1944 on the New Deal and the war; a democratic leader using radio to explain and reassure, as against the dictators' mass rallies.
- [ ] R5 The power of radio: Orson Welles's The War of the Worlds (CBS, 30 October 1938), a drama about a Martian invasion; newspaper reports of mass panic were exaggerated, but it showed radio's hold on listeners.
- [ ] R6 Radio Luxembourg (from 1933): commercial English-language programmes beamed into Britain, especially on Sundays, playing the dance music and American-style shows the BBC avoided; the first challenge to the BBC monopoly.
- [ ] R7 Wartime radio in Britain: the BBC Home Service (from September 1939) and the Forces Programme (from January 1940), lighter for the troops; news bulletins trusted at home and in occupied Europe; Churchill's broadcasts (cross-refer to the war notes); de Gaulle's appeal to the French on the BBC (18 June 1940); the "V for Victory" campaign (1941); coded messages to resistance groups. Comedy such as ITMA in one clause (the Home Front notes tell it).
- [ ] R8 American wartime radio: Edward R. Murrow's CBS broadcasts from London during the Blitz built American sympathy for Britain; the American Forces Network in Britain (from 4 July 1943) played American music to troops and, unofficially, to British listeners; Glenn Miller's American band broadcast to the forces (Miller disappeared over the English Channel in December 1944).
- [ ] R9 Enemy radio: William Joyce ("Lord Haw-Haw") broadcast Nazi propaganda to Britain from 1939, more mocked than believed, and was hanged for treason in January 1946; the song "Lili Marleen" (Lale Andersen's recording, 1939), broadcast nightly from the German forces' station in Belgrade from 1941, was loved by soldiers on both sides in North Africa.

Cinema
- [ ] C1 Cinema as the most popular entertainment of the age: in Britain about 20 million admissions a week by the late 1930s; large "picture palaces"; audiences strongly young, female and working-class; "going to the pictures" a weekly habit.
- [ ] C2 Talkies: The Jazz Singer (Warner Bros, 1927), starring Al Jolson, the first feature with synchronised singing and speech; by the early 1930s silent films were finished; effects: new stars, musicals, language barriers in Europe (dubbing and subtitles), and the strength of English-language films in Britain.
- [ ] C3 The Hollywood studio system: a few major studios (MGM, Paramount, Warner Bros, 20th Century Fox, RKO) controlled production, distribution and many cinemas (vertical integration); stars under long contracts; genres (musicals, gangster films, westerns, comedies, cartoons such as Disney's Snow White, 1937); the Production Code ("Hays Code", 1930, strictly enforced from 1934) governing morality on screen.
- [ ] C4 Hollywood's dominance of Europe and the British response: American films filled most British screen time; the Cinematograph Films Act (1927) required a rising quota of British films; it produced many cheap "quota quickies" but also protected a home industry.
- [ ] C5 British cinema: Alexander Korda's The Private Life of Henry VIII (1933), a success in America; Alfred Hitchcock's The 39 Steps (1935); the popular comedians Gracie Fields and George Formby.
- [ ] C6 Newsreels: short news films before the main feature; British Pathé and British Movietone News (from 1929) in Britain; the American March of Time (from 1935); for many people the only moving pictures of events; generally patriotic and deferential. Their use by Germany and Italy for propaganda belongs to other notes (one clause).
- [ ] C7 Wartime films in Britain: the Ministry of Information's films division; documentaries by Humphrey Jennings (Listen to Britain, 1942; Fires Were Started, 1943); Noël Coward's naval drama In Which We Serve (1942); Laurence Olivier's Henry V (1944) as patriotic spectacle; films stressing a "people's war" of all classes.
- [ ] C8 Wartime films in America: Mrs Miniver (1942), a picture of an English family in the war that won the Oscar for Best Picture and built support for Britain; Casablanca (1942); Frank Capra's Why We Fight series (1942–45) explaining the war to American servicemen.
- [ ] C9 The dictatorships and American film: Fascist Italy built the Cinecittà studios (opened by Mussolini, 1937), began the Venice Film Festival (1932) and, from 1938, gave the state a monopoly on importing foreign films, after which the big American studios pulled out of Italy; Nazi Germany censored and restricted American films in the 1930s and banned them in the war. Hollywood stayed popular with audiences in both countries.

Key personality: Charlie Chaplin (one block, in order)
- [ ] P1 Who he was: born in London, 16 April 1889, into poverty; a child performer in the music hall; toured America with Fred Karno's comedy company (from 1910); joined Mack Sennett's Keystone studio in late 1913.
- [ ] P2 The Tramp (first seen 1914): the little man with bowler hat, cane and baggy trousers, poor but dignified; developed in The Kid (1921), The Gold Rush (1925) and City Lights (1931, still silent with a music score); co-founder of United Artists (1919) with Mary Pickford, Douglas Fairbanks and D.W. Griffith, so he owned his films and worked outside the big studios; he resisted talkies longer than anyone.
- [ ] P3 Modern Times (1936): the Tramp as a factory worker caught in the machine, unemployment and the Depression; social comment through comedy; the first film in which his voice is heard (a nonsense song); the Tramp's last appearance.
- [ ] P4 The Great Dictator (1940): his first full talking picture; he played Adenoid Hynkel, dictator of Tomania (a parody of Hitler), and a Jewish barber who is mistaken for him; a parody of Mussolini as Napaloni of Bacteria; it ends with the barber's speech for peace and humanity; made while the USA was still neutral; his biggest box-office success and five Academy Award nominations; in his autobiography (1964) he wrote that he could not have made it had he known the truth of the camps.
- [ ] P5 How contemporaries and historians saw him: the world's most famous film star, loved across Europe; the Nazis banned his films; in the USA he was attacked for his left-wing views and private life, and in 1952, while he was travelling to London, his permission to re-enter the USA was withdrawn; he settled in Switzerland; honorary Oscar (1972) and knighthood (1975). Historians see him as one of the founders of film comedy as an art and as proof that popular cinema could carry political and social criticism. Participant: he shaped popular culture, not a witness to it.

Key personality: Bing Crosby (one block, in order)
- [ ] B1 Who he was: born in Tacoma, Washington, 3 May 1903; sang with Paul Whiteman's band as one of the Rhythm Boys in the late 1920s; his own CBS radio show from 1931; his soft "crooning" style used the microphone rather than projecting the voice, and suited radio and records.
- [ ] B2 Radio and records: host of NBC's Kraft Music Hall from the mid-1930s to 1946; recorded for Decca from 1934 and became the best-selling recording artist of his time; radio, records and film fed each other, a model of the modern entertainment industry.
- [ ] B3 Films: Paramount star; the Road films with Bob Hope and Dorothy Lamour (from Road to Singapore, 1940); the Oscar for Best Actor as a priest in Going My Way (1944).
- [ ] B4 "White Christmas": Irving Berlin's song, sung by Crosby in Holiday Inn (1942); its longing for home made it the song of soldiers far from home; his recording is often called the best-selling single of all time.
- [ ] B5 Entertaining the troops: broadcasts for the forces and war-bond drives; a USO tour of camps in England and France in 1944. How he was seen: the most popular entertainer of the age, the relaxed, friendly face of America; historians see him as central to the rise of recorded popular music and to American cultural influence in Europe.

The spread of American culture in Europe and reactions to it
- [ ] A1 Jazz and the "jazz age": the Original Dixieland Jass Band in London (1919); Josephine Baker in Paris (1925); dance crazes such as the Charleston; American slang, clothes, hairstyles and film-star glamour copied especially by the young.
- [ ] A2 Reactions in the democracies: British critics feared Americanisation of speech, taste and morals; the film quota and the BBC's public-service ethos were partly defences against it; audiences went on choosing American films and dance music.
- [ ] A3 Reactions in the dictatorships: Nazi Germany banned jazz from German radio (1935) and showed it in the "Degenerate Music" exhibition (1938) as racially alien; the "Swing Youth" of Hamburg and other cities defied the regime and faced arrest from 1941, some sent to camps; Fascist Italy promoted Italian films against Hollywood (C9). Bans did not end American culture's appeal.
- [ ] A4 Wartime Americanisation: American troops in Britain from January 1942, well over a million by 1944, bringing swing music, chewing gum, nylons and money; admiration and resentment summed up in the saying that they were "overpaid, oversexed and over here"; Vera Lynn ("the Forces' Sweetheart"), "We'll Meet Again" (1939) as the British counterpart. After 1945 American music and film dominated western Europe. One or two bullets.

- [ ] K1 keyTerms: Reichskirche (the key concept) plus Roman Question, Lateran Treaties, concordat, Catholic Action, Non abbiamo bisogno, Reich Concordat, German Christians, Reich Bishop, Confessing Church, Barmen Declaration, Mit brennender Sorge, Kirchenkampf ("church struggle", the name for the conflict between the Nazi state and the churches), public-service broadcasting, fireside chat, talkie, studio system, Hays Code, newsreel, Cinematograph Films Act (quota), crooning, Americanisation.

Assessment
- [ ] Z1 Assessment block on church–state relations, as paired points: Mussolini's gains (Catholic support, prestige, the Roman Question solved) vs limits (the Church kept its own youth, press and moral authority; open dispute in 1931 and 1938); Hitler's gains (the Concordat's prestige, the end of the Centre Party, most churchgoers conformed) vs limits (the Reichskirche failed, the Confessing Church and Galen showed totalitarian control stopped at the church door); the churches' survival vs their failure to defend the regimes' victims.
- [ ] Z2 Assessment block on popular culture, as paired points: entertainment vs persuasion (the same media served morale and propaganda); democratic use of radio and film (BBC news, fireside chats, Chaplin's satire) vs dictatorial control; Americanisation as liberation for the young vs a threat to national cultures; its impact in war (morale, the troops' songs and films, information to occupied Europe).
- [ ] Z3 Contemporary vs later views: the Lateran Treaties hailed by Catholics in 1929 vs later criticism that the Church lent Fascism respectability; the Reich Concordat and Pius XII's wartime silence as a continuing historians' debate; Chaplin and Crosby as entertainers in their day and as historical evidence of the new mass culture now.

## 3. Traps

Do not use (doubtful or wrong in the current notes):
- Do not use the factCard "Chaplin's film parodied Adolf Hitler with the character of a Jewish barber": Hitler is parodied by the dictator Adenoid Hynkel; the barber is a separate character played by Chaplin. Use P4.
- Do not use "he went to the United States in 1913 and joined the Keystone studio" as his arrival: he first toured the USA with Fred Karno's company from 1910 and joined Keystone late in 1913.
- Do not use "He was a participant in the Hollywood studio system": Chaplin co-founded United Artists, owned his own studio and films, and worked outside the major studios. Say he worked independently of them.
- Do not use the factCard "The British Broadcasting Company was founded in 1922, and John Reith was its first director-general": Reith was general manager of the Company; he became the first director-general of the Corporation in 1927.
- Do not use "The American Forces Network broadcast ... from 1942": AFN began broadcasting in Britain on 4 July 1943.
- Do not use "the Church ... withdrew its support over the racial laws of 1938": the Church protested, mainly over the marriage ban, but did not break with the regime.
- Do not use "Under the treaties the Church gained ... state-paid priests": not a clear Lateran term. Use the terms in M3.
- Do not use "The Reichskirche plan sought ... to replace Christian doctrine with an ideology of race and national blood": the German Christians wanted a Christianity reshaped by Nazi racial ideas (the Aryan paragraph, dropping the Old Testament), not to abolish Christian doctrine. Neo-paganism (H12) was a different, fringe current.
- Do not use "the programme was halted in August 1941" without qualification: it was officially halted, and killing went on in secret.
- Do not use "Achievement for Hitler: the Concordat secured the Catholic institutions in Germany": the regime broke the Concordat within a few years. Hitler's gain was prestige and the end of political Catholicism.
- Do not use "Britain's Ministry of Information backed films such as In Which We Serve": the film was a private production made with the Navy's help. Name it as a wartime morale film without that claim.
- Do not use "In Germany American films were ... in effect excluded by 1940": the timing is not settled. Say restricted in the 1930s and banned in the war.
- Do not use "Fascist Italy imposed quotas on American films" as the main measure: use the 1938 import monopoly in C9.
- Do not use "Nazi Germany condemned jazz and swing as 'degenerate'" in quotation marks as a quotation; describe it, and name the 1938 exhibition (A3).
- Do not use the vague current lines "the Vatican's relationship with Italy was affected by the alliance with Germany, and the Church's independence was limited by the regime's pressure on the Catholic press and youth" or "the Church opposed the racial laws in part, since they challenged the Catholic doctrine that all people shared a common humanity": replace with M6.
- Do not repeat facts: the current notes give the Lateran terms in three bullets of one block (the 1.75 billion lire twice, religious teaching and marriage twice) and repeat the compensation in two factCards. Each fact once.
- Do not quote Niemöller's "First they came ..." (the wording exists in many versions), Hitler's reported remarks on Christianity from Rauschning or table talk, or Pius XI's words about Mussolini and Providence. Describe them, or leave them out.
- Do not give a sales figure for "White Christmas" or a death toll for clergy at Dachau; no checked figure.

## 4. What exams ask (topics only)

- Higher Level essay (100 marks) on church–state relations under Mussolini and Hitler: marks go to a comparative thesis argued throughout, not two separate narratives. A strong line: both began with an agreement (1929, 1933) and both moved to conflict over youth and race, but Mussolini kept a working partnership while Hitler moved from courtship to persecution. Each paragraph needs dated evidence: Lateran terms; Catholic Action 1931 and Non abbiamo bisogno; the 1938 racial laws; the Reich Concordat and the Centre Party's end; the Reichskirche, Müller and the German Christians; Barmen, Niemöller, Bonhoeffer; Mit brennender Sorge; Galen 1941.
- Higher Level judgement on church–state relations: who gained more, dictator or Church; how far the churches resisted (defending their own rights vs defending victims); whether the churches show the limits of totalitarianism. A closing paragraph on the Pius XII debate or the limits of church protest earns evaluation marks.
- Higher Level essay on the impact of Anglo-American popular culture (radio and cinema): marks for naming the forms of impact and arguing them: leisure and mass entertainment; news and information; politics (fireside chats, wartime broadcasting, Chaplin's satire); Americanisation of European youth; reactions (British quota and BBC ethos, Nazi and Fascist bans); wartime morale. Use named programmes, films, people and dates rather than general claims about "the media".
- Higher Level essay through personalities (Chaplin, Crosby, often with Riefenstahl): marks for showing what each contributed and comparing them: Chaplin (comedy as art and social comment, satire of dictatorship), Crosby (radio, records, film and troop morale), Riefenstahl (film as state propaganda). Organise by theme (entertainment, politics, war), not three biographies.
- Ordinary Level short paragraphs (30 or 40 marks), most often on the Lateran Treaties: date, signatories, Vatican City, compensation, Catholicism the state religion, religious teaching, marriage; then why each side signed. Also common: the Concordat and the Reichskirche; Chaplin's career; Crosby's career; radio (BBC, Reith, fireside chats, wartime).
- Precision examiners reward: date the Lateran Treaties (11 February 1929) and the Reich Concordat (20 July 1933) exactly; name the two encyclicals with their years and the country each addressed; give the years of Modern Times (1936) and The Great Dictator (1940); say the BBC became a corporation in 1927 under Reith.
- Cause and result pairs that suit tips: Mussolini's need for Catholic support → Lateran Treaties → Catholic backing and a lasting settlement; Fascist youth monopoly → 1931 clash → compromise; Axis with Germany → 1938 racial laws → papal protest without a break. Concordat → Centre Party gone → Concordat broken → Mit brennender Sorge. Reichskirche drive → Barmen and the Confessing Church → Reichskirche failure. Talkies (1927) → end of silent film and English-language advantage → European quotas and protection.
- Achievements vs limits pairs: Lateran gains vs the Church's independence; Concordat prestige vs failure to control the churches; BBC public service vs paternalism; Hollywood's appeal vs fears of Americanisation; popular culture as entertainment vs as propaganda.
