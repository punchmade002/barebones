## 1. Must cover (one bullet or block for every item; tick each off)

Background (assumed knowledge)
- [ ] X1 (assumed) Bismarck's alliance system by 1890: the Dual Alliance (1879), the Triple Alliance (1882) and the Reinsurance Treaty (1887), designed to keep France isolated and avoid a two-front war. One bullet only; the detail is in the notes on Bismarck's foreign policy.
- [ ] X2 (assumed) Bismarck's dismissal in 1890 and Wilhelm II's "new course". One clause only.
- [ ] X3 (assumed) French hostility to Germany after 1871 over Alsace-Lorraine (revanche) as the fixed point of European diplomacy. One clause only.
- [ ] X4 (assumed) The second industrial revolution created demand for raw materials and markets. One clause only; the detail is in the notes on German industrialisation.

The structures of diplomacy after 1890
- [ ] A1 The lapse of the Reinsurance Treaty (1890): Caprivi and Holstein, and their reasons.
- [ ] A2 Franco-Russian rapprochement: French loans from 1888, the French fleet's visit to Kronstadt and the political understanding (1891).
- [ ] A3 The military convention (1892) and its ratification (completed January 1894); its defensive terms, including mobilisation if any Triple Alliance power mobilised.
- [ ] A4 Significance of the Franco-Russian Alliance: France's isolation ended; Germany faced the two-front danger (the Schlieffen Plan in one clause only); French capital in Russian railways and industry; it lasted until the Russian revolution of 1917.
- [ ] A5 "Splendid isolation" and why Britain abandoned it: European hostility during the Boer War, German naval building (one clause; detail in the notes on the naval policy), and the failure of Anglo-German alliance talks (1898–1901).
- [ ] A6 The Anglo-Japanese Alliance (1902), renewed 1905 and 1911.
- [ ] A7 The Entente Cordiale (1904): Delcassé, Edward VII's visit to Paris (1903), the signatories, and its terms (Egypt and Morocco; other colonial disputes).
- [ ] A8 The Entente was not an alliance, but it tightened: secret staff talks from 1906; the naval arrangement of 1912.
- [ ] A9 The Anglo-Russian Entente (1907): why (Russia weakened by defeat by Japan, shared worry about Germany) and its terms (Persia, Afghanistan, Tibet).
- [ ] A10 The Triple Entente (loose understanding, no common military commitment) facing the formal Triple Alliance (renewed several times, last in 1912).
- [ ] A11 Italy's unreliability: the secret agreement with France (1902) and the war on the Ottoman Empire for Libya (1911–12).
- [ ] A12 Hardening of the blocs after 1905: German talk of "encirclement", British and French fear of German ambitions; each crisis seen as a test between blocs; the change in the balance of power from Bismarck's system to two armed camps.

Causes of the New Imperialism
- [ ] I1 What the New Imperialism was: the rush from about 1880 to acquire colonies and spheres of influence, how it differed from earlier empire-building (speed, scale, number of powers, Africa and China), and its spread to the USA and Japan.
- [ ] I2 Economic motives: raw materials (rubber, cotton, palm oil, copper, gold, diamonds), protected markets as tariffs rose from the late 1870s, outlets for investment.
- [ ] I3 The economic theory of imperialism: Hobson (1902) and Lenin (written 1916); why historians dispute it.
- [ ] I4 Strategic motives: naval bases, coaling stations, routes to India; the Suez Canal and Britain's occupation of Egypt (1882), leading to control of the Nile valley.
- [ ] I5 Nationalist motives: colonies as a sign of Great Power status; popular press, colonial societies and public opinion; France seeking compensation after 1871 under Jules Ferry.
- [ ] I6 Missionary and humanitarian motives: spreading Christianity; ending the slave trade; Livingstone and Stanley.
- [ ] I7 Ideological motives: Social Darwinism ("survival of the fittest", Spencer), racial superiority, the civilising mission (mission civilisatrice; Kipling's "The White Man's Burden").
- [ ] I8 Technology made conquest possible: quinine, steamships, railways, the telegraph, breech-loading rifles and the Maxim gun.
- [ ] I9 Competition fed on itself: pre-emptive annexation for fear of being shut out.

The Berlin Conference and the Scramble for Africa
- [ ] S1 Extent: about a tenth of Africa under European rule in 1880 (mostly coastal), about nine tenths by 1914.
- [ ] S2 Triggers: Leopold II's Congo claims, France in Tunisia (1881) and West Africa, Britain in Egypt (1882).
- [ ] S3 The Berlin Conference (1884–85): called by Bismarck, 14 states including the USA, its rules (notification, effective occupation, free trade and navigation on the Congo and Niger), no African present; it did not itself divide Africa but sped up and legalised partition.
- [ ] S4 Rival plans: Britain's Cape-to-Cairo belt (Rhodes) and France's east-west belt from the Atlantic to the Red Sea.
- [ ] S5 The other partitioners: Germany (South West Africa, Togo, Cameroon, East Africa), Italy (Eritrea, Somaliland, Libya 1911–12), Portugal (Angola, Mozambique).
- [ ] S6 Settlement by agreement: the Heligoland–Zanzibar Treaty (1890) as an example of colonial compromise between powers.
- [ ] S7 African resistance: Adwa (1896); only Ethiopia and Liberia independent by 1914.

Leopold II and the Congo
- [ ] C1 Leopold II, the International African Association (1876) and Stanley's work for him.
- [ ] C2 The Congo Free State (proclaimed 1885) as Leopold's personal possession, on promises of free trade and ending slavery.
- [ ] C3 The "red rubber" system from the 1890s: quotas, hostage-taking, beatings, mutilation, burning of villages, enforced by the Force Publique; demand for rubber driven by tyres.
- [ ] C4 The human cost: the population fell by millions from violence, disease, flight and famine; estimates vary widely. Give no figure.
- [ ] C5 The protest campaign: E.D. Morel, Roger Casement's report (1904), the Congo Reform Association (1904); Conrad and Twain.
- [ ] C6 Belgium takes over the colony (1908): significance as the first international humanitarian campaign against a colonial regime.

Rivalry in Asia and the Pacific
- [ ] P1 South-East Asia: French Indochina, British Burma and Malaya, the Dutch East Indies.
- [ ] P2 China's defeat by Japan (1894–95) and the Triple Intervention (1895).
- [ ] P3 The scramble for concessions (1897–98): leased ports and spheres of influence (Germany at Kiaochow in one clause; detail in the notes on Weltpolitik).
- [ ] P4 The USA: war with Spain (1898), the Philippines, Guam and Puerto Rico; Hay's "Open Door" (1899).
- [ ] P5 The Boxer Rising (1899–1901) and the eight-nation relief of the Beijing legations (1900).
- [ ] P6 Japan's rise (modernisation from 1868) and the Russo-Japanese War (1904–05) in outline: Russia turned back to Europe and the Balkans, and the way opened for the 1907 Entente. Detail belongs in the notes on Tsarist Russia.

Fashoda, 1898
- [ ] F1 Background: Britain's control of Egypt and the Nile; France's east-west plan.
- [ ] F2 Events: Marchand at Fashoda (July 1898); Kitchener's victory at Omdurman and arrival (September 1898); the stand-off.
- [ ] F3 France backs down (November 1898): why (naval weakness, isolation, the Dreyfus Affair at home, in one clause), Delcassé's decision; the boundary agreement (March 1899).
- [ ] F4 Significance: worst Anglo-French crisis of the period, but it convinced both sides to settle disputes, leading to 1904.

The Boer War, 1899–1902
- [ ] B1 Background: the Transvaal and Orange Free State; gold on the Witwatersrand (1886); the Uitlanders; Rhodes and the Jameson Raid (1895–96); the Kruger telegram (one clause; detail in the notes on Weltpolitik).
- [ ] B2 The war: Boer early successes and Black Week (December 1899); Mafeking (1900); British reinforcements; guerrilla war; farm-burning and the concentration camps.
- [ ] B3 The Treaty of Vereeniging (1902): the republics annexed.
- [ ] B4 Significance: British isolation and European hostility exposed; British prestige and military reputation damaged; a push to end splendid isolation; Hobson's critique arose from it.

The Moroccan crises
- [ ] M1 Background: the 1904 Entente gave France a free hand in Morocco; Germany's aims (test or break the Entente, gain compensation, assert its say in colonial questions).
- [ ] M2 The Tangier visit (1905) and Delcassé's resignation (June 1905).
- [ ] M3 The Algeciras Conference (1906): Germany supported only by Austria-Hungary; France and Spain to police Morocco's ports; a diplomatic defeat for Germany.
- [ ] M4 Results of the first crisis: the Entente strengthened; Anglo-French staff talks began.
- [ ] M5 The Agadir crisis (1911): French troops in Fez; the gunboat Panther at Agadir; Lloyd George's Mansion House speech.
- [ ] M6 The settlement (November 1911): Morocco for France, part of the French Congo for Germany; the French protectorate (Treaty of Fez, 1912).
- [ ] M7 Results of the second crisis: humiliation felt in Germany; British and French drew closer (the 1912 naval arrangement); Italy seized the moment to attack Libya.

How imperialism affected Europe and international tension
- [ ] E1 Imperial rivalry produced crises that raised tension (Fashoda, the Boer War, Morocco) and war scares between Great Powers.
- [ ] E2 Imperial rivalry was also settled by compromise (Heligoland–Zanzibar 1890, Fashoda 1899, the 1904 and 1907 agreements); colonial settlements became the basis of the ententes.
- [ ] E3 It changed the balance of power: Britain moved from isolation into the Entente, and Germany's clumsy colonial diplomacy left it more isolated.
- [ ] E4 It fed nationalism, jingoism and militarism at home: popular press, celebrations such as Mafeking night (1900), demands for navies and armies.
- [ ] E5 Economic effects: raw materials and investment returns, but colonies often cost more than they earned (Germany's colonies took a tiny share of its trade).
- [ ] E6 Humanitarian reaction: the Congo and Boer-camp scandals showed public opinion could expose and restrain abuses.

Assessment (end with this block, headed "Assessment: …")
- [ ] J1 Did colonial rivalry cause the war? Colonial crises were mostly resolved peacefully, and war came from the Balkans and the European alliance system; but Morocco hardened the blocs.
- [ ] J2 Contemporary views (German complaints of encirclement; British and French blame of German pressure; Hobson and Lenin linking capitalism, imperialism and war) vs later historians (imperialism one factor alongside alliances, militarism and nationalism).
- [ ] J3 The alliance system: did it keep the balance of power and deter war, or turn local crises into a general war? Paired points.

Key concepts and terms (keyTerms)
- [ ] T1 New Imperialism, as used for 1880–1914.
- [ ] T2 Balance of power, as it changed from Bismarck's system to two blocs.
- [ ] T3 Nationalism, as a motive for empire and a source of rivalry.
- [ ] T4 Other terms: Triple Entente; Triple Alliance; Franco-Russian Alliance; Entente Cordiale; Anglo-Russian Entente; splendid isolation; encirclement; Scramble for Africa; Berlin Conference; effective occupation; sphere of influence; Social Darwinism; mission civilisatrice; Congo Free State; Open Door; Uitlanders; concentration camps (Boer War sense); Algeciras Conference.

## 3. Traps

Do not use (doubtful or overstated in the current notes):
- Do not use "most trade and investment went to areas outside the empires" as a general rule: it holds for Germany and much of British investment, not for every power. Use the I3 wording.
- Do not say "under Kitchener, burned farms and interned Boer civilians": farm-burning and the camps began under Roberts in 1900 and expanded under Kitchener. Use "from 1900, and especially under Kitchener".
- Do not say "the Berlin Conference recognised the Congo Free State in 1885": the powers recognised Leopold's association during the conference, and the Free State was then proclaimed. Use the S3 and C2 wording.
- Do not say the United States "backed France" at Algeciras: its role was as a participant and mediator. Say Germany was supported only by Austria-Hungary, and Britain and Russia backed France.
- Do not say the Agadir crisis "led directly" to the 1912 naval arrangement as its sole cause; say it drew Britain and France closer and the naval arrangement followed in 1912.
- Do not give Lenin's book as published in 1916; say "written 1916" or give no year.
- Do not give any Congo death toll (for example "10 million"); estimates vary widely.
- Do not give a total for Boer War camp deaths beyond "over 20,000 Boer civilians"; give no figure for black African deaths.
- No quotations from Wilhelm II at Tangier or from Lloyd George's Mansion House speech: describe them without quotation marks.

Confusables:
- Franco-Russian Alliance (1894, a real military alliance) vs Entente Cordiale (1904) and Anglo-Russian Entente (1907) (colonial settlements, not alliances). Students often call the Triple Entente an alliance.
- Triple Entente (Britain, France, Russia) vs Triple Alliance (Germany, Austria-Hungary, Italy). Students swap them.
- The Berlin Conference (1884–85, Africa) vs the Congress of Berlin (1878, the Balkans; in the notes on Bismarck's foreign policy).
- Algeciras (the 1906 conference, first crisis) vs Agadir (the 1911 port, second crisis). Tangier (1905) is where Wilhelm II landed.
- Fashoda (1898, Britain vs France, Sudan) is not a Moroccan crisis and did not involve Germany.
- Lord Kitchener (Omdurman and Fashoda 1898; Boer War commander from late 1900) is the same man in both; Lord Roberts commanded first in the Boer War.
- Cecil Rhodes (Cape politician) vs Leander Starr Jameson (led the raid).
- Leopold II's Congo Free State (personal, to 1908) vs the Belgian Congo (Belgian state colony from 1908).
- E.D. Morel (journalist, campaigner) vs Roger Casement (consul, report author). Casement's later Irish career is outside this topic: one clause at most ("later executed for his part in the 1916 Rising" is true but not needed; leave it out).
- The Sino-Japanese War (1894–95, Japan beat China) vs the Russo-Japanese War (1904–05, Japan beat Russia).
- Delcassé (French foreign minister) vs Paul Cambon (ambassador who signed in London).
- Hobson (British liberal economist, 1902) vs Lenin (Marxist, 1916).

## 4. What exams ask (topics only)

- Why the powers sought colonies (Higher, 100 marks; Ordinary short answers): reward a range of motives (economic, strategic, nationalist, missionary, ideological), each backed by a named example, plus the enabling role of technology and the point that rivalry fed on itself. Top answers weigh motives rather than list them (for example, prestige and strategy mattered more than profit for many states, since colonies often did not pay).
- Causes and effects of the New Imperialism (Higher, recurring): answers need both halves. Effects should be on Europe and international relations, not only on colonised peoples: crises, the realignment of Britain, bloc hardening, jingoism and militarism, and compromise settlements.
- "To what extent" colonial rivalries caused international tension: the marking style rewards a judgement with both sides. Tension: Fashoda, the Boer War, Morocco. Against: most disputes settled peacefully, ententes grew out of colonial deals, and the 1914 crisis came from the Balkans. Morocco is the best case for imperialism hardening the blocs.
- Main tensions in Europe 1871–1914: imperial rivalry and the alliance blocs are two strands among Franco-German hostility, Balkan rivalry and the naval race; examiners credit linking them (for example, Morocco tested the Entente and tied Britain to France).
- The alliance system: how Europe split into two blocs after 1890 (dates and terms of 1894, 1904, 1907), why Britain left isolation, and whether the blocs kept the balance of power or made a general war likely.
- Ordinary Level short paragraphs on the Scramble for Africa: extent by 1914, the Berlin Conference rules, the main colonies of named powers, Fashoda or the Congo. Accurate dates and named examples earn the marks.
- Document questions in this chapter have used sources on colonial policy and German views of colonies: students should be able to place a source on colonies, Fashoda or Morocco in its context.
- Cause and result marks: for each crisis (Fashoda, Boer War, Tangier/Algeciras, Agadir) know one cause, the events in order, the outcome and one effect on alliances.
- Contemporary vs later views: Hobson and Lenin's economic explanation vs historians who stress prestige, strategy and local rivalries.
