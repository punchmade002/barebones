## 1. Must cover (one bullet or block for every item; tick each off)

Background (assumed)
- [ ] A1 (assumed) Radio broadcasting was state-run: 2RN opened in Dublin in 1926 and became Radio Éireann; it was part of the Department of Posts and Telegraphs.
- [ ] A2 (assumed) Before 1961 some viewers (east coast, border counties) received BBC and Ulster Television signals, which showed demand for television.
- [ ] A3 (assumed) Lemass's government (Taoiseach 1959–66) stressed economic modernisation and opening Ireland to outside influences; Lynch succeeded him in November 1966.
- [ ] A4 (assumed) The Catholic Church (Archbishop McQuaid of Dublin) had great influence on morals, and the state censored books and films.

Key concepts (defined as used here; also in keyTerms)
- [ ] K1 Censorship: official restriction of broadcasts or other material (here: Section 31 and ministerial pressure).
- [ ] K2 Liberalisation: relaxation of controls on what could be discussed or done in public life.
- [ ] K3 Secularisation: decline of religion's influence on public life and personal behaviour.
- [ ] K4 Instrument of public policy: Lemass's description of RTÉ's role.
- [ ] K5 Section 31 and the RTÉ Authority (essential terms).

Establishing Telefís Éireann
- [ ] B1 The Broadcasting Authority Act 1960: set up the Authority, appointed by the government, to run radio and the new television service.
- [ ] B2 Funding: licence fees and advertising.
- [ ] B3 Ministerial powers: Section 31, and the Act's duty of impartiality in news and current affairs.
- [ ] B4 Launch on 31 December 1961; de Valera's opening speech (a warning about television's power).
- [ ] B5 Spread across the country and into most homes by the early 1970s.

Programmes
- [ ] P1 Imported programmes (British and American) vs home-produced output (news, sport, current affairs, drama, light entertainment).
- [ ] P2 Gaelic games and sport drew large audiences.
- [ ] P3 The Riordans (1965): rural drama, popular in the countryside.
- [ ] P4 Telefís Scoile (1964): programmes for schools.
- [ ] P5 Irish-language programming: present from the start, small share of airtime.
- [ ] P6 Effect on writers, actors and musicians reaching a national audience.

The Late Late Show and Gay Byrne
- [ ] L1 Start in July 1962 as a live Friday night summer filler; became a fixture.
- [ ] L2 Format and Byrne's style: talk, guests, audience participation, humorous but probing.
- [ ] L3 Topics once taboo: sex, marriage, contraception, unmarried mothers, the church.
- [ ] L4 The "Bishop and the Nightie" (1966): what happened, Bishop Ryan's condemnation, the public split.
- [ ] L5 Significance: television provoking debate on morality and church authority.

Current affairs and clashes with government
- [ ] C1 Seven Days (1966): young producers and reporters investigating social issues.
- [ ] C2 Ministers' resentment, complaints to the Authority, pressure to restrain current affairs.
- [ ] C3 The 1969 moneylending programme: inquiry, row over independence, reorganised current affairs.
- [ ] C4 The Troubles from 1969 as a source of conflict over coverage.
- [ ] C5 The government's financial and legal power over the broadcaster.

Lemass and the debate over RTÉ's role
- [ ] D1 Lemass: television a means of national development and public information; "an instrument of public policy", not a mouthpiece.
- [ ] D2 The opposing view: a broadcaster serves the public only if free to criticise.
- [ ] D3 Documents-based questions on Lemass's attitude to RTÉ (the 2018 style): what he wanted and how he handled the Authority.

Effects on society
- [ ] E1 Rural life: evenings at home, less isolation, same culture as city people (dances and visiting affected).
- [ ] E2 Religion: social issues aired that print censorship had kept out; church concern, but also church use of television (Mass, the Angelus).
- [ ] E3 Politics: leaders speaking directly to voters; image and television appearances in campaigns.
- [ ] E4 Mixed reactions (welcome vs threat to Irish values).

The crisis of 1971–72
- [ ] X1 First Section 31 order, 1 October 1971, Minister Gerry Collins.
- [ ] X2 The Mac Stíofáin interview, 19 November 1972, This Week, Kevin O'Kelly.
- [ ] X3 The sacking of the whole Authority by Collins in late November 1972.
- [ ] X4 Reactions: journalists' protests, Oireachtas debate; O'Kelly's later prosecution.
- [ ] X5 Section 31 widened later in the 1970s and ended in 1994.

Key personality: Gay Byrne (one block, every point)
- [ ] G1 Who he was: born Dublin 1934; worked in Britain in the 1950s, then returned to Irish radio and television.
- [ ] G2 What he did: presented The Late Late Show 1962–1999; radio programme from the early 1970s.
- [ ] G3 Shaped events and was shaped by them: set the agenda on sex, marriage and religion; reflected changes already under way.
- [ ] G4 Participant: he hosted the 1966 episode and handled the controversy.
- [ ] G5 Neutral host style, yet a liberalising effect.
- [ ] G6 Contemporaries: welcomed as modern vs attacked by some clergy and traditionalists.
- [ ] G7 Historians' view; his death in 2019.

Differing views
- [ ] V1 Government/Lemass, RTÉ staff, the church, Irish-language activists, rural viewers, opposition politicians (one point each).
- [ ] V2 Historians: RTÉ both reflected and sped up change (Savage); relations with the state complex (Horgan).

Assessment (paired judgement points, final block)
- [ ] S1 Entertained (popular programmes).
- [ ] S2 Challenged (current affairs, chat show, taboo subjects).
- [ ] S3 Changed (wider horizons, questioning of church and state, habit of debate).
- [ ] S4 Limits (state power, Section 31, Irish language struggled).
- [ ] S5 Contemporary vs later views; other causes of change (education, emigration, EEC entry, the economy).

## 4. What exams ask

- Higher 40-mark question (2018 style) and 100-mark essay: did RTÉ entertain, challenge or change viewers, or what impact television had on Irish society. Marks go to a clear judgement, then paired points on all three verbs, each backed by a named programme or event with a date. Programme names alone, without effect, score weakly.
- Essays often pair RTÉ with another force (Vatican II, the language, education reform): state which mattered more and why; use Byrne, Seven Days and Section 31 as evidence, and note other causes of change.
- DBQs on Lemass's attitude: students must show what he wanted from RTÉ (support for government policy) and what documents say about how he dealt with the Authority; cite document and detail, and note authorship and purpose when judging a source's value.
- Ordinary Level 40-mark paragraphs: launch date, the Late Late Show's start and presenter, its subjects (sex, marriage, contraception, church), the early 1970s household figure phrased as "most households", with each dated fact earning marks.
- Ordinary Level short answers: who Gay Byrne was, what the Late Late Show did, how television changed home life and awareness of the outside world; reading a personal account of first seeing television.
- Section 31 questions: first order (1971), the interview (1972), dismissal of the Authority, and why it showed limits on independence.
- Strong answers separate short-term entertainment from longer-term attitudinal change and acknowledge that television reflected changes already under way.
