# Package: hist-ire6-s4 — Economic and social challenges of the 1970s and 1980s: Lynch, Haughey and FitzGerald

- **loId:** `hist-ire6-s4` · **chapterId:** `hist-ire6`
- **Syllabus:** Later Modern Ireland, Higher Level (I6 P): "economic and social challenges of the 1970s and 1980s". Outline scope: Lynch's first government (1966-73), the Arms Crisis (1970) and his handling of the North; the oil crises (1973, 1979), inflation and unemployment; the 1977 Fianna Fáil manifesto and borrowing; Haughey's 1980 address on living beyond our means, spending and the national debt; the instability of 1981-82 (three elections, GUBU); FitzGerald's coalitions (1981-82, 1982-87), the constitutional crusade, the New Ireland Forum and the Anglo-Irish Agreement; the 1983 and 1986 referendums; unemployment and 1980s emigration; Haughey's 1987 government and the Programme for National Recovery; each leader's strengths and weaknesses. Key personalities: Jack Lynch, Charles Haughey, Garret FitzGerald. Key concepts: balance of payments, liberalisation, pluralism.

## 1. Must cover (one bullet or block for every item; tick each off)

Background (assumed)
- [ ] A1 (assumed) Lynch succeeded Lemass in 1966 and inherited the 1960s boom; Fianna Fáil, Fine Gael and Labour were the three main parties, and coalition (Fine Gael-Labour) was the alternative to Fianna Fáil rule.
- [ ] A2 (assumed) The Troubles began in Northern Ireland in 1968-69; the Republic had to decide how to respond.
- [ ] A3 (assumed) Ireland joined the EEC on 1 January 1973 (detail belongs to the EEC notes).

Key concepts and essential terms (also in keyTerms)
- [ ] K1 Balance of payments: the record of dealings with the rest of the world; a deficit means more flows out than in.
- [ ] K2 Liberalisation: relaxing legal or social controls (contraception, divorce).
- [ ] K3 Pluralism: a society of several groups and beliefs, which the state accommodates rather than favours one.
- [ ] K4 National debt: the total owed by the state, which rose rapidly through borrowing.
- [ ] K5 Arms Crisis, GUBU, New Ireland Forum, Programme for National Recovery, Tallaght Strategy, Eighth Amendment (each defined as used).

Economic shocks of the 1970s
- [ ] E1 The first oil shock (October 1973): Ireland's dependence on imported oil; inflation; end of the post-war boom; effect on exports.
- [ ] E2 The Cosgrave coalition (1973-77): recession, unemployment and a payments deficit.
- [ ] E3 The second oil shock (1979, Iranian Revolution): renewed inflation.
- [ ] E4 Ireland joins the European Monetary System (March 1979) and the link with sterling is broken.
- [ ] E5 Government response: spending and borrowing to protect jobs and services, and the rise in the national debt.

Key personality: Jack Lynch (one block, every point)
- [ ] L1 Who he was: Cork, born 1917, hurler (six All-Ireland medals in a row), barrister, Dáil from 1948.
- [ ] L2 Career: Minister for Education, Industry and Commerce, Finance; Taoiseach from 10 November 1966 as a compromise choice.
- [ ] L3 Terms of office: 1966-73 and 1977-79; won in 1969; lost in 1973; won again in 1977.
- [ ] L4 August 1969: broadcast ("can no longer stand by"), field hospitals, no military intervention.
- [ ] L5 Northern policy: unity by consent, opposition to violence, condemnation of internment (August 1971), response to Bloody Sunday (January 1972); December 1972 amendment to the Offences Against the State Act.
- [ ] L6 How he shaped events / was shaped by them: held Fianna Fáil together through the Arms Crisis; was brought down by the economic position after 1977 and the by-election defeats.
- [ ] L7 Contemporaries: popular, decent, conciliatory but accused of indecision.
- [ ] L8 Historians: skilful party manager and a moderating influence; weaker on economic management; record stronger externally than internally.
- [ ] L9 Strengths and weaknesses stated explicitly.

The Arms Crisis, 1970
- [ ] AC1 Cause: April 1970 information that ministers might be involved in a plan to import arms for Northern nationalists.
- [ ] AC2 The dismissals (6 May 1970): Haughey and Blaney sacked; Boland resigned.
- [ ] AC3 The arms trial: those charged; Blaney's charge dropped; acquittals in October 1970.
- [ ] AC4 Results: a lasting division in Fianna Fáil; Haughey's years outside the cabinet; disputed accounts of who knew what.

The 1977 election and Lynch's second government
- [ ] N1 The manifesto: abolition of domestic rates and car tax; job creation through spending.
- [ ] N2 The result: 84 of 148 seats, then the largest majority in the state's history.
- [ ] N3 The policy and its consequences: tax cuts, spending, borrowing, deficit and debt rising; early growth then crisis by 1979.
- [ ] N4 Lynch's fall: two Cork by-election defeats (November 1979), resignation (December 1979).

Key personality: Charles Haughey (one block, every point)
- [ ] H1 Who he was: born Castlebar 1925, accountant, married a daughter of Seán Lemass, wealthy.
- [ ] H2 Career: ministries before 1970, dismissal in the Arms Crisis, rebuilding in the 1970s, Health and Social Welfare in 1977.
- [ ] H3 Becoming Taoiseach: defeated Colley 44 votes to 38; Taoiseach on 11 December 1979.
- [ ] H4 The address of 9 January 1980: living beyond our means, a call for sacrifice, followed by continued spending and borrowing.
- [ ] H5 Terms of office: 1979-81, March-December 1982, 1987-92.
- [ ] H6 The 1987 government: minority government, Tallaght Strategy, MacSharry's cuts.
- [ ] H7 The Programme for National Recovery (October 1987).
- [ ] H8 Contemporaries: divided between admirers and critics; later tribunals found he received large sums from businessmen.
- [ ] H9 Historians: gifted but flawed; poor record on public finances before 1987; later cuts and the PNR helped prepare recovery.
- [ ] H10 Strengths and weaknesses stated explicitly.

Political instability, 1981-82
- [ ] I1 Three elections (June 1981, February 1982, November 1982) and weak governments.
- [ ] I2 FitzGerald's first coalition (1981-82) and its fall over the January 1982 budget (VAT on children's clothes and shoes).
- [ ] I3 Haughey's minority government of 1982 (Workers' Party, Tony Gregory and others).
- [ ] I4 The 1982 scandals: phone-tapping, leadership challenge, the MacArthur arrest and the Attorney General (August 1982).
- [ ] I5 GUBU: origin of the term and what it came to mean.
- [ ] I6 The fall of the Haughey government (November 1982) and FitzGerald's return.

Key personality: Garret FitzGerald (one block, every point)
- [ ] F1 Who he was: born 1926, son of Desmond FitzGerald, economist and lecturer.
- [ ] F2 Career: Foreign Affairs 1973-77, Fine Gael leader 1977, Taoiseach 1981-82 and 1982-87.
- [ ] F3 The constitutional crusade (1981): aims and its link to Northern unionists.
- [ ] F4 Its limited results: the Eighth Amendment (1983) and the failed divorce referendum (1986).
- [ ] F5 Liberalisation achieved: the 1985 contraception law.
- [ ] F6 Northern initiatives: the New Ireland Forum (1983-84) and the Anglo-Irish Agreement (1985), each in one line.
- [ ] F7 Economic record: deficit, debt, unemployment; Labour leaves government (January 1987).
- [ ] F8 Contemporaries and historians: honest, intellectual, perhaps unworldly; credited with opening Irish politics to new ideas; weakest on the economy.
- [ ] F9 Strengths and weaknesses stated explicitly.

Referendums of 1983 and 1986 (pluralism debate)
- [ ] R1 The Eighth Amendment: date, result, content (see Traps for the wording), pressures behind it.
- [ ] R2 The divorce referendum: date, result.
- [ ] R3 What the results showed about the church's influence and how far pluralism had advanced.

Unemployment, emigration and the public finances in the 1980s
- [ ] U1 Unemployment rising to over 15 per cent in the mid-1980s; the live register.
- [ ] U2 Emigration returns on a large scale; who left and where they went.
- [ ] U3 The debt burden, interest payments and high taxation.
- [ ] U4 The turn after 1987 and the base for the 1990s recovery.

Assessment (paired judgement points, final block)
- [ ] S1 Lynch: success (kept the state out of the Northern conflict, held the party together) vs limit (economic difficulty after 1977).
- [ ] S2 Haughey: success (1987 discipline) vs limit (earlier deficits, scandal).
- [ ] S3 FitzGerald: success (agenda of pluralism and the North) vs limit (referendums, economy).
- [ ] S4 Contemporary view: governments repeatedly turned out.
- [ ] S5 Later view: the 1980s as a lost decade for growth, with disagreement on credit for the recovery.

## 3. Traps

Do not use (wrong or doubtful facts in the current notes):
- Do not use: "broke its link with sterling for the first time since the 1820s", because the dating of the earlier link is doubtful; say only that the link was broken in 1979.
- Do not use: the Eighth Amendment "recognising the equal right to life of the unborn" (the keyTerm and fact wording), because the amendment acknowledged the right to life of the unborn and, with due regard to the equal right to life of the mother, guaranteed to respect and defend it. Say "the right to life of the unborn, with due regard to the equal right to life of the mother".
- Do not use: "both Fianna Fáil and Fine Gael leaders gave way to the pressure" as a flat statement; say instead that the amendment was backed by anti-abortion campaigners and that FitzGerald had reservations about the wording.
- Do not use: "largest majority in the history of the state" without "then"; later elections have produced different majorities.
- Do not use: inflation "about 20 per cent in 1981" as an exact date; say "about 20 per cent around 1981".
- Do not use: any exact tax, deficit, debt-interest or unemployment figure other than those in section 2.

Confusable people, terms and dates
- Lynch (Fianna Fáil, Taoiseach 1966-73 and 1977-79) vs Cosgrave (Fine Gael, 1973-77): the 1973-77 coalition was Cosgrave's, not Lynch's.
- Haughey was dismissed (Arms Crisis, 1970) and later became Taoiseach (1979): do not confuse the two periods; he returned as Taoiseach again in 1982 (March to December) and 1987.
- FitzGerald's two terms (1981-82, 1982-87) vs Haughey's short 1982 government between them.
- Three elections: June 1981, February 1982, November 1982.
- GUBU: the phrase was applied to the events; Conor Cruise O'Brien coined the acronym.
- Eighth Amendment (1983, anti-abortion, passed) vs the 1986 divorce referendum (defeated).
- Tallaght Strategy (Fine Gael's support for cuts, 1987) vs the Programme for National Recovery (agreement with social partners, October 1987).
- Colley (rival, lost 1979 leadership vote) vs Lemass (Haughey's father-in-law).
- The New Ireland Forum (1983-84, a report) vs the Anglo-Irish Agreement (1985, a treaty).

## 4. What exams ask

- Higher 100-mark essay on the challenges of the 1970s and 1980s: how well governments coped. Marks go to a clear judgement and to balanced points on economic shocks (oil, inflation, unemployment, debt), political instability, the North and social issues, with leaders tied to specific actions and dates.
- Higher 100-mark essay on Lynch: how effective was he in internal and external affairs. Credit for separating the two (external: kept out of the Northern conflict; internal: Arms Crisis, 1977 manifesto, borrowing), and for ending on a verdict.
- Higher 100-mark essay on Haughey and/or FitzGerald: contribution of one or both, or comparison of two leaders. Credit for named actions with dates (1980 address, 1987 programme; constitutional crusade, 1985 contraception law, the referendums) and for explicit strengths and weaknesses.
- Ordinary Level 30/40-mark paragraphs on each of the three personalities: career dates, three or four actions, one strength and one weakness. Each correct, dated point earns a mark, so each paragraph should include dates and offices.
- Ordinary Level short answers: who was sacked in the Arms Crisis, what Haughey told the country in 1980, what GUBU stood for, what the 1983 and 1986 referendums decided.
- Cause and result: why the 1977 manifesto led to borrowing and a crisis; why Lynch resigned; why three elections in eighteen months; why the divorce referendum failed.
- Contemporary vs later views: strongest answers pair what people thought at the time with what historians say now, and keep the economic record apart from the political record.
