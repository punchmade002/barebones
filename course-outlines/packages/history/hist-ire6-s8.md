## 1. Must cover (one bullet or block for every item; tick each off)

Background (assumed)
- [ ] A1 (assumed) Ireland in 1949 was a conservative, Catholic, mainly rural society; the Church shaped law and attitudes on sex, marriage and the family (the 1937 Constitution gave the family and the home a central place for women).
- [ ] A2 (assumed) The stagnant economy of the 1950s drove mass emigration; the turn to expansion from 1958 and EEC entry in 1973 changed the economy (detail belongs to neighbouring subsections).
- [ ] A3 (assumed) Contraception: the 1935 law banned its sale and import; the Church opposed artificial contraception (Paul VI's encyclical of 1968 reaffirmed this).

Key concepts (each defined as used in this topic; also in keyTerms)
- [ ] K1 Discrimination: unequal treatment on grounds such as sex or marital status.
- [ ] K2 Liberalisation: relaxing legal or social restrictions (contraception, censorship, homosexuality).
- [ ] K3 Equality of opportunity: the same access to education, jobs and promotion regardless of sex.
- [ ] K4 Other essential terms for keyTerms: marriage bar, net emigration, Commission on the Status of Women, Irish Women's Liberation Movement, contraceptive train, McGee v Attorney General, Employment Equality Agency.

Demographic change
- [ ] D1 Population fell to 1961 (census figures for 1951 and 1961); the cause was emigration.
- [ ] D2 Population growth from the 1960s: 1966, 1971, 1981, 1986 censuses; a young population and high birth rate; peak births about 1980.
- [ ] D3 Slowdown after 1986 and the small fall recorded in 1991.
- [ ] D4 Emigration in the 1950s: scale, destination (Britain), young workers.
- [ ] D5 Emigration fell in the 1960s with economic expansion.
- [ ] D6 The 1970s reversal: net inward migration, returning emigrants, Britain's recession, EEC entry.
- [ ] D7 The return of emigration in the 1980s: scale, unemployment, the educated emigrant, destinations.
- [ ] D8 Consequences of emigration: family life, rural depopulation, politics.
- [ ] D9 Urbanisation: urban population passes half in the early 1970s; the growth of Dublin and its suburbs; rural decline.
- [ ] D10 Marriage and birth rates: earlier and more marriage in the 1960s–70s than in the 1950s; falling fertility afterwards; links to contraception, education and women's work.

Women's status: employment and the Commission
- [ ] W1 The marriage bar: what it was; removal for primary teachers (1958) and the civil service (1973); other employers' bars; unequal pay.
- [ ] W2 The Commission on the Status of Women: set up 1970 (Lynch), chaired by Thekla Beere; interim report 1971 (equal pay); final report December 1972 with 49 recommendations; its influence.
- [ ] W3 Women's participation in the workforce, especially married women.

The women's movement and contraception
- [ ] M1 The Irish Women's Liberation Movement (1970): founders, manifesto Chains or Change (1971), demands.
- [ ] M2 The contraceptive train, 22 May 1971: what happened and why it mattered.
- [ ] M3 Later groups and services: Women's Aid (1974), the Well Woman Centre (1977).
- [ ] M4 McGee v Attorney General (December 1973): Mary McGee, the case, the 4-1 Supreme Court decision, the right to marital privacy.
- [ ] M5 The 1974 bill and Cosgrave's vote against his own government's bill.
- [ ] M6 The Health (Family Planning) Act 1979: Haughey, the "Irish solution" description, prescription for bona fide family planning, in force 1980.
- [ ] M7 The 1985 amendment (sale to over-18s without prescription); the Church's opposition set against how married Catholics behaved.

Equality legislation
- [ ] E1 The Anti-Discrimination (Pay) Act 1974: equal pay for the same or similar work, from the end of 1975, driven by EEC membership.
- [ ] E2 The Employment Equality Act 1977: sex and marital status in recruitment, training, promotion.
- [ ] E3 The Employment Equality Agency: its role and limits.
- [ ] E4 Other measures: Juries Act 1976, Family Home Protection Act 1976, Maternity Protection of Employees Act 1981; social welfare equality in the 1980s under EEC pressure.

Women in politics
- [ ] P1 Few women in the Dáil: 1977 and 1987 figures.
- [ ] P2 Eileen Desmond (cabinet 1981), Gemma Hussey (Education 1982–87), Nuala Fennell (Minister of State for women's affairs from 1982).

Key personality: Sylvia Meehan (one block, every point)
- [ ] S1 Who she was: Dublin-born 1929, teacher, vice-president of the Association of Secondary Teachers, Ireland; chair of the ICTU women's committee.
- [ ] S2 What she did: first chairperson of the Employment Equality Agency, 1977–1992.
- [ ] S3 How she shaped events: turning the equality laws into working practice (research, investigations, advice); the agency's limits.
- [ ] S4 Participant: she led the agency as the laws were implemented.
- [ ] S5 Contemporaries: effective and determined advocate; resisted by some employers and politicians.
- [ ] S6 Historians: a key figure in making the 1970s laws work, with the proviso that the agency's powers were limited.

Key personality: Mary Robinson to 1989 (one block, every point)
- [ ] R1 Who she was: Ballina 1944, Trinity, barrister 1967, Harvard, Reid Professor of Law at 25.
- [ ] R2 Senator for the University of Dublin 1969–1989, independent.
- [ ] R3 Private member's bills on contraception (early 1970s), refused debate; later efforts on homosexuality law.
- [ ] R4 Jury service for women (Juries Act 1976).
- [ ] R5 Landmark cases as a barrister: the 1980 married couple's tax case, David Norris's challenge, and the Airey case on legal aid.
- [ ] R6 Political path: Labour 1976, left 1985 over the Anglo-Irish Agreement; Dublin city councillor 1979–83.
- [ ] R7 Contemporaries: liberal reformer, able lawyer, attacked by conservatives and the Church, respected for persistence.
- [ ] R8 Historians: she built a legal and political basis for reform that prepared for the 1990s and her presidency (1990, outside this period).

Assessment (paired judgement points, final block)
- [ ] X1 Achievements: marriage bar ended, equal pay, employment equality, contraception available, new organisations and visibility.
- [ ] X2 Limits: few women in politics and senior jobs, pay gaps, divorce and abortion still prohibited, Church influence.
- [ ] X3 Contemporary views: conservatives feared for the family; reformers saw reform as too slow.
- [ ] X4 Later view: the start of liberalisation driven by economic change, EEC law and campaigning; uneven; Ireland more conservative than much of Europe.

## 3. Traps

- Do not use: "the population in 1986 was the highest since the 1860s" (the current notes say this), because 1986 was the highest since about the 1880s; say instead that it was the highest for about a century.
- Do not use: "growth faster than at any time since the Famine", because it is unverified.
- Do not use: "net emigration of about 100,000 in the 1960s", because the decade figure is uncertain; use the 1961–66 figure of about 80,000 only.
- Do not use: "equal pay for the public service was introduced in 1973", because equal pay was phased in over the early 1970s and took effect in law from the end of 1975; 1973 is the date of the civil service marriage bar's removal.
- Do not use: Sylvia Meehan's death date (2018 in the current notes), because it is unchecked.
- Do not use: that The Late Late Show covered the contraceptive train, because it is unverified.
- Do not say the Employment Equality Agency itself decided individual complaints; it promoted equality, investigated, researched and advised.
- Confusable: Eileen Desmond (Labour, Minister for Health and Social Welfare 1981) and Barry Desmond (Labour, later Minister for Health); Mary Robinson the senator and lawyer here, and Mary Robinson the President (1990, outside this period).
- McGee was about importing contraceptives for a married woman, not legalising sale; sale stayed restricted until 1979 and later.
- Contraception was legalised in stages (1973 ruling, 1979 Act, 1985 amendment); do not say one event legalised it.

## 4. What exams ask

- The status or role of women, 1949–89 (long questions, a recurring topic): a good answer gives the legal changes in order (marriage bar, 1974 and 1977 Acts, contraception), the pressures behind them (campaigners, the Commission, EEC membership) and a judgement that change was real but uneven.
- Meehan and Robinson, one or both: short parts reward who she was, a dated career fact, and one named achievement (Meehan: first chair of the Employment Equality Agency 1977 to 1992; Robinson: senator 1969 to 1989, bills, cases). Long parts want the contrast: Meehan worked through enforcement, Robinson through law and politics.
- Short-answer parts: the marriage bar (what it was, when removed), what the Commission recommended, what the 1974 Act required, what the Employment Equality Agency did. Give the act's name and year plus one concrete effect.
- Contraception: the sequence McGee (1973), failed bill (1974), Health (Family Planning) Act (1979) with Haughey's phrase, and the Church's stance. Marks go to the order and the reasons for slow change.
- Social and economic change, 1949–89 (broad): use population and emigration as evidence, with the 1950s fall, the 1960s to 1970s growth and return, and the 1980s emigration return. Name the cause each time (economy, EEC, recession).
- Assess-style statements ("change was uneven and incomplete"): pair achievements with limits, and add the different views of contemporaries (conservatives, reformers) and later historians.
- Typical weak points: listing acts with no dates; saying equal pay happened in 1973; treating the Church as the only obstacle; confusing the contraceptive train (a protest) with McGee (a court case).
