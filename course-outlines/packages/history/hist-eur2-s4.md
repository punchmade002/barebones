## 1. Must cover (one bullet or block for every item; tick each off)

Background (assumed)
- [ ] X1 (assumed) The Eastern Question: the decline of Ottoman power in the Balkans left a vacuum that Austria-Hungary and Russia both wanted to fill. One bullet.
- [ ] X2 (assumed) Austria-Hungary as a multinational empire (Germans, Hungarians, Czechs, Poles, South Slavs and others), so any nationalist movement threatened its survival. One bullet.
- [ ] X3 (assumed) Pan-Slavism: the idea that Russia, the largest Slav and Orthodox power, should protect and lead the Slavs of the Balkans. One clause.
- [ ] X4 (assumed) The two blocs by 1907: the Triple Alliance (Germany, Austria-Hungary, Italy) and the Triple Entente (Britain, France, Russia). One clause; the detail is in the notes on European diplomacy.
- [ ] X5 (assumed) Russia weakened by defeat by Japan (1904–05) and the 1905 revolution. One clause; the detail is in the notes on Tsarist Russia.
- [ ] X6 (assumed) Marxism in outline: class struggle, the belief that workers of all countries share interests, and the aim of replacing capitalism with socialism; the SPD as Germany's Marxist party. One bullet.

Serbia as a fulcrum of Great Power rivalry
- [ ] S1 The Congress of Berlin (1878): Serbia's full independence recognised and its territory enlarged; Bosnia-Herzegovina occupied and administered by Austria-Hungary under nominal Ottoman rule.
- [ ] S2 Serbia a kingdom from 1882 under the Obrenović dynasty; a client of Austria-Hungary, whose market took its exports of pigs and livestock.
- [ ] S3 South Slav nationalism: the aim of uniting Serbs, Croats, Slovenes and the Slavs of Bosnia; why it threatened Austria-Hungary.
- [ ] S4 The 1903 coup: officers murdered King Alexander and Queen Draga; Peter I Karadjordjević king; Apis among the conspirators; Serbia turned from Vienna to Russia (and France).
- [ ] S5 Russia's interest: protector of the Balkan Slavs; aim to stop Austrian domination of the Balkans; interest in the Straits.
- [ ] S6 The Pig War (from 1906): Austria-Hungary closed its border to Serbian livestock; its reasons; Serbia found other markets and Austrian influence fell.
- [ ] S7 Conrad von Hötzendorf, Austrian Chief of the General Staff, repeatedly urging preventive war on Serbia.
- [ ] S8 Why Serbia was a "fulcrum": a small state whose quarrel with Austria-Hungary drew in Russia, and through the alliances Germany and France.

The Bosnian annexation crisis, 1908–09
- [ ] B1 Trigger: the Young Turk revolution (July 1908).
- [ ] B2 The Buchlau bargain (September 1908): Aehrenthal and Izvolsky; annexation for Austria in return for support on opening the Straits to Russian warships.
- [ ] B3 The annexation announced (6 October 1908) before Russia had won anything on the Straits; Russia felt cheated.
- [ ] B4 Serbian reaction: outrage (Bosnia's large Serb population), demands for compensation, the Narodna Odbrana founded.
- [ ] B5 The German note (March 1909): Russia told to accept; Russia, still weak, gave way; Serbia also had to accept the annexation.
- [ ] B6 Results: Russia and Serbia humiliated and determined not to back down again; Russian rearmament; Austria-Hungary and Germany drew closer; Germany's backing of Austria in the Balkans set a precedent.

The Balkan Wars, 1912–13
- [ ] W1 The Balkan League (1912) of Serbia, Bulgaria, Greece and Montenegro, with Russian encouragement; Italy's war for Libya (1911–12) had exposed Ottoman weakness.
- [ ] W2 The First Balkan War (October 1912 – May 1913): the Ottomans lost most of their European lands.
- [ ] W3 The London Conference of ambassadors (1912–13), under Grey: an independent Albania, backed by Austria-Hungary and Italy, denied Serbia an Adriatic port.
- [ ] W4 The Second Balkan War (June–August 1913): Bulgaria attacked Serbia and Greece, was beaten, and Romania and the Ottomans joined against it; the Treaty of Bucharest (August 1913).
- [ ] W5 Serbia's gains: it almost doubled in size over the two wars (Kosovo and much of Macedonia).
- [ ] W6 Austria's ultimatum to Serbia (October 1913) to withdraw its troops from Albania; Serbia complied.
- [ ] W7 Significance: conference diplomacy contained the wars, but Austria-Hungary concluded that next time it must fight; Russia's prestige depended on Serbia.

The Black Hand and Sarajevo
- [ ] H1 The Black Hand (Unification or Death), founded May 1911 by Serbian officers; Apis (Dimitrijević), head of military intelligence; aim of a Greater Serbia.
- [ ] H2 Young Bosnia: young South Slav nationalists in Bosnia, armed and trained with Black Hand help.
- [ ] H3 Franz Ferdinand's visit to Sarajevo (28 June 1914) for army manoeuvres; Vidovdan and Kosovo 1389.
- [ ] H4 The assassination: Čabrinović's failed bomb; the wrong turn; Princip shot Franz Ferdinand and his wife Sophie.
- [ ] H5 Princip: 19-year-old Bosnian Serb; too young for the death penalty; died in prison of tuberculosis (1918).
- [ ] H6 How far the Serbian government (Pašić) knew of the plot is disputed; the weapons came from Serbia through the Black Hand.
- [ ] H7 Austria-Hungary decided to use the murder to crush Serbia, but first sought Germany's backing.

The July crisis, 1914
- [ ] J1 The "blank cheque" (5–6 July): Wilhelm II and Bethmann Hollweg promised unconditional support; hope of a short, localised war.
- [ ] J2 Poincaré and Viviani in St Petersburg (20–23 July): French support for Russia reaffirmed.
- [ ] J3 The ultimatum (23 July): ten demands, 48 hours; suppression of anti-Austrian propaganda and organisations; Austrian officials to take part in the inquiry inside Serbia; meant to be refused.
- [ ] J4 Serbia's reply (25 July): accepted most demands but refused Austrian officials a part in the judicial inquiry; Austria-Hungary broke off relations that day.
- [ ] J5 Grey's proposal of a conference (26 July) declined by Germany; the Tsar–Kaiser telegrams; failure of mediation.
- [ ] J6 Austria-Hungary declared war on Serbia (28 July); Belgrade shelled.
- [ ] J7 Mobilisations: Russia partial (29 July) then general (30 July); German demand to stop; general mobilisation in Germany and France (1 August); German declaration of war on Russia (1 August).
- [ ] J8 The Schlieffen Plan: why mobilisation meant war in the west; defeat France first through Belgium, then turn on Russia; Schlieffen and Moltke.
- [ ] J9 Germany declared war on France (3 August); ultimatum to Belgium (2 August) rejected; invasion of Belgium (4 August).
- [ ] J10 Britain's entry (4 August): the 1839 Treaty of London guarantee of Belgian neutrality, the balance of power and the Entente with France; Bethmann Hollweg's "scrap of paper".
- [ ] J11 Italy declared neutrality (August 1914): the Triple Alliance was defensive and Austria had not consulted it.
- [ ] J12 How a Balkan quarrel became a European and, through the empires, a world war.

The Second International
- [ ] I1 What it was: a federation of socialist and labour parties, founded in Paris in 1889; the International Socialist Bureau in Brussels from 1900; belief in workers' solidarity across borders.
- [ ] I2 Its parties: the German SPD the largest and most respected (110 seats in 1912, the biggest party in the Reichstag); French socialists (Jaurès); British Labour; Russian and Polish Marxists (Lenin, Luxemburg).
- [ ] I3 Its divisions: reform (revisionism) vs revolution; how to stop a war (Jaurès and a general strike vs SPD caution).
- [ ] I4 The Stuttgart resolution (1907) and the Luxemburg–Lenin–Martov amendment.
- [ ] I5 The Basel manifesto (November 1912), during the First Balkan War.
- [ ] I6 The last efforts: the International Socialist Bureau met in Brussels at the end of July 1914; Jaurès assassinated (31 July 1914).
- [ ] I7 The collapse: SPD Reichstag deputies voted for war credits (4 August 1914); the French union sacrée and socialist ministers; British Labour largely backed the war; Liebknecht's lone vote against (December 1914).
- [ ] I8 Why it collapsed: nationalism stronger than class loyalty; fear of repression and of Tsarist Russia (in Germany) or of German invasion (in France); workers' own patriotism.
- [ ] I9 Results: the split into pro-war socialists, pacifists and a revolutionary minority; Zimmerwald (1915) and Kienthal (1916); the Third International (Comintern, 1919).

Key personality: Rosa Luxemburg (one block)
- [ ] L1 Origins: born 1871, Zamość, Russian Poland, Jewish family; a childhood hip problem left a limp; studied in Zurich (doctorate).
- [ ] L2 Polish socialism: co-founded the Social Democracy of the Kingdom of Poland (1893, later the SDKPiL); opposed Polish nationalism in favour of international class struggle.
- [ ] L3 Germany: moved there in 1898, citizenship by marriage, joined the SPD on its left.
- [ ] L4 Reform or Revolution (1899) against Bernstein's revisionism.
- [ ] L5 The 1905 revolution: went to Warsaw, arrested (1906); The Mass Strike (1906) argued for the mass strike as a revolutionary weapon.
- [ ] L6 SPD party school teacher (1907–14); The Accumulation of Capital (1913), an economic theory of imperialism.
- [ ] L7 Anti-war campaigning: speeches urging workers not to fight; sentenced in 1914, imprisoned from 1915; work in the Second International (the Stuttgart amendment).
- [ ] L8 4 August 1914: horror at the SPD's vote; founded with Liebknecht and others the International group (1915), which became the Spartacus group (1916).
- [ ] L9 Prison: the Junius pamphlet (written 1915, published 1916); in prison or "protective custody" for most of the war; freed in November 1918.
- [ ] L10 Views on the Russian Revolution: welcomed it, but criticised Bolshevik dictatorship and the dissolution of the Constituent Assembly; "freedom for the one who thinks differently".
- [ ] L11 The German revolution: Die Rote Fahne; co-founded the KPD (30 December 1918 – 1 January 1919); urged taking part in the National Assembly elections and thought a rising premature, but backed the January 1919 rising once it began.
- [ ] L12 Her murder (15 January 1919) with Liebknecht by Freikorps officers; body in the Landwehr Canal.
- [ ] L13 Participant (Stuttgart, 1905, Spartacists, KPD) and witness (the SPD's 1914 vote, the Russian Revolution).
- [ ] L14 Contemporary views: "Red Rosa" to enemies, a dangerous agitator to the SPD leaders and the right; to supporters a principled, courageous internationalist; Lenin's mixed praise in one clause.
- [ ] L15 Historians' views: major Marxist thinker; critic of both militarism and dictatorship; her democratic socialism admired; debate over whether her ideas were practical; later martyr of the German left.

## 3. Traps

Do not use (wrong or doubtful in the current notes):
- Do not use "the Treaty of Bucharest gave Serbia much of Macedonia, including Kosovo", because Kosovo is not part of Macedonia and Serbia took it in the First Balkan War; Bucharest confirmed its share of Macedonia. Use the W5 wording ("almost doubled over the two wars").
- Do not use "Serbia doubled in size by the Treaty of Bucharest", because the gains came from both wars; say "almost doubled over the two wars".
- Do not use "Luxemburg was jailed in 1914 for urging soldiers to disobey orders", because she was sentenced in February 1914 and began her sentence in February 1915. Use the L7 wording.
- Do not use "the Pig War punished Serbia's trade links with France", because the reasons were the Serbian–Bulgarian customs agreement and Serbia buying French rather than Austrian guns. Do not name the countries that took Serbian exports (the current notes list Germany, Italy and France); just say "other markets". Do not give an end date for the Pig War (sources differ between 1908, 1909 and 1911).
- Do not use "Serbia mobilised" in 1908–09 as a fact; say Serbia protested, prepared for war and demanded compensation.
- Do not use "Austria broke off relations and declared war on Serbia on 28 July" as one event, because relations were broken on 25 July and war declared on 28 July.
- Do not use "Luxemburg opposed the Spartacist rising" without qualification, because she thought it premature but backed it in Die Rote Fahne once it began. Use the L11 wording.
- Do not use "the Spartacus League (1916)" as the formal name for 1916, because it was the Spartacus group until November 1918; "Spartacists" is safe for both.
- Do not use "Britain, bound by the Treaty of London", because historians dispute whether the guarantee obliged Britain to fight; say Britain was a guarantor and used the invasion of Belgium as its reason, with the balance of power and the Entente with France.
- Do not use "the split led directly to the communist parties", because "led directly" overstates; say it helped produce them and the Comintern (1919).
- Do not quote the Basel manifesto, the Stuttgart resolution, the ultimatum or Luxemburg's writings other than the L10 sentence. Do not use "Since 4 August German Social Democracy is a stinking corpse" or any other attributed line that is not in section 2.
- Do not give casualty figures, vote shares or membership totals for the International or the SPD beyond "110 seats in 1912".
- Do not give the date of the 1903 coup as a day: sources give different dates because Serbia used the old-style calendar. Say June 1903.

## 4. What exams ask (topics only)

- Causes of the First World War (Higher, 100 marks, recurring): examiners reward a judgement that there was no single cause, with long-term causes (alliances, militarism and war plans, naval and imperial rivalry, nationalism) linked to the Balkan trigger and the July crisis in date order. Top answers show how each link led to the next (blank cheque, ultimatum, Russian mobilisation, Schlieffen Plan, Belgium, British entry), not a list.
- International tensions as a cause of war (Higher): the Austro-Russian rivalry in the Balkans is one strand among Franco-German hostility, the blocs and the naval race. Know the Bosnian crisis and the Balkan Wars well enough to show how each made the next crisis more dangerous (Russia and Serbia vowing not to back down; Austria deciding it must fight).
- "My study of World War I" style answers (Higher) open with how a Balkan quarrel became a general war: a tight paragraph from Sarajevo to 4 August with correct dates earns the marks.
- Responsibility: contemporary German vs Entente views, Fischer vs Clark; examiners credit paired points and a reasoned conclusion on how much blame Germany and Austria-Hungary bear.
- Serbia (Ordinary, 30-mark paragraph): independence and kingdom (1878, 1882), the South Slav aim and why it threatened Austria-Hungary, the 1903 coup and turn to Russia, the Pig War, Bosnia, the Balkan Wars, the Black Hand and Sarajevo. Accurate dates and the cause-and-result link to 1914 earn the marks.
- The Second International (Ordinary, 30-mark paragraph, very frequent): founding (Paris 1889, Brussels bureau), the SPD's strength, Stuttgart 1907 and Basel 1912, Jaurès's murder, the war-credits vote of 4 August 1914 and why it collapsed. Explaining why nationalism beat internationalism scores higher than describing congresses.
- Rosa Luxemburg (Ordinary 30/40 marks, frequent; Higher as one of three personalities): a career in order with dates (birth, Poland, Germany 1898, Reform or Revolution, 1905, The Accumulation of Capital, anti-war sentence, Spartacists, Junius pamphlet, prison, KPD, murder). Marks go to her significance too: consistent opposition to war, criticism of the Bolsheviks' methods, and why her death mattered.
- Higher personality comparisons (Luxemburg with Leo XIII and Wilson, or with Curie and women in the workforce): judge her impact on European affairs; achievements (ideas, opposition to war) vs limits (small following, failed rising, short career).
- Cause and result marks: for the Bosnian crisis and the Balkan Wars know one cause, the events in order, the outcome and the effect on later crises.
