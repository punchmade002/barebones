## 1. Must cover (one bullet or block for every item; tick each off)

Background (assumed)
- [ ] A1 (assumed) The Kaiser's powers under the Reich constitution: he appointed and dismissed the Chancellor, commanded the armed forces and directed foreign policy; but the Reichstag voted the budget, so every Navy Law and naval tax needed a Reichstag majority.
- [ ] A2 (assumed) Bismarck's cautious foreign policy (Germany a "satiated" power, France isolated, no colonies of note before 1884) and his dismissal in 1890: one or two lines only, as the contrast for Weltpolitik.
- [ ] A3 (assumed) Britain's naval supremacy: an island empire dependent on sea trade and food imports; the two-power standard; "splendid isolation" from alliances until 1902.
- [ ] A4 (assumed) The two blocs: the Triple Alliance (Germany, Austria-Hungary, Italy) and the Franco-Russian Alliance (1894). One line; detail belongs elsewhere.
- [ ] A5 (assumed) Naval terms: battleship (capital ship), cruiser; a battle fleet (to fight a decisive battle in home waters) vs a cruiser fleet (to protect trade and colonies overseas).

Key concepts (defined as used in this topic; also in keyTerms)
- [ ] K1 Balance of power: no single state or bloc strong enough to dominate; Britain traditionally opposed any power dominating the Continent and treated naval supremacy as its own security; the German fleet threatened that, and pushed Britain towards France and Russia.
- [ ] K2 Nationalism: in Germany, pride in the new Reich and the belief that a great nation needed colonies, world influence and a fleet; mass nationalist pressure groups; in Britain, nationalist and imperial fear of the German fleet.
- [ ] K3 Weltpolitik ("world policy"): the attempt from about 1897 to make Germany a world power through colonies, trade, influence and a battle fleet, as opposed to Bismarck's Europe-centred policy (Realpolitik / continental policy).
- [ ] K4 Personal rule (persönliches Regiment): Wilhelm's attempt to govern himself rather than through a strong Chancellor; how far he achieved it is debated.
- [ ] K5 Encirclement (Einkreisung): the German belief that Britain, France and Russia were hemming Germany in; critics say German policy itself caused it.
- [ ] K6 Sammlungspolitik ("politics of rallying together") and social imperialism: using an assertive world and naval policy to unite industrialists, landowners and the middle classes behind the monarchy and against the SPD.

Key personality: Wilhelm II
- [ ] W1 Birth, family and disability: grandson of Queen Victoria; a withered left arm from a difficult birth.
- [ ] W2 Accession in 1888, the year of the three emperors, aged 29.
- [ ] W3 Clash with Bismarck and Bismarck's resignation (March 1890); Wilhelm's wish to rule personally (K4).
- [ ] W4 The "new course" (Caprivi, 1890–94): the Reinsurance Treaty not renewed; the Heligoland–Zanzibar Treaty with Britain; the Anti-Socialist Law allowed to lapse and workers' protection measures; lower-tariff trade treaties; then Wilhelm turned against the SPD and Caprivi fell in 1894.
- [ ] W5 His Chancellors: Caprivi, Hohenlohe, Bülow, Bethmann Hollweg (dates in section 2).
- [ ] W6 Character: energetic, intelligent, impulsive, theatrical, militaristic, easily influenced; a love–hate relationship with Britain and his British relations; his passion for the navy (honorary British admiral from 1889).
- [ ] W7 Indiscretions that damaged Germany's image: the Kruger telegram (cross-refer to WP6), the "Hun speech" (1900), the Daily Telegraph affair (1908) and its domestic consequences.
- [ ] W8 Personal diplomacy: his correspondence with Nicholas II ("Willy–Nicky") and the Treaty of Björkö (1905), which their governments did not put into effect.
- [ ] W9 The "war council" of 8 December 1912 and the debate over its meaning.
- [ ] W10 1914 and after, briefly: the "blank cheque" (cross-refer to the notes on the outbreak of war); a declining role in wartime as the generals took over; abdication and exile (November 1918); death at Doorn (1941).
- [ ] W11 Participant or witness, and shaped events vs shaped by them: he chose Tirpitz and Bülow and backed the fleet throughout, but worked within pressures from nationalist groups, industry and the military.
- [ ] W12 How contemporaries saw him: admired by nationalists and navalists as the symbol of Germany's rise; seen abroad as erratic and a threat; criticised at home after 1908.
- [ ] W13 How historians judge him: Röhl (real personal rule, heavy responsibility) vs Clark (inconsistent, a weaker hand than he seemed, responsibility shared); Fischer's school places German leadership at the centre of 1914.

Weltpolitik
- [ ] WP1 Its start and meaning (1896–97); Wilhelm's "Our future lies on the water" (1898).
- [ ] WP2 Economic causes: Germany's rapid industrial growth, the need for markets, raw materials and secure sea routes; a growing merchant fleet.
- [ ] WP3 Domestic causes: Sammlungspolitik and social imperialism (K6); pressure groups: the Colonial Society, the Pan-German League, the Navy League.
- [ ] WP4 Nationalism and prestige: Germany "late" to empire, resentment at Britain's and France's empires, Social Darwinist ideas of rival nations (K2).
- [ ] WP5 Bülow: Foreign Secretary 1897, Chancellor 1900–09; "a place in the sun" (December 1897).
- [ ] WP6 The Kruger telegram (3 January 1896) and British reaction.
- [ ] WP7 China: the seizure of Kiaochow (1897) and the lease (1898); the Boxer rising and the German-led expedition (1900).
- [ ] WP8 Pacific gains: the Caroline, Mariana and Palau islands (1899) and the partition of Samoa (1899).
- [ ] WP9 The Ottoman Empire: Wilhelm's 1898 tour; the Baghdad Railway concession (1899, 1903); British and Russian suspicion.
- [ ] WP10 The failed Anglo-German alliance talks (1898–1901) and Britain's turn to Japan (1902) and France (1904).
- [ ] WP11 The Moroccan crises (1905–06, 1911) as Weltpolitik in action: one line each with the result for Germany (humiliation, stronger Entente); detail belongs to the notes on colonial rivalries.
- [ ] WP12 Weltpolitik and the balance of power: Germany's gains were small, but it alarmed every other power, pushed Britain into the Entente and left Germany relying on Austria-Hungary.

Naval policy case study: background and causes
- [ ] N1 The German navy before 1897: small, a coastal defence force; Germany's strength was its army.
- [ ] N2 Wilhelm's personal enthusiasm for a navy to rival Britain's (W6).
- [ ] N3 Mahan's The Influence of Sea Power upon History (1890) and its influence on Wilhelm and German navalists.
- [ ] N4 Tirpitz: appointed State Secretary of the Imperial Navy Office (1897); his plan for a battle fleet concentrated in the North Sea, aimed in practice at Britain, rather than a cruiser fleet.
- [ ] N5 The risk theory (Risikogedanke); the "danger zone" while the fleet was being built; the aim of a fleet about two-thirds the size of Britain's in home waters; the hope of an alliance or colonial concessions from Britain.
- [ ] N6 Domestic aims: the fleet as a national project to rally the middle classes and isolate the SPD; Navy Laws fixing building for years ahead so the Reichstag could not easily cut it.
- [ ] N7 Propaganda: the Navy Office's press and information campaign; "fleet professors" (academics who lectured and wrote for the fleet); the Navy League (1898), backed by industry, a mass organisation.
- [ ] N8 Industrial interests: Krupp (armour plate, guns), shipyards such as Blohm & Voss; heavy industry gained orders.

Naval policy case study: the events in order
- [ ] N9 The First Navy Law (1898).
- [ ] N10 The Second Navy Law (1900): doubled the battle fleet; passed amid German anger at Britain during the Boer War (the seizure of German mail steamers).
- [ ] N11 British perception and first response: the Admiralty's growing focus on Germany; Fisher as First Sea Lord (1904): scrapping obsolete ships, bringing ships home from distant stations, concentrating against Germany; invasion-scare literature.
- [ ] N12 HMS Dreadnought (1906): all-big-gun, turbine-driven; it made older battleships obsolete, cutting Britain's existing lead but also giving Germany a chance to compete on equal terms.
- [ ] N13 German response: its own dreadnoughts (Nassau class); the supplementary laws (Novellen) of 1906 and 1908 (four capital ships a year from 1908); the widening of the Kiel Canal for dreadnoughts.
- [ ] N14 The 1908–09 British naval scare and "We want eight and we won't wait" (1909); Britain lays down eight dreadnoughts in the 1909–10 programme; Britain decides to out-build Germany at any cost.
- [ ] N15 Failed naval talks (1906–11): Germany wanted a British promise of neutrality before limiting building, Britain wanted limits first; Bethmann Hollweg's attempt at an agreement from 1909.
- [ ] N16 The Haldane mission (February 1912): aims, why it failed; the simultaneous announcement of a new Navy Law.
- [ ] N17 The 1912 supplementary law; Churchill (First Lord from 1911) and the "naval holiday" proposal; British ships withdrawn from the Mediterranean and the Anglo-French naval arrangements (1912).
- [ ] N18 The end of the race: from 1912–13 Germany shifted money to the army; Britain kept a clear lead; a limited Anglo-German détente in 1913–14 (Baghdad Railway, Portuguese colonies) that did not remove mistrust.

Naval policy case study: outcomes
- [ ] N19 Cost and domestic politics: rising naval spending, debt and taxes; the Reich finance crisis of 1908–09; Bülow's fall (1909); SPD and left-liberal opposition to the cost; the navy drew money from the army.
- [ ] N20 Effect on Anglo-German relations: turned a possible partner into an opponent; Britain's agreements with Japan, France and Russia and its naval redeployment (with the debate over how far the navy, rather than colonial issues, caused them).
- [ ] N21 Effect on the outbreak of war: the fleet did not deter Britain; it helped make Britain see Germany as the main threat and bound Britain more closely to France, so Britain entered the war in August 1914 (the immediate trigger, Belgium, belongs to the notes on the outbreak of war).
- [ ] N22 The fleet in the war, briefly: kept mostly in harbour; Jutland (1916) indecisive; the British blockade never broken; the sailors' mutinies of 1918 helped start the German revolution; the fleet scuttled at Scapa Flow (1919). Detail belongs to the notes on the conduct of the war and on the German revolution.

Naval policy case study: significance and differing views
- [ ] N23 Contemporary views: British press, Admiralty and public alarm (invasion scares); German nationalists' pride and the "encirclement" view; SPD and left-liberal criticism of cost.
- [ ] N24 Historians: Kehr and Berghahn (domestic motives, "primacy of domestic politics"); Fischer (deliberate drive for world power); Kennedy (the naval race central to Anglo-German antagonism); Ferguson and others (Britain had won the race by about 1912, so it was not a direct cause of war).

Assessment (paired judgement points, final block)
- [ ] S1 Achievement: Germany built the world's second-largest navy in under two decades; national pride and support for the Kaiser.
- [ ] S2 Limit: it never came near matching Britain; the risk theory failed because Britain out-built Germany and made friends with its rivals.
- [ ] S3 Achievement of Weltpolitik: Kiaochow, the Pacific islands, Samoa, the Baghdad Railway concession; Germany's voice in world affairs.
- [ ] S4 Limit: gains were small and unprofitable; the Morocco crises ended in humiliation; Germany became more isolated, relying on Austria-Hungary.
- [ ] S5 Domestic: the fleet rallied the right for a time, but its cost caused the 1908–09 finance crisis and did not halt the SPD's growth (largest party in the Reichstag in 1912).
- [ ] S6 Overall and contemporary vs later views: contemporaries in Germany saw a necessary national project and in Britain a direct threat; most historians judge Weltpolitik and the fleet a strategic mistake that helped make the Triple Entente and brought Britain into the war, while some stress that Anglo-German relations were improving by 1914.

## 3. Traps

Do not use (wrong or doubtful facts in the current notes):
- Do not use: Wilhelm II was "first cousin to Tsar Nicholas II", because they were only distant cousins; George V and Tsarina Alexandra were his first cousins.
- Do not use: the First Navy Law authorised "19 battleships, 8 large cruisers and 12 small cruisers", because the cruiser figures are wrong or doubtful. Give 19 battleships, with cruisers and coastal ships, by 1904.
- Do not use: the Second Navy Law planned "20 large cruisers" or "four squadrons of eight, plus a flagship and reserve" as exact composition, because the breakdown is doubtful; give 38 battleships over about twenty years.
- Do not use: the Second Navy Law "was passed amid British anger over the Boer War"; the anger was German, at the British stopping of German mail steamers (January 1900).
- Do not use: "as a boy he had been impressed by Queen Victoria's fleet at Spithead", because the childhood detail is unverified; use his honorary British admiralty and the 1889 Spithead review.
- Do not use: the Navy League "grew to over a million members", because the figure counts affiliated bodies and sources differ; call it a mass organisation.
- Do not use: "diplomats such as Holstein ... saw him as unpredictable and dangerous" as a named contemporary view, because the attribution is doubtful; describe critics in general terms.
- Do not use: "after the Daily Telegraph affair the Reichstag demanded that the Kaiser submit to constitutional restraint ... Bülow resigned in 1909" as cause and effect; the Reichstag criticised him and Wilhelm promised restraint, and Bülow fell in July 1909 over the finance reform (N19), having lost the Kaiser's trust.
- Do not use: Bülow as Chancellor from 1897; he was Foreign Secretary from 1897 and Chancellor only from October 1900.
- Do not use: "German pressure over Morocco ... gained little" combined with "Tangier (1905) and Agadir (1911) ended in humiliation" as if Tangier itself was a defeat; the humiliation came at Algeciras (1906), and in 1911 Germany did gain some French Congo territory.
- Do not use: "Britain ended its isolation ... partly because of the German navy" as settled fact; the 1904 Entente was mainly a colonial settlement. Present the navy's role as debated.
- Do not use: "Volker Berghahn ... a case of 'social imperialism'" as Berghahn's phrase; social imperialism is associated mainly with Wehler. Give Berghahn's view as in section 2.
- Do not use: the Dreadnought "made existing battleships obsolete, so Germany had to start again on the same footing as Britain" without the qualification that Britain still had a head start in building dreadnoughts and a far larger fleet overall.
- Do not use: "In 1914 he ... left for his annual cruise; he later tried to restrain Austria but could not stop the machinery of mobilisation" in this subsection beyond one line; the July crisis belongs to the notes on the outbreak of war.

Quotations
- Only these may appear in quotation marks: "a place in the sun" (Bülow, 1897); "Our future lies on the water" (Wilhelm II, 1898); "We want eight and we won't wait" (British slogan, 1909); "Dropping the Pilot" (cartoon title, 1890).
- Do not quote the "Hun speech", the Kruger telegram, the Daily Telegraph interview or Wilhelm on Mahan; give them as reported.
- Do not attribute "a place in the sun" to Wilhelm II; it was Bülow's speech.

- Bülow (Foreign Secretary 1897, Chancellor 1900–09) vs Bethmann Hollweg (Chancellor 1909–17) vs Caprivi (1890–94) vs Hohenlohe (1894–1900).

## 4. What exams ask

- Higher 100-mark essay on the problems the naval policy caused for Germany and Europe (asked often, 2006–2025). Marks go to a clear judgement sustained throughout: it alienated Britain, set off an arms race Germany could not win, strained finances (1908–09 crisis), took money from the army, failed its own risk-theory aim and helped bring Britain into the war. Dated laws (1898, 1900, 1908, 1912), the Dreadnought (1906) and the Haldane mission (1912) are the expected evidence.
- Higher essay comparing naval policy with another problem (e.g. the Kulturkampf) or with another cause of war (Franco-German tension, colonial rivalry, the Balkans): students must judge which mattered more and why, not narrate both. The usual line: the naval race had the wider international effect because it turned Britain against Germany.
- Higher essay on how far Weltpolitik damaged international relations (1897–1914): link each episode to its effect on a named power (Kruger telegram and naval laws on Britain, Baghdad Railway on Russia and Britain, Morocco on France and the Entente) and conclude on the balance of power.
- Higher essay comparing Bismarck and Wilhelm II, or tracing German foreign policy from Bismarck to Wilhelm II and its effect on the balance of power: the lapse of the Reinsurance Treaty (1890) is the turning point; Bismarck's caution vs Weltpolitik's provocation; Germany moved from the centre of diplomacy to "encirclement". Students need dates for both and a verdict (usually Bismarck the more effective).
- Ordinary 40-mark paragraph on the naval policy: each accurate dated statement earns marks: Wilhelm's admiration for the Royal Navy, Mahan, Tirpitz (1897), the risk theory, the Navy Laws 1898 and 1900, the Navy League, the Dreadnought 1906, "We want eight" (1909), Haldane 1912, results.
- Ordinary 40-mark paragraph on Wilhelm II and Weltpolitik: dismissal of Bismarck 1890, the Reinsurance Treaty lapse, Bülow and "a place in the sun", Kiaochow 1897, the Kruger telegram 1896, Baghdad Railway, Morocco.
- Ordinary document questions (DBQ style): extracts from German or British writers or Wilhelm on the need for a navy and whether it threatened Britain. Students should identify each writer's view (Germany as a peaceful trading nation needing protection vs British fear of a fleet in the North Sea) and add one outside fact, e.g. the Dreadnought (1906).
- Cause and result points examiners reward: why Germany built a fleet (prestige, Mahan, trade and colonies, domestic politics, Wilhelm's personality, risk theory); why Britain responded (dependence on sea power, fleet's North Sea location); why talks failed (neutrality pledge vs building limits); results for Germany at home and abroad.
- Judgement points: achievements vs limits as paired points (second-largest navy vs never close to Britain; colonies gained vs small and costly; domestic rallying vs finance crisis and SPD growth); contemporary (German pride, British alarm) vs later views (Fischer, Kehr/Berghahn, Kennedy, Ferguson), naming at least one historian with a view. On Wilhelm himself: Röhl vs Clark on how much he really ruled.
