## 1. Must cover (one bullet or block for every item; tick each off)

Background (assumed)
- [ ] A1 (assumed) In 1870 Irish women had no parliamentary vote; the law favoured husbands (a married woman's property passed to her husband).
- [ ] A2 (assumed) The Irish Parliamentary Party (Redmond, Dillon) sought Home Rule; the Third Home Rule Bill (1912) was the main political issue, with unionists resisting it.
- [ ] A3 (assumed) Before 1878 secondary schools were mostly private or run by religious orders, with little state funding; Trinity College Dublin was Protestant in ethos and the Catholic hierarchy banned Catholics from it.

Key concepts (each defined as used in this topic; also in keyTerms)
- [ ] K1 Feminism: belief in women's equal rights and public roles.
- [ ] K2 Suffragette: militant campaigner for women's votes (direct action); contrast suffragist (peaceful, constitutional). The term was coined by the British press.
- [ ] K3 Democracy: government through elected representatives; the argument that it was incomplete without women.

Suffrage movement: early phase
- [ ] E1 Early suffrage as a respectable, middle-class campaign linked to property, education and local government.
- [ ] E2 Married women's property: the Acts of 1870 and 1882; Tod's campaigning.
- [ ] E3 Anna and Thomas Haslam, Dublin Women's Suffrage Association (1876), later the Irish Women's Suffrage and Local Government Association; methods (petitions, meetings).
- [ ] E4 Women and local government: the Local Government (Ireland) Act 1898 (vote in county and district elections; stand for district councils and boards of guardians); poor law guardians.
- [ ] E5 Many early suffragists were unionists; others backed Home Rule, so the societies stayed neutral on it.

Key personality: Isabella Tod (one block, every point)
- [ ] T1 Who: Presbyterian reformer, born Edinburgh 1836, settled in Belfast, died 1896.
- [ ] T2 Belfast suffrage society, early 1870s, first in Ulster.
- [ ] T3 Married women's property campaign (Act of 1882).
- [ ] T4 Girls' education: Ladies' Institute (1867), work with Margaret Byers, inclusion of girls in the Intermediate Act and the Royal University charter.
- [ ] T5 Other causes: temperance (Belfast Women's Temperance Association, 1874), Contagious Diseases Acts.
- [ ] T6 Unionism: Liberal Unionist, opposed Home Rule (1886); Irish Women's Liberal Unionist Association.
- [ ] T7 Participant in the campaigns; contemporaries' view (respected speaker, limited among nationalists); historians' view (pioneer).

Suffrage movement: 1900-1914
- [ ] S1 Irish Women's Suffrage Federation (1911), peaceful; Irish Women's Franchise League (1908), more radical.
- [ ] S2 Irish Women's Franchise League: founders (Hanna Sheehy-Skeffington, Margaret Cousins, James Cousins, Francis Sheehy-Skeffington), aim (votes on the same terms as men).
- [ ] S3 The Irish Citizen (from May 1912 to 1920): editors, role.
- [ ] S4 Militancy 1912: window-breaking arrests (June), Asquith's Dublin visit (July) and the hatchet, prison, hunger strike, force-feeding of English prisoners.
- [ ] S5 Hanna's 1913 imprisonment and release under the Cat and Mouse Act.
- [ ] S6 Hostile crowds; nationalist and labour criticism of militancy.
- [ ] S7 The Irish Party's opposition: Conciliation Bill defeated March 1912; Redmond and Dillon feared complicating Home Rule; Redmond personally opposed.
- [ ] S8 Suffragists' attempt to amend the Third Home Rule Bill; the argument that Home Rule without women's votes was incomplete.
- [ ] S9 Suffragists divided over nationalism and unionism: Ulster Day women's declaration (September 1912); Cumann na mBan (April 1914) and Sheehy-Skeffington's objection.
- [ ] S10 Outcome: votes for women over 30 (with property qualification) in 1918.

Key personality: Hanna Sheehy-Skeffington (one block, every point)
- [ ] H1 Who: born Kanturk, Co. Cork 1877, daughter of nationalist MP David Sheehy; MA, Royal University (1902); died 1946.
- [ ] H2 Marriage (1903) to Francis Skeffington; both took the joint surname.
- [ ] H3 Co-founded the Irish Women's Franchise League (1908); contributor and later editor of the Irish Citizen.
- [ ] H4 Imprisonment: window-breaking 1912, again 1913; hunger strike.
- [ ] H5 Critic of the Irish Party and Cumann na mBan; links of feminism to nationalism and socialism.
- [ ] H6 Francis shot by Captain Bowen-Colthurst in April 1916; she campaigned for an inquiry and refused compensation (after this topic's dates; brief).
- [ ] H7 Contemporaries' view (courageous, divisive); historians' view (key figure of Irish feminism).

Educational reforms
- [ ] D1 Intermediate Education (Ireland) Act 1878: Intermediate Board, public examinations (junior, middle, senior) from 1879, open to boys and girls.
- [ ] D2 Payment by results: results fees per pupil passing; prizes and exhibitions.
- [ ] D3 Effects: financed denominational schools without controlling them; girls' schools grew; criticism (cramming, narrow curriculum).
- [ ] D4 Girls' secondary schools: Alexandra College (Dublin, 1866), Victoria College (Belfast, founded 1859 as the Ladies' Collegiate School by Margaret Byers), Loreto and Dominican convent schools.
- [ ] D5 Primary education: Revised Programme of 1900 ended payment by results in national schools, broadened the curriculum, Irish allowed as an ordinary subject; bilingual programme 1904.
- [ ] D6 Royal University of Ireland (1879): an examining body, degrees open to women, first women graduates early 1880s; replaced Queen's University.
- [ ] D7 The Catholic university question: Trinity's ethos, bishops' ban, Catholic grievance.
- [ ] D8 Irish Universities Act 1908 (Birrell): NUI (colleges at Dublin, Cork, Galway) and Queen's University Belfast; NUI acceptable to Catholics, open to women; Walsh as first chancellor.
- [ ] D9 Irish compulsory for NUI matriculation: decided 1910, applied from 1913 (Gaelic League campaign).

Assessment (paired judgement points, final block)
- [ ] J1 Achievements: local government vote and office, access to degrees and professions, an organised movement and a newspaper.
- [ ] J2 Limits: no parliamentary vote before 1918; Home Rule Bills offered none; militancy gained little and divided opinion.
- [ ] J3 Education achievements: wider access to secondary and university education, especially for girls and Catholics.
- [ ] J4 Education limits: cramming under payment by results; denominational divisions persisted.
- [ ] J5 Contemporary vs later views: Irish Party saw suffrage as a distraction; suffragists saw a right; historians stress division by the national question and the experience women gained.

Marks go to a clear judgement stated at the start, supported by dated points: organisations, methods, opposition, results (local government 1898, vote 1918), and the education gains women won.
- Strong answers separate methods (constitutional societies of the 1870s-1900s vs militancy from 1912), and separate achievements (local government, education, organisation, newspaper) from limits (no parliamentary vote before 1918, Irish Party opposition, division over nationalism and unionism).

- Education (2024 style): what the 1878 Act did, how payment by results worked and why it was criticised, and what the 1900 programme changed; universities: why the Catholic grievance existed and how 1908 answered it.
