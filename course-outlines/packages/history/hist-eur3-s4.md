## 1. Must cover (one bullet or block for every item; tick each off)

Background (assumed)
- [ ] A1 (assumed) The Bolsheviks seized power in Petrograd in October 1917 (November by the Western calendar) and set up the Council of People's Commissars (Sovnarkom) under Lenin. One bullet; the revolution itself is in the notes on Tsarist Russia and revolution.
- [ ] A2 (assumed) Marxism in brief: history as class struggle; the working class (proletariat) would overthrow capitalism; a "dictatorship of the proletariat" would come before a classless communist society. Lenin's addition: a disciplined party of professional revolutionaries leads the workers.
- [ ] A3 (assumed) Russia's backwardness: a mainly peasant country (about four-fifths of the people lived on the land) with little modern industry compared with Britain, Germany or the USA.
- [ ] A4 (assumed) How the Communist Party ruled: the Party Congress, the Central Committee, the Politburo (the real decision-making body) and the General Secretary, who ran the party machine; the party controlled the soviets and the government.
- [ ] A5 (assumed) The Western (Gregorian) calendar was adopted in February 1918, so all dates in this subsection are Western dates.

Key concepts (each defined as used in this topic; also in keyTerms)
- [ ] K1 Communism: Marxist ideology and system in which the state owns land, industry and banks and one party claims to rule for the working class.
- [ ] K2 Collectivisation: forcing individual peasant farms into large state-controlled collective farms (kolkhozy), from 1929.
- [ ] K3 Personality cult: the presentation of a leader as infallible and heroic through propaganda, ritual and images; the Lenin cult after 1924 as precedent, the Stalin cult from 1929.
- [ ] K4 Totalitarianism: a state that tries to control every part of public and private life through one party, an ideology, secret police, terror and propaganda; the Stalinist USSR as a leading example (and the historians' debate in J6).

Lenin's regime, 1917–1924
- [ ] L1 The Cheka (December 1917, Dzerzhinsky): arrests and executions; the Red Terror after the attempt on Lenin's life (August–September 1918).
- [ ] L2 The one-party state: the Constituent Assembly dispersed (January 1918, background only); Kadets, Mensheviks and Socialist Revolutionaries suppressed and rival parties banned during the civil war; the Communist Party the only legal party.
- [ ] L3 The civil war (1918–1921) in outline: Reds against Whites and foreign intervention; Trotsky's Red Army; why the Reds won (central position, unity, discipline, terror; divided Whites). One block only; its detail is in the notes on Tsarist Russia and revolution.
- [ ] L4 War Communism (1918–1921): nationalisation of industry and banks, grain requisitioning, rationing, ban on private trade.
- [ ] L5 The crisis of 1920–21: peasant revolts (the Tambov rising), strikes, economic collapse and the famine of 1921–22.
- [ ] L6 The Kronstadt rising (March 1921): sailors and workers who had backed the Bolsheviks in 1917 demanded free elections to the soviets and freedom of speech; crushed by the Red Army.
- [ ] L7 The NEP (Tenth Party Congress, March 1921): grain requisitioning replaced by a tax in kind; peasants could sell their surplus; small private trade and industry allowed; the state kept the "commanding heights" (large industry, banks, foreign trade).
- [ ] L8 The ban on factions at the same congress (1921): no organised groups inside the party, a rule Stalin later used against his rivals.
- [ ] L9 Results of the NEP: food supplies and output recovered by the mid-1920s; Nepmen (private traders) and better-off peasants (kulaks); many Communists saw it as a retreat from socialism.
- [ ] L10 Electrification: the 1920 slogan "Communism is Soviet power plus the electrification of the whole country" and the state electrification plan (GOELRO).
- [ ] L11 The USSR formed (30 December 1922): Russia, Ukraine, Belorussia and the Transcaucasian republic.
- [ ] L12 Lenin's illness and death: strokes from May 1922; death on 21 January 1924; embalmed and displayed in a mausoleum in Red Square; Petrograd renamed Leningrad.

The struggle for succession, 1922–1929
- [ ] U1 Stalin's power base: Commissar for Nationalities, General Secretary from April 1922; control of party appointments; the "Lenin enrolment" of new members in 1924.
- [ ] U2 Lenin's Testament (late 1922 to January 1923): it criticised all the leading candidates and, in its postscript, called Stalin "too rude" and urged his removal as General Secretary; it was not published at the time.
- [ ] U3 The rivals and their weaknesses: Trotsky (brilliant, creator of the Red Army, but a late convert to Bolshevism in 1917, seen as arrogant and a possible "Bonaparte"); Zinoviev and Kamenev (Leningrad and Moscow party bosses); Bukharin (the party's leading theorist, champion of the NEP).
- [ ] U4 The policy debate: Trotsky's "permanent revolution" and rapid industrialisation vs Stalin's "socialism in one country" (1924), which appealed to party members tired of waiting for world revolution.
- [ ] U5 The defeat of the Left: Stalin, Zinoviev and Kamenev against Trotsky (1923–25); Zinoviev and Kamenev joined Trotsky in the United Opposition (1926); its leaders expelled from the party (late 1927).
- [ ] U6 Trotsky's fate: internal exile in Alma-Ata, Kazakhstan (1928), deported from the USSR (1929), killed in Mexico on Stalin's orders (1940).
- [ ] U7 The defeat of the Right: Bukharin, Rykov and Tomsky backed the NEP; after the grain procurement crisis of 1927–28 Stalin adopted forced industrialisation and grain collection; the Right was defeated in 1928–29 and Bukharin removed from the Politburo (1929).
- [ ] U8 Why Stalin won: his control of the party machine, his rivals' mistakes and underestimation of him, the ban on factions, and his skill in using one faction against another.

The Five-Year Plans and industrialisation
- [ ] F1 Why: to end backwardness, build socialism, strengthen the USSR against attack, and secure Stalin's power; Stalin's 1931 warning that the USSR was "fifty or a hundred years behind" and must catch up in ten years.
- [ ] F2 Gosplan and the First Five-Year Plan (from October 1928): targets for coal, steel, oil, electricity and machinery; heavy industry before consumer goods; declared complete in four years and three months (end of 1932).
- [ ] F3 New projects and cities: Magnitogorsk (southern Urals), the Dnieper dam (completed 1932), the Moscow Metro (opened 1935).
- [ ] F4 Labour: workers drawn from the villages; wage differences and bonuses; harsh discipline for lateness and absence; internal passports; propaganda and "shock workers".
- [ ] F5 Stakhanov (August 1935): the reported record shift, the Stakhanovite movement, and why the feat was staged and resented (higher norms for others).
- [ ] F6 Scapegoats: engineers and managers blamed for failures as "wreckers", beginning with the Shakhty trial (1928). One bullet; the later trials are in the notes on Stalin's show trials.
- [ ] F7 The Second (1933–37) and Third (1938–41) Plans: more realistic, some attention to consumer goods and transport, then a growing shift to armaments as war approached.
- [ ] F8 Results: large rises in steel, coal, oil and electricity; the USSR one of the world's leading industrial powers by 1940; new industrial regions in the Urals and Siberia that proved vital in the war.
- [ ] F9 Costs: falling real wages and living standards in the early 1930s, overcrowded housing, poor-quality goods, waste, inflated statistics, and forced (Gulag) labour on projects such as the White Sea–Baltic Canal (1931–33).

Collectivisation and the famine of 1932–33
- [ ] C1 Why: the grain procurement crisis of 1927–28; to feed the growing towns and export grain to pay for machinery; to bring the peasants under state and party control; ideological hostility to private farming and the kulaks.
- [ ] C2 How: launched at the end of 1929 with Stalin's call for the "liquidation of the kulaks as a class"; collective farms (kolkhozy) delivering quotas to the state; state farms (sovkhozy); machine tractor stations (MTS) supplying machinery and political control.
- [ ] C3 Dekulakisation: "kulak" applied to anyone who resisted; families shot, sent to camps or deported to Siberia, the Urals and Kazakhstan.
- [ ] C4 Peasant resistance: slaughtering livestock, burning crops, revolts; women's protests.
- [ ] C5 "Dizzy with Success" (March 1930): Stalin blamed local officials for excesses and allowed peasants to leave the collectives; many left; pressure resumed later in 1930.
- [ ] C6 The famine of 1932–33: high requisitions taken despite poor harvests; several million deaths, mainly in Ukraine (where it is called the Holodomor), the North Caucasus and Kazakhstan; the law of 7 August 1932 against taking collective farm property; movement restricted; the famine officially denied; grain still exported.
- [ ] C7 Results: over 90 per cent of peasant households collectivised by the late 1930s; state control of grain achieved; livestock numbers and farm output fell and took years to recover; peasants' small private plots produced a large share of vegetables, milk and meat.

The cult of Stalin and the Stalinist state
- [ ] P1 The cult's start: Stalin's fiftieth birthday celebrations (December 1929).
- [ ] P2 Rewriting the past: the Short Course history of the party (1938) put Stalin at the centre of the revolution; Trotsky and other fallen leaders written out and removed from photographs.
- [ ] P3 Images and names: portraits, statues, poems, songs and films presenting Stalin as the wise "father of the peoples" and Lenin's true heir; towns and places named after him (Tsaritsyn became Stalingrad in 1925).
- [ ] P4 Why the cult: legitimacy as Lenin's successor, unity behind a single leader during upheaval, a substitute for religious and tsarist loyalties.
- [ ] P5 The Stalin Constitution (1936): universal suffrage, secret ballot and civil rights on paper; one-party rule and the NKVD unchanged.
- [ ] P6 The machinery of terror: the secret police (OGPU, absorbed into the NKVD in 1934); the Gulag camp system; internal passports (from December 1932). One block. The Kirov murder, the Moscow trials, the army purge and the Great Terror of 1936–38 are in the notes on Stalin's show trials: give one cross-reference bullet only.

Soviet society
- [ ] O1 Education: mass literacy campaigns, expansion of schools and technical colleges; after 1931 a return to discipline, exams and uniforms; education also as indoctrination.
- [ ] O2 Youth: the Young Pioneers and the Komsomol (Communist youth league).
- [ ] O3 Women: legal equality, easy divorce and the legalisation of abortion (1920); the Zhenotdel (women's department of the party, 1919–1930); women drawn into industry and the professions; the "double burden" of work and housework; the 1936 family law banning abortion and making divorce harder to raise the birth rate.
- [ ] O4 Religion: church property seized, clergy arrested, churches closed or destroyed (Moscow's Cathedral of Christ the Saviour demolished in 1931); the League of the Godless (1925); atheism taught in schools.
- [ ] O5 The arts: experiment in the 1920s (Eisenstein's films, e.g. Battleship Potemkin, 1925); from 1932 independent groups replaced by state unions; socialist realism declared the official method at the first Congress of Soviet Writers (1934); the attack on Shostakovich's opera Lady Macbeth of Mtsensk (1936); persecution of writers such as Osip Mandelstam.
- [ ] O6 The "new Soviet man": collective values, heroes of labour, parades and posters.

The "Soviet alternative" seen from the West
- [ ] V1 Why it appealed in the Depression: the West had mass unemployment and falling output while the USSR claimed full employment, rapid growth and planning.
- [ ] V2 Sympathetic visitors and writers: Lincoln Steffens (1919, early example); George Bernard Shaw's visit (1931); Sidney and Beatrice Webb, Soviet Communism: A New Civilisation? (1935).
- [ ] V3 The famine and the press: Walter Duranty of the New York Times denied the famine; Gareth Jones and Malcolm Muggeridge reported it (1933) and were attacked for doing so.
- [ ] V4 Critics: liberals, conservatives, the churches and many socialists pointed to the famine, the lack of freedom and the show trials; fear of communism helped fascism in Europe (cross-reference to the notes on Mussolini's Italy).
- [ ] V5 Growing respectability: recognition by the United States (1933) and membership of the League of Nations (1934).

Soviet foreign policy, 1920–1941
- [ ] X1 The Treaty of Rapallo with Germany (1922): two outcast states restored relations; secret military co-operation followed.
- [ ] X2 The Comintern (1919): control of foreign communist parties; the "social fascist" line against Social Democrats split the German left before 1933.
- [ ] X3 Collective security after Hitler's rise: Litvinov as Foreign Commissar; the League (1934); pacts with France and Czechoslovakia (1935); Popular Fronts; aid to the Spanish Republic (from 1936).
- [ ] X4 Munich (1938), from which the USSR was excluded, and the switch to Molotov (May 1939); the Nazi–Soviet Pact (23 August 1939) and its secret protocol dividing eastern Europe.
- [ ] X5 Gains of 1939–40: eastern Poland (from 17 September 1939); the Katyn massacre of Polish officers (1940); the Winter War with Finland (1939–40), which exposed Red Army weakness; the Baltic states annexed (1940).
- [ ] X6 Unready in June 1941: Stalin ignored repeated warnings of a German attack; the army was weakened by the purges (one line; detail in the show trials notes).

The Stalinist state in war, 1941–1945
- [ ] W1 Operation Barbarossa (22 June 1941): huge early losses of men, territory and aircraft; Leningrad besieged (September 1941 to January 1944).
- [ ] W2 Stalin's response: the State Defence Committee (30 June 1941) under his chairmanship; his radio broadcast of 3 July 1941 ("brothers and sisters"); Supreme Commander (August 1941); stayed in Moscow during the German advance of autumn 1941.
- [ ] W3 The Battle of Moscow: the German advance stopped and the Soviet counter-offensive of December 1941.
- [ ] W4 The war economy: about 1,500 factories moved east to the Urals, Siberia and Central Asia, mostly in 1941; total mobilisation of labour, including women; tank and aircraft output rising sharply by 1942 and soon exceeding Germany's; the T-34 tank.
- [ ] W5 Stalingrad (August 1942 to February 1943): the German Sixth Army encircled and surrendered; the turning point in the east.
- [ ] W6 Kursk (July 1943) and the advance west to Berlin (April–May 1945).
- [ ] W7 Discipline and terror: Order 227 (July 1942, "Not one step back"), blocking detachments and penal battalions; the NKVD behind the lines.
- [ ] W8 Lend-Lease from Britain and the USA (trucks, food, equipment) as a significant support. One bullet; Lend-Lease in general is in the notes on the Second World War.
- [ ] W9 Patriotism over ideology: the "Great Patriotic War"; appeals to Russian history and heroes; the Orthodox Church allowed a patriarch again (1943); the Comintern dissolved (1943).
- [ ] W10 Punishment of whole peoples and of returned prisoners: the Volga Germans (1941), Chechens and Ingush and Crimean Tatars (1944) deported; returning prisoners of war treated as suspects and many sent to camps.
- [ ] W11 The human cost: about 27 million Soviet dead, military and civilian.
- [ ] W12 Stalin as war leader: disastrous early errors (ignoring warnings, forbidding retreat in 1941), then growing reliance on able generals such as Zhukov; his firmness and his control of production; Generalissimo (1945); the Soviet share in the Allied victory (most German army losses were on the Eastern Front). Wartime conferences in one line, with cross-reference to the notes on the Second World War.
- [ ] W13 Outcome by 1945: the USSR a superpower with the Red Army in control of eastern Europe; victory used to strengthen Stalin's authority and the claim that the system had been proved right.

Key personality: Vladimir Ilyich Lenin (block of 5–6 points)
- [ ] KL1 Who he was: born Vladimir Ulyanov at Simbirsk (1870); his elder brother Alexander executed (1887) for plotting against Alexander III; Bolshevik leader from 1903.
- [ ] KL2 What he did, with dates: return in April 1917 and the April Theses (background); the seizure of power (October 1917); head of Sovnarkom; the Cheka, terror, War Communism; the NEP (1921); the USSR (1922).
- [ ] KL3 How he shaped events: architect of the one-party state; flexible enough to retreat to the NEP to save the regime; the ban on factions; his Testament.
- [ ] KL4 Participant, not witness: he led and decided at every stage until his strokes.
- [ ] KL5 How contemporaries saw him: to the party the unquestioned authority on Marxism; to opponents (Mensheviks, SRs, Kronstadt sailors) a dictator; the Lenin cult after his death.
- [ ] KL6 How historians judge him: continuity view (Lenin's terror, one-party rule and ban on factions made Stalinism possible) vs discontinuity view (the NEP and the Testament show a different path that Stalin abandoned).

Key personality: Josef Stalin (block of 5–6 points)
- [ ] KS1 Who he was: born Ioseb Dzhugashvili at Gori, Georgia (1878); trained for the priesthood at the Tiflis seminary; Bolshevik revolutionary, several times exiled to Siberia; took the name Stalin ("man of steel").
- [ ] KS2 What he did, with dates: General Secretary (1922); defeat of rivals by 1929; the Five-Year Plans (from 1928) and collectivisation (from 1929); the Terror (1936–38, see the show trials notes); the Nazi–Soviet Pact (1939); war leader (1941–45); died 1953.
- [ ] KS3 How he shaped and was shaped by events: he drove the "revolution from above"; he was also shaped by the grain crisis, fear of foreign attack and the Bolshevik tradition of terror.
- [ ] KS4 How contemporaries saw him: Lenin's warning in the Testament; the cult's "father of the peoples"; Western admirers (Shaw, the Webbs); critics, including Trotsky in exile.
- [ ] KS5 How historians judge him: Khrushchev's secret speech (February 1956) denouncing the cult; Robert Conquest and the "totalitarian" view; Isaac Deutscher's view of Stalin as a brutal moderniser; later social historians such as Sheila Fitzpatrick on everyday life and pressures from below.

Assessment: Lenin and Stalin's Soviet Union (final block, paired points)
- [ ] J1 Lenin: achievement (won the civil war, held the state together, NEP recovery) vs limit (terror, one-party rule, famine of 1921–22, Kronstadt).
- [ ] J2 Industry: achievement (an industrial and military power by 1941 that out-produced Germany in the war) vs limit (low living standards, waste, forced labour, inflated statistics).
- [ ] J3 Agriculture: achievement (state control of grain, mechanisation through the MTS) vs failure (the famine, the destruction of the kulaks, output below 1928 levels for years).
- [ ] J4 Society: achievement (literacy, education, women's work and professional opportunities) vs limit (no political freedom, persecution of religion and the arts, the 1936 reversal on family policy).
- [ ] J5 Foreign policy and war: achievement (collective security efforts, survival and victory, superpower status) vs failure (the Nazi–Soviet Pact left the USSR unready in 1941; about 27 million dead).
- [ ] J6 Contemporary vs later views: the "Soviet alternative" admired by some Western intellectuals in the Depression vs criticism then and later; the totalitarian school vs social historians; continuity vs break between Lenin and Stalin.

## 3. Traps (known errors and boundaries)

- Do not use: "In 1945 Stalin joined Churchill and Roosevelt at Yalta and Potsdam" (current notes), because Roosevelt died in April 1945: Potsdam was with Truman, and Churchill was replaced by Attlee during it. Use the W12 row.
- Do not use: "in 1943 Stalin … calling the conflict the Great Patriotic War" (current notes), because the name was used from the first days of the war in 1941. 1943 is right only for the Church concession and the Comintern's dissolution.
- Do not use: "Socialist realism became the official style of art and literature in 1932" (current notes and factCard), because 1932 was the decree that dissolved independent groups and set up the single Writers' Union; socialist realism was proclaimed the official method at the Writers' Congress of 1934. Use the O5 row.
- Do not use: "The League of the Militant Godless, founded in 1925" as written, because it was founded as the League of the Godless and took "Militant" in 1929. Use the O4 row.
- Do not use: "Soviet industry relocated about 1,500 factories … in 1941 and 1942" as written; the figure of about 1,500 refers to the evacuation of the second half of 1941. Use the W4 row.
- Do not use: "the Gulag … held millions of prisoners" as a figure for any one time; say more than a million at a time by the late 1930s, with millions passing through.
- Do not use: a single death toll for the 1932–33 famine; estimates differ widely. Say "several million". Do not call it a genocide as a fact: whether it was is debated (Ukraine and many states recognise it as one; give that only as a view if needed).
- Do not use: the claim that Stalin tricked Trotsky into missing Lenin's funeral as fact; it is Trotsky's account. If used, give it as Trotsky's claim.
- Do not use: the claim that Stalin had a nervous breakdown after the invasion; historians disagree. Say only that he withdrew briefly in late June 1941 before taking charge.
- Do not use: literacy percentages, Five-Year Plan target figures, livestock losses or kulak deportation numbers; none is checked here.
- Stalin's birth year is 1878, not 1879 (the official Soviet date). His death in 1953 is outside the topic's dates: one mention in his personality block only.
- The First Five-Year Plan was officially backdated to begin in October 1928, though approved only in 1929. Say "from October 1928".
- Lenin's Testament criticised Trotsky, Zinoviev, Kamenev and Bukharin too, not only Stalin.
- Kulak means a better-off peasant; in practice the label was used against any peasant who resisted.
- Kolkhoz (collective farm, members shared income) vs sovkhoz (state farm, paid workers).
- War Communism (1918–21) vs NEP (1921–28): the NEP was a retreat to save the regime, not a new socialist policy; the "commanding heights" stayed with the state.
- Cheka (1917) → GPU/OGPU (1922–34) → NKVD (from 1934). Don't call the 1920s police the NKVD.
- The Left Opposition (Trotsky, then Zinoviev and Kamenev) wanted faster industrialisation; the Right (Bukharin, Rykov, Tomsky) wanted to keep the NEP. Stalin defeated the Left with the Right's help, then took up the Left's policy.
- The Nazi–Soviet Pact was a non-aggression pact, not a military alliance.
- Neighbouring subjects. Leave these out or give one line with a cross-reference by subject:
  - The Kirov murder, the Moscow show trials (Zinoviev and Kamenev, Pyatakov and Radek, Bukharin and Rykov), Vyshinsky, Yezhov, the Tukhachevsky army purge, the Great Terror and its numbers, and foreign reactions to the trials: the notes on Stalin's show trials.
  - The 1917 revolutions, Brest-Litovsk and the civil war in detail: the notes on Tsarist Russia and revolution.
  - The course of the Second World War, the Grand Alliance and its conferences, Lend-Lease in general, and the technology of warfare: the notes on the Second World War.
  - Hitler's foreign policy, appeasement and Munich from the Western side: the notes on the Nazi state and Hitler's foreign policy.
  - The Einsatzgruppen and the Holocaust in the occupied Soviet Union: the notes on anti-Semitism and the Holocaust.
  - The Popular Front in France: the notes on France; Keynes and Britain's Depression: the notes on inter-war Britain.
  - Mussolini and Hitler as comparison: one line each at most in the Assessment block.

## 4. What exams ask (topics only)

- Essay: how far Stalin transformed the Soviet economy and society (the most frequent Higher topic). Credit goes to a clear "to a great extent, but at enormous cost" judgement, each area with evidence: the plans (named projects, Stakhanov, output figures said to be official), collectivisation (kolkhozy, dekulakisation, famine), society (education, women, religion, arts). Pair every achievement with its cost.
- Essay: Stalin's rule and leadership in peace and war, or the main characteristics of his rule. Expected strands: one-man power (defeat of rivals), the cult of personality, forced industrialisation, collectivisation, terror (one paragraph with cross-reference to the show trials), control of culture, then war leadership. War paragraphs need: early errors in 1941, the GKO, the evacuation of industry, Stalingrad and Kursk, Order 227, and a verdict on how much of the victory was his.
- Essay: the contributions of Lenin and Stalin to communism in Russia, or how communism developed under them. Answers that compare are rewarded: Lenin built the one-party state, the Cheka and the NEP; Stalin turned it into a personal dictatorship with a planned economy. Use the continuity vs break debate for the top band.
- Essay: the Soviet Union's role in, and the challenges it faced in, the Second World War, or across 1924–1945 in peace and war. Give the peace challenges (succession, backwardness, feeding the towns) and the war challenges (invasion, survival, production), each with the response and outcome.
- Essay comparing dictators' use of propaganda and terror (Stalin with Mussolini or Hitler). The Soviet side needs the cult (birthday, Short Course, rewriting photographs, names), socialist realism, and the secret police and Gulag, with the purpose of each.
- Ordinary Level: a short Lenin personality account (Cheka, civil war, War Communism, Kronstadt, NEP, USSR, death); the NEP's terms and results; the Five-Year Plans and collectivisation; Stalin as war leader.
- Ordinary Level documents: cartoons of the Nazi–Soviet Pact (identify the leaders, explain why it was a surprising partnership, what happened in June 1941); a worker's account of factory life (discipline, monotony, pressure to raise output); a description of a new Soviet industrial city (planned avenues and parks set against poor workers' housing). Students should link details to the Five-Year Plans and say why Stalin wanted collectivisation (grain for towns and export, removing the kulaks).
- Judgement throughout: achievements vs costs; contemporary Western admiration (the Webbs, Shaw) vs critics and later historians; "how far" questions need a stated verdict in the introduction and the conclusion.
