## 1. Must cover (one bullet or block for every item; tick each off)

Background (assumed knowledge)
- [ ] X1 (assumed) The early factory system in cotton: water-powered spinning mills, long hours, child labour, fines and beatings as discipline; pauper apprentices (workhouse children bound to mill owners) were common in rural mills. One or two bullets; the general story of factory conditions belongs to the notes on the changing economy and society.
- [ ] X2 (assumed) Laissez-faire as the dominant view: most employers and politicians thought the state should not regulate wages or hours, which is why Owen's ideas and his call for a factory law were unusual.
- [ ] X3 (assumed) Why a rural mill needed a village: water-powered mills stood where there was falling water, far from towns, so owners had to build housing and bring in workers, including pauper children.

Owen's background
- [ ] O1 Birth and early work: born in Newtown, Montgomeryshire, Wales, 1771; left school at about ten; worked for drapers (Stamford, London, then Manchester).
- [ ] O2 Manchester: by about twenty he was managing a large cotton-spinning mill; later a partner in the Chorlton Twist Company; his Manchester years showed him how mill workers lived.
- [ ] O3 His central idea: character is formed by circumstances (environment), not by the individual; so changing the environment changes people. This idea explains every reform at New Lanark.
- [ ] O4 Marriage to Caroline Dale (1799), daughter of David Dale; in 1799 Owen and his Manchester partners bought New Lanark from Dale; Owen became manager on 1 January 1800.

New Lanark before Owen
- [ ] N1 The site: on the River Clyde near Lanark, Scotland, powered by the Falls of Clyde; founded in the mid-1780s by David Dale, at first in partnership with Richard Arkwright; one of the largest cotton-spinning works in Britain.
- [ ] N2 David Dale: Glasgow merchant and banker, thought a comparatively humane employer (dormitories, food and evening schooling for the pauper children), but the children still worked long days.
- [ ] N3 The population in 1800: about 2,000 people, including about 500 pauper children from the poorhouses of Edinburgh and Glasgow.

Conditions Owen found
- [ ] C1 Work: a mill day of about thirteen hours including meal breaks; children from about six worked in the mills and then attended evening classes when exhausted.
- [ ] C2 The village, by Owen's account: dirty, overcrowded houses, filthy streets, poor sanitation.
- [ ] C3 The people, by Owen's account: drunkenness, theft, disorder and distrust of an outside manager; discipline by fines and punishment.
- [ ] C4 Owen's reading of these conditions: the result of environment, not individual wickedness (links to O3). Note that this picture comes largely from Owen's own writings.

Reforms in the mill
- [ ] R1 Child labour: Owen stopped taking pauper apprentices and raised the minimum working age, so that by 1816 no children under ten worked in the mills; younger children went to school instead.
- [ ] R2 Hours: he shortened the working day (see section 2 for the safe wording) and argued that output did not fall.
- [ ] R3 Discipline without beating: physical punishment ended; reward and example instead.
- [ ] R4 The silent monitor: the four-coloured block beside each worker, turned daily to show the previous day's conduct; conduct also recorded in books. Explain how it worked and why Owen used it (no shouting or beating; shame and pride as motives).
- [ ] R5 Wages during the cotton shortage: during an American embargo on cotton the mills stopped, and Owen paid full wages to idle workers for about four months, which won their trust.

Reforms in the village
- [ ] V1 Housing and sanitation: houses improved and repaired, streets cleaned, rubbish collected, households encouraged to keep their homes clean.
- [ ] V2 The village store: good-quality goods sold at little more than cost; its profits helped pay for the schools; the sale of spirits was controlled to cut drunkenness.
- [ ] V3 A sick fund to which workers contributed part of their wages, giving help in illness.
- [ ] V4 The Institute for the Formation of Character (opened 1816): a building for the infant school and children's classes by day, and for evening classes, lectures, music and dancing for older children and adults.
- [ ] V5 The infant school (1816): children from as soon as they could walk (about one) to six; taught by kindness, through play, songs, dancing, pictures, maps and natural objects rather than books and rote learning; no punishment. Often called the first infant school in Britain; its teacher James Buchanan later ran an infant school in London.
- [ ] V6 Schooling for older children (about six to ten) before they started work, plus evening classes for working young people: reading, writing, arithmetic, singing, dancing, geography.

Profits, partners and disputes
- [ ] P1 Profit: the mills remained profitable under Owen, which was his proof that humane treatment and profit could go together.
- [ ] P2 First partners' objections: his earlier partners objected to spending profits on schools; the dispute ended with the mills sold at auction in 1813.
- [ ] P3 The 1813 partnership: new backers including the philosopher Jeremy Bentham and the Quaker William Allen, who agreed to take a 5 per cent return on their capital, the rest of the profit going to the community.
- [ ] P4 Later disputes: Allen and other partners objected to dancing, drill and the lack of religious teaching in the schools and to Owen's attacks on religion; in the 1820s they imposed changes, which helped push Owen out.

Owen's writings and campaigns
- [ ] W1 A New View of Society (1813–14): essays arguing that character is formed by circumstances and that a society could improve its people by improving conditions and education.
- [ ] W2 The background of state action: the 1802 Health and Morals of Apprentices Act, promoted by Sir Robert Peel the elder, had limited pauper apprentices' hours (one clause).
- [ ] W3 The campaign for a factory law: from 1815 Owen pressed for limits on child labour; he gave evidence to the elder Peel's select committee in 1816.
- [ ] W4 The Cotton Mills and Factories Act (1819): no children under nine in cotton mills; children aged nine to sixteen limited to twelve hours a day; enforced by magistrates with no inspectors, so poorly enforced; weaker than Owen wanted.
- [ ] W5 Villages of co-operation: Owen's 1817 plan for self-supporting co-operative communities for the unemployed poor, presented to a committee for the relief of the poor in London, and his Report to the County of Lanark (1820); William Cobbett mocked such villages.
- [ ] W6 The attack on religion (August 1817): at a public meeting in London Owen denounced all organised religions, losing much support among the respectable and powerful.

After New Lanark: co-operation and socialism
- [ ] A1 New Harmony: Owen went to the United States in 1824; in 1825 he bought the town of Harmony, Indiana, from a religious community (the Rappites) and founded New Harmony; quarrels and poor organisation ended it within about two years; Owen left in 1827.
- [ ] A2 End of the New Lanark connection: Owen gave up his interest in New Lanark in 1828–29; the mills continued under others and spun cotton until 1968.
- [ ] A3 Owenite communities in Britain also failed (Orbiston in Scotland, Queenwood in Hampshire): one bullet.
- [ ] A4 The National Equitable Labour Exchange (London, 1832): goods exchanged for labour notes based on hours of work; it collapsed in 1834.
- [ ] A5 Owen and the Grand National Consolidated Trades Union (1834): Owen was its inspiration and leading figure; it aimed at one great union of all workers and a co-operative society, and collapsed in 1834 after employers' lock-outs and the Tolpuddle case. One or two bullets; trade union history belongs to the notes on responses to industrial society.
- [ ] A6 The co-operative movement: Owen's ideas inspired co-operative societies; the Rochdale Pioneers' store (1844) began the lasting consumer co-operative movement.
- [ ] A7 Socialism: Owen's followers (Owenites) were among the first to be called "socialists" in Britain; Marx and Engels later called him a "utopian" socialist, meaning he hoped to change society by example and persuasion rather than class struggle. Owen died in Newtown in 1858.

Why the model was not widely copied
- [ ] M1 Reasons: other owners thought welfare too costly in a competitive industry and that profits depended on low wages and long hours; laissez-faire beliefs; the success depended on Owen's personal control and unusual partners; urban steam-powered mills did not need to house their workers; Owen's attacks on religion and his socialism discredited his example; his larger schemes (New Harmony, the Labour Exchange) failed.
- [ ] M2 Partial imitation: later employers built model villages such as Saltaire (1850s), but these were paternalist (the owner in charge), not co-operative; infant schools spread.

Reputation then and since
- [ ] D1 Contemporary admirers: thousands of visitors came to see New Lanark, including reformers, churchmen and royalty (the future Tsar Nicholas I).
- [ ] D2 Contemporary critics: employers who thought him a dangerous dreamer; churchmen and his own partners offended by his views on religion; radicals such as Cobbett who saw his villages as a way of controlling the poor.
- [ ] D3 Later views: a pioneer of industrial welfare, infant education, factory reform and co-operation, often called the father of British socialism and of the co-operative movement; but also paternalist, since New Lanark depended on supervision and control (the silent monitor, house inspections) by one man. New Lanark became a UNESCO World Heritage Site in 2001.

Assessment (end with this block, headed "Assessment: …")
- [ ] J1 Achievements vs limits at New Lanark: shorter hours, no young child labour, schools and a decent village combined with profit; but it was one village, depended on Owen, and the partners curbed it.
- [ ] J2 Achievements vs limits of Owen's wider influence: helped bring the 1819 Act, the infant school movement and co-operation; but the Act was weak, and New Harmony, the Labour Exchange and the GNCTU failed.
- [ ] J3 Contemporary vs later views (paired points), and a short comparison with Peel as a reformer: Owen worked by example outside government; Peel changed the law for the whole country (one or two points; the detail of Peel's reforms is in the notes on responses to industrial society).

Key concepts and terms (keyTerms)
- [ ] T1 Socialism, as Owen used it: common ownership and co-operation instead of competition. Utopian socialism.
- [ ] T2 Laissez-faire, as the view Owen challenged.
- [ ] T3 Other terms: model village; pauper apprentice; silent monitor; Institute for the Formation of Character; infant school; village store; co-operation / co-operative movement; villages of co-operation; A New View of Society; Cotton Mills and Factories Act 1819; New Harmony; labour notes / Equitable Labour Exchange; GNCTU; Rochdale Pioneers; paternalism.

## 3. Traps

Do not use (doubtful or wrong in the current notes):
- Do not use "A New View of Society (essays published 1813–1816)", because the essays appeared in 1813–14 (a collected edition followed later). Use W1.
- Do not use "By 1816 he had reduced the working day to ten and three-quarter hours", because sources give either ten and a half or ten and three-quarter hours, and the exact figure appears in a past-paper document. Use the R2 wording.
- Do not use "In the American embargo of 1806", because the year is doubtful (Owen dated it 1806; the American embargo law was passed late in 1807). Give no year (R5).
- Do not use "managing a cotton-spinning mill by the age of about nineteen", because he took the post in the early 1790s at about twenty. Use O2.
- Do not use "The Institute for the Formation of Character ... was a meeting hall and school for adults", because it housed the infant school and children's classes by day as well as adult classes and entertainments in the evening. Use V4.
- Do not use "The Institute and school had no formal religious instruction" as an absolute, because the partners did force religious teaching in during the 1820s; say Owen kept religious teaching out as far as he could, which his partners opposed (P4).
- Do not use "the Grand National Consolidated Trades Union of 1834, which he led", because Owen was its inspiration and best-known figure rather than its formal leader. Use A5.
- Do not use "the village store sold goods at cost price, so that workers did not pay a shopkeeper's profit" (current fact card), because the store did make a small profit, which went towards the schools. Use V2.
- Do not use "In 1816 Owen stopped employing children under ten", because the change was gradual; say "by 1816" (R1).
- Do not use "finally ended his connection with New Lanark in about 1829" alone; give "1828–29" or "by 1829" (A2).
- Do not use "its reforms influenced the development of ... public health policies", because there is no clear line from New Lanark to public health law. Say education, factory reform and co-operation (D3).
- Do not use "Owen rebuilt ... the village housing": he improved and repaired it. Use V1.

Figures and quotations not to use:
- No workforce totals, wage figures, profit totals, purchase prices (for New Lanark or Harmony), the cost of paying idle workers, or an exact visitor count. Use only the figures in section 2.
- Do not copy figures that appear only in past-paper documents (for example a workforce total of 1,800, or a working day cut from eleven and three-quarter to ten and three-quarter hours).
- No quotations except Cobbett's "parallelograms of paupers" and the labels "utopian socialist" and "father of British socialism", the last unattributed. Do not quote Owen's own writings or the past-paper documents (for example the phrase about children not being "annoyed with books").

Confusables:
- David Dale (founder, Owen's father-in-law) vs Richard Arkwright (early partner, inventor of the water frame) vs Owen (manager from 1800, then part-owner).
- The thirteen-hour mill day (including meal breaks) vs hours of actual work; do not mix the two when comparing before and after.
- The 1802 Act (pauper apprentices, the elder Peel) vs the 1819 Act (all children in cotton mills, after Owen's campaign) vs the 1833 Factory Act (inspectors; notes on the changing economy and society) vs the 1844 Factory Act (the younger Peel; notes on responses to industrial society).
- Sir Robert Peel the elder (cotton manufacturer, factory acts of 1802 and 1819) vs his son Robert Peel, the Prime Minister and key personality of the notes on responses to industrial society.
- The Institute for the Formation of Character (the building, 1816) vs the infant school (inside it) vs the day school for older children.
- New Lanark (Scotland, a reformed mill village that worked) vs New Harmony (Indiana, a co-operative community that failed) vs Owen's plans for villages of co-operation (never built as he planned).
- The Labour Exchange (1832) vs the GNCTU (1834) vs the Rochdale Pioneers (1844, not founded by Owen).
- Paternalism (an owner improving workers' lives under his control) vs co-operation (workers owning and running things together). New Lanark was largely paternalist; Owen's later schemes aimed at co-operation.

## 4. What exams ask (topics only)

- Higher 100-mark essay on the main characteristics of New Lanark and why it was not generally adopted: marks go for specific reforms (child labour, hours, silent monitor, store, housing, Institute, infant school) and for several distinct reasons it was not copied (cost and competition, laissez-faire, dependence on Owen, his religious views, failure of his later schemes, rural vs urban mills). Answers need both halves.
- Higher essay on Owen's aims at New Lanark and how far he achieved them: state the aims (prove that environment forms character; prove humane treatment is compatible with profit; spread the model), then judge each against evidence, with achievements and limits paired.
- Higher comparison essays: Owen vs Peel as reformers (the most frequent), Owen with O'Connor and Peel, and Owen with Marx and Bakunin. From this subsection the student needs Owen's methods (example, education, persuasion, co-operation, campaigning outside Parliament), his concrete results (New Lanark, the 1819 Act, infant schools, co-operation), and his failures; the best answers reach a clear verdict on who was more effective and why (Peel changed national law; Owen's example stayed local).
- Ordinary Level 40-mark and 30-mark paragraphs: why Owen set up New Lanark and his aims; conditions he found; his reforms; why New Lanark was different from other mills; whether it was a success. Each SRP is one specific fact, so many short dated facts score best (1800, 1813 partners and the 5 per cent, 1816 Institute and infant school, under-ten rule, 1819 Act, 1825 New Harmony).
- Ordinary Level document questions on New Lanark's schools and workforce: students should recognise Owen's teaching methods (kindness, no punishment, pictures, maps, objects, singing and dancing), why he expected better output from better-treated workers, and the aim of factory reformers (better pay and conditions). Answers should use the document's own evidence plus context from the notes.
- Judgement material (Assessment block): success vs failure of New Lanark; local success vs national influence; contemporary praise vs criticism; pioneer vs paternalist. Marks go for paired points with evidence and a final verdict.
