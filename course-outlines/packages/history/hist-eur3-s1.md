## 1. Must cover (one bullet or block for every item; tick each off)

Background (assumed)
- [ ] A1 (assumed) Liberal Italy before 1922: a constitutional monarchy under King Victor Emmanuel III, with an elected Chamber of Deputies; the King appointed the Prime Minister and commanded the army.
- [ ] A2 (assumed) Italy entered the First World War on the Allied side in 1915 under the secret Treaty of London, which promised it territory from Austria-Hungary.
- [ ] A3 (assumed) The Russian Revolution of 1917 made socialists and communists across Europe look like a real threat to property, the Church and the monarchy.
- [ ] A4 (assumed) The League of Nations (set up by the peace settlement) was meant to settle disputes and could impose sanctions on an aggressor.

Key concepts (each defined as used in this topic; also in keyTerms)
- [ ] K1 Fascism: nationalism, a single party, a leader, violence against opponents, hostility to socialism and liberal democracy.
- [ ] K2 Dictatorship: rule by one leader or party without free elections, an independent parliament or a free press.
- [ ] K3 Personality cult: the leader presented as infallible and heroic through propaganda, ritual and his image (Il Duce).
- [ ] K4 Totalitarianism: the state seeks to control all public and private life through one party, secret police and propaganda; the term's origin with Amendola (1923), then adopted by Mussolini.

Conditions favouring fascism, and its characteristics
- [ ] C1 The war's legacy in Italy: heavy casualties and debt; the peace settlement gave less than the Treaty of London promised; the "mutilated victory" (D'Annunzio's phrase).
- [ ] C2 Weak democracy: five prime ministers 1919–1922 (Orlando, Nitti, Giolitti, Bonomi, Facta); proportional representation from 1919 produced weak coalitions dependent on the Socialists and the Catholic Popolari.
- [ ] C3 Fear of communism: the Biennio Rosso (1919–1920): strikes, peasant land seizures, factory occupations in the north; landowners, industrialists and the middle classes feared revolution.
- [ ] C4 Economic crisis: inflation, unemployment, food shortages; angry ex-servicemen who felt unrewarded.
- [ ] C5 Who supported fascism: ex-servicemen, the lower middle class, Po valley landowners, industrialists.
- [ ] C6 D'Annunzio's seizure of Fiume (September 1919 to December 1920): showed the state's weakness and gave fascism its style (black shirts, balcony speeches, Roman salute, mass ritual).
- [ ] C7 Characteristics of fascism (each a separate bullet): extreme nationalism, nation above the individual; the leader principle and personality cult; one party and a totalitarian state (the 1925 formula, section 2); hostility to communism, socialism and liberal democracy; glorification of violence, war and youth; corporatism instead of class conflict.
- [ ] C8 Comparison only: other inter-war authoritarian regimes (Horthy, Primo de Rivera, Piłsudski, Salazar, Metaxas), mostly conservative military or clerical dictatorships rather than mass fascist movements. One bullet.

The rise to power, 1919–1922
- [ ] R1 Mussolini founded the Fasci di Combattimento, Milan, March 1919, with a mix of radical and nationalist ideas.
- [ ] R2 The squadristi: armed squads, often of ex-servicemen, attacking socialist offices, union halls and Popolari premises, especially in the Po valley and Tuscany; often tolerated by local police and army commanders.
- [ ] R3 May 1921 election: Fascists inside Giolitti's electoral bloc won 35 seats, Mussolini elected; Giolitti underestimated them and thought he could control them.
- [ ] R4 The Pacification Pact (August 1921) with the socialists, ignored by local squads, collapsed; the National Fascist Party (PNF) founded November 1921.
- [ ] R5 By 1922 Fascists controlled many local councils in the north and centre; fascism posed as the defender of order against socialism.
- [ ] R6 The March on Rome (27–30 October 1922): the quadrumvirs, about 30,000 men, Mussolini waiting in Milan; Facta asked for a state of siege.
- [ ] R7 Victor Emmanuel III refused to sign the state of siege decree (fear of civil war, doubts about the army); invited Mussolini on 29 October; Mussolini took office 30 October 1922.
- [ ] R8 Why the March succeeded: it was a show of force and a bluff; power was handed over legally by the King, with the support or acceptance of conservatives, rather than seized.

From coalition to dictatorship, 1922–1929 (the totalitarian state)
- [ ] D1 The first government was a coalition with Fascists in a minority; parliament granted emergency powers for a year (November 1922).
- [ ] D2 The Fascist Grand Council (December 1922) and the Fascist militia (MVSN, January 1923), loyal to Mussolini rather than the King.
- [ ] D3 The Acerbo Law (1923): two-thirds of the seats to the list with the most votes, provided it had at least 25 per cent.
- [ ] D4 The April 1924 election: the Fascist-led national list won about 65 per cent amid intimidation and violence.
- [ ] D5 Matteotti: denounced fraud and violence (30 May 1924); abducted in Rome on 10 June 1924 by men linked to the squads and murdered; body found in August.
- [ ] D6 The Aventine Secession: opposition deputies walked out, hoping the King would dismiss Mussolini; he did not.
- [ ] D7 Mussolini's speech of 3 January 1925 accepting political responsibility for the violence: the start of open dictatorship.
- [ ] D8 The Leggi Fascistissime (1925–26): Mussolini responsible only to the King; rule by decree; opposition parties banned; press censored; elected mayors replaced by appointed podestà; the Special Tribunal for the Defence of the State (1926); confino (internal exile) on islands such as Lipari; the OVRA secret police (late 1920s).
- [ ] D9 Violet Gibson's attack (7 April 1926) used to justify more repression.
- [ ] D10 The 1928 electoral law (a single list of 400 candidates drawn up by the Grand Council) and the 1929 plebiscite (about 98 per cent yes).
- [ ] D11 Limits of totalitarianism in Italy: the King, the Church, the army and big business kept independent power, which is why the King could dismiss Mussolini in 1943.

The corporate state and the economic "battles"
- [ ] E1 The Rocco Law (1926): only state-recognised syndicates; strikes and lockouts banned.
- [ ] E2 The Charter of Labour (1927): principles of the corporate system.
- [ ] E3 Corporations for the main sectors (1934); the Chamber of Fasces and Corporations replaced the Chamber of Deputies (1939).
- [ ] E4 The corporate state existed largely on paper: employers kept control of production; the corporations had little real power.
- [ ] E5 The Battle for Grain (from 1925): self-sufficiency in wheat through tariffs and cereals on poor land; did not make Italy self-sufficient in food.
- [ ] E6 The Battle for Births (from about 1927): rewards for large families, a tax on bachelors.
- [ ] E7 The Battle for the Lira, Quota 90: announced at Pesaro, August 1926, reached 1927; 90 lire to the pound for prestige; exports dearer, deflation, wage cuts.
- [ ] E8 Public works: draining and resettling the Pontine Marshes (1930s); the autostrade motorways; rail electrification.
- [ ] E9 The response to the Depression: IRI (1933) rescued banks and industry, leaving the state owning a large share of banking, shipbuilding and steel.

Propaganda, youth and the cult of the leader
- [ ] P1 Opera Nazionale Balilla (1926), reorganised as the Gioventù Italiana del Littorio (1937).
- [ ] P2 The Dopolavoro (after-work) organisation from 1925: leisure under fascist supervision.
- [ ] P3 Schools and universities: a single state textbook in elementary schools from 1929; the university oath of loyalty (1931), refused by only about a dozen professors.
- [ ] P4 The Istituto Luce (1924): newsreels and documentaries; the Ministry of Popular Culture (Minculpop, 1937) controlling press, radio, cinema and theatre.
- [ ] P5 The cult of Il Duce: posters, photographs (harvesting grain, bare-chested), balcony speeches from the Palazzo Venezia, wall slogans (section 2).
- [ ] P6 The Church and public ritual used for legitimacy, though the Church kept its own youth and press organisations; the Lateran Treaties (1929) named as a success, in one bullet.
- [ ] P7 Propaganda plus terror: consent was won by propaganda and public works while the militia, the Special Tribunal, the OVRA and confino deterred opposition. (Exams pair the two.)

Foreign policy, 1923–1940
- [ ] F1 Corfu (August 1923): General Tellini's boundary commission murdered on the Greek–Albanian border; Italy bombarded and occupied Corfu until Greece paid compensation.
- [ ] F2 Fiume annexed in 1924 by treaty with Yugoslavia.
- [ ] F3 Relations with Hitler before the Axis: troops to the Brenner Pass after the murder of the Austrian Chancellor Dollfuss (July 1934); the Stresa Front with Britain and France (April 1935).
- [ ] F4 Abyssinia: the Walwal incident (December 1934); invasion on 3 October 1935; revenge for the defeat at Adowa (1896); poison gas used; League sanctions that did not include oil; the Hoare–Laval Plan (December 1935) abandoned after protest; Addis Ababa taken 5 May 1936; Victor Emmanuel III declared Emperor of Ethiopia.
- [ ] F5 Drawing closer to Germany: German support over Abyssinia; joint intervention for Franco in Spain (1936–39), one bullet only; the Rome–Berlin Axis announced in Milan on 1 November 1936; the Anti-Comintern Pact (1937); acceptance of the Anschluss (1938).
- [ ] F6 Munich (September 1938), Mussolini as peacemaker; the invasion of Albania (April 1939); the Pact of Steel (22 May 1939); non-belligerence in September 1939.
- [ ] F7 War: declared on Britain and France on 10 June 1940 when France was already beaten; defeats in Greece (from October 1940) and North Africa made Italy dependent on Germany.

The fall of Mussolini and the Salò republic
- [ ] S1 Allied landing in Sicily (10 July 1943); the Grand Council passed Grandi's motion (night of 24–25 July 1943).
- [ ] S2 The King dismissed and arrested Mussolini on 25 July 1943; Badoglio formed a government; the armistice announced 8 September 1943; German forces took over the north and centre.
- [ ] S3 The Gran Sasso rescue (12 September 1943) by German paratroopers and SS commandos including Otto Skorzeny.
- [ ] S4 The Italian Social Republic (Salò, Lake Garda): a German puppet state; Ciano and other Grand Council members executed at Verona (January 1944).
- [ ] S5 Mussolini and Clara Petacci shot by partisans near Lake Como (28 April 1945); bodies displayed in Piazzale Loreto, Milan.

Key personality: Benito Mussolini (one block, NOTES_STANDARD order)
- [ ] M1 Who he was: born 1883 at Predappio (Romagna); trained as a teacher; socialist journalist; editor of Avanti! from 1912.
- [ ] M2 Expelled from the Socialist Party (1914) for backing intervention in the war; served and was wounded (1917).
- [ ] M3 What he did: founded the Fasci (1919) and the PNF (1921); Prime Minister 1922; dictator from 1925; deposed 1943.
- [ ] M4 Participant who shaped events: organised the March on Rome from Milan; the January 1925 speech; the decisions on Abyssinia, the Axis and entry into the war.
- [ ] M5 How contemporaries saw him: admired by conservatives, the Vatican and foreign statesmen (Churchill in Rome, 1927) as the defeater of socialism; denounced by Socialists, Popolari and liberals such as Amendola (died 1926 after a Fascist beating).
- [ ] M6 How historians judge him: De Felice (real consent, 1929–1936) vs Mack Smith (opportunist and propagandist); fascism as an incomplete totalitarianism.

Assessment (final block, paired points)
- [ ] J1 Achievements vs limits: one-party state by 1926 vs the corporate state on paper; Lateran Treaties and public works vs the Battle for Grain and Quota 90; territory in Abyssinia and Fiume vs a costly war and dependence on Germany; collapse in 1943.
- [ ] J2 Contemporary view (saviour of order against Bolshevism) vs later view (violence, the alliance with Hitler, the 1938 racial laws, defeat).
- [ ] J3 How effective was Mussolini: very effective at winning and keeping power (1922–1926), much less effective at transforming the economy, society or Italy's place in the world.

## 3. Traps

Do not use (wrong or doubtful facts in the current notes):
- Do not use: "any list winning at least 25 per cent" for the Acerbo Law, because the bonus went to the list with the most votes, provided it passed 25 per cent. Use the wording in section 2.
- Do not use: the OVRA as one of the 1925–26 laws or dated 1926, because it was organised in the late 1920s and its exact founding date is unclear. Say "the OVRA secret police" with no exact year.
- Do not use: Violet Gibson as "an Englishwoman", because she was Irish, from a Dublin family. Call her an Irishwoman (a good detail for Irish students).
- Do not use: "about 12 of some 1,200" professors as exact figures, because sources vary slightly. Say "about a dozen of over 1,200".

Other errors and doubtful claims to avoid:
- "Mussolini made the trains run on time" is a propaganda claim, not a fact. If used, call it a myth spread by the regime.
- Mussolini did not march on Rome: he waited in Milan and arrived by train after the King's invitation. The King appointed him legally; it was not a coup in form.
- Don't give an exact death toll or the attackers' names for Matteotti's murder, or a figure for Abyssinian casualties, wheat output or birth rates: none is checked.
- Don't claim the Battle for Births raised the birth rate: it did not succeed (say the population target was not met, with no figures).
- Don't call Mussolini's Italy fully totalitarian without qualification: the King, Church, army and big business kept power.

Confusable names, terms and dates:
- Fasci di Combattimento (March 1919, a movement) vs the National Fascist Party (PNF, November 1921).
- Squadristi (unofficial squads, 1920–22) vs the MVSN militia (state-paid, 1923) vs the OVRA (secret police).
- Fascist Grand Council (party body, 1922) vs the Chamber of Deputies vs the Chamber of Fasces and Corporations (1939).

## 4. What exams ask

- Conditions favouring fascism (Higher, 100 marks, often across Europe): the war and peace settlement, fear of communism, economic crisis and weak democracy, each with an Italian example (mutilated victory, Biennio Rosso, inflation, five prime ministers). Marks go for explaining why each condition helped fascism, not just listing it.
- Characteristics of fascism, often with Italy and Germany as examples: nationalism, leader and cult, one party, totalitarian control, violence and terror, anti-communism, corporatism, propaganda and youth. Each characteristic needs a concrete Italian example (the 1925 formula, the Balilla, the Special Tribunal).
- Why Italy (and Germany) embraced dictatorship: cause-and-result answers linking weak parliamentary government and fear of revolution to the King's and conservatives' decision to hand power to Mussolini.
- How Mussolini came to power and built a dictatorship: a chronological answer from 1919 to 1926 (Fasci, squads, 1921 election, March on Rome, Acerbo Law, Matteotti, 3 January 1925, Leggi Fascistissime). Ordinary Level short answers ask about the Fasci, the squads, the King's refusal and the March on Rome.
- How effective, successful or "disastrous" Mussolini's leadership was: judgement questions. Top answers pair achievements with limits (one-party state and public works vs the paper corporate state, Battle for Grain, Quota 90, the war and 1943 collapse) and reach a clear verdict; a useful line is "effective at gaining power, ineffective at transforming Italy".
- Mussolini's domestic policies (corporate state, the battles, youth, propaganda): give the policy, its aim, and its result.
- Propaganda and terror, comparing Mussolini with Hitler or Stalin: examiners reward showing how the two worked together (propaganda won consent; terror removed opponents), with named Italian bodies on each side (Istituto Luce, Minculpop, Balilla; militia, Special Tribunal, OVRA, confino).
- Foreign policy and the drift to war: Corfu, Abyssinia and the League's failure, the Axis, the Pact of Steel and entry into war in 1940, with the result (dependence on Germany, defeat).
- Source-based questions (Ordinary Level) on a foreign visitor's account of Mussolini's Italy: picking out facts from the source (Palazzo Venezia, police presence, praise for Italy's energy) and linking them to one-party dictatorship.
- Ordinary Level 40-mark paragraphs on Mussolini's success or failure: about six developed points, each with a date or name.
