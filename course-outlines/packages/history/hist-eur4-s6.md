## 1. Must cover (one bullet or block for every item; tick each off)

Background (assumed)
- [ ] A1 (assumed) The Second World War ended in 1945 with much of Europe in ruins; the USA emerged as the leading economy and the Cold War began to divide Europe (1947 onwards).
- [ ] A2 (assumed) The inter-war background: the Great Depression, protectionism and unemployment, which post-war planners wanted to avoid.
- [ ] A3 (assumed) Capitalism (private ownership, markets, profit) versus communism; Western Europe stayed capitalist, but with a large state role.

Key concepts (each defined as used in this topic; also in keyTerms)
- [ ] K1 Welfare state: the state provides health care, pensions, unemployment benefit and education, funded by taxation and insurance.
- [ ] K2 Capitalism: private ownership and competitive markets, here a "mixed economy" with state planning, nationalisation and welfare.
- [ ] K3 Common market: free movement of goods, services, capital and workers inside, common external tariff.
- [ ] K4 Other essential terms: Marshall Aid, Bretton Woods system, GATT, guest worker, social market economy, Wirtschaftswunder, trente glorieuses, OEEC.

Post-war devastation and the need for recovery
- [ ] D1 War damage to cities, industry, transport; food and fuel shortages; rationing.
- [ ] D2 The harsh winter of 1946–47 and coal shortages.
- [ ] D3 The "dollar gap": Europe needed American goods but had no dollars; Britain's dependence (the 1945 loan, 1949 devaluation).
- [ ] D4 Refugees and expellees in West Germany: a burden at first, then a source of labour.
- [ ] D5 The Cold War link: fear that poverty would help communist parties (France, Italy).

Marshall Aid and the OEEC
- [ ] M1 Marshall's offer (Harvard, June 1947), the Soviet refusal, 16 states accepting.
- [ ] M2 The European Recovery Programme, 1948–52: about 13 billion dollars, mostly grants.
- [ ] M3 The OEEC (April 1948) coordinated aid; later the OECD (1961).
- [ ] M4 How aid was used (raw materials, food, machinery; counterpart funds) and the biggest recipients.
- [ ] M5 Conditions: cooperation, trade liberalisation, financial stability; the European Payments Union (1950).
- [ ] M6 Assessment of its impact: dollars and confidence vs a modest share of investment (Milward); political purpose (containment).

Bretton Woods, GATT and free trade
- [ ] B1 Bretton Woods (1944): IMF, World Bank, fixed rates tied to the dollar, dollar convertible to gold at 35 dollars an ounce.
- [ ] B2 GATT (1947, in force 1948) and its tariff-cutting rounds, including the Kennedy Round.
- [ ] B3 Currency convertibility restored in Western Europe, end of 1958.
- [ ] B4 Benelux customs union (agreed 1944, operating 1948).
- [ ] B5 ECSC (1952), EEC (1958) and EFTA (1960): what each did for trade (unity politics belongs to the neighbouring subsection).
- [ ] B6 The EEC customs union completed in July 1968; the Common Agricultural Policy; EFTA's industrial free trade without farm produce.

Growth in West Germany, France and Italy
- [ ] G1 West Germany: the June 1948 currency reform and the end of price controls; Erhard; the social market economy; the Wirtschaftswunder; growth and low unemployment.
- [ ] G2 France: Monnet Plan, Commissariat général du Plan, state planning and nationalised industries; the trente glorieuses.
- [ ] G3 Italy: the miracle of the late 1950s and early 1960s; the north-south divide and internal migration.
- [ ] G4 Britain, briefly, as the slower grower (also in the factors block).
- [ ] G5 Common features: reconstruction, catch-up with American methods, cheap oil, skilled and mobile labour, rising exports and domestic demand.

Labour shortages and immigration
- [ ] I1 Why labour was short (war losses, growth, rising education and living standards).
- [ ] I2 West Germany: Gastarbeiter agreements, the Berlin Wall of 1961 ending the flow from East Germany, the 1973 halt.
- [ ] I3 France: colonial and ex-colonial migrants (Algeria, Morocco, Tunisia) plus Portuguese, Spanish and Italian workers; the 1962 Algerian settlers.
- [ ] I4 Britain: Windrush (1948), the British Nationality Act (1948), Commonwealth migrants, the 1962 and 1968 restrictions, the 1965 Race Relations Act.
- [ ] I5 Consequences: economic benefit, discrimination, housing pressure, settlement of families, multicultural societies.

The welfare state
- [ ] W1 Beveridge Report (1942): five giant evils; the principle of national insurance.
- [ ] W2 Britain under Attlee, 1945–51: National Insurance Act, the NHS (5 July 1948), nationalisation, the Education Act of 1944.
- [ ] W3 France: the 1945 social security system (health, family allowances, pensions).
- [ ] W4 West Germany: the social-insurance tradition within the social market economy.
- [ ] W5 Scandinavia, especially Sweden: universal systems.
- [ ] W6 Why welfare was accepted across party lines (the post-war consensus) and how it grew in the 1950s and 1960s.

Factors that helped and hindered growth
- [ ] F1 Helped: Marshall Aid, investment, labour supply, cheap oil, American technology and management methods, freer trade, Keynesian demand management and full-employment policies, political stability, the Korean War boom.
- [ ] F2 Hindered: war damage and dollar shortage, Cold War defence spending, Britain's weak productivity, strikes and stop-go policies, inflation and dollar strains in the late 1960s.

Assessment (final block, paired points)
- [ ] S1 Achievements: unprecedented growth, rising living standards, full employment, welfare provision.
- [ ] S2 Limits: dependence on cheap oil and the dollar system, inflation, uneven growth (Britain, southern Italy), the position of migrants.
- [ ] S3 The Bretton Woods system broke down: 15 August 1971; floating currencies by 1973; the end of the boom belongs to the Oil Crisis notes.
- [ ] S4 Historians: how far American aid caused recovery; catching-up explanation; contemporary vs later views.

## 3. Traps

Do not use (wrong or doubtful facts in the current notes):
- Do not use: "About 12 million German refugees and expellees arrived in West Germany", because about 12 million is the figure for both German states and the wider flight; say "several million" arrived in West Germany.
- Do not use: the German currency reform or price decontrol as "carried out by Ludwig Erhard" in one phrase, because the currency reform was an Allied measure; Erhard abolished most price controls days later.
- Do not use: "four-year plans" for the French Commissariat, because the first plan ran about 1947–53 and later plans had different lengths; say "successive national plans".
- Do not use: "over 800,000 settlers (pieds-noirs)", because estimates vary widely; say several hundred thousand.
- Do not use: the Netherlands receiving migrants from Surinam as a 1945–73 example, because most Surinamese came around 1975; keep to Indonesia (Dutch-Indonesians from 1945 onwards).
- Do not use: "from the cradle to the grave" as a Beveridge phrase; it was not in the report.
- Do not use: "Marshall Aid supported Christian Democratic and social democratic parties against the communists" as a claim of direct party funding; say aid was meant to strengthen stability and weaken communism's appeal.
- Do not use: the Common Agricultural Policy as dated 1968; it began in 1962, with the 1968 date relating to the removal of industrial tariffs and the common farm prices.

## 4. What exams ask

- Higher essay, the commonest form: what helped and hindered economic growth in Western Europe, 1945–1973. Marks go to grouped factors (state action, foreign aid and trade, labour and energy) with dated examples and a judgement about which mattered most; a list without explanation scores poorly.
- Marshall Aid and free trade: how aid worked (grants, OEEC, counterpart funds), its limits, and how trade liberalisation (GATT, EPU, ECSC, EEC, EFTA) built on it; an answer should compare the two.
- Assessment questions on the strengths and weaknesses of the Western economies: strengths (growth, employment, welfare) with weaknesses (inflation, dependence on oil, regional gaps, discrimination against migrants).
- Link questions with the Oil Crisis: the boom of 1945–73 as the context; show what the crisis ended. A single paragraph on the 1971 collapse of Bretton Woods earns credit.
- Ordinary Level short answers (30 marks): Marshall Aid in three or four dated statements (speech, 1948–52, about 13 billion dollars, grants); the welfare state (what it provides, Beveridge, NHS 1948, one other country); guest workers (who, where, why, when it stopped). Each correct, relevant and specific statement earns a mark.
- Immigration questions: explain why labour was short, name the schemes and countries, and give both benefits and tensions; naming a specific law or date is credited.
- Welfare questions: contrast Britain, France, Germany and Scandinavia; mention the cross-party consensus.
