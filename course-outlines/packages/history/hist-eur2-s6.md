## 1. Must cover (one bullet or block for every item; tick each off)

Background (assumed)
- [ ] X1 (assumed) The two sides: the Central Powers (Germany, Austria-Hungary, the Ottoman Empire, Bulgaria) against the Allies (Britain, France, Russia to 1917, Italy from 1915, the USA from 1917). One clause.
- [ ] X2 (assumed) Germany's position in 1918: Russia out of the war (Brest-Litovsk, March 1918); the failure of the German spring offensive and the Allied counter-attacks from August 1918; American troops arriving; the home front exhausted by the British blockade and food shortages. One bullet; the fighting is in the notes on the conduct of war.
- [ ] X3 (assumed) The Kaiserreich's political system in outline: the Chancellor answered to the Kaiser, not the Reichstag; by 1916–18 the army leaders Hindenburg and Ludendorff dominated government. One bullet.
- [ ] X4 (assumed) The split of German socialism: the Majority SPD (Ebert, Scheidemann), which had backed the war, the Independent Social Democrats (USPD, split off in 1917) and the Spartacists on the far left (Luxemburg, Liebknecht). One bullet; Luxemburg's career is in the notes on the Second International.
- [ ] X5 (assumed) Austria-Hungary as a multinational empire whose peoples (Czechs, Slovaks, Poles, South Slavs, Romanians and others) increasingly wanted independence by 1918. One clause.

The collapse of the Central Powers
- [ ] C1 Bulgaria's armistice (end of September 1918) and the Ottoman armistice of Mudros (30 October 1918).
- [ ] C2 Ludendorff's demand for an armistice (29 September 1918) after Allied attacks broke into the Hindenburg Line and Bulgaria gave up.
- [ ] C3 Prince Max of Baden as Chancellor (3 October 1918) with SPD and Centre ministers; Germany's request to Wilson for an armistice on the basis of the Fourteen Points.
- [ ] C4 Wilson's replies demanding an end to military autocracy; the October reforms making the Chancellor responsible to the Reichstag; Ludendorff's resignation (26 October).
- [ ] C5 Austria-Hungary's defeat by Italy at Vittorio Veneto and its armistice (Villa Giusti, 3 November 1918).

The German revolution, November 1918
- [ ] R1 The naval order for a last battle; sailors' refusal at Wilhelmshaven; the Kiel mutiny from 3 November spreading to ports and cities.
- [ ] R2 Workers' and soldiers' councils on the Russian model; Eisner's Bavarian republic (7 November).
- [ ] R3 9 November: general strike and crowds in Berlin; Prince Max announced the Kaiser's abdication without his consent and handed over to Ebert; Scheidemann proclaimed a republic from the Reichstag; Liebknecht proclaimed a socialist republic from the palace.
- [ ] R4 Wilhelm II's flight to the Netherlands (10 November); his formal abdication later in November.
- [ ] R5 The armistice (11 November 1918, Compiègne, Erzberger): its terms (evacuation, surrender of weapons and ships, Allied occupation west of the Rhine, Brest-Litovsk cancelled, blockade kept on).
- [ ] R6 The Council of People's Representatives (three Majority SPD, three USPD); the USPD members left at the end of December 1918.
- [ ] R7 The Ebert–Groener pact (10 November): the army backs the government against the far left; the government respects officers' authority. Significance: the old army, civil service and judiciary survived.
- [ ] R8 The Stinnes–Legien agreement (November 1918): unions recognised, eight-hour day.
- [ ] R9 The choice: a council republic on the Russian model (the far left) or an elected parliament (the SPD leaders).

The Spartacist rising and the Weimar republic
- [ ] S1 The KPD founded (30 December 1918 – 1 January 1919) by the Spartacists and others.
- [ ] S2 The Spartacist (January) rising, 5–12 January 1919: the trigger (dismissal of Eichhorn, the USPD Berlin police chief); newspaper buildings occupied; crushed by troops and Freikorps under Noske.
- [ ] S3 The murder of Luxemburg and Liebknecht (15 January 1919) by Freikorps officers. Two clauses at most; her life is in the notes on the Second International.
- [ ] S4 Further left-wing risings crushed: the Bavarian Soviet republic (April–May 1919), after Eisner's murder (February 1919).
- [ ] S5 The National Assembly election (19 January 1919): first national vote with women voting; the SPD largest party; the Weimar coalition (SPD, Centre, DDP).
- [ ] S6 The Assembly met at Weimar (February 1919), away from Berlin's unrest; Ebert elected President, Scheidemann head of government.
- [ ] S7 The Weimar constitution (1919) in one bullet: president elected for seven years, proportional representation, emergency powers under Article 48, votes for men and women over 20. Detail belongs to a later topic.
- [ ] S8 The "stab in the back" myth and its falsehood (the army leaders themselves had asked for an armistice); the "November criminals".
- [ ] S9 The Kapp Putsch (March 1920) as a sign of the right's hostility; defeated by a general strike. One bullet.

Austria-Hungary's break-up
- [ ] A1 Emperor Charles's manifesto offering a federal empire (16 October 1918), too late.
- [ ] A2 The new states declared: Czechoslovakia (28 October 1918); the South Slav state, then the Kingdom of Serbs, Croats and Slovenes (1 December 1918); Poland; Hungary separate.
- [ ] A3 Charles withdrew from state affairs (11 November 1918); German-Austria a republic (12 November), its assembly voting to join Germany.
- [ ] A4 Hungary's turmoil: Béla Kun's Soviet republic (March–August 1919), overthrown with Romanian intervention. One bullet.

Wilson's Fourteen Points
- [ ] F1 Announced to Congress, 8 January 1918, as US war aims and a basis for peace; partly a reply to the Bolsheviks' publication of the secret treaties and call for peace.
- [ ] F2 Points 1–5: open covenants openly arrived at; freedom of the seas; removal of economic barriers (free trade); reduction of armaments; impartial adjustment of colonial claims, with the interests of the peoples counting.
- [ ] F3 Points 6–8: evacuation of Russia and Belgium; Alsace-Lorraine to France. Points 9–13: Italy's frontiers on lines of nationality; autonomous development for the peoples of Austria-Hungary; the Balkans; the Ottoman Empire (Turkish parts sovereign, others autonomous, the Dardanelles open); an independent Poland with access to the sea.
- [ ] F4 Point 14: a general association of nations guaranteeing independence and territorial integrity to great and small states alike, which became the League.
- [ ] F5 The pre-armistice agreement (November 1918): the Allies accepted the Points as the basis, with Britain's reservation on freedom of the seas and compensation for civilian damage.
- [ ] F6 German belief that Versailles broke the pre-armistice agreement.

The Paris Peace Conference, 1919
- [ ] P1 Opened 18 January 1919 in Paris; about thirty states; the defeated powers excluded; Soviet Russia not invited.
- [ ] P2 The Council of Ten, then the Council of Four (from March) took the main decisions; terms drafted by commissions.
- [ ] P3 Wilson's aims: a just peace on self-determination, the League built into the treaty; first US president to go to Europe in office.
- [ ] P4 Clemenceau ("the Tiger"): security for France, invaded in 1870 and 1914; a weak Germany, a Rhineland buffer, heavy reparations.
- [ ] P5 Lloyd George: the December 1918 election and "make Germany pay" pressure vs his wish for a peace that would not provoke revenge (the Fontainebleau memorandum, March 1919); protect British trade, empire and naval power; avoid French dominance.
- [ ] P6 Orlando: the secret Treaty of London (1915) promises; the Fiume dispute; his walk-out (April 1919); the "mutilated victory".
- [ ] P7 Japan: the rejected racial equality clause; gained German rights in Shandong.
- [ ] P8 Ireland: the Dáil's envoy Seán T. O'Kelly sought a hearing for Irish self-determination and was refused.
- [ ] P9 The terms handed to the German delegation (7 May 1919) with no negotiation; German counter-proposals mostly rejected; the threat of renewed war.

The Treaty of Versailles (28 June 1919)
- [ ] V1 Signed in the Hall of Mirrors, five years to the day after Sarajevo (and where the German Empire had been proclaimed in 1871).
- [ ] V2 Article 231 (the war guilt clause): what it said, and that it was the legal basis for reparations.
- [ ] V3 Territory in the west and north: Alsace-Lorraine to France; Eupen-Malmedy to Belgium; North Schleswig to Denmark after a plebiscite.
- [ ] V4 Territory in the east: most of West Prussia and Posen to Poland; the Polish Corridor cutting off East Prussia; Danzig a Free City under the League; Memel under Allied control; plebiscites in Upper Silesia and East Prussian districts.
- [ ] V5 The Saar: League rule for fifteen years, its coal mines to France, a plebiscite due in 1935.
- [ ] V6 Losses overall: about 13 per cent of Germany's European territory and about a tenth of its population.
- [ ] V7 Colonies: all taken and made League mandates under Britain, France, Japan and others.
- [ ] V8 Disarmament: army of 100,000 volunteers, no conscription, no tanks, air force or submarines, a small navy; the Rhineland demilitarised and the left bank occupied by the Allies for up to fifteen years.
- [ ] V9 Anschluss (union with Austria) forbidden.
- [ ] V10 Reparations: no total in the treaty; an interim payment demanded; the Reparations Commission's total of 132 billion gold marks (1921) and the bond schedule, of which about 50 billion was expected in practice.
- [ ] V11 Article 227 (trial of the Kaiser; the Dutch refused to hand him over); the League Covenant as part of the treaty.
- [ ] V12 German reaction: the "Diktat"; Scheidemann's government resigned rather than sign; the Assembly accepted under the threat of invasion; signed by Müller and Bell.

The other treaties
- [ ] T1 St Germain with Austria (10 September 1919): the new states recognised; army of 30,000; union with Germany forbidden; South Tyrol to Italy; the Sudeten German areas to Czechoslovakia.
- [ ] T2 Trianon with Hungary (4 June 1920): lost about two-thirds of its territory (Transylvania to Romania, Slovakia to Czechoslovakia, Croatia to Yugoslavia, Burgenland to Austria); over three million Hungarians left outside; lasting bitterness.
- [ ] T3 Neuilly with Bulgaria (27 November 1919): Western Thrace to Greece, losing its Aegean coast; reparations; army of 20,000.
- [ ] T4 Sèvres with the Ottoman Empire (10 August 1920): the empire divided; Armenia and a Kurdish area promised; Mustafa Kemal's nationalists rejected it, defeated Greece, and the Treaty of Lausanne (1923) replaced it.
- [ ] T5 Middle East mandates: Britain (Palestine including Transjordan, Iraq); France (Syria and Lebanon).

Self-determination applied and denied
- [ ] D1 Applied: Poland, Czechoslovakia and the Kingdom of Serbs, Croats and Slovenes; Finland, Estonia, Latvia and Lithuania, independent from Russia.
- [ ] D2 Plebiscites: Schleswig (1920), Allenstein and Marienwerder (1920), Carinthia (1920), Upper Silesia (1921, partitioned).
- [ ] D3 Denied to Germans: union of Germany and Austria forbidden though Austria had voted for it; about three million Sudeten Germans in Czechoslovakia; Germans in the Corridor and Danzig.
- [ ] D4 Denied elsewhere: German-speaking South Tyrol to Italy for a strategic frontier; Hungarians left in Romania, Czechoslovakia and Yugoslavia.
- [ ] D5 The new states were multinational; the minority treaties under League protection.
- [ ] D6 Not applied to the colonies (mandates), nor to Ireland, Egypt or India.
- [ ] D7 Why it could not be applied fully: mixed populations, strategic and economic needs (ports, railways, defensible frontiers), the victors' interests and wartime promises.

The League of Nations and the US Senate
- [ ] L1 Founded 10 January 1920, seat in Geneva; the Assembly, the Council, the Secretariat; the Permanent Court of International Justice and the International Labour Organisation.
- [ ] L2 Collective security: members to respect each other's independence (Article 10), settle disputes by arbitration, and apply economic sanctions and possibly force against an aggressor (Article 16).
- [ ] L3 Germany and Soviet Russia excluded at first (Germany joined 1926, the USSR 1934).
- [ ] L4 The Senate fight: the Republican majority led by Lodge; reservations, above all on Article 10; Wilson's refusal to compromise; his speaking tour and stroke; the treaty rejected (November 1919, March 1920).
- [ ] L5 Results: the USA never joined; a separate peace with Germany (1921); France and Britain left to enforce the settlement; the Anglo-American guarantee to France lapsed.

Problems Europe faced after the war
- [ ] E1 Human cost: about 9–10 million soldiers dead, millions wounded and disabled, widows and orphans; the influenza pandemic of 1918–19.
- [ ] E2 Economic dislocation: war debts, inflation, damaged regions in France and Belgium, broken trade links between the successor states, the reparations dispute.
- [ ] E3 Political instability: fear of Bolshevism; risings in Germany and Hungary; fragile new democracies; the Kapp Putsch.
- [ ] E4 Disputed borders and wars: the Polish–Soviet war (1919–21), the Russian Civil War, the Greco-Turkish war, d'Annunzio's seizure of Fiume (1919).
- [ ] E5 Refugees and minorities.
- [ ] E6 Revisionist states (Germany, Hungary, Bulgaria, and Italy as a disappointed victor) vs status quo states (France and the new states).

Key personality: Woodrow Wilson (one block)
- [ ] W1 Who he was: born 1856, Staunton, Virginia; a historian and political scientist; president of Princeton University; Governor of New Jersey; Democrat, elected President in 1912, President 1913–21.
- [ ] W2 Neutrality from 1914; the Lusitania (1915); "peace without victory" (January 1917); re-elected in 1916 as the man who "kept us out of war".
- [ ] W3 Entry into the war: unrestricted submarine warfare resumed and the Zimmermann Telegram; his war message (2 April 1917), "the world must be made safe for democracy"; war declared 6 April; the USA as an "associated power".
- [ ] W4 The Fourteen Points (January 1918) and his use of "self-determination" (February 1918); his part in the armistice exchanges (October–November 1918), pressing for the Kaiser's removal.
- [ ] W5 At Paris: the welcome in Europe (December 1918); chaired the commission that wrote the League Covenant and insisted it be part of the treaty.
- [ ] W6 His compromises: reparations and Article 231, the Saar, the Rhineland occupation, Shandong, South Tyrol, in return for the League and the mandate system.
- [ ] W7 Defeat at home: the Republican Congress elected in November 1918; his refusal to accept Lodge's reservations; the tour and stroke (October 1919); his wife Edith controlling access; rejection by the Senate; the Nobel Peace Prize (1919); died 1924.
- [ ] W8 Participant (decisive in the armistice and treaty-making) rather than witness.
- [ ] W9 Contemporary views: hailed in Europe as a prophet of a new order; seen by Clemenceau and others as an unworldly idealist; Keynes portrayed him as outmanoeuvred; German disillusion; many Americans felt he had entangled the USA in Europe; nationalists in Ireland, Egypt and elsewhere disappointed.
- [ ] W10 Historians' views: founder of liberal internationalism whose League foreshadowed the United Nations vs self-righteous, inconsistent on self-determination and politically inept with the Senate.

Assessment (end with this block, headed "Assessment: …")
- [ ] J1 Achievements of the settlement: new national states where empires had ruled; the League as a forum (the Aaland Islands, 1921); disarmament of Germany; Germany kept as a united state.
- [ ] J2 Limits: large minorities; revisionist resentment; enforcement left to Britain and France, who disagreed; the USA and Russia outside the system.
- [ ] J3 "Too harsh to be accepted, too soft to keep Germany down": the core judgement as a paired point.
- [ ] J4 Contemporary views: Keynes (reparations unrealistic and ruinous); Foch's "armistice for twenty years"; the German "Diktat" and war-guilt grievance.
- [ ] J5 Later views: terms milder than Brest-Litovsk and Germany's grievance exaggerated; MacMillan's view that later failures, not the treaty itself, led to the Second World War.
- [ ] J6 The German revolution: achievements (a democratic republic, women's vote, the eight-hour day) vs limits (old elites kept, the left split and embittered by the Freikorps killings, the republic tied to defeat and Versailles).

- [ ] K3 Other terms: armistice; Fourteen Points; Council of People's Representatives; Ebert–Groener pact; Spartacists; KPD; Freikorps; National Assembly; Weimar republic; stab in the back; reparations; Diktat; Polish Corridor; Free City; mandate; plebiscite; demilitarisation; Anschluss; League of Nations; Covenant; collective security; sanctions; successor states; minority treaties; revisionism (treaty revision).

## 3. Traps

Do not use (wrong or doubtful in the current notes):
- Do not use "the Weimar constitution was adopted in August 1919", because the Assembly passed it on 31 July and Ebert signed it on 11 August. Use the S7 wording.
- Do not use "the defence minister Gustav Noske" for January 1919, because he became Reichswehr minister only in February 1919. Call him the SPD member of the government in charge of military affairs.
- Do not use "Upper Silesia was split after a rising and a League decision in 1922", because the League's partition decision came in October 1921 (the transfer was carried out in 1922). Use D2.
- Do not use "Trianon cut Hungary to about a third of its former territory", because it kept under a third. Say "lost about two-thirds of its territory".
- Do not use "Poland and Czechoslovakia contained large German, Ukrainian, Slovak and other minorities", because the Slovaks were one of Czechoslovakia's two founding peoples, not a minority. Use D5.
- Do not use "Orlando left when Fiume was refused" as if Fiume had been promised in 1915, because the Treaty of London did not promise it. Say Italy claimed Fiume in addition to the 1915 promises.
- Do not use "Britain received Palestine, Iraq and Transjordan" as three separate 1920 mandates; say "Palestine (including Transjordan) and Iraq".
- Do not use "the defeated powers were excluded from the League" as a permanent rule, because Austria and Bulgaria were admitted in 1920. Say Germany and Soviet Russia were excluded at first.
- Do not use "the Allied blockade continued until the peace was signed"; it was lifted in July 1919, after Germany ratified. Say the blockade continued after the armistice until July 1919.
- Do not quote Clemenceau mocking Wilson (the "fourteen commandments" jibe is not well attested). Say only that he was sceptical of Wilson's idealism.
- Do not say the Fourteen Points used the word "self-determination"; the phrase came in Wilson's speeches of 1918 (W4). The Points proposed frontiers "along lines of nationality" and "autonomous development".
- Do not say Article 231 used the word "guilt" (V2).
- Do not give the reparations figure as 132 billion "set at Versailles"; it was fixed in 1921.
- Do not give seat totals for the 1919 election, or a figure for the Assembly's women members; use "about 38 per cent" only.
- Do not quote Lloyd George's campaigners ("squeeze until the pips squeak") or any other line not in section 2.
- Do not give exact casualty totals for the war or the influenza; use the E1 wording.

Confusables:
- The armistice of 11 November 1918 (end of fighting) vs the Treaty of Versailles of 28 June 1919 (the peace).
- Scheidemann's republic (parliamentary, from the Reichstag) vs Liebknecht's "free socialist republic" (from the palace), both on 9 November 1918.
- Majority SPD (Ebert, Scheidemann, Noske) vs USPD (Eisner, Eichhorn) vs Spartacists/KPD (Luxemburg, Liebknecht).
- The Council of People's Representatives (the provisional government, November 1918 – February 1919) vs the workers' and soldiers' councils (local bodies).
- Kurt Eisner's Bavarian republic (November 1918) vs the Bavarian Soviet republic (April 1919).
- The Treaty of London of 1915 (secret promises to Italy) vs the Treaty of London of 1839 (Belgium) in the notes on the outbreak of war.
- Council of Ten vs Council of Four (the Big Four).
- St Germain (Austria) vs Trianon (Hungary) vs Neuilly (Bulgaria) vs Sèvres (Ottoman Empire) vs Lausanne (Turkey, 1923). Versailles is with Germany only.
- Danzig (Free City, League) vs Memel (Allied control) vs the Saar (League rule, French mines).
- Article 231 (war guilt) vs Article 227 (trial of the Kaiser) vs Covenant Article 10 (guarantee) vs Article 16 (sanctions) vs Weimar Article 48 (emergency powers).
- The Hall of Mirrors: German Empire proclaimed (18 January 1871); treaty signed (28 June 1919). The conference opened on 18 January 1919, the anniversary.
- Mandate (League territory) vs plebiscite (popular vote on a border).
- Prince Max of Baden (Chancellor October–November 1918) vs Ebert (from 9 November).

## 4. What exams ask (topics only)

- Woodrow Wilson (Ordinary 30/40 marks, very frequent; Higher as one of two or three personalities): marks go to an accurate sequence with dates (neutrality, the 1916 re-election, entry in April 1917 and its two causes, the Fourteen Points and what Point 14 proposed, the armistice exchanges, Paris and the Covenant, compromises, the Senate's rejection, the stroke). Higher answers must judge his impact: decisive in ending the war and shaping the treaty and the League vs failure at home and inconsistency on self-determination.
- Wilson's role in European affairs (Higher, 100 marks): examiners want the change in his role from neutral mediator to war leader to peacemaker, with a reasoned conclusion on how far his ideals were realised in the treaties.
- The Paris Peace Conference and Versailles (Ordinary 30 marks): the Big Four and each one's aim, the exclusion of the defeated, and the treaty's terms grouped as territory, colonies, military, reparations and Article 231. Explaining why Germans called it a Diktat earns more than listing terms.
- "My study of World War I" (Higher, with Haig, women or Wilson): a paragraph on how the war ended and what the settlement did, with the self-determination and war-guilt concepts used correctly.
- Problems facing Europe after the war (Higher, 100 marks): group them as human, economic, political and territorial, and link each to the war or the treaties (minorities and revisionism, reparations and inflation, fear of Bolshevism and the risings, the USA's absence from the League). A conclusion on which problem was most serious is rewarded.
- War and revolution in Germany: cause-and-result chains score: defeat, then the request for an armistice, then Wilson's demands, then the mutiny and the councils, then the abdication and the republic; the Ebert–Groener pact explained as both the republic's survival and its weakness.
- The Spartacist rising (often through Luxemburg questions): its trigger, the KPD's founding, how it was crushed, the murders and their lasting effect on the left.
- Self-determination: know cases where it was applied (the new states, plebiscites) and denied (Anschluss, Sudetenland, South Tyrol, Hungarians, colonies, Ireland), and explain why it could not be applied fully.
- Judgement on the settlement: paired points (too harsh vs too lenient; achievements vs limits of the League; contemporary Keynes and Foch vs later historians) with a stated conclusion.
