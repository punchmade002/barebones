## 1. Must cover (one bullet or block for every item; tick each off)

Background (assumed)
- [ ] A1 (assumed) The Troubles began in 1968–69 and the army was deployed in August 1969; Direct Rule came in March 1972 (detail in other notes).
- [ ] A2 (assumed) The IRA split in December 1969 into the Provisional and Official IRA; the Provisionals rejected the Officials' policy and took up armed defence of Catholic areas and then a campaign against British rule.
- [ ] A3 (assumed) Northern Ireland's two communities: unionist/loyalist and mostly Protestant; nationalist/republican and mostly Catholic. Loyalists are unionists who accept violence; republicans seek a united Ireland.

Key concepts (defined as used here; also in keyTerms)
- [ ] K1 Terrorism: violence against civilians and the state for political aims, used by both republican and loyalist groups.
- [ ] K2 Sectarianism: hostility and violence between Protestants and Catholics based on religious identity and political allegiance.
- [ ] K3 Propaganda: murals, publications, broadcasts and statements used to win opinion, and the broadcast bans that tried to deny it.

Republican paramilitaries
- [ ] R1 The Provisional IRA: formed December 1969, aims (end British rule, united Ireland) and targets (army, RUC, UDR, civilians); methods (bombs, shootings, car bombs).
- [ ] R2 Bloody Friday (21 July 1972) in Belfast.
- [ ] R3 Bombings in Britain: Guildford and Birmingham (1974) and the wrongful convictions overturned; Hyde Park and Regent's Park (1982); Harrods (1983); Brighton (1984); Warrington (1993).
- [ ] R4 Mountbatten and Warrenpoint (27 August 1979).
- [ ] R5 Enniskillen (8 November 1987).
- [ ] R6 Attacks on Protestants: La Mon (1978), Kingsmill (1976), Teebane (1992).
- [ ] R7 The Shankill bomb (23 October 1993).
- [ ] R8 The Irish National Liberation Army and the Ballykelly bombing (1982).
- [ ] R9 Arms supply: the Eksund (1987); Irish-American funding (NORAID).

Loyalist paramilitaries
- [ ] L1 The UVF (revived 1966), the UDA (1971) and the UFF (1973): aims (defend the Union, oppose a united Ireland and power sharing).
- [ ] L2 McGurk's Bar (4 December 1971).
- [ ] L3 The Dublin and Monaghan bombings (17 May 1974).
- [ ] L4 The Miami Showband killings (1975).
- [ ] L5 The Shankill Butchers (1975–77): who, what, convictions, why they were notorious.
- [ ] L6 Greysteel (30 October 1993) and Sean Graham's bookmakers (1992): loyalist retaliation and the rise of loyalist killing in the early 1990s.
- [ ] L7 Most loyalist victims were Catholic civilians.

Casualties and security forces
- [ ] C1 Total deaths and the split of responsibility (republicans, loyalists, security forces), about half civilians; peak in 1972.
- [ ] C2 Loughgall (1987) and Gibraltar (1988): the "shoot to kill" controversy; the Stalker and Sampson inquiries.
- [ ] C3 Republicans' view that security operations fed recruitment.

Propaganda
- [ ] P1 Murals on both sides; republican publications (An Phoblacht); Irish-American publicity.
- [ ] P2 The British broadcasting ban of October 1988 (lifted 1994) and criticism of it.
- [ ] P3 Section 31 in the Republic (lapse 1994).

The Republic's response
- [ ] D1 Lynch's broadcast of 13 August 1969 and the request for a UN peacekeeping force.
- [ ] D2 Field hospitals, army presence at the border and refugees (Gormanston).
- [ ] D3 The Arms Crisis (1970): dismissals, arms trial and acquittals; the divide in Fianna Fáil.
- [ ] D4 Bloody Sunday reaction and the burning of the British embassy (2 February 1972).
- [ ] D5 The Offences against the State Act 1939 and its Amendment Act 1972; the Special Criminal Court.
- [ ] D6 The Cosgrave coalition: Sunningdale and the Council of Ireland; Garda–RUC cooperation; the Criminal Law (Jurisdiction) Act 1976; the Emergency Powers Act 1976 and President Ó Dálaigh.
- [ ] D7 Extradition as a recurring dispute; the 1987 Act after Enniskillen.
- [ ] D8 FitzGerald, the New Ireland Forum and the Anglo-Irish Agreement (brief); Haughey's Fianna Fáil opposition in 1985 and acceptance in power from 1987; the Dublin summit of December 1980.

Assessment (paired judgement points, final block)
- [ ] S1 Impact: deaths, deeper division, harder compromise, effect on British and Irish policy.
- [ ] S2 Failure: neither republicans nor loyalists achieved their aims; ceasefires only in 1994.
- [ ] S3 Contemporary views: governments condemned terrorism; republicans claimed to defend Catholics and fight an occupation; loyalists claimed to defend the Union.
- [ ] S4 Later view: most deaths were caused by paramilitaries, not the state; violence did not win; limits of the Republic's responses (security measures vs political aims).

## 3. Traps

Do not use (wrong or doubtful facts in the current notes):
- Do not use: "INLA formed in 1975", because it was founded in December 1974 and became public in 1975. Say "mid-1970s".
- Do not use: "claims of security force collusion [in the Dublin and Monaghan bombings] were never proven", because collusion remains disputed and later inquiries took differing views. Leave collusion out.
- Do not use: "loyalist groups killed more people than republican groups in 1992 to 1994", because the exact year-by-year balance is uncertain. Say only that loyalist killing rose sharply in the early 1990s.
- Do not use: "the Kingsmill massacre was carried out by republicans who claimed it", because the Provisional IRA denied it and it was claimed under a cover name. Say "attributed to republicans".
- Do not use: "the Emergency Powers Act led President Ó Dálaigh to refer the bill and then resign" as one step, because he resigned after a minister's criticism of his referral, not directly because of the bill.
- Do not use: the Irish government "condemned" the burning of the British embassy; say the burning took place during a demonstration after Bloody Sunday.
- Do not use: "Section 31 barred interviews with Sinn Féin from 1977"; give the extension to Sinn Féin in the 1970s without a year.
- Do not use: Loughgall or Gibraltar as proof of an official "shoot to kill" policy; it was an allegation.
- Do not use: Lord Mountbatten "and three others" killed by the IRA at Warrenpoint; the boat bomb was at Mullaghmore.
- Do not use: Warrington as a loyalist bomb or Greysteel as an IRA reprisal; Greysteel was the UFF's response to the Shankill bomb.

## 4. What exams ask

- Higher 100-mark essay, on the impact of terrorism on Northern Ireland: marks go to a clear thesis, both republican and loyalist violence covered, named attacks with dates, effects on deaths, sectarian division, political initiatives and daily life, and a judgement (impact vs failure to achieve aims). Description of events without judgement scores poorly.
- Ordinary Level 30-mark paragraph on the Provisional IRA: aims (end British rule, united Ireland), the split of December 1969, Seán Mac Stíofáin, and three or four dated attacks (Bloody Friday, Birmingham, Enniskillen). Each correct, dated and relevant statement earns marks.
- Ordinary Level questions on loyalist groups: name the UVF, UDA and UFF, say what they aimed at, and give one or two attacks (McGurk's Bar, Dublin and Monaghan, Greysteel) with the year.
- Responses of the Republic: students need the sequence Lynch's broadcast (1969), the Arms Crisis (1970), embassy burning (1972), the 1972 Act and Special Criminal Court, Sunningdale, extradition, and the Anglo-Irish Agreement, with one result or reaction for each. A list without links to the Northern crisis earns little.
- Casualty and responsibility figures are rewarded when stated as about and attributed to the three groups; do not give false precision.
- Propaganda questions reward both sides: murals, publications, the British voice ban and Section 31, with the reason for each and a view of how effective it was.
- Contemporary vs later views and impact vs failure are marked as paired points; name the aims (a united Ireland, or defence of the Union) and say neither side achieved them by 1993.
