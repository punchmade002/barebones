## 1. Must cover (one bullet or block for every item; tick each off)

Background (assumed)
- [ ] A1 (assumed) France's decolonisation and the Algerian war (1954–62) ended in Algerian independence; Algerians had been French nationals before 1962.
- [ ] A2 (assumed) Post-war labour migration from the Maghreb and West Africa, and the 1974 halt to labour migration followed by family reunification (detail belongs to the immigration subsection).
- [ ] A3 (assumed) The French republican ideal: citizens are individuals equal before the law, and the state is secular (laïcité); assimilation, not group recognition.
- [ ] A4 (assumed) Politics: Socialist President Mitterrand from May 1981; the Right's win in the 1986 parliamentary elections gave a Chirac government (1986–88, "cohabitation"); the Left returned in 1988.

Key concepts (each defined as used in this case study; also in keyTerms)
- [ ] K1 Racism: ranking people by race and treating them differently; shown in violence, discrimination and exclusion.
- [ ] K2 Assimilation: migrants and their children expected to adopt French language, values and civic culture.
- [ ] K3 Identity: the second generation as French citizens of North African heritage.
- [ ] K4 Cultural diversity: many cultures and religions in one society, made visible by the banlieues and the second generation.
- [ ] K5 Laïcité: state secularism, based on the 1905 law (essential term; at stake in 1989).
- [ ] K6 Jus soli: nationality by birth in the territory (essential term; at stake in 1986–87).

Background: the banlieues and the second generation
- [ ] B1 Banlieues: working-class suburbs and high-rise estates (HLM public housing) around French cities, built mostly from the 1950s–60s, rundown by the 1980s.
- [ ] B2 Les Minguettes in Vénissieux near Lyon as the best-known estate.
- [ ] B3 The second generation, "beurs" (verlan for "Arabes"): born or raised in France, French or entitled to be.
- [ ] B4 Unemployment: youth unemployment far above the national average; migrant factory workers hit first after the end of the post-war boom (1973 oil shock).
- [ ] B5 Policing: identity checks and stop-and-search as a constant grievance; poor relations with police.
- [ ] B6 Algerians the largest foreign nationality in France at the 1982 census.

The Minguettes unrest of 1981 and the Socialist response
- [ ] M1 Summer 1981: car-burning ("rodéos") and clashes with police at Les Minguettes; national television coverage.
- [ ] M2 Spread to other banlieues; national attention to second-generation living conditions.
- [ ] M3 Mitterrand's government (elected May 1981) responded with social programmes and promised reform of immigration law.
- [ ] M4 Priority Education Zones (ZEP, 1981); Banlieues 89 (1983); Dubedout report (1983) and local development programmes; criticised as too limited.
- [ ] M5 Regularisation of undocumented migrants (1981–82) and the ten-year residence card (1984 law).
- [ ] M6 Promise of local voting rights for foreign residents and the "right to be different"; voting rights never delivered.

The March for Equality and against Racism, 1983
- [ ] R1 Cause: the shooting and wounding of Toumi Djaïdja by a police officer (June 1983); organisers included the priest Christian Delorme and pastor Jean Costil.
- [ ] R2 Route: set out from Marseille on 15 October 1983 with about 30 people; grew; reached Paris 3 December 1983 with tens of thousands.
- [ ] R3 Outcome: a delegation received by Mitterrand; the ten-year residence card promised.
- [ ] R4 Demands: equal treatment in jobs, housing and policing; treated as French citizens.
- [ ] R5 Significance: showed the second generation could run a national campaign; press name "Marche des Beurs".

SOS Racisme and the anti-racist movement
- [ ] S1 Founded in Paris 1984 (launched October) by young activists including Harlem Désir and Julien Dray; national branches.
- [ ] S2 Slogan "Touche pas à mon pote" and the open-hand badge; solidarity across ethnic lines.
- [ ] S3 Support from the Socialist government, celebrities and trade unions; concerts and school and workplace campaigns.
- [ ] S4 Concert in the Place de la Concorde, 15 June 1985, hundreds of thousands of young people.
- [ ] S5 Criticism: it focused on individual racism not social causes; some 1983 marchers said the Socialists had taken over the movement.

The rise of the Front National
- [ ] F1 Founded October 1972 by Jean-Marie Le Pen (ex-paratrooper who served in Algeria) with backing from far-right groups including Ordre Nouveau; Algérie française supporters.
- [ ] F2 Policies: restrict immigration, expel illegal migrants, "national preference" in jobs and housing.
- [ ] F3 Dreux, September 1983: Jean-Pierre Stirbois's list about 17 per cent in the first round; the right's second-round alliance won the town and broke the taboo on allying with the FN.
- [ ] F4 1984 European elections: about 11 per cent and 10 seats.
- [ ] F5 1986 parliamentary elections (proportional system): 35 seats in the National Assembly.
- [ ] F6 Strongest in the south and east, in areas of high unemployment and large migrant communities (Marseille, Nice).
- [ ] F7 1988 presidential election: Le Pen about 14.4 per cent in the first round, over four million votes; only one seat in the 1988 parliamentary election under two-round constituency voting.
- [ ] F8 Le Pen: youngest deputy in 1956 as a Poujadist; his September 1987 remark calling the gas chambers a "detail", condemned widely.

Government responses: Pasqua laws, nationality, integration
- [ ] G1 Chirac government 1986–88, Charles Pasqua at the Interior: tougher controls, identity checks, easier deportation; the September 1986 law and visas for most foreigners.
- [ ] G2 October 1986 charter flight deporting about 100 Malians.
- [ ] G3 Nationality plan: end automatic acquisition at 18 for French-born children of foreign parents (a formal declaration instead); mass protest including SOS Racisme; dropped 1987; Long commission; Méhaignerie law 1993 later introduced the declaration.
- [ ] G4 Left's return 1988: Joxe law (1989) eased 1986 restrictions; Haut Conseil à l'intégration (December 1989); "integration" preferred to "assimilation".
- [ ] G5 Death of Malik Oussekine (December 1986), beaten by riot police during student protests; outrage; Chirac dropped the university reform bill.

The headscarf affair, 1989
- [ ] H1 October 1989 (autumn 1989): the head of a collège in Creil excluded three Muslim girls for wearing headscarves.
- [ ] H2 Education Minister Lionel Jospin sought the Conseil d'État's opinion; its ruling of 27 November 1989: headscarves allowed unless ostentatious or used for pressure or propaganda; Jospin circular of December 1989.
- [ ] H3 Significance: laïcité vs Muslim identity; debated across the parties; the approach lasted until the 2004 law banning conspicuous religious symbols in state schools.

The colonial past and Algeria
- [ ] C1 Algerian-origin families the largest group of the second generation; the Algerian war shaped how many French people saw North African migrants.
- [ ] C2 The harkis and their families: a sensitive issue.
- [ ] C3 Évian Accords (1962) and the departure of the pieds-noirs (800,000 to a million), many settling in southern France.
- [ ] C4 The FN drew on Algerian war veterans and Algérie française supporters; its voters were not only former settlers.
- [ ] C5 Double jus soli: children born in France to a parent born in Algeria before independence were French from birth.

Comparison with Britain
- [ ] P1 Similarities: urban unrest early 1980s; far-right party gaining from anti-immigrant feeling; police-youth confrontation.
- [ ] P2 Differences: assimilation vs a multicultural framework; no ethnic statistics in France; universal-rights arguments.
- [ ] P3 Responses: Britain's Scarman Report (1981) and Race Relations Acts vs French urban regeneration programmes.
- [ ] P4 Voting rights: Commonwealth citizens in Britain voted; non-citizen residents in France did not.

Assessment (paired judgement points, final block)
- [ ] X1 Achievement: the second generation found a national voice (1983 march, SOS Racisme).
- [ ] X2 Limit: government responses centred on policing, immigration control and limited regeneration; banlieue disadvantage persisted.
- [ ] X3 Achievement: the Conseil d'État framework for schools (1989) vs limit: the FN's success pulled mainstream parties towards its themes.
- [ ] X4 Contemporary views (assimilationists: failure of integration; activists: racism and exclusion; Le Pen's supporters: threat to identity) vs later views (housing policy, deindustrialisation and the colonial past, not immigration alone).

## 3. Traps

Do not use (wrong or doubtful facts in the current notes):
- Do not use: "France had no race relations act" as a flat claim, because the loi Pleven (1972) already made racist incitement and discrimination offences. Say France lacked the British framework (a series of Race Relations Acts and a commission), and did not record ethnic statistics.
- Do not use: Le Pen's "conviction" for the 1987 remark without detail, because the court case came later and the exact outcome is easily confused. Say it was widely condemned.
- Do not use: the claim that the double jus soli rule was a "target of the right's proposed reforms" in 1986, because the 1986–87 plan was about the declaration at 18. State the rule only as in section 2.
- Do not use: any number for the Paris arrival of the 1983 march or for the 1985 concert beyond "tens of thousands" and "hundreds of thousands"; sources range from about 60,000 to 100,000 for the march.
- Do not use: "Dreux by-election"; it was a municipal election held after the earlier result was annulled (say "municipal election").
- Do not use: the Malians figure as exact; say about 100.
- Do not use: ZEP and Banlieues 89 as "criticised" without saying who; keep to "limited in scale".
- Do not use: a date for "Minguettes unrest" other than summer 1981; a further wave followed in 1983, which is not on the list.

## 4. What exams ask

- Higher 100-mark essay on how France became multi-racial and how race relations developed in the 1980s: marks go to two parts, the long-term causes (decolonisation, labour migration, the Algerian war) briefly, then the 1980s events with dates in order (Minguettes 1981, the 1983 march, SOS Racisme 1984, FN advance 1983–88, Pasqua laws 1986, headscarves 1989).
- Higher essay linking the loss of empire to 1980s racial tension: tie each 1980s event to the colonial past (Algerian origin of the largest group, harkis and pieds-noirs, FN's Algérie française roots, nationality rules for Algerian-born parents). A narrative of the 1980s without that link caps the answer.
- Comparison with Britain (often set with Britain): a short paired list of similarities and differences with a named example for each side (Scarman vs regeneration programmes; no ethnic statistics; FN vs far-right in Britain); say which country managed better and why.
- Judgement: balanced achievements vs limits (second-generation voice and anti-racist movement vs persistent disadvantage and FN success); a judgement stated early and sustained.
- Ordinary Level 40-mark paragraph: who the beurs were, why the banlieues were angry (unemployment, policing), two or three events with dates, the FN's rise with Dreux, the 1986 laws and the headscarf affair; each dated, correct statement earns marks.
- Explaining cause and result: why the 1983 march began (a police shooting), why SOS Racisme took off, why the FN gained votes, why the nationality plan was dropped.
- Terms: define assimilation, racism and identity precisely and use them in the answer rather than leaving them to a glossary.
