## 1. Must cover (one bullet or block for every item; tick each off)

Background (assumed)
- [ ] A1 (assumed) The Catholic Church in the 1950s: led by the pope from Rome, Latin Mass, a centralised Roman Curia, priests and bishops clearly separate from the laity.
- [ ] A2 (assumed) Pius XII (pope 1939–58) ruled in a centralised, authoritarian style; the Church felt under pressure from secularisation, a fall in practice among workers, communism and modern ideas.
- [ ] A3 (assumed) The Reformation split Christianity into Catholic, Orthodox and Protestant churches; the Catholic Church had long viewed other churches with suspicion.
- [ ] A4 (assumed) The Holocaust and the history of Christian anti-Judaism, which made Catholic-Jewish relations an urgent question.
- [ ] A5 (assumed) A council is a meeting of the bishops of the Church; this was the twenty-first ecumenical council, the first since Vatican I (1869–70).

Key concept
- [ ] K1 Ecumenical movement: the movement among Christian churches to cooperate and move towards unity; the Council endorsed it (Unitatis Redintegratio) and welcomed observers from other churches.
- [ ] K2 Aggiornamento: John XXIII's term, "updating" (also in keyTerms).
- [ ] K3 Other essential terms for keyTerms: vernacular liturgy, collegiality, laity, religious liberty, constitution (as the top rank of Council document), Nostra Aetate, Humanae Vitae.

Why the Council was called
- [ ] B1 John XXIII: Angelo Roncalli, elected 28 October 1958 aged 76, seen as a caretaker pope.
- [ ] B2 He announced the Council on 25 January 1959 (surprising the cardinals).
- [ ] B3 Aims: aggiornamento; a pastoral Council to renew teaching and practice rather than to condemn errors; help the Church speak to the modern world; Christian unity.
- [ ] B4 The pressures: secularisation, declining practice, the loss of the working class, theologians' demands for renewal, the division of Christians, the nuclear age and mass media.
- [ ] B5 Preparation from 1960 by commissions dominated by Curia officials (Cardinal Ottaviani); expectation of a short Council approving prepared drafts.

The sessions, 1962-65
- [ ] S1 Opening: 11 October 1962, St Peter's Basilica, about 2,500 bishops, observers from other Christian churches.
- [ ] S2 First session (autumn 1962): bishops declined to accept the Curia's lists and drafts and took control of the agenda (progressive cardinals such as Frings and Suenens).
- [ ] S3 John XXIII's death (3 June 1963); election of Paul VI (21 June 1963); Paul VI kept the Council going.
- [ ] S4 Second session (autumn 1963): the nature of the Church; collegiality; Sacrosanctum Concilium approved in December 1963.
- [ ] S5 Third session (autumn 1964): Lumen Gentium and Unitatis Redintegratio approved; the tense "Black Week".
- [ ] S6 Fourth session (autumn 1965): Dei Verbum, Nostra Aetate, Dignitatis Humanae and Gaudium et Spes approved; the Council closed on 8 December 1965; Synod of Bishops set up (1965).
- [ ] S7 Participants: bishops from all continents, theological advisers (Ratzinger; also Wojtyła as a bishop), observers from Orthodox, Anglican and Protestant churches, press coverage.

The main documents
- [ ] D1 Sacrosanctum Concilium (1963): liturgy; local languages; fuller participation.
- [ ] D2 Lumen Gentium (1964): the Church as the people of God; role of laity; collegiality of bishops.
- [ ] D3 Dei Verbum (1965): revelation and Scripture; Bible study encouraged.
- [ ] D4 Gaudium et Spes (1965): the Church in the modern world; peace, marriage, economic life, social justice.
- [ ] D5 Unitatis Redintegratio (1964): the decree on ecumenism.
- [ ] D6 Nostra Aetate (1965): non-Christian religions; Judaism; rejection of collective Jewish guilt.
- [ ] D7 Dignitatis Humanae (1965): religious liberty and conscience.
- [ ] D8 The classification: the four constitutions are D1-D4; D5 is a decree; D6 and D7 are declarations.

Changes
- [ ] C1 The vernacular Mass: local languages from 1964-65; the new Roman Missal (1969-70); priest facing the people became common.
- [ ] C2 Lay participation: readings, ministers, parish councils; the laity's wider role.
- [ ] C3 Ecumenism: dialogue with other churches; observers; the lifting of the 1054 excommunications in December 1965 (Paul VI and Athenagoras).
- [ ] C4 Relations with Judaism: Nostra Aetate as the basis for dialogue; John Paul II in the Great Synagogue of Rome (13 April 1986).
- [ ] C5 Religious orders and priests asked to renew (habits, rules); religious liberty accepted as a principle.

Reactions
- [ ] R1 Traditionalists: Archbishop Lefebvre, the Society of St Pius X (1970), excommunication (1988).
- [ ] R2 Progressives: wanted more on women, collegiality, sexual ethics; felt reforms stalled.
- [ ] R3 Humanae Vitae (25 July 1968): reaffirmed the ban on artificial contraception.
- [ ] R4 Why it was a turning point: the papal commission's majority favoured change; shock; open dissent; a gap between teaching and lay practice; weaker clerical authority.
- [ ] R5 Dissent in the Netherlands and elsewhere; the Dutch pastoral council (1968-70); Rome reasserted control.

Key personality: Pope John Paul II (religious role; one block, every point)
- [ ] P1 Who he was: Karol Wojtyła, born Wadowice 1920, priest, bishop of Kraków (1958), archbishop (1964).
- [ ] P2 Participant at the Council; contribution to Gaudium et Spes.
- [ ] P3 Election 16 October 1978; first non-Italian pope since 1523.
- [ ] P4 Travels: over a hundred foreign journeys; Poland 1979.
- [ ] P5 Visit to Ireland, 29 September to 1 October 1979: Phoenix Park Mass, Drogheda appeal.
- [ ] P6 Conservative teaching: contraception, abortion, ordination of women, clerical celibacy; discipline of theologians; opposition to liberation theology.
- [ ] P7 Promotion of new movements, World Youth Days.
- [ ] P8 Reaching out: synagogue visit (1986); apology for historical wrongs of Christians.
- [ ] P9 Role in Poland and the fall of communism (briefly): 1979 visit, support for Solidarity, meeting Gorbachev (1 December 1989).
- [ ] P10 Shot 13 May 1981; forgave his attacker; died April 2005.
- [ ] P11 Contemporaries: crowds and supporters vs critics; communist rulers saw him as a threat.
- [ ] P12 Historians: defender of human dignity and a world figure vs a pope who resisted reform and handled abuse scandals badly.

Influence on religious practice
- [ ] I1 Attendance and vocations fell in Western Europe from the 1960s; the Council coincided with wider social change (secularisation, prosperity, the media, the 1960s), so cause is disputed.
- [ ] I2 Protestant churches declined too without a Council.
- [ ] I3 Ireland: practice stayed high into the 1980s, with decline and a loss of authority later; Poland remained an exception.
- [ ] I4 Positives: more lay involvement, interest in social justice and peace.

Assessment (final block, paired points)
- [ ] J1 Achievements: vernacular liturgy, new relations with other faiths and churches, a more open Church.
- [ ] J2 Limits: falling practice and vocations, traditionalist-progressive conflict, Humanae Vitae.
- [ ] J3 Contemporary views (new beginning vs betrayal of tradition) vs later views (wider social trends; effects differ by country).
- [ ] J4 Traditionalists blame the Council; progressives blame failure to implement it.

## 3. Traps

Do not use (wrong or doubtful facts in the current notes):
- Do not use: that the Council "reversed the earlier teaching" on religious liberty and "ended the ideal of the Catholic confessional state", because historians and theologians dispute how far it was a break rather than a development. Say that Dignitatis Humanae accepted that the state should not coerce people in religion, a shift from the Church's earlier hostility to such liberty.
- Do not use: the figure that Mass attendance "dropped by half or more by the 1980s" in France and the Netherlands, because I am not sure of the figures. Say it fell sharply.
- Do not use: "the first pope in modern times to enter a synagogue" as flat fact; say it was widely described as the first papal visit to a synagogue.
- Do not use: "priest faced the congregation" as a Council rule. The Council did not order it; the 1969-70 Missal made it common practice.
- Do not use: an exact number of observers, or exact attendance figures for later sessions; the bishops numbered about 2,500 at the opening only.
- Do not use: Unitatis Redintegratio as a "declaration"; it is a decree.
- Do not use: "John XXIII was not expected to do anything" as a quotation; say only that he was seen as a caretaker.
- Do not use: Black Week details (the three controversies: delayed vote on religious liberty, changes to the ecumenism text, delay of the Eastern Churches decree) beyond one line.

Confusable people, terms and dates
- Vatican I (1869-70, papal infallibility) vs Vatican II (1962-65).
- John XXIII (called and opened the Council, died 1963) vs Paul VI (completed it, issued Humanae Vitae) vs John Paul II (a participant, pope 1978-2005).
- Constitution (highest rank) vs decree vs declaration.
- Nostra Aetate (non-Christian religions) vs Unitatis Redintegratio (other Christian churches) vs Dignitatis Humanae (religious liberty).
- Dates of promulgation (1963, 1964, 1965) are not the session dates; the notes must not put all documents in 1965.
- Vernacular changes began 1964-65; the new Missal is 1969-70.
- Lefebvre's Society (1970) vs his excommunication (1988).
- Humanae Vitae (1968, after the Council closed) is Paul VI's, not the Council's teaching.
- John Paul II's 1979 Poland visit (June) vs his Ireland visit (Sept-Oct 1979).
- The Reformation-era division vs the 1054 split (East-West) lifted in 1965.

## 4. What exams ask

- Higher 100-mark essay, usually Vatican II paired with John Paul II as two contributions, or John Paul II paired with another figure (a political leader, a European statesman). Marks go to naming specific changes, documents and dates for the Council, and to a balanced assessment of John Paul II; a clear judgement on which mattered more or how each contributed is rewarded. In the pairing, give each part similar weight.
- The commonest Ordinary Level paragraph (30 or 40 marks): the changes brought by the Council (vernacular Mass with the new Missal, lay role, ecumenism, relations with Judaism), or why it was called (John XXIII, aggiornamento, renewal, modern world) and what it did.
- A John Paul II profile paragraph: Wadowice 1920, Kraków archbishop, participation at the Council, election 1978 as first non-Italian since 1523, travels (over a hundred), Poland and Solidarity, conservative teaching, Ireland 1979.
- Short answers: who was pope when it began; what aggiornamento means; the year it began and ended; name a document; one change to Mass.
- Documents, not just titles: marks usually go to document name plus year plus what it said, so pair each with a one-line content.
- Humanae Vitae and the reaction to the Council: explaining why it divided Catholics earns credit for causes and consequences; link to practice.
- Assessment: a strong answer separates what the Council intended and enacted from what happened in practice, and uses both contemporaries and later historians; the decline of practice should be hedged because social change is also a cause.
- Common marks lost: stating that Vatican II "changed doctrine", mixing up popes, dating all changes to 1965, or giving John Paul II's political role only without his religious role.
