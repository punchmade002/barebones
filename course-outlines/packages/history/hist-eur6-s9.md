## 1. Must cover (one bullet or block for every item; tick each off)

Background (assumed)
- [ ] A1 (assumed) The Reconstruction amendments (13th, 14th, 15th) and their collapse: by the 1890s southern states had taken back control and stripped black men of the vote.
- [ ] A2 (assumed) The Great Migration of black Americans to northern and western cities, and the Second World War and Truman's desegregation of the armed forces (1948), as the start of postwar pressure for change.
- [ ] A3 (assumed) The Constitution, federalism and states' rights: southern states claimed the right to run their own race laws; the federal government and Supreme Court could override them.

Key concepts (also in keyTerms)
- [ ] K1 Discrimination: unequal treatment because of race, by law (the South) or custom (housing and jobs everywhere).
- [ ] K2 Liberalism: belief in an active federal government that uses its power for civil rights and social welfare; the basis of Johnson's legislation and the War on Poverty.
- [ ] K3 Jim Crow (term).

## 3. Traps

Do not use (wrong or doubtful facts in the current notes):
- Do not use: "the city agreed to desegregate its shops in May after bombings and riots", because the Birmingham agreement (10 May 1963) came first and the bombings and riots followed it (11 May). Use the order in section 2.
- Do not use: "a filibuster lasting 54 days" (or any day count), because sources count the Senate debate differently. Say the longest filibuster to that date, ended by cloture on 10 June 1964.
- Do not use: "sit-ins spread to more than fifty cities within months"; say "dozens of cities".
- Do not use: "Reagan's Just Say No campaign, launched in 1982 with his wife Nancy"; it was Nancy Reagan's campaign. Ronald Reagan's own measures were enforcement and the 1986 Act.
- Do not use: "King ... was the youngest recipient" without the words "up to that time"; later recipients were younger.
- Do not use: "he [Kennedy] supported the March on Washington despite fears of violence" as a flat statement; he was first reluctant and then backed it.
- Do not use: "the Voting Rights Act banned literacy tests" without "in areas with a history of discrimination"; Congress extended the ban nationwide later (1970).
- Do not use: "the Albany Movement failed because ... a lesson that shaped the choice of Birmingham" as a certain fact; say Albany showed that violence by police helped the movement and Birmingham's Bull Connor supplied it.
- Do not use: "affirmative action was ... limited, but not ended, by Bakke" without saying what Bakke decided (quotas out, race as one factor).
- Do not use: Nixon's war on drugs as only "enforcement"; his 1971 programme also funded treatment, but the later 1980s policy was enforcement-led.

Doubtful quotations: do not use
- Any quotation of King other than "I Have a Dream" (title), "Letter from Birmingham Jail" (title) and the Kerner phrase; Johnson's "we shall overcome" is a phrase, not a long quotation.
- Malcolm X's "by any means necessary" and "the chickens coming home to roost": do not use.

Confusable people, terms and dates
- NAACP (lawyers, courts, 1909) vs SCLC (church-based, King, 1957) vs SNCC (students, 1960) vs CORE (Freedom Rides, 1961).
- Brown (1954, ruling) vs Brown II (1955, "all deliberate speed") vs Little Rock (1957, enforcement by troops).
- Civil Rights Act 1957 (weak, voting) vs 1964 (public places, jobs) vs Voting Rights Act 1965 vs Fair Housing Act 1968.
- Birmingham (1963, success, Bull Connor) vs Albany (1961–62, failure) vs Selma (1965, voting); Little Rock is a school crisis, not a march.
- Malcolm X (Nation of Islam, Harlem, d. 1965) vs Stokely Carmichael (Black Power, SNCC, 1966) vs Black Panthers (Oakland, 1966): three different things.
- Watts (1965, Los Angeles) vs Detroit and Newark (1967).
- Kerner Report (riots, 1968) vs Moynihan Report (family, 1965).
- Medgar Evers (murdered June 1963) vs Emmett Till (1955) vs Chaney, Goodman and Schwerner (1964).
- Kennedy's bill (June 1963) vs Johnson's Act (July 1964).
- The "war on drugs" began under Nixon (1971) and was escalated by Reagan (1980s).

## 4. What exams ask

- Higher 100-mark essay, the commonest form (several recent years): how far racial equality was achieved, or how race relations changed, 1945–68 or 1945–89. Marks go to a judgement stated early and sustained: law changed (Brown, 1964, 1965, 1968), everyday life changed less (poverty, housing, schools, policing, imprisonment). Separate legal from economic equality, and 1945–68 from 1968–89.
- Racial conflict, urban poverty and drugs or crime as a combined question: each strand needs dated evidence (Watts and Detroit, the Kerner Report, the War on Poverty, 1971, 1986) and a link between them (poverty and ghettos fed riots, drugs and crime).
- King as a Higher essay: his contribution and methods (non-violence, media, Birmingham, March on Washington, Selma, Nobel), limits (Albany, Chicago, Black Power criticism) and a judgement on how decisive he was against other activists. Students must show that the leader-vs-movement debate exists.
- Why events happened: why Birmingham was chosen, why the 1964 and 1965 Acts passed (violence on television, Kennedy's death, Johnson's skill, Democratic majorities), why riots broke out (northern ghettos, police, unemployment, laws that did not reach the North), why the War on Poverty fell short.
- Johnson-based questions: his civil rights legislation and the War on Poverty named with dates and terms (Job Corps, Community Action); his strengths there against his weaknesses over Vietnam.
- Ordinary Level short answers: identify King, a segregation scene from a document, why the South was worse than the North, what Kennedy and Johnson did for civil rights (a speech, a bill, two Acts with dates), the Harrington and Johnson link, Nixon's "public enemy number one". Give the single correct fact in one sentence.
- Ordinary Level 30/40-mark answers: three or four dated points each (for example a bill, an Act, a march, a riot) with a short explanation; vague statements without a date or name earn little.
- Document questions use King's and activists' writings and photographs: students should know who wrote the Birmingham letter, the aims of Freedom Rides and sit-ins, and what each organisation stood for, to judge reliability and bias.
- Black Power and Malcolm X: compare their aims with King's (separation and self-defence vs integration and non-violence), and say how far they helped or harmed the cause.
