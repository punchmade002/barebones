## 1. Must cover (one bullet or block for every item; tick each off)

Background (assumed)
- [ ] A1 (assumed) The Papal States and the First Vatican Council: until 1870 the Pope ruled central Italy as a temporal (worldly) ruler; the Council declared papal infallibility in July 1870, just before Rome was lost.
- [ ] A2 (assumed) Germany in 1871: a Protestant-led Reich under Prussia, with a large Catholic minority (about a third) in the south, the Rhineland and the Polish east; Bismarck as Chancellor. One bullet; his wider domestic policy is in the notes on Bismarck.
- [ ] A3 (assumed) France after 1870: defeat by Prussia, the loss of Alsace-Lorraine, the Third Republic divided between republicans (often anticlerical) and monarchist Catholics; the army as the symbol of national pride and revenge.
- [ ] A4 (assumed) An encyclical: a formal letter from the Pope to the Church, known by its opening Latin words.
- [ ] A5 (assumed) Russia's Jews: tsarist autocracy, the Orthodox Church as the state church, and Russification of minorities; Alexander II assassinated in 1881.
- [ ] A6 (assumed) The Western Front in one bullet: trench deadlock, gas, shelling and huge casualties from 1915, which explain the change in war writing. Detail is in the notes on the conduct of the war.

Key concept: nationalism, and national identity in the arts
- [ ] N1 Definition (also in keyTerms): loyalty to the nation and the belief that each nation should be united, strong and self-governing; cultural nationalism used history, myth, folk tradition, language and landscape to prove a people's distinct identity.
- [ ] N2 Two kinds: the culture of established, imperial states (Germany, Britain) vs the culture of peoples without their own state (Czechs, Finns, Norwegians).
- [ ] N3 Richard Wagner: operas built on Germanic myth; the Ring cycle first staged in full at his Bayreuth theatre in 1876; died 1883; the Bayreuth Festival as a shrine of German culture; his anti-Jewish writing (links to the anti-Semitism blocks; one clause).
- [ ] N4 German national monuments: the Hermann Monument (1875) and the Monument to the Battle of the Nations at Leipzig (1913).
- [ ] N5 Bedřich Smetana's Má vlast (including Vltava, "The Moldau"): Czech identity inside Austria-Hungary.
- [ ] N6 Jean Sibelius's Finlandia: Finnish resistance to Russian rule (Russification).
- [ ] N7 Edvard Grieg's music for Ibsen's Peer Gynt: Norwegian folk idiom (Norway in union with Sweden until 1905).
- [ ] N8 Edward Elgar: Pomp and Circumstance March No. 1 and "Land of Hope and Glory": British imperial confidence.
- [ ] N9 Rudyard Kipling: poet of Empire (Recessional, The White Man's Burden); his son John killed at Loos in 1915; his work for the Imperial War Graves Commission (bridge to war literature).
- [ ] N10 States and nationalist societies spread national identity through schools, national history, museums, theatres and national languages.
- [ ] N11 Link to war: pre-1914 cultural nationalism fed the patriotic mood of 1914 and the early war poetry.

The literature of World War I
- [ ] L1 The change in attitude as the organising idea: patriotic idealism 1914–15 → disillusionment, anger and pity 1916–18 → the post-war "disenchanted" view of the late 1920s.
- [ ] L2 Rupert Brooke: the 1914 sonnets, The Soldier; death on the way to Gallipoli (April 1915); celebrated as the ideal patriot.
- [ ] L3 Other early-war voices: Julian Grenfell (Into Battle) and John McCrae (In Flanders Fields).
- [ ] L4 Siegfried Sassoon: Military Cross; his public statement against the war (July 1917); sent to Craiglockhart instead of a court martial; satire in The Old Huntsman and Counter-Attack.
- [ ] L5 Other soldier-poets in one bullet: Isaac Rosenberg (killed 1918), Edmund Blunden, Ivor Gurney.
- [ ] L6 Henri Barbusse, Le Feu (Under Fire, 1916): the French infantryman's war; Prix Goncourt; written during the war.
- [ ] L7 Ernst Jünger, Storm of Steel (1920): a German officer who found meaning in combat, a contrast to the protest writers.
- [ ] L8 Erich Maria Remarque, All Quiet on the Western Front (1929): the post-war German view of a lost generation; a worldwide bestseller; burned by the Nazis in 1933 (one clause).
- [ ] L9 The war-books boom of 1928–33: Robert Graves, Goodbye to All That (1929); Vera Brittain, Testament of Youth (1933), a woman's view.
- [ ] L10 Why writing changed: the scale of casualties (the Somme 1916), trench conditions, the gap between home-front propaganda and soldiers' experience; censorship limited what soldiers could say in letters and the press.
- [ ] L11 The literature as historical evidence: strengths (eyewitness detail, feeling) and limits (mostly educated officers, written for effect; many soldiers kept their belief in the cause).

Key personality: Wilfred Owen (one block, structured as NOTES_STANDARD requires)
- [ ] O1 Who he was: born Oswestry, Shropshire, 1893, eldest child of a railway worker; lay assistant to a vicar at Dunsden; taught English in Bordeaux, France, before the war.
- [ ] O2 Enlisted in the Artists Rifles (October 1915); commissioned in the Manchester Regiment (1916); reached the front on the Somme sector in January 1917 (near Beaumont-Hamel), in severe winter conditions.
- [ ] O3 Spring 1917: concussion from a fall, then blown into the air by a shell and lay for several days beside a dead fellow officer; diagnosed with shell shock (neurasthenia).
- [ ] O4 Craiglockhart War Hospital, Edinburgh (from June 1917): meets Sassoon (August 1917), who encouraged him; meets Robert Graves.
- [ ] O5 The major poems, mostly written or revised between autumn 1917 and autumn 1918: Dulce et Decorum Est, Anthem for Doomed Youth, Strange Meeting, Futility, Exposure.
- [ ] O6 Method and message: graphic detail of gas, mud and death; pararhyme; "the pity of War"; attack on the patriotic "old Lie" and on those at home who spread it.
- [ ] O7 Return to France (autumn 1918); action at Joncourt (1 October 1918), for which he was awarded the Military Cross (announced after his death).
- [ ] O8 Killed on 4 November 1918 crossing the Sambre–Oise canal near Ors, aged 25, a week before the armistice; his parents received the telegram on Armistice Day.
- [ ] O9 Participant and witness: an officer who fought, and who wrote as a witness for the men he led.
- [ ] O10 Contemporaries: almost unknown in his lifetime (only about five poems published); Sassoon's edition of 1920; Blunden's fuller edition of 1931; Yeats left him out of the Oxford Book of Modern Verse (1936).
- [ ] O11 Later reputation: Britten's War Requiem (1962) set his poems; Owen became the best-known English poet of the war, taught in schools.
- [ ] O12 Historians' and critics' views: biographers (Jon Stallworthy, Dominic Hibberd) stress his craft and development; revisionist historians (e.g. Dan Todman) argue that the war poets' view became the "myth" of a futile war and was not shared by most soldiers.

Church and state in Germany: the Kulturkampf
- [ ] K1 Causes: papal infallibility (1870) and Bismarck's fear of divided loyalty; the Catholic Centre Party (founded 1870) as a "Reichsfeind"; Polish Catholics in the east; the alliance with the anticlerical National Liberals.
- [ ] K2 The name: "struggle for civilisation", Rudolf Virchow (a liberal), 1873.
- [ ] K3 Measures in order: the abolition of the Catholic department of the Prussian Ministry of Culture (1871); the Pulpit Law (1871); the School Supervision Law (1872); the Jesuit Law (1872); Bismarck's "Canossa" speech (1872).
- [ ] K4 The May Laws (1873) under Adalbert Falk, Prussian Minister of Culture: state education and exams for priests, state approval of church appointments; further laws to 1875 (civil marriage; the "breadbasket" law cutting state payments; dissolution of most religious orders).
- [ ] K5 Resistance: Pius IX's condemnation (1875); bishops imprisoned or exiled; many parishes without priests; Catholic solidarity behind the Centre under Ludwig Windthorst; the Centre's vote roughly doubled in 1874.
- [ ] K6 Why Bismarck ended it: the policy had failed and strengthened the Centre; he needed Centre votes for tariffs (1879) and against the socialists (the new enemy); the break with the National Liberals; the death of Pius IX and the election of Leo XIII (1878); Falk's resignation (1879).
- [ ] K7 The ending: negotiations with Leo XIII; mitigation laws from 1880; the peace laws of 1886–87; what remained (civil marriage, state school inspection, the Pulpit Law, the Jesuit ban to 1917).
- [ ] K8 Results: a political defeat for Bismarck; the Centre a permanent, large Reichstag party; Catholic alienation from the Protestant-led Reich; a model of state–church conflict that many saw as a failure of liberalism.

Church and state in Italy: the Roman Question
- [ ] I1 Background: the Kingdom of Italy (1861) took most of the Papal States; Italian troops entered Rome on 20 September 1870; Rome became the capital (1871).
- [ ] I2 The Law of Guarantees (May 1871): the Pope's person inviolable, use of the Vatican, the Lateran and Castel Gandolfo, a yearly payment; Pius IX rejected it and called himself a "prisoner in the Vatican".
- [ ] I3 The Roman Question defined (also in keyTerms): the unresolved dispute over the Pope's loss of Rome and his status.
- [ ] I4 Non expedit: Catholics told neither to vote in nor stand for parliamentary elections; effects (Catholics outside national politics; a narrow liberal ruling class); Catholics active in local politics and social organisations (the Opera dei Congressi, Catholic banks, co-operatives, newspapers).
- [ ] I5 The liberal state's anticlerical measures in one bullet: secular state schooling, civil marriage, the closing of religious houses.
- [ ] I6 Leo XIII kept the hard line on Rome; Pius X relaxed non expedit (from 1905); the Gentiloni Pact (1913); Don Luigi Sturzo's Italian Popular Party (1919); settlement only in the Lateran Treaty (1929), beyond the period (one clause).
- [ ] I7 Comparison with Germany: Germany's conflict was an attack by the state on the Church and was ended by compromise; Italy's was the Church's refusal to accept the state, and lasted until 1929.

Church and state in France (only as needed for Leo XIII and Dreyfus)
- [ ] F1 One bullet: the republic's anticlerical laws (the Ferry laws on free, compulsory, secular primary schooling, 1881–82; the Law of Associations, 1901; the Separation of Church and State, 1905), and how the Dreyfus Affair sped them up.

Key personality: Leo XIII (one block, structured as NOTES_STANDARD requires)
- [ ] P1 Who he was: Gioacchino Pecci, born 1810; nuncio in Brussels; Bishop of Perugia for over thirty years; elected pope on 20 February 1878 after Pius IX's confrontational reign; pope until his death in 1903.
- [ ] P2 Ending the Kulturkampf by negotiation in the 1880s; his arbitration of the Caroline Islands dispute between Germany and Spain (1885) at Bismarck's request.
- [ ] P3 Rerum Novarum (15 May 1891): rejected socialism and class war; defended private property; upheld a just wage, decent conditions, the right to form trade unions and the duty of the state to protect workers.
- [ ] P4 Legacy of Rerum Novarum: the foundation of Catholic social teaching; Catholic trade unions and Christian democratic parties; Pius XI's Quadragesimo Anno (1931) on its fortieth anniversary.
- [ ] P5 The ralliement (1890–92, Au milieu des sollicitudes 1892): French Catholics urged to accept the republic; largely refused by monarchist Catholics; wrecked by the Dreyfus Affair and the anticlerical laws.
- [ ] P6 Scholarship and the Church: Aeterni Patris (1879, Thomas Aquinas); the Vatican archives opened to scholars (1881).
- [ ] P7 Limits: he kept the "prisoner in the Vatican" stance and non expedit in Italy; conservative on doctrine.
- [ ] P8 Irish link (one bullet): the 1888 Vatican condemnation of the Plan of Campaign and boycotting.
- [ ] P9 Contemporaries: respected by governments and many workers as a moderate reformer; distrusted by conservative Catholics (accommodation with republics) and by socialists (rejection of socialism).
- [ ] P10 Historians: the first "modern" pope, who moved the papacy from lost temporal power to moral and social influence; critics see Rerum Novarum as cautious and paternalist and the ralliement as a failure.

Key concept: anti-Semitism
- [ ] S1 Definition (also in keyTerms): hostility to Jews as a people; the shift from older religious prejudice to political and racial anti-Semitism after 1870; Wilhelm Marr popularised the word (1879).
- [ ] S2 Why it grew: economic depression from 1873 and resentment of Jewish success in finance, trade and the professions; nationalism that defined the nation by blood or religion; pseudo-scientific race theory; Jews blamed for modernity, capitalism and socialism at once.

Anti-Semitism in France
- [ ] J1 Background: Jews were a small, largely assimilated minority (French citizens since the Revolution); defeat in 1870–71, the Panama scandal (1892) and economic fears gave anti-Semites targets.
- [ ] J2 Édouard Drumont: La France juive (1886), a bestseller; La Libre Parole (1892).
- [ ] J3 The arrest and conviction of Captain Alfred Dreyfus (1894): a Jewish artillery officer from Alsace on the General Staff; the bordereau; a closed court martial using a secret file; public degradation (January 1895); Devil's Island.
- [ ] J4 Picquart's discovery that Esterhazy wrote the bordereau (1896); Picquart moved away; Esterhazy acquitted (January 1898).
- [ ] J5 Zola's "J'accuse" in L'Aurore (13 January 1898); Zola convicted of libel and fled to England.
- [ ] J6 France divided: Dreyfusards (Zola, Clemenceau, Jaurès, the League of the Rights of Man) vs anti-Dreyfusards (army leaders, much of the Catholic press, nationalists, monarchists, anti-Semitic leagues); anti-Jewish riots (1898).
- [ ] J7 The forgery by Henry exposed and his suicide (1898); the Rennes retrial (1899): guilty "with extenuating circumstances"; pardon by President Loubet (1899).
- [ ] J8 Full rehabilitation (1906): verdict quashed, Dreyfus reinstated and decorated.
- [ ] J9 Results: a republican victory; the army and Church discredited; the anticlerical laws and the 1905 separation (cross-refer F1); a new nationalist right (Action Française, 1899) and anti-Semitism that survived; Herzl and the birth of political Zionism (Der Judenstaat 1896, Basel congress 1897).

Anti-Semitism in Russia
- [ ] R1 Background: about five million Jews in 1897, the largest Jewish population in the world; the Pale of Settlement (from 1791) in the western provinces; mostly poor artisans and traders; tsarist and Orthodox hostility; Russification.
- [ ] R2 The pogroms of 1881–82: after Alexander II's assassination, rumours blamed Jews; violence began at Elizavetgrad (April 1881) and spread across the south (Kiev, Odessa); officials slow to act or blamed the victims.
- [ ] R3 The May Laws (Temporary Regulations) of 1882: no new settlement in rural areas, restrictions on acquiring land and property outside towns.
- [ ] R4 Further restrictions: quotas on Jews in secondary schools and universities (from 1887); expulsion of Jews from Moscow (1891–92); limits on the professions.
- [ ] R5 Official anti-Semitism: Pobedonostsev, Alexander III's and Nicholas II's adviser; Alexander III and Nicholas II personally hostile.
- [ ] R6 The Kishinev pogrom (April 1903): about 49 dead, hundreds injured; international outrage.
- [ ] R7 Pogroms during the 1905 revolution (Odessa and elsewhere); the Black Hundreds and the Union of the Russian People (from 1905).
- [ ] R8 The Protocols of the Elders of Zion: forged in Russia, first printed 1903; exposed as a plagiarism in 1921 (one clause); spread worldwide after 1917.
- [ ] R9 The Beilis case (1911–13): a Kiev Jew tried for "ritual murder" and acquitted.
- [ ] R10 Jewish responses: emigration of about two million from the Russian Empire 1881–1914, mostly to the USA; the Jewish socialist Bund (1897); Zionism; many young Jews joined revolutionary parties.
- [ ] R11 End of the legal system: the Provisional Government abolished the Pale and Jewish disabilities (1917); pogroms in the civil war (one clause, no figures).

Assessment: culture, faith and exclusion (final block, paired points)
- [ ] X1 National identity in the arts: liberating for stateless peoples (Czechs, Finns, Norwegians) vs feeding imperial pride, exclusive nationalism and the war spirit of 1914 (Germany, Britain).
- [ ] X2 War literature: 1914–15 patriotism vs 1917–18 protest; contemporaries preferred Brooke, later generations Owen and Sassoon; value as evidence vs the "myth" argument.
- [ ] X3 Kulturkampf: success in state control of schools and marriage vs political failure (Centre strengthened, Bismarck forced to compromise).
- [ ] X4 Italy: the liberal state survived and kept Rome vs a lasting split from Catholic Italy until 1929.
- [ ] X5 Leo XIII: achievements (ending the Kulturkampf, Rerum Novarum, papal prestige) vs limits (the failed ralliement, the Roman Question unresolved).
- [ ] X6 Anti-Semitism compared: in France a popular and press movement that a republic finally defeated in the courts (justice for Dreyfus) vs in Russia a state-backed policy of laws and tolerated violence that drove mass emigration; prejudice survived in both.

## 3. Traps

Do not use (wrong or doubtful facts in the current notes):
- Do not use: "the Centre Party doubled its seats", because its seats rose from about 60 to about 90 in 1874; it was the vote that roughly doubled. Use K5.
- Do not use: Owen "fought on the Somme" without a date, because it suggests the Battle of the Somme of 1916; he reached the Somme sector in January 1917. Use O2.
- Do not use: Owen spent "weeks in a shell-hole", because it was several days. Use O3.
- Do not use: "Sassoon and Edith Sitwell published a first edition in 1919–20", because the first collection, Poems, appeared in 1920 (Sitwell printed seven poems in her anthology Wheels in 1919; leave that out). Use O10.
- Do not use: Owen "won the Military Cross ... in October" as if he received it in his lifetime; it was announced after his death. Use O7.
- Do not use: Leo XIII "opened the Vatican archives to scholars in 1883", because the archives were opened in 1881. Use P6.
- Do not use: Wilhelm Marr "coined" the word anti-Semitism, because the term existed earlier; he popularised it. Use S1.
- Do not use: "Wartime censorship ... so the most critical works came out in 1918 or after", because Le Feu (1916) and Sassoon's statement and The Old Huntsman (1917) were published during the war. Use L10 instead.
- Do not use: Barbusse "attacked the war's profiteers" as the book's main point; say it showed the ordinary French soldier's suffering and turned against the war.
- Do not use: "the Orthodox Church and the Black Hundreds spread anti-Semitic propaganda" as a blanket charge in the 1880s, because the Black Hundreds date from 1905; say some clergy and, from 1905, the Union of the Russian People.
- Do not use: "Jews joined socialist movements such as the Bund and Zionism", because Zionism was a national movement, not a socialist one. Keep them separate (R10).
- Do not use: Herzl "was influenced" by Dreyfus as a settled fact; say Herzl reported on the case and later said it convinced him. Historians debate how much it mattered.
- Do not use: "Colonel Henry"; give his name without a rank, or "Lieutenant-Colonel Henry" (he had been promoted by 1898). Do not call the 1894 trial a "secret court martial"; it sat in closed session with a secret file.
- Do not use: the Pobedonostsev "one third will die, one third emigrate, one third convert" remark; it is not reliably attested.
- Do not use: Kishinev death figures other than "about 49", or any death toll for the 1905 Odessa pogrom or the civil-war pogroms; figures vary widely.
- Do not use: Yeats's "blood, dirt and sucked sugar-stick" remark; give only the fact of the exclusion.

Confusable people, terms and dates
- The May Laws of 1873 (Prussia, Kulturkampf, Falk) vs the May Laws of 1882 (Russia, anti-Jewish) vs the Anti-Socialist Law of 1878 (Germany). Always name the country.
- Pius IX (1846–78: infallibility, Syllabus of Errors, "prisoner in the Vatican", Kulturkampf at its height) vs Leo XIII (1878–1903: peace with Bismarck, Rerum Novarum, ralliement) vs Pius X (1903–14: relaxed non expedit) vs Pius XI (Lateran Treaty 1929, Quadragesimo Anno).
- Falk (Prussian minister who drafted the May Laws) vs Windthorst (Centre Party leader) vs Virchow (liberal who named the Kulturkampf).
- The Law of Guarantees (Italy, 1871, rejected by the Pope) vs the Lateran Treaty (1929, accepted).
- Non expedit (Italy, Catholics stay out of national elections) vs the ralliement (France, Catholics accept the republic): opposite advice to Catholics in two countries.
- Dreyfus (accused) vs Esterhazy (real author of the bordereau) vs Picquart (discovered the truth) vs Henry (forger) vs Zola (J'accuse) vs Drumont (anti-Semitic writer).
- The Dreyfus Affair dates: arrest 1894, J'accuse 1898, Rennes and pardon 1899, rehabilitation 1906. A pardon (1899) is not an acquittal (1906).
- The Pale of Settlement (where Jews could live, from 1791) vs a pogrom (an attack) vs the May Laws (1882 restrictions).
- Kishinev (1903) vs the 1881–82 pogroms vs the 1905 pogroms.
- Brooke (patriotic, died 1915 of illness, never saw major combat) vs Owen and Sassoon (protest, frontline officers). Sassoon survived the war (died 1967); Owen did not.
- Barbusse (French, written in 1916) vs Remarque (German, published 1929) vs Jünger (German, 1920, positive about combat).
- Craiglockhart is in Edinburgh, Scotland; Owen was born in Oswestry, England, near the Welsh border, not in Wales.
- Wagner died in 1883; Bayreuth after 1883 belongs to his "legacy" (the festival continued under his widow Cosima).
- Smetana and Sibelius wrote for peoples ruled by empires; Elgar and Kipling for an imperial power. Keep the distinction in N2.

## 4. What exams ask

- Higher 100-mark essay on the literature of World War I (the commonest Higher question on this subsection): marks go to a clear thesis about change over time (early idealism → disillusionment → post-war disenchantment), supported by at least three named writers with dated works and a short, accurate quotation or close reference for each. Answers that only retell Owen's life, or list poems without saying what they show about attitudes to the war, lose marks.
- Higher essay asking whether one writer (usually Owen) made the most important contribution: students must compare him with at least two others (Brooke, Sassoon, Barbusse or Remarque) and reach a judgement; naming the "myth" debate (most soldiers did not share the protest view) earns the evaluation marks.
- Higher essay on Owen as soldier and poet: needs both halves with dates (enlistment 1915, the Somme sector 1917, shell shock, Craiglockhart, Military Cross 1918, death 4 November 1918) and the poems' themes and techniques.
- Higher essay on national identity in literature and the arts, sometimes combined with war literature: students need at least four examples from different countries, each tied to the nation it expressed and to the political situation (stateless vs imperial), and a link to the patriotism of 1914.
- Higher essay on church–state tensions in Germany and/or Italy, often combined with anti-Semitism: marks go to causes (infallibility, Bismarck's "enemies of the Reich", the loss of Rome), dated measures (1871–75), resistance and outcome (Centre strength; compromise by 1887; Italy unresolved), and a comparison of the two countries. Answers that narrate the laws without judging success or failure lose marks.
- Higher essay comparing how states dealt with churches and religious minorities: students should contrast the Kulturkampf, the Roman Question and France's secular laws with the treatment of Jews in France and Russia, and say which state was most and least successful.
- Higher essay on anti-Semitism in France and Russia (asked very often): marks go to explaining the difference (France: a press- and street-led prejudice inside a republic with a free press and courts, ending in Dreyfus's rehabilitation; Russia: state laws, official tolerance of pogroms and mass emigration), with dated events from both countries and the impact on each society (France divided, Church and army discredited, secular laws; Russia's Jews driven to emigrate or to revolution).
- Higher personality essays combining Leo XIII with Luxemburg, Wilson or Curie: judge his impact on European affairs (relations between Church and states, social teaching, diplomacy) with achievements vs limits.
- Ordinary 30-mark paragraph on Leo XIII: marks go to dated, specific statements: elected 1878, ended the Kulturkampf, Rerum Novarum 1891 with its main points (property, just wage, unions, no socialism), the ralliement, Aeterni Patris 1879, died 1903.
- Ordinary 30- or 40-mark paragraph on the Kulturkampf: causes (1870 infallibility, the Centre Party), measures with dates (1871 pulpit law, 1872 Jesuits and schools, 1873 May Laws, Falk), Catholic resistance, and why and how it ended (Leo XIII, the Centre's support needed, laws repealed by 1887).
- Ordinary paragraph on Owen: the life in order with dates plus two named poems and what they say about war.
- Ordinary paragraph on war poets (Brooke vs Sassoon or Owen): one contrast in attitude with dates.
- Ordinary paragraph on anti-Semitism in France (Drumont, the Dreyfus Affair in order, Zola, the outcome) or in Russia (the Pale, 1881 pogroms, the 1882 May Laws, Kishinev, the Protocols, emigration).
- Cause and result points that earn marks: why Bismarck began and ended the Kulturkampf; why the Pope rejected the Law of Guarantees; why the Dreyfus Affair divided France and what it changed; why the Russian state encouraged anti-Semitism (scapegoating, Russification, Orthodox nationalism) and its result (emigration, revolutionary Jews, international outrage); why war writing changed.
- Key concepts examiners may ask students to explain: nationalism (cultural and political, liberating and exclusive) and anti-Semitism (religious, economic, racial and political forms, with one example of each country).
