## 1. Must cover (one bullet or block for every item; tick each off)

Background (assumed)
- [ ] A1 (assumed) The Great Famine (1845–52) and emigration cut the Irish-speaking population; English was the language of state, law, commerce and the national schools (system begun 1831).
- [ ] A2 (assumed) The GAA (founded 1884) was the first of the cultural bodies; its founding and its crisis to 1891 are in the case study on the GAA.
- [ ] A3 (assumed) The post-Famine Catholic Church under Paul Cullen (Synod of Thurles, 1850) and the Catholic majority's political weight (Home Rule movement, Parnell).
- [ ] A4 (assumed) Who the "Anglo-Irish" were: the mainly Protestant landowning and professional class with English ties, many of them unionist.

Key concepts (define as used here; also in keyTerms)
- [ ] K1 Anglicisation: adoption of English language, dress, games, names and manners.
- [ ] K2 De-anglicisation: Hyde's programme to reverse it by reviving Irish language, games, music and customs.
- [ ] K3 Irish Ireland: Moran's idea of a Gaelic and Catholic nation built on Irish language, industry and games.
- [ ] K4 Anglo-Irish: as in A4; here, the writers of the Literary Revival and their critics' label for them.
- [ ] K5 Devotional revolution (essential term, not an outline key concept).
- [ ] K6 Cultural nationalism (essential term): nationhood defined by language, culture and tradition rather than by parliaments.

Hyde's 1892 lecture
- [ ] H1 The lecture: date, audience, title.
- [ ] H2 The argument: Ireland anglicised in language, names, dress, games and ideas; the Irish neither English nor Irish; revive Irish and native customs.
- [ ] H3 The non-political stance: the aim was cultural, to appeal across religion and party.
- [ ] H4 Its effect: the programme of the Gaelic League and the language movement (the GAA had pointed the same way in 1884).

The Gaelic League
- [ ] G1 Founding: date, place, founders (Hyde, MacNeill), Hyde as president.
- [ ] G2 Official stance: non-political and non-sectarian; who joined.
- [ ] G3 Activities: classes, timirí, feiseanna, the Oireachtas.
- [ ] G4 An Claidheamh Soluis (1899) and Pearse as editor (1903–09).
- [ ] G5 Success: Irish in schools (1900 programme).
- [ ] G6 Success: NUI matriculation campaign (decided 1910, applied 1913).
- [ ] G7 Success: St Patrick's Day processions and holiday (1903); Irish names and signs; membership and branches.
- [ ] G8 Social range: women, teachers, the urban middle class; the Dublin-based leadership.
- [ ] G9 Politicisation: IRB members joining from about 1910; Pearse; Hyde's resignation in 1915 (later context only).
- [ ] G10 Limits: Irish did not revive as an everyday language; the Church's limited support (Fr Michael O'Hickey, 1909).

D.P. Moran and Irish Ireland
- [ ] D1 The Leader (1900) and The Philosophy of Irish Ireland (1905).
- [ ] D2 His creed: Gaelic and Catholic nation; support for Irish industry, language and games; "West Briton" attacks on the Irish Party and the Anglo-Irish writers.
- [ ] D3 Effect: divided the revival between Hyde's inclusive approach and a narrower Catholic and Gaelic nationalism.

The Anglo-Irish Literary Revival

- [ ] L2 The Irish Literary Theatre (1899): founders, first production.
- [ ] L3 Cathleen ni Houlihan (1902), with Maud Gonne.

- [ ] L7 The Playboy riots (January 1907): causes and nationalist objections.
- [ ] L8 The Countess Cathleen controversy (1899): Catholic criticism.
- [ ] L9 The Hugh Lane gallery dispute (1912–1915 and after).
- [ ] L10 The tension between Anglo-Irish and Irish Ireland visions, and how it ran on after independence.

Key personality: Douglas Hyde (one block, every point)
- [ ] P1 Who he was: born Castlerea, Co. Roscommon, 1860; Church of Ireland rector's son; learned Irish at Frenchpark; Trinity College Dublin.
- [ ] P2 Writings: Love Songs of Connacht (1893); pen name An Craoibhín Aoibhinn; Irish-language play Casadh an tSúgáin (1901).
- [ ] P3 Participant: the 1892 lecture; co-founder and president of the League for twenty-two years.
- [ ] P4 Shaped by events: the League's politicisation overtook his non-political aim; resignation 1915.
- [ ] P5 Contemporaries: admired for scholarship and cross-religious appeal; criticised by Irish Irelanders for tolerating Anglo-Irish culture and later by republicans as the League turned political.
- [ ] P6 Later life: senator; first President of Ireland (1938–45); died 1949.
- [ ] P7 Historians: credited with launching the revival and a mass interest in the language, though its later political use went beyond his intentions.

Key personality: W.B. Yeats (one block, every point)
- [ ] Y1 Who he was: born Sandymount, Dublin, 1865; son of the painter John Butler Yeats; Sligo and London childhood; Protestant.
- [ ] Y2 Early work: The Wanderings of Oisin (1889) and Irish myth; friendship with Maud Gonne and John O'Leary.
- [ ] Y3 Participant: National Literary Society, Irish Literary Theatre, Abbey (director); Cathleen ni Houlihan.
- [ ] Y4 Shaped by events: the Countess Cathleen and Playboy controversies, and the Lane dispute, pushed him towards a critique of nationalist Ireland.
- [ ] Y5 September 1913 (8 September, Irish Times) and its refrain.
- [ ] Y6 Contemporaries: nationalists admired the work but distrusted his Anglo-Irish background; Moran mocked him; Catholic critics attacked The Countess Cathleen.
- [ ] Y7 Later life: senator 1922–28; Nobel Prize 1923; died 1939.
- [ ] Y8 Historians: central to Irish cultural life (R.F. Foster); critics see an elitist streak in his politics.

The consolidation of Catholic identity
- [ ] C1 Devotional revolution: rising Mass attendance, missions, retreats, confraternities, devotions (Rosary, Sacred Heart).
- [ ] C2 Church building: new churches and cathedrals, funded by the farming and trading middle class.
- [ ] C3 Control of schools: clerical managers of national schools; secondary schools run by religious orders (Christian Brothers, Jesuits, convent orders).
- [ ] C4 Maynooth and the clergy's social position, and clerical involvement in politics and the GAA.
- [ ] C5 Confraternities and sodalities as lay devotion.
- [ ] C6 Pioneer Total Abstinence Association (1898, Fr James Cullen SJ).
- [ ] C7 Ne Temere (1908): terms and Protestant reaction.
- [ ] C8 The Catholic middle class: land reform and education produced the nationalist movement's personnel.
- [ ] C9 How Catholic and nationalist identity merged: bishops and Home Rule after 1885; a largely Catholic Irish Party.
- [ ] C10 The unionist reaction: "Home Rule is Rome Rule"; Ne Temere; the Church's role in the Parnell split (1890).

The GAA after 1891
- [ ] GA1 Recovery in the 1890s: reorganised clubs and county boards, competitions resumed.
- [ ] GA2 IRB influence in its leadership.
- [ ] GA3 The ban on foreign games (early 1900s) and Jones's Road (1908).
- [ ] GA4 Comparison with the Gaelic League: mass, rural, male, sport vs women, teachers, urban middle class, language; politically committed vs non-political; shared aims and members.

Assessment (final block)
- [ ] S1 Achievement: lasting institutions and symbols; confidence for the nationalist movement.
- [ ] S2 Limit: the language did not revive as an everyday tongue.
- [ ] S3 Limit: the Gaelic and Catholic emphasis excluded many Protestants and unionists.
- [ ] S4 Contemporary views: unionists saw a disguise for separatism; Moran saw the Literary Revival as too Anglo-Irish.
- [ ] S5 Historians differ on whether the revival caused 1916 (as Pearse and the IRB claimed) or only supplied a language for politics with other causes.

## 4. What exams ask

- Higher 100-mark essay, the commonest form: assess the contribution of the GAA, the Gaelic League and the Literary Revival (or two of them) to cultural revival, or compare the GAA and the League. Marks go to a clear judgement, one paragraph per body, with dates and named people, then a comparison (mass vs language vs literary; non-political vs political; Catholic and Gaelic vs Anglo-Irish).
- Hyde and Yeats as a pair: what each did (lecture, League; Literary Theatre, Abbey), their backgrounds (both Protestant, both outside the Catholic majority), and how far each shaped Irish identity. Answers need personal profiles with dates.
- Controversy or contribution: Playboy riots, Countess Cathleen, the League's politicisation. Answers should explain who objected and why, and what each controversy showed about the Anglo-Irish and Irish Ireland visions.
- Cultural and religious identity (the 2019-style question): link the devotional revolution, school control and Ne Temere to a Catholic and nationalist identity, then the Gaelic and Anglo-Irish strands; balance with the unionist reaction and the limits of exclusion.
- Ordinary Level 30-mark profile of Hyde or Yeats: birth, background, what he did with dates (lecture and League; National Literary Society, Literary Theatre, Abbey), and his importance. Each correct, dated statement earns marks.
- Ordinary Level 30-mark account of the Gaelic League: founding date, founders, non-political stance, activities (classes, feiseanna, timirí, paper), and achievements.
- Achievement vs limits is rewarded: the language did not revive, but the institutions and confidence did.
- Naming a historian's view (Foster on Yeats; debate on 1916) adds weight to higher answers.
