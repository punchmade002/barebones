## 1. Must cover (one bullet or block for every item; tick each off)

Background (assumed)
- [ ] A1 (assumed) Autocracy: the tsar ruled with no parliament and no constitution before 1905, backed by the Orthodox Church, the nobility, the army, the bureaucracy and the secret police (the Okhrana).
- [ ] A2 (assumed) The two calendars: Russia used the old (Julian) calendar, 13 days behind the Western (Gregorian) one in the twentieth century, until February 1918. Give Western dates first with the Russian date in brackets, which is why the revolutions are "February" and "October" though they happened in March and November.
- [ ] A3 (assumed) Marxism in one bullet: history as class struggle; capitalism creates an industrial working class (proletariat) that will overthrow it; Russian Marxists therefore welcomed industrialisation.
- [ ] A4 (assumed) Defeat in the Crimean War (1853–56) convinced the government that Russia must modernise; this led to Alexander II's reforms. One bullet only.
- [ ] A5 (assumed) St Petersburg was renamed Petrograd in 1914; the capital moved to Moscow in 1918.

Russia in 1871: autocracy and backwardness
- [ ] B1 Emancipation of the serfs (1861) by Alexander II; redemption payments to the state over 49 years left peasants in debt.
- [ ] B2 The mir (village commune): held land collectively, redistributed strips periodically, discouraged investment; backward farming methods.
- [ ] B3 Population and peoples: the 1897 census (about 126 million; about four-fifths peasants); a multinational empire in which Russians were less than half the population.
- [ ] B4 Economic backwardness in 1871: little heavy industry, few railways for its size, weak banking; grain exports; reliance on foreign capital and technology.
- [ ] B5 Alexander II's other reforms (zemstva, courts, army) were limited; his assassination by People's Will (13 March 1881).
- [ ] B6 Alexander III (1881–94) and Nicholas II (1894–1917) upheld autocracy and pursued Russification of national minorities; Nicholas II's "senseless dreams" (1895).
- [ ] B7 Opposition parties underground: the Social Democrats (Marxist, 1898; split into Bolsheviks under Lenin and Mensheviks in 1903) and the Socialist Revolutionaries (SRs, peasant-based, 1901–02); the liberal Union of Liberation (1903–04).

Witte's industrialisation (the "great spurt")
- [ ] W1 Witte, Finance Minister 1892–1903: industry was needed for Russia to remain a Great Power; the state built railways, placed orders and protected industry with tariffs.
- [ ] W2 How it was paid for: foreign loans and investment (French and Belgian, helped by the Franco-Russian Alliance), heavy indirect taxes, grain exports. Vyshnegradsky's earlier grain-export policy (one bullet).
- [ ] W3 The gold standard (1897) to attract foreign investors.
- [ ] W4 Railways: the Trans-Siberian Railway begun 1891, through route to Vladivostok by about 1904, completed on Russian soil in 1916; the network nearly doubled in the 1890s.
- [ ] W5 Industrial regions: coal, iron and steel in the Donbas (Ukraine); oil at Baku; engineering and textiles in St Petersburg and Moscow.
- [ ] W6 Growth rate: industrial output grew at about 8 per cent a year in the 1890s; coal and pig-iron output roughly tripled 1890–1900.
- [ ] W7 Huge factories (the Putilov works, St Petersburg) concentrated workers, making organisation and protest easier.
- [ ] W8 The cost: the peasantry carried the burden through taxes, and grain was exported even in hunger years.
- [ ] W9 The industrial depression of 1900–03: unemployment and strikes; Witte removed from the Finance Ministry in 1903.
- [ ] W10 Russia by 1914: often ranked the world's fifth industrial power by total output, but output per head low and dependence on foreign capital high; strong growth again after about 1908.

The peasants and the 1891 famine
- [ ] P1 Peasant problems: small plots, old methods, rapid population growth, redemption payments, land hunger.
- [ ] P2 The 1891 famine: harvest failure across the Volga and black-earth provinces; cholera and typhus followed in 1892; deaths in the hundreds of thousands (range in section 2).
- [ ] P3 Slow government relief; zemstvo and private volunteers, including Tolstoy and Chekhov; the famine discredited the regime and revived opposition among educated Russians.
- [ ] P4 Peasant unrest: attacks on estates in Kharkov and Poltava provinces (1902); far wider violence in 1905–06.
- [ ] P5 Workers were mostly first-generation peasants who kept village links, spreading discontent between town and country.

The Russo-Japanese War, 1904–05
- [ ] R1 Causes: rivalry over Manchuria and Korea; Russia's lease of Port Arthur (1898); the Trans-Siberian route east; Plehve's reported wish for "a short victorious war".
- [ ] R2 Events in order: Japanese surprise attack on Port Arthur (8 February 1904); Port Arthur surrendered (January 1905); defeat at Mukden (February–March 1905); the Baltic Fleet destroyed at Tsushima (27–28 May 1905).
- [ ] R3 The Treaty of Portsmouth (5 September 1905), mediated by Theodore Roosevelt: Japan gained southern Sakhalin, the Port Arthur lease and a free hand in Korea.
- [ ] R4 Results: humiliation of the autocracy, exposure of military, transport and administrative weakness; the trigger of the 1905 revolution.

The 1905 Revolution
- [ ] V1 Bloody Sunday (22 January 1905): Father Gapon's petition procession to the Winter Palace; troops fired; the people's faith in the tsar as protector ("the little father") broken.
- [ ] V2 The spread of revolt in 1905: strikes, peasant risings, the Potemkin mutiny (June 1905), national movements in Poland and the Caucasus.
- [ ] V3 The October general strike and the St Petersburg Soviet (Trotsky a leading member).
- [ ] V4 The October Manifesto (17 October 1905, Russian calendar): civil liberties, an elected Duma with power over laws, a wider franchise; issued on Witte's advice; Witte then became the first chairman of the Council of Ministers (prime minister).
- [ ] V5 Why the regime survived: the Manifesto split the opposition (Octobrists accepted it, the Kadets wanted more, the socialists rejected it); the army stayed loyal once peace came; the Soviet's leaders arrested (December 1905) and the Moscow rising crushed (December 1905); redemption payments halved and then abolished from 1907.
- [ ] V6 The large French loan (April 1906) let the government survive without the Duma.
- [ ] V7 The Fundamental Laws (6 May 1906; 23 April Russian calendar): the tsar kept the title of autocrat, control of ministers, army and foreign policy, an absolute veto, the right to dissolve the Duma and to rule by decree between sessions.

The Dumas and Stolypin
- [ ] D1 The First Duma (1906) demanded land reform and responsible ministers and was dissolved after about ten weeks; the Second Duma (1907) also dissolved.
- [ ] D2 The changed electoral law of June 1907: the Third (1907–12) and Fourth (1912–17) Dumas dominated by landowners and the wealthy; the Duma as a place for criticism but not control.
- [ ] D3 Stolypin, Prime Minister 1906–11: repression through field courts-martial (more than a thousand executed; "Stolypin's necktie").
- [ ] D4 Stolypin's land reform (November 1906): peasants could leave the commune, own land privately and consolidate strips; the Peasant Land Bank; resettlement in Siberia; aim of a loyal class of prosperous peasants ("a wager on the strong").
- [ ] D5 Results by 1914: about two million households (about a fifth to a quarter) had left the commune; most stayed. Stolypin's call for twenty years of peace.
- [ ] D6 Stolypin's assassination by Dmitri Bogrov at the Kiev opera (September 1911).
- [ ] D7 Renewed protest: the Lena goldfields shooting (April 1912) and the strike wave to summer 1914.

The impact of World War I (key concept: world war)
- [ ] K1 Military defeat: a huge but poorly equipped army; Tannenberg and the Masurian Lakes (1914); the great retreat (1915); the Brusilov offensive (1916) succeeded at heavy cost. Fronts in one or two bullets only (detail in the notes on the conduct of the war).
- [ ] K2 Economic strain: about 15 million men mobilised, taking labour from farms; overloaded railways; inflation from printing money; food and fuel shortages in the cities by winter 1916–17; the loss of revenue when vodka sales were banned (1914).
- [ ] K3 Political failure: Nicholas II took personal command (September 1915), leaving Alexandra (German-born, so suspected) and Rasputin influential; rapid change of ministers; the Duma's Progressive Bloc (1915) demanded a government with public confidence.
- [ ] K4 Rasputin: a peasant mystic trusted by the empress because he seemed to ease the heir Alexei's haemophilia; murdered by aristocrats (December 1916), a sign of the regime's isolation.

The February Revolution, 1917
- [ ] F1 International Women's Day (8 March 1917; 23 February Russian calendar): women textile workers demanding bread; strikes became a general strike in Petrograd.
- [ ] F2 The garrison mutinied and joined the crowds instead of firing on them (the key difference from 1905).
- [ ] F3 The Duma's Provisional Committee and the revived Petrograd Soviet; the generals advised abdication.
- [ ] F4 Nicholas II abdicated (15 March 1917; 2 March Russian calendar); his brother Michael declined the throne; about 300 years of Romanov rule ended.

The Provisional Government and the October Revolution
- [ ] O1 Dual power: the Provisional Government (Lvov, then Kerensky from July) shared authority with the Petrograd Soviet; the Soviet's Order No. 1 (soldiers to obey the Provisional Government's military orders only if the Soviet agreed).
- [ ] O2 Its mistakes: it granted civil liberties but kept fighting the war and postponed land reform until a Constituent Assembly met; the failed July (Kerensky) offensive; desertions and peasant land seizures.
- [ ] O3 Lenin's return from Switzerland (April 1917, through Germany) and the April Theses: "all power to the Soviets"; "peace, land and bread".
- [ ] O4 The July Days (failed Bolshevik-backed rising; Lenin fled to Finland); the Kornilov affair (mid-September 1917; late August / early September Russian calendar), defeated with Bolshevik help, which armed the Red Guards and boosted the Bolsheviks.
- [ ] O5 Bolshevik majorities in the Petrograd and Moscow Soviets (September); Trotsky chairman of the Petrograd Soviet and organiser of its Military Revolutionary Committee.
- [ ] O6 The seizure of power (6–8 November 1917; 24–26 October Russian calendar): stations, bridges and the telegraph seized; the Winter Palace taken; the Second Congress of Soviets; the Council of People's Commissars (Sovnarkom) under Lenin.
- [ ] O7 First decrees: peace, land, the rights of the peoples of Russia to self-determination; the Cheka (December 1917).
- [ ] O8 The Constituent Assembly: SRs about 40 per cent of the vote, Bolsheviks about a quarter; met once (January 1918) and was dispersed by force.

Brest-Litovsk and the civil war, 1918–20 (in outline)
- [ ] C1 Armistice (December 1917); Trotsky's "neither war nor peace" stalling; a renewed German advance; the Treaty of Brest-Litovsk (3 March 1918).
- [ ] C2 Losses at Brest-Litovsk: Poland, the Baltic provinces, Finland and Ukraine; about a third of the population of European Russia and much of its coal, industry and farmland.
- [ ] C3 The capital moved to Moscow (March 1918); the Romanovs shot at Ekaterinburg (July 1918).
- [ ] C4 Reds vs Whites: the Red Army built by Trotsky as Commissar for War; White leaders Kolchak, Denikin, Yudenich and Wrangel; the Czech Legion revolt (May 1918); Allied intervention by Britain, France, Japan and the USA, limited in effect.
- [ ] C5 War Communism (1918–21): grain requisitioning, nationalisation of industry, rationing; the Red Terror (from September 1918).
- [ ] C6 Course to 1920: Kolchak defeated in Siberia (1919), Yudenich before Petrograd and Denikin in the south (1919–20), Wrangel's last stand in the Crimea (November 1920). One or two bullets.
- [ ] C7 Why the Reds won: the central core with railways, industry, the capitals and the largest population; one command and one aim; Trotsky's leadership and use of former tsarist officers; the Whites divided, scattered, linked to landlords and foreign powers.
- [ ] C8 The Polish–Soviet war: the Red Army's advance on Warsaw defeated (August 1920).

Key concept: self-determination
- [ ] S1 Definition (also in keyTerms): the right of a people to choose its own government and state.
- [ ] S2 Applied: the Bolshevik declaration of November 1917; Finland, Estonia, Latvia, Lithuania and Poland independent by 1920.
- [ ] S3 Denied: Ukraine and other borderlands were brought back under Bolshevik rule by the Red Army; for Lenin, self-determination was subordinate to the revolution. One bullet; Wilson's version belongs to the notes on the Peace Settlement.

Assessment: modernisation, crisis and revolution (final block, paired points)
- [ ] X1 Achievement: Witte built railways and heavy industry; growth resumed after 1908; Stolypin began to create independent farmers.
- [ ] X2 Limit: growth rested on foreign capital and a poor peasantry and created a discontented working class in huge factories.
- [ ] X3 Limit: political reform stopped short; weak Dumas; Nicholas II mistrusted reformers (Witte let go in 1906; Stolypin's position weak by 1911) and wasted the chance of 1905–06.
- [ ] X4 Why the regime survived 1905 but fell in 1917 (army loyal and opposition split in 1905; army exhausted, cities starving, soldiers joined strikers in 1917).
- [ ] X5 Long-term (economic backwardness, autocracy, peasantry) vs short-term (war) causes of 1917: a judgement on which mattered most.
- [ ] X6 Historians: "optimists" (Russia was modernising and the war caused the collapse) vs "pessimists" (revolution likely anyway; Haimson's argument from the 1912–14 strike wave); Gerschenkron on state-led industrialisation; Figes on the failure to reform; Pipes on October as a coup.
- [ ] X7 Contemporary views: liberals blamed the tsar's obstinacy; the Bolsheviks presented October as a workers' revolution; opponents called it a seizure of power by a minority party.

## 3. Traps

Do not use (wrong or doubtful facts in the current notes):
- Do not use: the Kornilov affair as "early September 1917 (late August)" or "August–September"; the Russian dates (about 25 August to 1 September) are about 7 to 14 September in Western dates. Say mid-September 1917 (late August / early September Russian calendar).
- Do not use: the Fundamental Laws as "April 1906" with no calendar; they are dated 6 May 1906 (23 April Russian calendar).
- Do not use: Leopold Haimson as an "optimist", because his best-known argument is the pessimist one (Russia was heading for revolution before 1914). Also drop Hans Rogger as an optimist "in some respects"; it is too vague to teach. Use X6.
- Do not use: the Bolsheviks took the bridges and the telegraph "and the Winter Palace fell" all on the night of 6–7 November, because the Winter Palace fell in the early hours of 8 November (26 October). Use O6.
- Do not use: "17 October 1905" without saying it is the Russian date (30 October Western). The same applies to every Russian-calendar date: always say which calendar.
- Do not use: "output of pig iron and coal rose several times" between 1890 and 1900, because "roughly tripled" is the defensible figure.
- Do not use: "the main line to Vladivostok was open by about 1904" as all-Russian; the 1904 through route ran partly across Manchuria (the Chinese Eastern Railway). Use W4.
- Do not use: "the Putilov works ... employed tens of thousands of workers" for the 1890s or 1905; it had about 12,000 in 1905. Use W7.
- Do not use: "the famine encouraged educated Russians to form ... the SRs" with the date 1903 for both; the SRs were founded 1901–02, the Union of Liberation 1903–04.
- Do not use: "the Manifesto split the opposition: liberals (Octobrists) accepted it" as if all liberals accepted; the Kadets did not fully accept it. Use V5.
- Do not use: "Witte himself recognised the risk and warned about peasant discontent"; it is vague and unsourced.
- Do not use: "Order No. 1 told soldiers to obey the Soviet's orders" without the condition; it said Provisional Government military orders were to be obeyed only if they did not conflict with the Soviet's. Use O1.
- Do not use: "Lenin signed an armistice ... and accepted Brest-Litovsk" as if Lenin signed the treaty personally; the treaty was signed by a Soviet delegation (do not name the signatory).
- Do not use: Brest-Litovsk percentages (e.g. "90 per cent of coal", "a third of farmland", "half of industry"), because figures differ between sources. Use C2 wording.
- Do not use: the Stolypin "twenty years" remark inside quotation marks; give it as a paraphrase.
- Do not use: "By 1914 about two million households (about a quarter)" as exact; say about two million, roughly a fifth to a quarter.
- Do not use: Bloody Sunday as "killing and wounding hundreds" without noting that figures vary; use V1.
- Do not use: the Romanovs "ending 300 years" as exact; say about 300 years (from 1613).

Confusable people, terms and dates
- Russian (old-style) vs Western (new-style) dates: February Revolution = March 1917; October Revolution = November 1917. Give the Western date first and the Russian date in brackets, consistently.
- Witte (Finance Minister 1892–1903; prime minister 1905–06; industrialisation and the October Manifesto) vs Stolypin (Prime Minister 1906–11; land reform and repression). Students regularly swap them.
- Vyshnegradsky (Finance Minister 1887–92, grain exports) vs Witte (his successor).
- Plehve (Interior Minister, "short victorious war", killed 1904) vs Stolypin (killed 1911).
- The October Manifesto (October 1905, promises) vs the Fundamental Laws (6 May 1906, limits that took much back).
- The Petrograd Soviet (a council of workers' and soldiers' deputies) vs the Duma (the elected parliament) vs the Provisional Government (the ministers drawn from the Duma).

## 4. What exams ask

- Higher 100-mark essay on crisis in Tsarist Russia or why the regime collapsed (the commonest Higher question on this subsection): marks go to a clear judgement in the first sentence that weighs long-term causes (backwardness, autocracy, peasant poverty, the costs of rapid industrialisation) against the short-term cause (World War I), then explains how each led to the next. Answers that narrate events from 1861 to 1917 without linking them to "crisis" or "collapse" lose marks.
- Higher essay on economic crisis and war, or on war and revolution, 1900–1920: students need two wars and two revolutions in clear order (Japan → 1905; World War I → February 1917 → October 1917 → Brest-Litovsk → civil war), with cause-and-result links at each step. Answers that stop at 1917 lose the Brest-Litovsk and civil-war marks when the question runs to 1920.
- The comparison examiners reward most: why the tsar survived 1905 but fell in 1917 (army loyalty, the October Manifesto splitting the opposition, peace with Japan, the French loan vs mutiny of the garrison, war exhaustion and food shortages, no concessions left).
- Higher essay on industry in Europe, sometimes combining Russia with Germany: students should describe Russian industrialisation as state-led (Witte, foreign loans, tariffs, railways) and explain its social result (a concentrated urban working class, peasant burden), with dated facts (1892, 1891 Trans-Siberian, 1897 gold standard, 1900–03 depression).
- Higher essay combining anti-Semitism, economic crisis and war and revolution in Russia: students need a short, dated paragraph on each strand; the anti-Semitism facts come from the notes on anti-Semitism.
- Ordinary 30-mark paragraph on Witte's industrialisation or on the economic crisis: marks go to dated, specific statements (Finance Minister 1892–1903, the Trans-Siberian begun 1891, the gold standard 1897, foreign (French) loans, indirect taxes and grain exports, the industries and regions, the 1900–03 depression), plus at least one result (growth vs the burden on the peasants and the discontent of workers).
- Ordinary paragraph on peasant conditions: emancipation 1861, redemption payments over 49 years, the mir, the 1891 famine, Stolypin's land reform, with dates.
- Cause and result points that earn marks: why Russia industrialised (to stay a Great Power, military need, railways for a vast country); why the 1891 famine mattered (discredited the government, revived opposition); why the Russo-Japanese War caused revolution; why Stolypin failed (too little time, most peasants stayed in the commune, his assassination, the tsar's distrust); why the Provisional Government fell (it continued the war and delayed land reform; Kornilov boosted the Bolsheviks); why the Reds won the civil war.
- Judgement points examiners reward, as paired points: Witte's achievement vs its cost; the October Manifesto as real reform vs a tactic taken back by the Fundamental Laws; Stolypin as reformer vs repressor; October 1917 as a popular revolution vs a coup by a minority party, naming at least one historian with their view.
- Key concepts examiners may ask students to explain: world war (how total war broke the Russian economy and state) and self-determination (the Bolshevik declaration and the new states, set against the Red Army's reconquest of borderlands).
