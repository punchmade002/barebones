## 1. Must cover (one bullet or block for every item; tick each off)

Background (assumed)
- [ ] A1 (assumed) The Troubles began in 1969 and continued to the early 1990s (and beyond); the chapter's period ends 1993. This subsection covers effects, not causes or events.
- [ ] A2 (assumed) Northern Ireland's economy before 1969: heavy dependence on shipbuilding, linen and engineering; efforts at modernisation in the 1960s (new industries, including man-made fibres).
- [ ] A3 (assumed) Catholic grievances over housing allocation and jobs were central to the civil rights movement; the Troubles then added violence to existing inequality.
- [ ] A4 (assumed) Northern Ireland was part of the UK and received public services and funding from Westminster; direct rule from 1972.

Deaths and injuries
- [ ] D1 Total deaths: about 3,000 to 1993; about 3,500 or more for the whole conflict to about 2001 (sources differ).
- [ ] D2 1972 the worst year, with nearly 500 deaths.
- [ ] D3 About half the dead were civilians; security forces and both republican and loyalist paramilitaries also died.
- [ ] D4 Tens of thousands injured; physical and psychological injury.
- [ ] D5 Concentration of violence: Belfast (especially north and west), Derry and border areas; working-class districts suffered most.
- [ ] D6 Most deaths in Northern Ireland, some in Britain and the Republic (point to the terrorism notes for detail).

The economy
- [ ] E1 Loss of foreign investment: firms reluctant to open or expand because of bombing, disruption and bad publicity.
- [ ] E2 Tourism fell sharply.
- [ ] E3 Costs for business: bomb damage, high insurance, security spending, closures and relocations; the security zone and checkpoints in central Belfast (nicknamed the "ring of steel") hurt city-centre trade.
- [ ] E4 Compensation for criminal damage paid by government, adding to public spending.
- [ ] E5 Decline of manufacturing: shipbuilding, linen, engineering and man-made fibres lost jobs (a decline that began before 1969 and was worsened by the Troubles and by wider recession).
- [ ] E6 Harland and Wolff taken into public ownership (1975) to protect jobs.
- [ ] E7 Growth of public-sector jobs (health, education, administration) and security jobs (RUC, Ulster Defence Regiment, prison service, army, private security).
- [ ] E8 British subvention: Treasury money that kept public services and living standards above what local output could pay for; exposure to changes in British public spending.
- [ ] E9 The De Lorean episode: government-funded car factory at Dunmurry; aims, opening 1981, collapse 1982, John De Lorean's arrest and acquittal, significance.
- [ ] E10 Unemployment above the UK average through the 1970s and 1980s.
- [ ] E11 Unemployment differential: Catholic unemployment consistently higher than Protestant (roughly twice as high); causes include discrimination, location (west of the province) and the sectors where Catholics worked.
- [ ] E12 Fair employment law: the Fair Employment (Northern Ireland) Act 1976 and the Fair Employment Agency; the 1989 Act and the Fair Employment Commission, with compulsory monitoring.
- [ ] E13 The MacBride Principles (1984).
- [ ] E14 Deprivation in west Belfast and Derry; unemployed young men and paramilitary recruitment (a link historians raise, not a proven single cause).

Housing and population movement
- [ ] H1 The Northern Ireland Housing Executive, 1971: took over public housing from local councils (and the Housing Trust); points-based allocation, answering the civil rights complaint of discrimination.
- [ ] H2 Intimidation and population movement: families forced from mixed areas, especially 1969–73, into areas dominated by their own community; estimates in the tens of thousands.
- [ ] H3 Peace walls (peace lines): barriers between Protestant and Catholic streets, first built in Belfast from 1969, later in Derry and elsewhere.
- [ ] H4 Segregation made communities more distinct and reduced contact; it cut across the Executive's allocation policy in practice.

Education
- [ ] ED1 Segregated schooling: most children in either Catholic maintained or mainly Protestant (controlled) schools; Church control of Catholic schools; separate teacher training (St Mary's College and Stranmillis College).
- [ ] ED2 The integrated education movement: All Children Together (1974), parent-led.
- [ ] ED3 Lagan College, Belfast, 1981: the first integrated secondary school.
- [ ] ED4 The Education Reform (Northern Ireland) Order 1989: statutory support and a duty to encourage integrated education.
- [ ] ED5 Limits: few integrated schools by 1993; dependence on parents and charity at first; most children remained in separate schools.

Health
- [ ] HL1 Trauma care: the Royal Victoria Hospital (Belfast) developed international expertise in gunshot and blast injuries.
- [ ] HL2 Heavy demands on hospital and ambulance staff; staff risk at bomb incidents.
- [ ] HL3 Psychological effects: stress, trauma and other mental health problems among those who experienced bombings, shootings, bereavement and displacement.
- [ ] HL4 Security measures and fear affected access to health care.
- [ ] HL5 Health and social services reorganised in 1973 into four boards (context, not a result of the Troubles alone).

Everyday life
- [ ] L1 Checkpoints, searches and vehicle checks in Belfast and Derry; town centres sealed or controlled after bombings, with "security gates" for shoppers.
- [ ] L2 Divided towns and estates; avoidance of the other community's areas; limited social contact.
- [ ] L3 Children growing up with soldiers, barriers and memories of violence.

Key terms (also keyTerms)
- [ ] T1 Subvention. T2 Northern Ireland Housing Executive. T3 Peace walls. T4 Fair employment. T5 Integrated education. T6 Intimidation. T7 Public-sector employment. T8 Deprivation. T9 MacBride Principles. T10 Lagan College. T11 De Lorean.

Assessment (paired judgement points, final block)
- [ ] S1 Cost: investment, tourism and manufacturing damaged; reliance on public money and the security sector.
- [ ] S2 Cost: segregation in housing and schooling deepened division.
- [ ] S3 Achievement: Housing Executive, fair employment law and integrated schools were real reforms.
- [ ] S4 Limit: the Catholic-Protestant gap in unemployment and deprivation persisted; integrated schools were few.
- [ ] S5 Contemporary views: unionists stressed the subvention and public investment as proof the state supported the province; nationalists stressed discrimination and deprivation as rooted in state policy.
- [ ] S6 Later view: the Troubles were both a cause and a consequence of inequality; the costs fell on both communities, and most heavily on working-class areas. Also note the impact was uneven: some areas, and middle-class suburbs, were largely untouched.

## 3. Traps

Do not use (wrong or doubtful facts in the current notes):
- Do not use: "Short Brothers was taken into public ownership in 1977", because Short Brothers was nationalised in 1943 (during the Second World War). Only Harland and Wolff (1975) is a Troubles-era nationalisation.
- Do not use: "Derry" as the place where peace walls were first built; they began in Belfast in 1969. Derry had some later.
- Do not use: the Dublin and Monaghan bombings as a Troubles-in-the-North example of this subsection's deaths without saying they were in the Republic; keep to one passing mention.
- Do not use: any precise death or injury figure beyond the "about" figures in section 2, and do not use a precise number of people displaced.
- Do not use: "Catholic and Protestant districts of west Belfast" as a fact about De Lorean's location without saying the site was chosen partly for its position between Catholic and Protestant housing estates; this was a stated hope, not an outcome (the factory workforce was never as mixed as hoped).
- Do not use: the man-made fibre claim with named firms or closure dates.
- Do not present the decline of manufacturing as caused only by the Troubles; it began earlier and had international causes.

Confusable people, terms and dates
- Housing Executive (1971, housing) vs Fair Employment Agency (1976, jobs) vs Fair Employment Commission (1989).
- Fair Employment Acts 1976 vs 1989: 1976 investigated and advised; 1989 required monitoring and had stronger powers.
- Lagan College (1981, first integrated secondary school) vs All Children Together (1974, the parents' campaign); the 1989 Order came later.
- De Lorean the man (arrested in the USA, acquitted) vs the company (receivership, 1982); the factory is not the same as the car; the car had a stainless-steel body.
- Subvention (money from Britain to cover the gap) vs British government security spending: related but different.
- Peace walls built to protect people vs segregation they reinforced.
- The 1973 health boards were reorganised for administrative reasons at the same time as direct rule/Stormont reform; do not claim the Troubles caused them.

## 4. What exams ask

- Higher 100-mark essay, asked repeatedly: the social and economic effects (impact) of the Troubles, sometimes combined with cultural responses. Marks go to balanced coverage of both economic and social strands (with at least two examples each), accurate dated facts and a judgement on how serious the impact was. Writing only on deaths, or only on economy, scores poorly.
- Strongest answers structure by strand: economy (investment, tourism, manufacturing, subvention, unemployment differentials), society (housing and segregation, education, health, everyday life), then assess: costs vs reforms, and the unequal impact on working-class and Catholic areas.
- Terrorism-focused variant: impact of republican and loyalist violence on society and economy; use deaths, sectarian division, damage to the economy and effect on political initiatives; keep detail on the groups for the other subsection.
- Ordinary Level 30-mark paragraph: effects of the Troubles; credit for correct, relevant statements such as about 3,000 deaths 1969–93, about half civilians, tens of thousands injured, loss of investment and tourism, high insurance costs. Each point should be stated as a plain fact with a date or figure where possible.
- Credit comes from dated reforms: Housing Executive 1971, Fair Employment Act 1976, MacBride Principles 1984, Lagan College 1981, Education Reform Order 1989. Name the date and what it did.
- Students lose marks by treating the De Lorean episode as a story; say what it shows (government willingness to subsidise investment, its failure, and the difficulty of attracting investors).
- Linking points earn credit: how unemployment fed paramilitary recruitment; how intimidation caused segregation, which fed the demand for peace walls; how subvention made the economy dependent on Britain.
- Contemporary vs later views (unionist, nationalist, historian) are rewarded as paired points in the assessment.
