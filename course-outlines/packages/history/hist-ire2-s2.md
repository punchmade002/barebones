## 4. What exams ask

- Higher 100-mark essay on land agitation and land reform, 1870–1914 (a very common title): marks go to a clear judgement stated early (for example, that reform was largely achieved) and sustained by dated points from each phase: 1870 Act, Land League and 1881, the Plan of Campaign, constructive unionism, Wyndham 1903, Birrell 1909, outcome. A narrative without judgement scores poorly.
- Strong answers separate agitation (the cause of change) from legislation (the response) and show how pressure and concession alternated, ending in tenant ownership funded by the state. Naming the weaknesses (labourers, western smallholders, the land question not solving Home Rule) earns the balance mark.
- Questions often pair land with another topic (the co-operative movement, Belfast industry, the 1913 lockout or Home Rule); in such answers each side needs its own dated evidence and a closing comparison.
- Davitt and/or Parnell on land: students need each man's specific contribution (Davitt: organising, Irishtown, Fenian link, nationalisation, later labour work; Parnell: national leadership, Ennis speech, testing the 1881 Act, American tour) and a judgement of who contributed more, or how the two complemented each other.
- Davitt as a campaigner (including compared with Larkin): organising, ideas, results; say what he achieved and how long it lasted.
- Ordinary Level 30/40-mark paragraphs on Davitt: birth in Straide, eviction, IRB and fifteen-year sentence, release in 1877, Irishtown, the Land League, a later aim (labour or nationalisation). Each correct dated statement earns marks.
- Ordinary Level questions on land legislation: who passed which Act, what it did (the Three Fs in 1881; purchase loans in 1885 and 1903), and one limit.
- Documents-based use: this subsection provides the land context for the chapter's documents (Gladstone's linking of Home Rule and land reform; unionist cartoons showing the Land Bill beside Home Rule). Students should know the dates and key figures of each Act.
