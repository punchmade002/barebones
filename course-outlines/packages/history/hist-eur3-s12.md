## 1. Must cover (one bullet or block for every item; tick each off)

Background (assumed)
- [ ] A1 (assumed) Hitler became Chancellor on 30 January 1933; by mid-1933 Germany was a one-party state. Give this in one bullet as the setting; the detail of the takeover belongs to the Nazi state notes.
- [ ] A2 (assumed) The party's own organisations that appear at the rallies: the SA (stormtroopers), the SS, the Hitler Youth, the Labour Service; one bullet naming them.
- [ ] A3 (assumed) The Munich (Beer Hall) Putsch of November 1923, the origin of the Blood Flag; one bullet.
- [ ] A4 (assumed) The Night of the Long Knives (30 June 1934): Ernst Röhm and the SA leadership were murdered, which is why the 1934 rally stressed unity and why the 1933 rally film was withdrawn. One bullet; the purge itself belongs to the Nazi state notes.
- [ ] A5 (assumed) Core Nazi ideas the propaganda repeated: the national community (Volksgemeinschaft), the leader principle, anti-communism, anti-Semitism, the "shame" of Versailles. One bullet.

Key concepts (each defined as used in this topic; also in keyTerms)
- [ ] K1 Propaganda: the organised spreading of ideas, images and information to shape opinion and win loyalty to a regime, its leader and its policies.
- [ ] K2 Personality cult: the leader presented as an infallible, heroic saviour of the nation through propaganda, ritual and his image in daily life (the Hitler cult or "Führer myth").
- [ ] K3 Totalitarianism: the state seeks to control all public and private life through one party, secret police, terror and propaganda; control of the mass media and culture was one of its main tools.
- [ ] K4 Gleichschaltung of culture ("coordination"): bringing press, radio, film and the arts into line with Nazi ideology. Define it here only as applied to culture.

Aims and methods of Nazi propaganda
- [ ] P1 Aims: win and keep consent, build the Hitler cult, create the national community, spread racial ideas and prepare Germans for rearmament and war.
- [ ] P2 Methods: simple themes repeated constantly, emotional appeal rather than argument, the mixing of entertainment with political messages, and the use of every new medium.
- [ ] P3 Propaganda worked alongside terror (the Gestapo, the camps, the courts): one bullet, details belong to the Nazi state notes.

State control of the mass media
- [ ] M1 The Ministry of Public Enlightenment and Propaganda (March 1933) under Goebbels: press, radio, film, theatre, music and the arts.
- [ ] M2 The Reich Chamber of Culture (September 1933) and its sub-chambers (press, radio, film, music, theatre, fine arts, literature): anyone working in these fields had to be a member; Jews and opponents were excluded, so exclusion meant losing one's livelihood.
- [ ] M3 The press: the Editors' Law (October 1933) made editors responsible to the state and barred Jews; daily press conferences at the ministry told papers what to print and how; Max Amann's party publishing house bought up or closed independent papers.
- [ ] M4 Named papers: the Völkischer Beobachter (party daily), Der Angriff (Goebbels's Berlin paper), Der Stürmer (Julius Streicher's anti-Semitic paper).
- [ ] M5 Radio: broadcasting taken under the ministry's control in 1933; the Volksempfänger (People's Receiver), a cheap set; loudspeakers in factories, offices and squares for communal listening to major speeches; programmes mixed light music and entertainment with speeches and news.
- [ ] M6 Wartime radio: from September 1939 listening to foreign stations such as the BBC was a crime, with heavy penalties.
- [ ] M7 Cinema: the Reich Film Chamber (1933) controlled the industry; scripts were vetted; most feature films were entertainment, but every programme carried a newsreel; from 1940 a single newsreel, the Deutsche Wochenschau.
- [ ] M8 Propaganda films: Hitler Youth Quex (1933); the anti-Semitic Jud Süß (1940) and The Eternal Jew (1940); Kolberg (1945), a costly late-war film about resistance to the end.
- [ ] M9 Posters and slogans ("Ein Volk, ein Reich, ein Führer"), the "Heil Hitler" greeting, Hitler's portrait in public places, his birthday as a public celebration, photograph books by Heinrich Hoffmann.

Books, art and the Olympics
- [ ] B1 Book burning, 10 May 1933: students and party members burned books by Jewish, Marxist, liberal and pacifist authors in Berlin and other university towns; Goebbels spoke in Berlin; named authors from section 2; Heine's warning (section 2).
- [ ] B2 Degenerate art: modern art (Expressionism, abstract art) condemned and removed from galleries; the Degenerate Art exhibition, Munich, July 1937, beside the Great German Art Exhibition of approved art; named artists from section 2; the degenerate exhibition drew far more visitors.
- [ ] B3 Music: jazz condemned as "degenerate"; works by Jewish composers banned (one bullet, no names needed).
- [ ] B4 The 1936 Olympics (Winter Games at Garmisch-Partenkirchen in February, Summer Games in Berlin in August): a showcase of a peaceful, modern, efficient Germany; anti-Semitic signs removed for the games; at the Summer Games in Berlin, Germany won the most medals (Norway, not Germany, topped the Winter Games table); Jesse Owens's four gold medals as a limit on the racial message.

Propaganda in war
- [ ] W1 Propaganda in wartime: early victories celebrated in newsreels; after Stalingrad (early 1943) the message shifted to endurance and fear of the enemy, and credibility declined as defeats mounted.
- [ ] W2 The total war speech: Berlin Sportpalast, 18 February 1943, after Stalingrad; broadcast nationally.

Key personality: Joseph Goebbels (one block, NOTES_STANDARD order)
- [ ] G1 Who he was: born 1897 in Rheydt, Rhineland; a club foot kept him out of the army in the First World War; a doctorate in literature (Heidelberg); failed as a writer and journalist.
- [ ] G2 Joined the Nazi party in the mid-1920s, first on Gregor Strasser's more "socialist" northern wing, then won over by Hitler (1926).
- [ ] G3 Gauleiter of Berlin from 1926: street fights with the communists, mass meetings, his paper Der Angriff (from 1927); made the dead SA man Horst Wessel (1930) a martyr, his song becoming the party anthem.
- [ ] G4 Party propaganda chief from 1930: the election campaigns of 1930–1932, including Hitler's flights across Germany in the 1932 campaigns; posters, films, records, loudspeaker vans. One bullet; the campaigns themselves belong to the notes on the rise of Hitler.
- [ ] G5 Minister of Propaganda from March 1933 and head of the Reich Chamber of Culture; organiser of the book burning; a driving force behind the Kristallnacht pogrom (November 1938) with his Munich speech (one bullet, cross-refer to the notes on anti-Semitism and the Holocaust).
- [ ] G6 War: the total war speech (1943); Plenipotentiary for Total War (July 1944); named Reich Chancellor in Hitler's will; with his wife Magda poisoned their six children and killed himself in the Berlin bunker, 1 May 1945.
- [ ] G7 Participant: he directed the propaganda machine rather than witnessed it; he claimed the creation of the Führer myth as his greatest achievement; his diaries are a major source.
- [ ] G8 How contemporaries saw him: a brilliant, cynical speaker and organiser, loyal to Hitler; disliked and distrusted by rivals in the leadership (Göring, Himmler, Ribbentrop, Rosenberg); his power over culture was never complete.
- [ ] G9 How historians judge him: the most skilled propagandist of the age, but propaganda worked best when it reinforced existing beliefs (nationalism, anti-communism, the Hitler cult) and less well when it tried to change them; his influence fell in the late 1930s and rose again from 1943.

Key personality: Leni Riefenstahl (one block, NOTES_STANDARD order)
- [ ] R1 Who she was: born 1902 in Berlin; dancer, then actress in "mountain films"; directed and starred in The Blue Light (1932).
- [ ] R2 Admired Hitler, met him in 1932, and was commissioned by him to film the party rallies, with a large crew and resources.
- [ ] R3 Her rally films: Victory of Faith (1933 rally, withdrawn after Röhm's murder), Triumph of the Will (1935, of the 1934 rally), Day of Freedom (1935, a short film on the army at the 1935 rally).
- [ ] R4 Triumph of the Will: technique (opening with Hitler's plane descending through clouds over Nuremberg, moving cameras, low angles, the leader set against massed ranks, no narrator, music by Herbert Windt); its message of unity behind Hitler after the purge.
- [ ] R5 Olympia (1938), in two parts, on the 1936 Berlin Olympics: new camera techniques (underwater, tracking, slow motion); premiered on Hitler's birthday.
- [ ] R6 Contemporary reception: the German National Film Prize (1935) and a gold medal at the Paris World Exhibition (1937) for Triumph of the Will; praised as art at home and abroad, while foreign critics saw its propaganda purpose.
- [ ] R7 During the war: Tiefland, for which Sinti and Roma were taken from an internment camp as extras; released 1954.
- [ ] R8 After 1945: interned and put through several denazification hearings; classified as a "fellow traveller" (Mitläufer), not a major offender; she always claimed her films were art or documentary, not propaganda, and that she knew nothing of the crimes; later photographed the Nuba of Sudan; died in 2003, aged 101.
- [ ] R9 Participant or witness: a willing participant who gave the regime its most lasting images; how historians and critics judge her: technically brilliant and influential, but her claim to be non-political is widely rejected (Susan Sontag's 1975 essay "Fascinating Fascism").

Case study: the Nuremberg Rallies — background
- [ ] N1 What they were: the annual party congress (Reichsparteitag); earlier rallies in Munich (1923) and Weimar (1926); Nuremberg from 1927 and 1929, then every September 1933–1938, each lasting about a week.
- [ ] N2 Why Nuremberg: a medieval imperial city, a symbol of German history and greatness, linking the Third Reich to the First (the Holy Roman Empire); central position and good railway links; strong local party under Julius Streicher (Gauleiter of Franconia); open land for vast grounds.
- [ ] N3 The rally grounds, south-east of the city around the Dutzendteich lake: the Luitpold Arena (SA and SS assemblies, with the war memorial Hall of Honour); the Zeppelin Field with Speer's grandstand (modelled on the Pergamon Altar); the Congress Hall (begun, never finished); the Great Road; the German Stadium (planned, never built).
- [ ] N4 Albert Speer: the young architect who designed the rally grounds and staging and won Hitler's favour. One bullet; his later career belongs to the Nazi state notes.
- [ ] N5 Staging: monumental stone architecture, huge swastika banners and eagles, massed standards, torchlight processions, bands and drums, the "cathedral of light" (anti-aircraft searchlights pointed into the night sky around the Zeppelin Field, figures in section 2).

Case study — the rallies in order, 1933–1939
- [ ] N6 1933: the Rally of Victory, celebrating the seizure of power; filmed as Victory of Faith.
- [ ] N7 1934: the first rally after the Night of the Long Knives; staged to show unity of party, SA, SS, Labour Service and army behind Hitler; Hitler's reassurance to the SA; filmed as Triumph of the Will. Give no official name for this rally (see Traps).
- [ ] N8 1935: the Rally of Freedom (celebrating the return of conscription and rearmament); the Reichstag was summoned to Nuremberg and passed the Nuremberg Laws on 15 September 1935, and the swastika flag became the national flag. Cross-refer to the notes on anti-Semitism and the Holocaust for the laws' content.
- [ ] N9 1936: the Rally of Honour (after the remilitarisation of the Rhineland, March 1936); the Four Year Plan was proclaimed at the rally.
- [ ] N10 1937: the Rally of Labour.
- [ ] N11 1938: the Rally of Greater Germany, celebrating the Anschluss; Hitler's closing speech attacked Czechoslovakia over the Sudetenland weeks before the Munich Agreement.
- [ ] N12 1939: the planned Rally of Peace was cancelled because of the invasion of Poland and the outbreak of war.

Case study — the programme and the experience of participants
- [ ] N13 The programme: days set aside for particular groups (political leaders, the Labour Service, the Hitler Youth, the SA and SS, the armed forces); Hitler's opening proclamation and closing speech; parades through the old city.
- [ ] N14 Ritual: the 1934 walk of Hitler, Himmler and SA chief Viktor Lutze between the ranks at the Luitpold Arena to the war memorial; the consecration of new standards by touching them with the Blood Flag; oaths of loyalty; the Labour Service with spades; a quasi-religious atmosphere.
- [ ] N15 Participants: hundreds of thousands came by special trains and lived in huge tent camps run with strict rules (passes to leave the grounds, no open fires, silence at night, report sickness); long days of drilling, marching and waiting; for many a powerful experience of comradeship and belonging, for others exhausting.
- [ ] N16 Contemporary witnesses: German participants' enthusiasm; foreign diplomats and journalists such as William Shirer (who saw the 1934 rally) and Charles Bewley, Irish Minister in Berlin; the British ambassador Nevile Henderson's "cathedral of ice". These are the kinds of documents the exam uses: judge them by who wrote them and their attitude to the regime.

Case study — purpose, effect and significance
- [ ] N17 Purpose: display unity, order and strength; build the Hitler cult; bind the individual to the national community; announce policies (Nuremberg Laws, Four Year Plan, foreign policy claims); impress and intimidate foreign opinion.
- [ ] N18 Reach: radio, newsreels, the press and Riefenstahl's films carried the rallies to millions who never attended.
- [ ] N19 Afterwards: the US Army blew up the swastika on the Zeppelin Field grandstand in April 1945; the Nuremberg war crimes trials (1945–46) were held in the city partly for its symbolism (one bullet; cross-refer); the unfinished Congress Hall now holds a Documentation Centre (2001).

Assessment (final block, paired points)
- [ ] J1 Achievements vs limits: the Hitler cult and genuine popularity in the 1930s, the rallies' spectacle and the films' reach vs propaganda's failure to win over many workers and churchgoers, its dependence on terror, boredom with constant political content (hence so much entertainment), and its collapse in credibility as the war was lost.
- [ ] J2 Contemporary view (awe at order and unity; many foreigners impressed) vs later view (manipulation, the link between the rallies and racial laws and war; Riefenstahl's films studied as the classic example of film propaganda).
- [ ] J3 How far propaganda explains support: historians (Ian Kershaw) stress that propaganda reinforced existing beliefs and that economic recovery, foreign policy success and fear also mattered; reports from the exiled Social Democrats (Sopade) show grumbling and limits to belief.
- [ ] J4 Goebbels vs Riefenstahl as contributors: the organiser who controlled what Germans saw and heard vs the artist who created the regime's most lasting images (an exam favourite).

## 3. Traps

Do not use (wrong or doubtful facts in the current notes):
- Do not use: the 1934 rally held "4 to 10 September", because the opening date is given differently in sources. Say "September 1934".
- Do not use: the cathedral of light "first used in 1934" or "about 130 searchlights" as firm facts, because sources disagree on both the first year (1934 or 1936) and the number (about 130 to 150). Use the section 2 wording. The current notes also say this twice in different blocks; say it once.
- Do not use: the rally grounds "to the south of the old city", because they lie to the south-east, around the Dutzendteich.
- Do not use: Hitler's speeches "usually delivered at night or in the evening when crowds were most receptive", because it is an unchecked generalisation (his main rally speeches were at various times). Name only the night-time ceremonies (the political leaders' assembly with the cathedral of light, torchlight processions).
- Do not use: "free food and accommodation" or "much of the cost was met by the party and by industry", because neither is checked. Say participants lived in tent camps and came on special trains.
- Do not use: "Germany won the most medals" in 1936 without saying which Games, because it is true only of the Berlin Summer Games; Norway topped the Winter Games table. Say "at the Summer Games in Berlin, Germany won the most medals".
- Do not use: the armed forces' day "from 1935", because army units took part in 1934 too. Say the armed forces had their own day, with no first year.
- Do not use: Riefenstahl "classified as a fellow traveller in 1952", because the hearings ran from 1948 to 1952 and sources differ on which ruling counts. Give no year.
- Do not use: Goebbels joined the party "in 1924", because sources give 1924 or 1925. Say "in the mid-1920s".
- Do not use: the fact card "three films: Triumph of the Will and the two parts of Olympia", because it ignores Victory of Faith and Day of Freedom. Name her films from section 2.
- Do not use: the Editors' Law "required editors to be members of Nazi-approved professional organisations" as its main point, because the key points were that editors became responsible to the state and Jews were barred. Use the section 2 and M3 wording.
- Do not use: "directed the Nazi response to the Jewish population through the media", because it is vague. Use his role in anti-Semitic films and Kristallnacht (G5).
- Do not use: listening to foreign broadcasts "punishable by prison or, in serious cases, death" with no qualification. Say "a crime with heavy penalties"; some people were executed for spreading what they heard.

Other errors and doubtful claims to avoid:
- No attendance figures for any rally, no radio-ownership figures, no number of films made: none is checked. Say "hundreds of thousands" for rally participants.
- Don't put Goebbels's name or wording on the Heine quotation: it is Heine's, from 1821, and it is now inscribed at the book-burning memorial in Berlin.
- Don't say Riefenstahl was a party member or that Goebbels commissioned her films: Hitler commissioned her, and she and Goebbels were on poor terms.
- Don't say the Nuremberg Laws were written for the rally long in advance or debate their drafting: give only that they were passed by the Reichstag summoned to Nuremberg during the 1935 rally.
- Don't invent quotations from Goebbels or Hitler; the only quotations allowed are the slogan, Heine's line, "Do you want total war?" and Henderson's "cathedral of ice".
- Don't copy the camp rules or document wording from past papers; describe the kind of rule (passes, fire safety, silence at night, reporting sickness) in your own words.

## 4. What exams ask

- Goebbels and Riefenstahl compared (Higher, 100 marks): top answers contrast the organiser and controller (ministry, chamber, press, radio, book burning, total war) with the artist and image-maker (Triumph of the Will, Olympia), then judge whose contribution mattered more and why, including the limits of each.
- Propaganda and terror together (Higher, 100 marks, often Hitler with Mussolini and/or Stalin): marks come for pairing a propaganda method with a terror method and judging which kept the dictator in power; Nazi examples need named institutions and dates (ministry 1933, Volksempfänger, rallies, Gestapo and camps from the Nazi state notes).
- Cinema and radio as mass media (Higher, 100 marks; with Chaplin and Crosby): Riefenstahl's films, newsreels, the People's Receiver and wartime radio are the German examples; answers must show how the medium was used, not just list films.
- The Nuremberg Rallies documents question (Higher and Ordinary): comprehension (what a document says about the crowd, Hitler's appeal, or order in the camps); comparison (do two documents agree, which gives a clearer picture of a rally); criticism (is a diplomat's or journalist's account objective; is a photograph or camp rule sheet a primary source, and what are its strengths and limits); contextualisation (40 marks Higher, 20 Ordinary) on the rallies and/or Goebbels. Practise: author, date, purpose, bias, and what a source cannot show.
- Ordinary Level long answers (40 marks) on the rallies: the themes and programme, the ritual (Blood Flag, Luitpold Arena walk, cathedral of light), Triumph of the Will, the 1935 Nuremberg Laws; and on Goebbels's career and methods.
- Ordinary Level short questions on a propaganda image or cartoon: identify the message (the Aryan ideal, the Hitler cult) or the satire; and "who made Triumph of the Will and what did it record" (Riefenstahl; the 1934 rally).
- Judgement wording to use: "how effective", "to what extent", "achievements and limits". Reward goes to answers that weigh genuine enthusiasm against compulsion, entertainment against political content, and the 1930s peak against wartime decline, citing a historian's view (Kershaw) and contemporary evidence (Sopade reports, foreign witnesses).
- Cause and result: why the regime staged rallies and controlled the media (aims) and what resulted (the Hitler cult, the publicising of racial law, foreign impressions); state each with a specific example and a date.
