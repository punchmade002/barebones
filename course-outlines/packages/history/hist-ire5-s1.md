## 1. Must cover (one bullet or block for every item; tick each off)

Background (assumed)
- [ ] A1 (assumed) Partition: the Government of Ireland Act (1920) created Northern Ireland (six counties) with its own parliament; the Unionist Party ruled from 1921.
- [ ] A2 (assumed) Two communities: mostly Protestant unionists (loyal to the Union and Crown) and mostly Catholic nationalists (who wanted a united Ireland); Catholics were about a third of the population.
- [ ] A3 (assumed) The Welfare State in Britain: the Beveridge Report (1942) and the Labour government's reforms of 1945–48, which Northern Ireland followed.

Key concepts
- [ ] K1 Gerrymandering: drawing electoral boundaries to give one group more seats than its votes justify (Derry the standard example).
- [ ] K2 Sectarianism: hostility and discrimination between Protestants and Catholics, tied to political allegiance.
- [ ] K3 Discrimination (as context): unequal treatment of Catholics in housing allocation, jobs and local voting.
- [ ] K4 Welfare state and parity (essential terms, also in keyTerms); eleven-plus; business vote.

Constitutional position and the political system
- [ ] C1 Republic of Ireland Act (in force 18 April 1949) and the Ireland Act (1949): the consent guarantee.
- [ ] C2 Permanent unionist majority at Stormont (1921–72); the nationalist opposition; the February 1949 election fought on the border.
- [ ] C3 Local government: end of proportional representation (1922), first-past-the-post, the business vote and ratepayer franchise.
- [ ] C4 The Flags and Emblems (Display) Act (1954).

Brookeborough's premiership, 1943–63
- [ ] B1 Dates and background: Prime Minister 1943–63; succeeded by Terence O'Neill; Viscount Brookeborough from 1952.
- [ ] B2 His attitudes: loyalty to the Union and Crown, stability first, no reform of local government or franchise; his 1930s speeches urging employers to hire Protestants.
- [ ] B3 The security state: the Special Powers Act (1922), the RUC, the B Specials; almost wholly Protestant.
- [ ] B4 The Nationalist Party: abstentionism, accepted the role of official opposition only in 1965; weak opposition.

The welfare state
- [ ] W1 Parity: National Insurance, unemployment benefit and pensions on a UK basis; the Social Services Agreement (1949); British Treasury paid the gap.
- [ ] W2 Health: the National Health Service began 5 July 1948.
- [ ] W3 Education Act (Northern Ireland) 1947: free secondary education; tripartite system; eleven-plus.
- [ ] W4 Education and religion: voluntary (mostly Catholic) schools kept management and received a capital grant; schools stayed segregated.
- [ ] W5 Effect on Catholics: wider access to grammar school and university, the beginnings of an educated Catholic middle class.
- [ ] W6 Housing: the Northern Ireland Housing Trust (1945) and its points system; council housing allocated by local councils.
- [ ] W7 Housing discrimination and segregation: unionist councils favouring Protestant families; separate estates.
- [ ] W8 Derry: Londonderry Corporation and the 1936 ward boundaries as the gerrymandering example.
- [ ] W9 Limits of the welfare state: Catholics benefited from the same services but discrimination in allocation and jobs continued.

Social and economic developments
- [ ] E1 Decline of shipbuilding (Harland and Wolff) and linen.
- [ ] E2 Unemployment above the UK level; emigration.
- [ ] E3 New industry (man-made fibres, Short Brothers, government grants), sited in Belfast and east of the Bann; the largely Catholic west (Derry) neglected.
- [ ] E4 Agriculture still a large employer, supported by British subsidies.

Discrimination and sectarianism
- [ ] D1 Under-representation of Catholics in the RUC, senior civil service, local-council posts and some large private employers.
- [ ] D2 The two interpretations: nationalists (systematic discrimination) v unionists (exaggerated; security needs).
- [ ] D3 Sectarianism in daily life: segregated schools, housing and workplaces; Orange Order and Ancient Order of Hibernians; parades and flags; mixed marriage uncommon.

The IRA border campaign, 1956–62
- [ ] I1 Operation Harvest: start (December 1956), tactics, aim (end partition by force).
- [ ] I2 The Brookeborough barracks raid (1 January 1957): Seán South and Feargal O'Hanlon, martyrs.
- [ ] I3 Responses: internment in the North and in the Republic (1957); the campaign lacked nationalist support.
- [ ] I4 End: called off in February 1962; consequences for republican strategy (left-wing turn).

Assessment (paired judgement points, final block)
- [ ] S1 Success: welfare reforms raised living standards for both communities; stability; the IRA defeated.
- [ ] S2 Failure: discrimination and gerrymandering untouched; weak economy; no outreach to Catholics.
- [ ] S3 Contemporary views: unionists v nationalists.
- [ ] S4 Later view: real welfare gains, but no power sharing or reform, so the grievances fed the 1960s civil rights movement.

## 3. Traps

Do not use (wrong or doubtful facts in the current notes):
- Do not use: "state scholarships" or the claim that the 1947 Act itself created university grants, because the specific mechanism is uncertain. Say only that state awards helped more Catholics reach university.
- Do not use: "the highest unemployment of any UK region for the whole period" because it varied; say "well above the UK average".
- Do not use: "ended in the late 1960s" for the business vote as a single date; say reforms from 1969 removed it.
- Do not use: that the tricolour was named in the 1954 Act; the Act applied to any flag, the tricolour was the target in practice.
- Do not use: the Catholic education grant as 65 per cent without the word "capital"; it was a capital grant only.
- Do not use: "Brooke retired because of the border campaign" or any link; no evidence.
- Do not use: "the Belfast riots of 1920 to 1922 killed hundreds" as a main point; it is pre-1949 background only.
