## 1. Must cover (one bullet or block for every item; tick each off)

Background (assumed)
- [ ] A1 (assumed) Both states were new in 1920–22 and each used culture to build or defend an identity: the Free State a Gaelic and Catholic one, Northern Ireland a Protestant and British one.
- [ ] A2 (assumed) The Gaelic revival before 1914 (Gaelic League, GAA) gave the Free State its cultural programme; the Church was the dominant institution in the South, and the Protestant minority was small after 1922.
- [ ] A3 (assumed) Partition (1920–22) and the Free State and Northern Ireland governments; the Catholic minority in the North, about a third of its population.

Key concepts (each defined as used in this topic; also in keyTerms)
- [ ] K1 Conformity: pressure to follow the accepted Catholic and Gaelic values, enforced through law, schools and public opinion.
- [ ] K2 Censorship: state control of what could be shown or read, by film and publications censors with power to ban.
- [ ] K3 Discrimination: unequal treatment of one group; in the North, of Catholics, including in school funding and control.

The South: the Irish language
- [ ] L1 Compulsory Irish in national schools (1922); infant classes taught through Irish.
- [ ] L2 Irish tied to jobs and exams: required for State exams and for the civil service and Garda; Blythe's role.
- [ ] L3 The Gaeltacht Commission (set up 1925, reported 1926).
- [ ] L4 An Gúm (1926).
- [ ] L5 Taibhdhearc na Gaillimhe (1928) and preparatory colleges for teachers.
- [ ] L6 Irish as first official language in the 1937 constitution.
- [ ] L7 Results of the language policy: more people with some Irish, little daily use, Gaeltacht still shrinking, resentment, symbolic value kept.

The South: education and the Church
- [ ] E1 Church management of national schools; state pays salaries and sets the curriculum.
- [ ] E2 School Attendance Act (1926).
- [ ] E3 Fee-paying secondary schools run by religious orders; limited access for the poor.
- [ ] E4 Vocational Education Act (1930).
- [ ] E5 Church influence on the content of teaching; McQuaid (Archbishop of Dublin from 1940).

The South: the Catholic ethos, censorship and conformity
- [ ] C1 Censorship of Films Act (1923).
- [ ] C2 The divorce ban: Dáil standing order (1925), Yeats's speech in the Seanad, Article 41 (1937).
- [ ] C3 Censorship of Publications Act (1929) and banned writers (Beckett, O'Brien, Ó Faoláin).
- [ ] C4 Public Dance Halls Act (1935).
- [ ] C5 Criminal Law Amendment Act (1935): contraceptives.
- [ ] C6 Article 44 of the 1937 constitution: the special position of the Catholic Church.
- [ ] C7 The Protestant minority: decline in numbers and sense of exclusion.

The South: cultural institutions and identity
- [ ] I1 The Tailteann Games (1924, 1928, 1932).
- [ ] I2 The Abbey Theatre subsidy (1925); the Gate Theatre (1928).
- [ ] I3 2RN (1926), Athlone (1932), Radio Éireann (1938).
- [ ] I4 The Irish Folklore Commission (1935) and the Schools' Collection (1937–38).
- [ ] I5 The GAA: Rule 27 and the Hyde affair (1938).
- [ ] I6 The ideal of a Gaelic and Catholic Ireland and who it left out.

The North
- [ ] N1 The Lynn Committee (1921) and the Education Act (NI) 1923 (Londonderry Act): aims.
- [ ] N2 Protestant objections; the 1925 amendment (Bible instruction).
- [ ] N3 Catholic refusal to transfer schools; partial funding; the 1930 amendment.
- [ ] N4 Result: a divided school system; discrimination as a grievance.
- [ ] N5 The Protestant ethos: Orange Order parades, loyalist bands, Union Jack, Stormont ceremonies; Craig's 1934 remark.
- [ ] N6 Nationalist culture: GAA, Irish language, Catholic schools; little state support; the Special Powers Act and bans on processions and emblems.

Key personality: Evie Hone (one block, every point)
- [ ] H1 Who she was: born Dublin 1894; art studies in London and Paris (Lhote, Gleizes); with Mainie Jellett one of the first Irish cubist painters.
- [ ] H2 What she did: stained glass from the 1930s, An Túr Gloine (founded 1903 by Sarah Purser).
- [ ] H3 Converted to Catholicism 1937; church commissions.
- [ ] H4 Key works: My Four Green Fields (1939); the Eton College east window (commissioned 1949, installed 1952).
- [ ] H5 Shaped by events: patronage of church and state; her place in the effort to give independent Ireland a modern identity.
- [ ] H6 Contemporaries and later historians: praise as modern Irish art of international standing.

Assessment (paired judgement points, final block)
- [ ] S1 Achievement: cultural institutions and arts gave the new state a distinct identity and preserved Irish culture.
- [ ] S2 Limit: Irish not revived as the daily language; the Gaeltacht declined.
- [ ] S3 Limit: censorship and moral legislation narrowed life; minorities excluded; conformity.
- [ ] S4 North: divided schools and separate cultures deepened communal division; contrast with the South (each state promoted its own community's identity).
- [ ] S5 Contemporary vs later views: defenders of Irish identity vs critics (Ó Faoláin) and later historians stressing the tension between revival and restriction and the Church's influence.

## 3. Traps

Do not use (wrong or doubtful facts in the current notes):
- Do not use: Hone "learned from Wilhelmina Geddes", because Hone trained in An Túr Gloine under Catherine O'Brien; the link to Geddes is doubtful.
- Do not use: *My Four Green Fields* "won first prize for stained glass", because the prize is not certain. Say it was made for the 1939 New York World's Fair.
- Do not use: "Historians such as Nicola Gordon Bowe place her...", because the attribution to this historian is not checked. Say "later writers on Irish art" or give the view without a name.
- Do not use: the Lynn Committee "reported in 1923" as a flat statement, because it issued reports in 1922 and 1923; give "reported in 1922–23".
- Do not use: "Craig's statement of 1934 that Northern Ireland was a Protestant state" as a paraphrase in quotation marks; use only the short phrase in section 2, attributed to Craig.
- Do not use: a "first prize" or any exact census figure beyond section 2; do not give the Protestant fall as a percentage.
- Do not use: "Irish became compulsory in secondary schools in 1922". The 1922 change was in national schools; second-level requirements came later.

## 4. What exams ask

- Higher 100-mark essay (2006, 2007, 2009, 2020, 2025 style): to what extent did governments in North and South use language, religion and education to promote a cultural identity, and was it a narrow one. Marks go to a judgement stated at the start, balanced between North and South, and developed with dated facts: Irish in schools and the civil service, the Gaeltacht Commission, censorship acts, the 1937 constitution, the Londonderry Act and its amendments.
- A strong answer states the identity promoted in each state (Gaelic and Catholic; Protestant and British) and explains that each excluded a minority: Protestants and urban people in the South, Catholics in the North.
- Language policy (2010, 2017, 2023 style): list measures with dates, then assess results (more knowledge of Irish, little everyday use, Gaeltacht decline). Say whether the policy succeeded and why it fell short; include the symbolic role of Irish.
- Conformity and censorship: link each law to the Catholic ethos with its date (1923 films, 1929 publications, 1935 contraceptives and dance halls, divorce 1925 and 1937); name a banned writer as evidence.
- Ordinary Level 30-mark paragraph on Evie Hone (set in nearly every year): birth and education (Dublin 1894; London and Paris; Lhote and Gleizes), cubism with Jellett, stained glass from the 1930s with An Túr Gloine, and a named work with a date (My Four Green Fields, Eton window). Each correct dated fact earns marks.
- Ordinary Level short answers and the 40-mark paragraph: name or date a language measure (compulsory Irish 1922, the Gaeltacht Commission 1925–26, An Gúm 1926), name the new radio station (2RN, 1926), and state two ways each state promoted culture.
- A common weakness: writing only about the South. Examiners reward the Northern education dispute (1923, 1925, 1930) and loyalist culture as the paired half.
- Document questions (as in 2015 on censorship) expect students to explain why Church and state wanted control over reading and cinema; use the censorship acts and the Catholic ethos to place the document.
