## 1. Must cover (one bullet or block for every item; tick each off)

Background (assumed knowledge)
- [ ] X1 (assumed) Hitler appointed Chancellor 30 January 1933 at the head of a coalition with only a few Nazi ministers; Papen and the conservatives expected to control him. One bullet only; the rise belongs to the Weimar notes.
- [ ] X2 (assumed) The Treaty of Versailles terms Hitler set out to overturn: army limited to 100,000, no conscription, no air force, demilitarised Rhineland, Saar under the League, union with Austria forbidden, the Polish Corridor and Danzig. One or two bullets only.
- [ ] X3 (assumed) Mein Kampf (1925–26) as the statement of Hitler's aims: overturn Versailles, unite all Germans, win Lebensraum in the east, racial struggle. One bullet.

Consolidating power, 1933–1934
- [ ] C1 The Reichstag fire (27 February 1933), van der Lubbe, and the Decree for the Protection of People and State (28 February) suspending civil liberties; mass arrests of Communists.
- [ ] C2 The March 1933 election: the Nazis short of a majority on their own, despite terror and propaganda.
- [ ] C3 The Enabling Act (23 March 1933): laws by the cabinet without the Reichstag for four years; Communist deputies absent; only the Social Democrats voted against.
- [ ] C4 Gleichschaltung (coordination): the states (Reich governors 1933; state parliaments abolished January 1934), the civil service (the April 1933 law removing Jews and opponents), courts, universities, culture.
- [ ] C5 Trade unions: offices seized 2 May 1933; replaced by the German Labour Front under Robert Ley.
- [ ] C6 The one-party state: the law of 14 July 1933 making the Nazi Party the only legal party.

The Night of the Long Knives and the Führer state
- [ ] N1 The SA problem: Röhm, several million members, the "second revolution", the wish to absorb the army; the army, conservatives and Papen's Marburg speech (June 1934) pressing Hitler.
- [ ] N2 The purge, 30 June–2 July 1934: Röhm and SA leaders; also Strasser, Schleicher and other opponents; the SS and Gestapo carried it out; number killed uncertain.
- [ ] N3 Retrospective legalisation (3 July 1934); the army's acceptance in return for the removal of the SA as a rival.
- [ ] N4 Hindenburg's death (2 August 1934); Hitler as Führer and Reich Chancellor; the army's personal oath to Hitler; the plebiscite approving it.
- [ ] N5 The Führer principle and polycracy: decisions flowed from Hitler, while rival party and state agencies competed for his favour.

Terror and the police state
- [ ] P1 Himmler and the SS (Reichsführer-SS from 1929; Chief of German Police 1936); Heydrich and the SD.
- [ ] P2 The Gestapo: founded in Prussia under Göring (1933), under Himmler and Heydrich from 1934; arrest without trial ("protective custody"); reliance on informers and denunciations.
- [ ] P3 The camps: Dachau (March 1933) the first SS camp and model for others; Sachsenhausen (1936), Buchenwald (1937); the first prisoners were political opponents, later also Jehovah's Witnesses, "asocials", homosexuals and Jews.
- [ ] P4 The courts: Special Courts (1933) and the People's Court (1934) for political cases.

The economy
- [ ] E1 Hjalmar Schacht: Reichsbank president 1933, Economics Minister 1934; Mefo bills to hide rearmament spending; the New Plan (1934) of bilateral trade deals and exchange controls.
- [ ] E2 Public works and job creation: autobahns (first section 1935), housing, military building; the Reich Labour Service (compulsory from 1935).
- [ ] E3 The fall in unemployment: about six million registered early 1933, under one million by 1937; labour shortages by the late 1930s; the hidden side (women and Jews removed from the figures, conscription, the Labour Service, wages held down, no free unions).
- [ ] E4 The Four Year Plan (1936) under Göring: ready for war in four years; autarky (synthetic oil and rubber, the Reichswerke Hermann Göring); guns before butter.
- [ ] E5 Schacht's resignation as Economics Minister (1937) over the pace of rearmament; the economy strained by 1939.

The Volksgemeinschaft: workers, youth, women and outsiders
- [ ] V1 The Volksgemeinschaft idea: a racially pure national community above class.
- [ ] V2 Workers: the German Labour Front; Strength Through Joy (holidays, cruises, theatre); Beauty of Labour; the Volkswagen savings scheme that delivered no cars to savers.
- [ ] V3 Youth: the Hitler Youth under Baldur von Schirach (Jungvolk, Hitler Youth, League of German Girls); the Hitler Youth Law (1936) and compulsory membership (1939); schools, rewritten textbooks, racial science, the Nazi Teachers' League.
- [ ] V4 Women: wife and mother; marriage loans (1933) with a quarter cancelled per child; the Mother's Cross (instituted December 1938); women pushed out of the civil service and higher professions, then drawn back into work by labour shortages in the late 1930s.
- [ ] V5 Outsiders excluded: forced sterilisation (law of July 1933); Jews excluded (one bullet naming the Nuremberg Laws of 1935; detail belongs to the Holocaust notes); opponents and "asocials" in camps.
- [ ] V6 Consent as well as terror: popularity of jobs, order and foreign successes; limited, mostly passive opposition (one bullet; church opposition belongs to the church–state notes).

Hitler's foreign policy, 1933–1935
- [ ] F1 Aims: overturn Versailles, unite German speakers in one Reich, Lebensraum in the east; caution while Germany was weak.
- [ ] F2 Leaving the League and the Disarmament Conference (14 October 1933).
- [ ] F3 The German-Polish non-aggression pact (January 1934).
- [ ] F4 The failed Austrian Nazi coup and the murder of Dollfuss (July 1934); Mussolini moved troops to the Brenner; Hitler backed off.
- [ ] F5 The Saar plebiscite (January 1935).
- [ ] F6 Rearmament in the open (March 1935): the Luftwaffe revealed; conscription announced 16 March.
- [ ] F7 The Stresa Front (April 1935) and its collapse over Abyssinia (one clause; Abyssinia belongs to the Italy notes).
- [ ] F8 The Anglo-German Naval Agreement (June 1935): 35 per cent; France not consulted; Versailles broken with British consent.

Expansion and the road to war, 1936–1939
- [ ] R1 The Rhineland (7 March 1936): breach of Versailles and Locarno; no French or British military response.
- [ ] R2 Alignment with Italy and Japan: the Rome–Berlin Axis (1936), the Anti-Comintern Pact (1936, Italy joining 1937); the Condor Legion in Spain (1936–39).
- [ ] R3 The Hossbach meeting (5 November 1937): Lebensraum by force by 1943–45 at the latest.
- [ ] R4 The Blomberg–Fritsch dismissals (February 1938): Hitler takes direct command of the armed forces; Ribbentrop Foreign Minister.
- [ ] R5 The Anschluss (12 March 1938): Schuschnigg, the cancelled plebiscite, German troops, the April plebiscite.
- [ ] R6 The Sudeten crisis: Henlein's Sudeten German Party; about three million Sudeten Germans; Chamberlain at Berchtesgaden and Bad Godesberg.
- [ ] R7 The Munich Agreement (29–30 September 1938): Chamberlain, Daladier, Hitler, Mussolini; Czechoslovakia and the USSR excluded; Sudetenland handed over 1–10 October; "peace for our time"; Churchill's criticism.
- [ ] R8 Prague (15 March 1939): Bohemia and Moravia a protectorate, Slovakia a client state; Memel taken (March 1939); the end of appeasement as Hitler's first seizure of non-German land.
- [ ] R9 Poland: demands for Danzig and the Corridor; the British guarantee (31 March 1939), with France.
- [ ] R10 The Nazi-Soviet Pact (23 August 1939): Ribbentrop and Molotov in Moscow; non-aggression; secret protocol dividing Poland and eastern Europe; removed the two-front danger.
- [ ] R11 War: invasion of Poland 1 September 1939; Britain and France declare war 3 September.

Appeasement
- [ ] A1 Definition and motives: guilt over Versailles, horror of another war, fear of bombing, rearmament not ready, dominions reluctant, communism seen as the greater threat, the economy.
- [ ] A2 Chamberlain (Prime Minister from 1937) as its main practitioner; Churchill as its main critic.
- [ ] A3 Views: popular at the time (Munich welcomed); later condemned ("Guilty Men" view); some historians defend it as buying time to rearm.

The Nazi state in war, 1939–1945 (outline only)
- [ ] W1 Speer, Armaments Minister (February 1942), raises arms output.
- [ ] W2 Goebbels's total war speech (18 February 1943) after Stalingrad; women registered for war work. One bullet; the German Home Front belongs to the wartime society notes.
- [ ] W3 Forced labour from occupied Europe. One bullet.
- [ ] W4 The July 1944 plot: Stauffenberg's bomb at the Wolf's Lair, Rastenburg (20 July); Hitler survived; mass arrests and executions.
- [ ] W5 Hitler's suicide in Berlin (30 April 1945). One clause.

Key personality: Adolf Hitler
- [ ] H1 Who he was: born 1889 at Braunau am Inn, Austria; soldier in the Bavarian army in the First World War (Iron Cross First Class); party leader from 1921; Munich Putsch and Mein Kampf; Chancellor 1933 (brief; the rise belongs to the Weimar notes).
- [ ] H2 What he did 1933–39, with dates (the stages above): dictatorship at home, rearmament, expansion, war.
- [ ] H3 How he shaped events: set the aims and took the key decisions (the purge, the Rhineland, the Anschluss, Czechoslovakia, Poland); his style of rule (late rising, avoiding paperwork, encouraging rivalry: "working towards the Führer").
- [ ] H4 Participant, not witness: he directed each stage.
- [ ] H5 Contemporaries: popularity in Germany after the Saar, Rhineland and Anschluss; abroad, some (Lloyd George after his 1936 visit) saw a reasonable revisionist, others (Churchill) a threat.
- [ ] H6 Historians: Bullock's opportunist adventurer seeking power, later revised; Trevor-Roper's consistent war aims; Taylor's opportunist; Mommsen's "weak dictator"; Kershaw's "working towards the Führer" synthesis.

Assessment (end with this block, headed "Assessment: …")
- [ ] J1 The Nazi state: how totalitarian? Totalitarian aims and terror vs polycracy, a small Gestapo dependent on denunciations, and consent won by jobs and success.
- [ ] J2 Economy: achievement (unemployment cut, recovery, rearmament) vs limits (hidden unemployment, controlled wages, strain and shortages by 1939, recovery begun before 1933).
- [ ] J3 Foreign policy: achievement (Versailles overturned and territory gained without war by 1939) vs limit (it led to a general war Germany was not ready for in 1939, and to the destruction of Germany).
- [ ] J4 How far Hitler caused the war: his aims and decisions (Mein Kampf, Hossbach, Prague, Poland) vs other causes (Versailles resentment, the weak League, appeasement, the Nazi-Soviet Pact, Mussolini). The usual verdict: Hitler's aims made war likely; the others shaped when and how it came.
- [ ] J5 Plan or opportunism: the intentionalist/programme view vs Taylor's opportunism; the usual balanced verdict: fixed long-term aims, opportunist timing and methods.
- [ ] J6 Contemporary vs later views of Munich: relief and Chamberlain cheered in 1938 vs later judged a failure that gave away Czechoslovakia's defences.

- [ ] T3 Herrenvolk (master race) and its use at home and in the east.

- [ ] T5 Other terms: Gleichschaltung; Enabling Act; Führer principle; Volksgemeinschaft; Night of the Long Knives; SS; SD; Gestapo; protective custody; Mefo bills; Four Year Plan; autarky; German Labour Front; Strength Through Joy; Hitler Youth; appeasement; Anschluss; Sudetenland; Munich Agreement; Hossbach Memorandum; Nazi-Soviet Pact; polycracy.

## 3. Traps

Do not use (doubtful or wrong in the current notes):
- Do not use "Dachau ... was the first concentration camp" without qualification: the SA ran makeshift early camps in 1933 too. Say "the first SS-run concentration camp and the model for later camps" (P3).
- Do not use "the Mother's Cross was awarded from 1938": it was instituted in December 1938 and first presented in 1939. Say "instituted in December 1938" (V4).
- Do not use "women were pushed out of ... professions such as law and medicine in 1933": women were not barred from medicine, and the ban on women judges and prosecutors came in 1936. Use the V4 wording.
- Do not use "thousands were arrested and several hundred executed" after the July plot: execution figures vary widely between sources (from about 200 directly involved to several thousand in the wider crackdown). Say "thousands arrested and many executed".
- Do not use "on 16 March 1935 Hitler announced the reintroduction of conscription and an air force" as one event: the Luftwaffe was revealed about a week earlier; 16 March was conscription. Use F6.
- Do not use "the conscription of women up to the age of 45 into war work": the January 1943 measure required registration of women aged 17 to 45. Use W2.
- Do not use "Trade unions were abolished on 2 May 1933" as if by law: their offices were seized by the SA and SS on that day and the unions dissolved. Use C5.
- Do not use "the SA ... had grown to millions" with a precise figure; "several million" is safe. Give no exact number.
- Do not say "the cabinet legalised the killings ... as acts of state self-defence" without the law's name: use N3.
- Do not say Hitler "was a participant in the foreign policy decisions": he directed them. Say he set the aims and took the key decisions (H3, H4).
- Do not apply "intentionalist" and "functionalist" to the foreign policy debate as if they were its standard names without explanation: they belong mainly to the debate on the Nazi state and the Holocaust. For foreign policy, frame it as plan (programme) vs opportunism, and if the two terms are used, say they are borrowed from the wider debate.
- Do not use the current notes' paragraph that judges "his foreign policy and the deaths of Jews ... together": it is vague. Leave the Holocaust to its own notes with a single cross-reference.

Quotations:
- "Peace for our time" is the correct wording; "peace in our time" is a common misquotation. Do not use the latter.
- Use only the quotations in section 2: "peace for our time", "a total and unmitigated defeat", "second revolution", "weak dictator", "working towards the Führer", "Kinder, Küche, Kirche", "One People, One Reich, One Leader". Do not quote Hitler's remarks about the "48 hours" after the Rhineland, his "last territorial demand", "Who still talks nowadays of the extermination of the Armenians?", or "our enemies are little worms": wording and sources are disputed or need context the notes cannot give.
- Do not quote Mein Kampf directly; paraphrase its aims.

Doubtful claims to avoid:
- Do not state as fact that German troops in the Rhineland had orders to withdraw if the French resisted: the evidence is disputed. Say only that Hitler took a gamble and the generals were nervous.
- Do not say the autobahns were a Nazi idea: plans and a first stretch (Cologne–Bonn, 1932) predate 1933. Say the Nazis expanded and publicised them.
- Do not say Nazi policy alone ended the Depression in Germany: recovery had begun before 1933 and world trade recovered. Present the debate as in J2.
- Do not give the Night of the Long Knives death toll as an exact figure; use "at least 85, possibly more".
- Do not give rearmament spending figures, Gestapo numbers, Strength Through Joy membership or Volkswagen saver numbers: none are checked here.
- Do not credit "Kinder, Küche, Kirche" to the Nazis as their coinage: it is older.

Confusables:
- The Reichstag Fire Decree (28 February 1933) vs the Enabling Act (23 March 1933): the first suspended rights; the second removed the Reichstag's law-making role.
- Gestapo (secret state police) vs SS (party elite force, Himmler) vs SD (SS intelligence, Heydrich) vs SA (brownshirts, Röhm, sidelined after 1934).
- Berchtesgaden meetings: with Schuschnigg (February 1938) and with Chamberlain (15 September 1938).
- Munich 1938 (the agreement) vs the Munich Putsch of 1923 vs the Nuremberg Rallies and Nuremberg Laws (1935).
- Locarno (1925, broken by the Rhineland reoccupation) vs Stresa (1935).
- The Rome–Berlin Axis (1936) and Anti-Comintern Pact (1936) vs the Pact of Steel (May 1939): the Pact of Steel and the Tripartite Pact belong to the wartime alliances notes; name the Pact of Steel in one clause at most.
- Schacht (Economics Minister, Mefo bills, New Plan) vs Göring (Four Year Plan, Luftwaffe, Gestapo founder in Prussia) vs Speer (architect, Armaments Minister from 1942).
- Chamberlain (Prime Minister 1937–40, Munich) vs Baldwin (Prime Minister 1935–37, Rhineland and Abyssinia).
- Hitler's title: Führer and Reich Chancellor from August 1934; he was never "President".

What belongs to neighbouring subsections (one clause or a cross-reference at most):
- Weimar Germany and the rise of Hitler: everything up to 30 January 1933 (Weimar's weaknesses, the Depression, the 1930–32 elections, Papen and Schleicher's intrigues).
- Nazi propaganda and the Nuremberg Rallies: Goebbels's ministry, radio, film, book burning, Riefenstahl, the rallies. Mention propaganda as a pillar of the state in one bullet only.
- Church–state relations: the Concordat (1933), the Reichskirche, the Confessing Church, Niemöller, Galen.
- Anti-Semitism and the Holocaust: the Nuremberg Laws in detail, Kristallnacht, the Final Solution. One bullet here.
- The Second World War: the course of the war after September 1939, the Pact of Steel and the Tripartite Pact, blitzkrieg.
- Society during World War II: Germany's Home Front (rationing, bombing, foreign labour, total war in detail).
- Mussolini's Italy: Abyssinia, the Italian side of the Axis and Stresa.
- Communism in Russia: Stalin's reasons for the Nazi-Soviet Pact and the Soviet war.

## 4. What exams ask (topics only)

- Higher Level essay (100 marks) on the main characteristics of the Nazi state 1933–39: marks for organising the answer around characteristics (one-party dictatorship and the Führer, terror and the police state, propaganda, control of youth and women, the economy and rearmament, racial policy), each backed by named laws, dates and organisations, not a chronological narrative.
- Higher Level essay on how far Hitler's foreign policy caused the Second World War: marks for a clear judgement ("to a great extent") argued throughout; the stages 1933–39 with dates; his aims (Mein Kampf, Lebensraum, Hossbach); and weighing other factors (Versailles, appeasement, the League, the Nazi-Soviet Pact). Narrative without judgement scores poorly.
- Higher Level essay on how Hitler reached and kept absolute control: the 1933–34 consolidation (Reichstag fire, Enabling Act, Gleichschaltung, one-party law, Night of the Long Knives, Hindenburg's death and the army oath) is the core of the second half; the rise to 1933 is the first half (Weimar notes).
- Higher Level essays on fascist characteristics, or propaganda and terror, use Nazi Germany as a main example: the SS, Gestapo, camps and courts give the terror side.
- Ordinary Level short paragraph (30 or 40 marks) on Hitler's foreign policy: a run of dated steps (League 1933, Saar and conscription 1935, Naval Agreement, Rhineland 1936, Anschluss and Munich 1938, Prague and the Nazi-Soviet Pact 1939, Poland) earns most marks; each accurate dated fact counts.
- Ordinary Level short paragraph on how Hitler consolidated power in 1933–34: dated steps with what each did.
- Ordinary Level document (often a cartoon of the Nazi-Soviet Pact): students must say who the figures are, when and where it was signed, what the symbols mean, why the alliance was surprising (enemies in ideology), and what followed (Poland 1939; Germany invaded the USSR in 1941).
- Judgement material examiners reward: achievements vs limits of the economy (real jobs vs hidden unemployment and strain); how far the state was totalitarian (terror vs consent and polycracy); plan vs opportunism; contemporary vs later views of appeasement and Munich.
- Precision: distinguish the Reichstag Fire Decree from the Enabling Act; give the Munich signatories and who was excluded; give the result of the Saar plebiscite as about 90 per cent.
