## 1. Must cover (one bullet or block for every item; tick each off)

Assumed knowledge
- [ ] A1 (assumed) The war of 1914–18 became a long war of attrition on the Western Front, so armies needed millions of men and huge quantities of shells for years; this, not ideology, is why states took over economies and why women were needed. One or two bullets only; the fighting itself is in the notes on the conduct of the war.
- [ ] A2 (assumed) Before 1914 women could not vote in national elections in Britain, France or Germany, and a women's suffrage movement existed in Britain (WSPU, NUWSS). Needed to judge "what changed".

Key concepts (each needs a keyTerm and at least one bullet using it)
- [ ] K1 World war: fought across several continents, with effects reaching civilians and economies as well as armies.
- [ ] K2 Total war: whole societies and economies mobilised; the state directs industry, labour, food and opinion; civilians become targets. (Essential term, not in the outline's key-concept list but in its cover list.)
- [ ] K3 Conscription: compulsory military service. France and Germany had it before 1914; Britain relied on volunteers until the Military Service Act of 1916. Conscription took men out of the workforce and so created the demand for women's labour.

Total war: state control (Britain and Germany)
- [ ] S1 DORA (Defence of the Realm Act, August 1914): powers over property, labour, the press and public behaviour; censorship; restricted pub opening hours. Extended during the war.
- [ ] S2 British state control of the economy: railways under government control from August 1914; coal mines (fully by 1917); shipping; new ministries (Munitions 1915; Labour, Food and Shipping 1916).
- [ ] S3 The Shell Crisis (spring 1915): shell shortage, fall of the Liberal government and formation of a coalition (May 1915), Lloyd George as Minister of Munitions.
- [ ] S4 British conscription: the Military Service Act (January 1916, in force from March 1916) for single men, extended to married men in 1916.
- [ ] S5 Germany's war economy: the War Raw Materials Department under Walther Rathenau (1914); the Hindenburg Programme (1916); the Auxiliary Service Law (December 1916) compelling men aged 17 to 60 into war work.
- [ ] S6 Propaganda and censorship: recruiting posters (Kitchener's "Your Country Needs You"), war bonds, keeping up morale and acceptance of shortages; censorship of newspapers and letters.
- [ ] S7 Rationing and food: German bread rationing from 1915; British compulsory rationing of sugar, meat and butter in 1918 after the submarine campaign; Britain's Corn Production Act 1917 to raise food output.
- [ ] S8 Civilians as targets: German naval bombardment of Scarborough (and Hartlepool and Whitby) in December 1914; Zeppelin and later Gotha bomber raids on Britain.

Effects on the economy
- [ ] E1 Paying for the war: borrowing, war loans and printing money rather than taxation alone; national debts multiplied.
- [ ] E2 Britain moved from the world's greatest creditor to a debtor of the USA; the USA became the leading financial power.
- [ ] E3 Inflation: prices rose faster than wages; savings lost value; in Germany, war funded mainly by loans and money creation laid the ground for post-war inflation (one clause; the 1923 hyperinflation is outside the topic's dates).
- [ ] E4 German shortages under the Allied blockade: the "Turnip Winter" of 1916–17 after a poor potato harvest; civilian deaths from malnutrition-related causes, estimates in the hundreds of thousands.
- [ ] E5 Industry converted to war production; chemicals, aircraft and engineering expanded; export trade lost to the USA and Japan.
- [ ] E6 Physical devastation of the fighting zones (northern France, Belgium); Russia's economic collapse contributed to revolution (one clause; Russia is covered in the notes on Tsarist Russia).
- [ ] E7 Agriculture lost men and horses to the army; governments pushed food production.

Effects on society
- [ ] P1 Casualties: about 9–10 million soldiers dead overall, with the country figures in section 2; many disabled; very many widows and orphans.
- [ ] P2 Trade unions gained strength and government recognition; British union membership roughly doubled 1914–20; strikes continued over pay and conditions.
- [ ] P3 Some loosening of class barriers and wider state welfare; the Education Act 1918 (Fisher Act) raised the school-leaving age to 14.
- [ ] P4 The influenza pandemic of 1918–19: killed tens of millions worldwide (estimates vary widely); young adults hit hard.
- [ ] P5 Demobilisation in 1919: unemployment fears, strikes and some army mutinies over slow demobilisation.

Case study: women in the workforce (at least four blocks: background/causes; events; outcomes; significance and views)
- [ ] W1 Women before 1914: most wage-earning women young and unmarried; domestic service the largest employer of women in Britain (well over a million), then textiles and clothing; pay about half of men's or less; excluded from most skilled trades, professions and many unions; married women expected to stay at home.
- [ ] W2 Women before 1914 in France (family farms, textile and clothing work) and Germany (agriculture, domestic service, textiles; women barred from political associations until 1908; women's movement split between middle-class feminists and the SPD women's movement under Clara Zetkin).
- [ ] W3 Women's suffrage before 1914 in Britain: the WSPU (Emmeline Pankhurst, founded 1903, militant from 1905) and the NUWSS (Millicent Fawcett, peaceful campaigning).
- [ ] W4 Why women were needed: volunteering and then conscription emptied workplaces; the Shell Crisis; the new Ministry of Munitions turned to women.
- [ ] W5 Dilution: the Treasury Agreement (March 1915) in which unions accepted unskilled workers, including women, on skilled work for the duration of the war; the Munitions of War Act (July 1915) backing dilution and banning strikes in munitions work.
- [ ] W6 Munitions work in Britain: filling shells and fuses, making cordite; Woolwich Arsenal and the national filling factories; hundreds of thousands of women by 1918; the name "munitionettes".
- [ ] W7 Conditions and dangers: long shifts (10–12 hours), night work, explosions at Silvertown (1917) and Chilwell (1918).
- [ ] W8 TNT poisoning (toxic jaundice): yellowed skin, the name "canary girls", illness and some deaths.
- [ ] W9 State welfare response in munitions: the Health of Munition Workers Committee (1915), welfare supervisors, canteens; done because poor conditions cut output.
- [ ] W10 Offices, shops and banks: women as clerks and typists in Britain, France and Germany.
- [ ] W11 Transport and public services: tram and bus conductors, railway porters and ticket collectors, postal workers; some women police and firefighters.
- [ ] W12 Farms: the Women's Land Army (formed 1917, as the submarine campaign made food a priority); women ran farms in France.
- [ ] W13 Nursing: Voluntary Aid Detachments (VADs, founded 1909) as nurses and ambulance drivers; Vera Brittain as a VAD nurse (her later book Testament of Youth: one clause only).
- [ ] W14 Edith Cavell: British nurse in Brussels executed by the Germans in October 1915 for helping Allied soldiers escape; used in Allied propaganda.
- [ ] W15 British women's auxiliary services: WAAC (1917), WRNS (1917), WRAF (1918): clerks, drivers, cooks, mechanics, telephonists, releasing men for the front.
- [ ] W16 France: women brought in the 1914 harvest and ran farms and businesses; from 1915 Albert Thomas (munitions) encouraged women into arms factories; "munitionnettes", including at Renault's Billancourt works; women as tram conductors, postal workers, clerks and nurses.
- [ ] W17 Germany: the National Women's Service (Nationaler Frauendienst, August 1914, organised by middle-class feminists including Gertrud Bäumer) for food distribution and welfare; more women in metal, chemical and engineering firms such as Krupp and Siemens; the Auxiliary Service Law increased pressure on women to fill gaps.
- [ ] W18 Germany: women bore the burden of food shortages, queued for food, and led food protests and riots from 1915.
- [ ] W19 Pay: women's pay rose compared with pre-war women's work (especially for former domestic servants) but stayed below men's for the same work.
- [ ] W20 Trade union attitudes: fear that women would undercut men's wages and skilled status; agreement to dilution only for the duration, with pre-war practices to be restored.
- [ ] W21 Pankhurst's wartime campaigning: in August 1914 Emmeline and Christabel Pankhurst suspended militancy and suffragette prisoners were released; they backed the war; the "Right to Serve" march in London, July 1915, with government funding, demanding war work for women.
- [ ] W22 Divisions in the movement: Sylvia Pankhurst opposed the war and worked among East End women; Emmeline and Christabel formed the Women's Party (1917).
- [ ] W23 Changing contemporary opinion: newspapers and ministers praised women's war work; Asquith, long an opponent, declared his support for women's suffrage in 1917, citing women's war service.
- [ ] W24 After 1918 in Britain: the Representation of the People Act (February 1918): men over 21 and women over 30 who met a property qualification, about 8.4 million women; Constance Markievicz elected in December 1918, the first woman elected to Westminster (she did not take her seat). The Equal Franchise Act (1928) equalised the vote at 21.
- [ ] W25 After 1918 in Germany: decree of 12 November 1918 giving women over 20 the vote and the right to stand; women voted in January 1919 and 37 women were elected to the National Assembly; the Weimar constitution made men and women equal in civic rights "in principle".
- [ ] W26 France gave women the vote only in 1944, despite equal or greater war work.
- [ ] W27 Women's return to the home: demobilisation decrees in Germany (1918–19) made women give up jobs to returning soldiers; Britain's Restoration of Pre-War Practices Act (1919); mass dismissals from munitions; many returned to domestic service, still a major employer in the 1920s.
- [ ] W28 Lasting gains: the Sex Disqualification (Removal) Act 1919 opened some professions and jury service to women in Britain; but marriage bars remained in many jobs.
- [ ] W29 Historians: Arthur Marwick (The Deluge, 1965): war as a catalyst of social change, including women's status; Gail Braybon (Women Workers in the First World War, 1981) and others: change was limited and temporary, and the vote owed much to pre-war campaigning and political calculation.

Assessment block (required by the standard)
- [ ] J1 Assessment: did the war change women's position? Paired points: achievements (proved capability, public presence and confidence, vote in Britain and Germany, some legal gains) vs limits (jobs lost 1918–19, lower pay, traditional roles reasserted, British vote limited to women over 30, French women gained nothing); contemporary view (proof of citizenship vs temporary emergency) vs historians' views (Marwick vs Braybon); differences between countries.
- [ ] J2 Assessment of the wider impact: the war as the first total war that enlarged the state, debt, union power and welfare, against the human and economic cost (casualties, devastation, inflation, loss of markets).

## 3. Traps

- Do not use: "the Fisher Act ... promised housing for returning servicemen", because the 1918 Education Act dealt only with schooling; the housing promise ("homes fit for heroes") came from Lloyd George's 1918 campaign and the 1919 Housing Act. Leave housing out.
- Do not use: "the suffragettes used militant tactics from 1903", because the WSPU was founded in 1903 but militancy began in 1905. Use the wording in section 2.
- Do not use: "in Germany about a third of women worked", because the figure is not checked. Describe German women's pre-war work without a proportion.
- Do not use: "government rules in 1916 set women's rates for munitions below men's", because the date and the rules are muddled (wage orders were issued from 1915 onwards and varied). Say only that women's pay stayed below men's.
- Do not use: "around 20,000 or more women" in the Land Army as a single figure; use the range in section 2.
- Do not present British Summer Time (1916) as a DORA measure; it came from a separate act.
- Do not give a single exact British death toll (sources range from about 700,000 to 750,000 for Britain alone).
- Exact days: give a day and month only for Edith Cavell's execution (12 October 1915) and the German suffrage decree (12 November 1918). Give months or years for everything else.
- "Munitionettes" (British) vs "munitionnettes" (French). "Canary girls" refers to TNT jaundice, not to a uniform.
- Suffragettes (WSPU, militant, Pankhurst) vs suffragists (NUWSS, peaceful, Fawcett). Emmeline and Christabel backed the war; Sylvia opposed it. Do not attribute the Right to Serve march to Sylvia.
- The Representation of the People Act (1918) vs the Equal Franchise Act (1928): the war-time act gave the vote to women over 30 only, with a property qualification.
- Markievicz was the first woman elected to Westminster but did not take her seat; do not call her the first woman MP to sit (that was Nancy Astor, 1919; do not name Astor).
- Dilution vs conscription: dilution is the use of unskilled workers on skilled jobs; conscription is compulsory military service.
- Do not copy figures or wording from past papers, such as the number of women employed by the Bank of England or the size of the Right to Serve march, or the extract's count of women entering paid work.
- Neighbouring subsections, leave out or cross-refer by subject:
  - Battles, weapons, the blockade and U-boat campaign as naval strategy, US entry, Haig, and conscription as a military matter: the notes on the conduct of the war.
  - The German revolution, the Weimar Republic's founding and the Peace Settlement: the notes on war and revolution and the Peace Settlement (mention the 1919 election only for women's votes).
  - Rosa Luxemburg and the Spartacists: the notes on the Second International and the outbreak of war.
  - Russia's war economy, shortages and the 1917 revolutions: the notes on Tsarist Russia.
  - The Krupp firm and pre-war German industrialisation: the notes on industrialisation in Germany (name Krupp here only as an employer of women).
  - War poetry and literature (Owen, Sassoon, Brittain's book as literature): the notes on culture and the literature of World War I.
  - Marie Curie's mobile X-ray units: the notes on science, technology and medicine.

## 4. What exams ask (topics only)

- Higher Level essays on the case study: what women did in the war, how their lives changed, and how far the change lasted. Marks go to judgement, not a list of jobs: pair each gain with its limit (jobs gained vs jobs lost in 1919; pay up vs still below men's; the vote won in Britain and Germany vs limited to over-30s in Britain and refused in France).
- Higher Level essays on the impact of the war on economy and society: answers that organise by theme (state control, finance and inflation, industry, casualties, unions and welfare, women) and explain cause and result score better than a narrative of battles.
- Combination essays: women in the workforce paired with Haig and the conduct of war, Wilson and the Peace Settlement, Marie Curie, or Rosa Luxemburg ("what did your study teach you"). The women's material must stand on its own with dates and named measures, and link to the theme of total war or women in public life.
- A wider essay on industry and society 1871–1918 can draw on war economies (state control, Hindenburg Programme, munitions).
- Ordinary Level documents questions: a poster, newspaper extract, letter or photograph about women's war work or the home front. Students must read the document closely, then use background knowledge: who issued a poster (the Ministry of Munitions), what work is shown, union or male hostility to women workers, the Right to Serve march and its demand, the German National Women's Service and food relief, and whether women kept their jobs after the war (expected answer: mostly no, men took back their jobs).
- Ordinary Level short paragraphs: why women replaced men, munitions work and its dangers, the Treasury Agreement and dilution, the vote in 1918, women before 1914.
- Explanations that earn marks: link conscription and volunteering to the labour shortage; link the Shell Crisis to the Ministry of Munitions and women's recruitment; link TNT to "canary girls"; link union fears to the "duration only" promise and the 1919 Act.
- Historians: naming Marwick and Braybon and stating each view in a sentence earns judgement marks in Higher essays.
