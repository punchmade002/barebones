## 1. Must cover (one bullet or block for every item; tick each off)

Background (assumed)
- [ ] A1 (assumed) The Enlightenment of the eighteenth century valued reason, order, universal rules and classical models in art; Romanticism was a reaction against it.
- [ ] A2 (assumed) The Vienna settlement (1815) drew frontiers by dynasty and the balance of power, not nationality, so Italians, Germans, Poles, Czechs and Hungarians were divided or ruled by others; this is why culture mattered to peoples without a state. Only this point belongs here.
- [ ] A3 (assumed) The French Revolution attacked the Catholic Church (Church lands seized, clergy persecuted); Napoleon's Concordat (1801) restored it under state control. The Church was therefore linked with monarchy and order, and republicans and liberals were often anticlerical.
- [ ] A4 (assumed) In Britain the Church of England was the established (state) Church; Nonconformists (Methodists, Baptists, Congregationalists and others) stood outside it; Catholics were a minority, many of them Irish.
- [ ] A5 (assumed) Industrialisation and rapid urban growth created crowded towns where the parish system and old forms of charity broke down; this is the "social change" the churches responded to. One bullet; the detail is in the economy notes.
- [ ] A6 (assumed) Most Christians in 1815 accepted the Bible's account of creation, with each species created separately and the earth thought to be young; this is why Darwin's theory was controversial.

Key concepts and essential terms (each defined as used in this topic; also in keyTerms)
- [ ] K1 Romanticism: the cultural movement that put feeling, imagination, nature, the individual and the national past above reason and classical rules.
- [ ] K2 Nationalism: the belief that a people sharing language, culture and history forms a nation that should rule itself; artists and scholars spread it.
- [ ] K3 Cultural nationalism: using music, art, literature, history and language to express and build a sense of nationhood, often before any political state existed.
- [ ] K4 Herder's idea that each people has its own spirit expressed in its language, folk songs and customs (later called the Volksgeist).
- [ ] K5 Essential terms: Catholic revival; ultramontanism (looking to the Pope in Rome for authority); anticlericalism; the Oxford Movement; Christian Socialism; temperance; natural selection; evolution; germ theory; electromagnetic induction; the electric telegraph.

Romanticism and cultural nationalism
- [ ] C1 What Romanticism rejected and valued (K1), and the turn to the national past, folklore and medieval legend.
- [ ] C2 Why cultural nationalism was strongest among peoples without their own state (Italians, Germans, Poles, Czechs, Hungarians, Finns), and how it fed political nationalism.
- [ ] C3 Hugo's preface to Cromwell (1827) as a manifesto of French Romantic drama (one bullet; Hugo's block covers the rest).

Music
- [ ] M1 Chopin: Polish dance forms (mazurka, polonaise); left Poland before the 1830–31 rising failed and lived in exile in Paris; his music became a voice of Polish national feeling.
- [ ] M2 Liszt: born in Hungary; the Hungarian Rhapsodies used what he took to be Hungarian folk music (much of it Romani); presented Hungary to European audiences.
- [ ] M3 Verdi: Nabucco (1842) and the chorus "Va, pensiero"; operas later heard as patriotic; "Viva VERDI" as a coded slogan around 1859; Verdi elected to the first Italian parliament (1861).
- [ ] M4 Wagner: German myth and legend in his operas (Tannhäuser, Lohengrin, the Ring from the Nibelung legend and Norse sagas); his part in the Dresden rising of 1849 and exile; the Ring completed after this period.
- [ ] M5 Smetana: Czech national opera (The Bartered Bride, 1866) using folk music; Má vlast (1870s) as a later example.
- [ ] M6 Glinka's A Life for the Tsar (1836) as the start of Russian national opera (optional, one clause).

Art
- [ ] P1 Delacroix's Liberty Leading the People (1830): the July Revolution, Liberty with the tricolour leading Parisians of different classes over a barricade; a symbol of liberty and of the French nation. Also his Massacre at Chios (1824), showing sympathy for the Greek cause.
- [ ] P2 Caspar David Friedrich's landscapes (Wanderer above the Sea of Fog, about 1818): Romantic feeling for nature, linked to German national feeling.
- [ ] P3 History painting: scenes from the national past gave citizens a shared image of their history (no named example needed).

Literature and language
- [ ] W1 The Grimm brothers: Children's and Household Tales (1812 and 1815) as the voice of the German people; Jacob Grimm's German Grammar (1819); the German Dictionary begun in 1838.
- [ ] W2 Manzoni: The Betrothed (I promessi sposi, 1827), set in Lombardy under Spanish rule (read as a comment on Austrian rule); revised (1840–42) into the Tuscan of Florence, a model for standard Italian.
- [ ] W3 Mickiewicz: Pan Tadeusz (1834), the Polish national epic, written in exile.
- [ ] W4 Pushkin: Eugene Onegin; created the modern Russian literary language.
- [ ] W5 Standardising national languages: grammars, dictionaries and schools turned dialects into national languages and built a single community of readers; Czech (Jungmann's dictionary, 1830s) and Finnish (Lönnrot's Kalevala, 1835) as examples; language as the main mark of nationality for nationalists.
- [ ] W6 Petőfi's "National Song" recited in Pest on 15 March 1848 (optional, one clause): literature acting directly in revolution.

Key personality: Victor Hugo, literary role (one block, every point; his politics and exile as opponent of Napoleon III belong elsewhere)
- [ ] H1 Who he was: born 1802 in Besançon, son of a Napoleonic general; poet, dramatist and novelist; leader of French Romanticism.
- [ ] H2 Early works and the Romantic battle: Odes (1822); Les Orientales (1829), with poems for the Greek cause; the preface to Cromwell (1827); Hernani (1830) and the "battle of Hernani" between Romantics and classicists.
- [ ] H3 Notre-Dame de Paris (1831): the Gothic past of Paris; helped the campaign to preserve and restore medieval buildings.
- [ ] H4 Recognition: elected to the Académie française (1841). One clause only on his peerage (1845).
- [ ] H5 Exile and literature: from 1855 on Guernsey (Hauteville House) after Jersey; Les Contemplations (1856), partly mourning his daughter Léopoldine (drowned 1843); Les Misérables (1862), written largely on Guernsey; The Toilers of the Sea (1866), set on Guernsey.
- [ ] H6 Les Misérables: Jean Valjean's story from 1815 to the early 1830s, ending at the Paris rising of June 1832; poverty, injustice, the law and redemption; a huge success across Europe.
- [ ] H7 Participant or witness: as a writer he shaped taste and national memory (an active participant in cultural life); his social novels gave a voice to the poor.
- [ ] H8 How contemporaries saw him: Romantics hailed him as their leader; classical critics attacked his breaking of rules; some critics found Les Misérables sentimental, but readers made it a best-seller.
- [ ] H9 How historians judge him: one of the greatest French writers; a national figure whose literary and political engagement went together; his state funeral (1885) and burial in the Panthéon show his standing. One clause on the funeral; no political detail.

The churches and social change in France
- [ ] F1 The Catholic revival after 1815: the Church restored and favoured by the Bourbons (throne and altar); new and returning religious orders; strong among women, peasants and in some regions; weak among urban workers and much of the middle class.
- [ ] F2 Liberal Catholicism: Lamennais, Lacordaire and Montalembert and the newspaper L'Avenir (1830), demanding freedom of the Church from the state, freedom of the press, education and association; condemned by Gregory XVI in Mirari vos (1832); Lamennais left the Church (1834).
- [ ] F3 Ozanam and the Society of St Vincent de Paul (1833): a lay Catholic charity of students visiting the poor at home; spread through France and abroad; a Catholic answer to urban poverty and to the charge that the Church ignored it.
- [ ] F4 The Falloux Law (1850): freedom to open (mainly Catholic) secondary schools, clergy on education councils; it greatly expanded Church schooling; part of the conservative reaction after 1848.
- [ ] F5 The Church under Napoleon III: support until 1859 (French troops protected the Pope in Rome); strain over Italian unification.
- [ ] F6 Popular piety: Lourdes (1858) and mass pilgrimage, helped by railways.
- [ ] F7 Ultramontanism under Pius IX: the Immaculate Conception (1854), the Syllabus of Errors (1864) condemning liberalism and progress; deepened the split between the Church and liberals, republicans and workers.
- [ ] F8 Anticlericalism and the de-Christianisation of the urban working class; Renan's Life of Jesus (1863) as an example of the challenge from scholarship (optional).

The churches and social change in Britain
- [ ] B1 Methodism (from John Wesley): strong among industrial workers and miners; chapels, preaching, self-improvement and discipline; also a training ground for trade-union and Chartist speakers.
- [ ] B2 The Oxford Movement: Keble's sermon (1833), the Tracts for the Times, Newman's conversion to Catholicism (1845); a revival of Catholic worship and ritual within the Church of England; later Anglo-Catholic priests worked in slum parishes.
- [ ] B3 Catholics in Britain: Catholic Emancipation (1829), Irish immigration to the industrial cities, the restoration of the Catholic hierarchy in England (1850).
- [ ] B4 Christian Socialism (from 1848): Maurice, Kingsley and Ludlow; co-operative workshops; the Working Men's College (1854); Kingsley's novel Alton Locke (1850).
- [ ] B5 Education: Sunday schools, the ragged schools (Ragged School Union, 1844) and church day schools; the churches' central role in schooling before state education. Detail of state education belongs elsewhere.
- [ ] B6 Temperance: drink seen as a cause of poverty; the Preston teetotal pledge (1832); temperance societies, many chapel-based.
- [ ] B7 Evangelical social reform: Lord Shaftesbury and factory and mines reform (the Mines Act 1842), the YMCA (1844); city missions and charity.
- [ ] B8 The religious census of 1851: showed that many working people, especially in the big towns, did not attend any church.
- [ ] B9 William Booth's East London mission (1865), later the Salvation Army (1878): one bullet.
- [ ] B10 France vs Britain comparison: in France one Church, tied to conservatism and opposed by anticlericals; in Britain many denominations, with Nonconformists linked to reform and self-help.

Developments in electricity
- [ ] E1 Faraday: electromagnetic rotation (the principle of the electric motor, 1821); electromagnetic induction (1831), the basis of the generator and transformer; the laws of electrolysis (1833–34).
- [ ] E2 The electric telegraph: Cooke and Wheatstone's patent (1837) and its use on British railways; Morse's line between Washington and Baltimore (1844) and Morse code.
- [ ] E3 Submarine and transatlantic cables: the 1858 cable failed within weeks; a lasting cable in 1866, laid by the Great Eastern; news and orders now crossed continents in minutes.
- [ ] E4 Maxwell's theory of the electromagnetic field (1865): light as an electromagnetic wave.
- [ ] E5 Siemens's self-excited dynamo (1866–67): practical large-scale generation of electricity followed.

Key personality: Charles Darwin (one block, every point)
- [ ] D1 Who he was: born 1809 in Shrewsbury; studied medicine at Edinburgh, then at Cambridge to become a clergyman; interest in natural history.
- [ ] D2 The voyage of HMS Beagle (1831–36) as naturalist: South America, the Galápagos Islands (1835), Australia; fossils, rocks, plants and animals; variation between islands (finches, tortoises, mockingbirds).
- [ ] D3 Influences: Lyell's Principles of Geology (an earth shaped slowly over vast time); Malthus's essay on population (the struggle for existence).
- [ ] D4 Developing the theory: from 1838; the long delay; Wallace's letter (1858) and the joint papers read at the Linnean Society (1858).
- [ ] D5 On the Origin of Species (November 1859): variation, struggle for existence, natural selection, common descent; the book sold out on publication.
- [ ] D6 The controversy: conflict with the Genesis account and the idea of design; the Oxford debate (1860) between Huxley and Bishop Samuel Wilberforce, Darwin absent; support from Huxley, Lyell (eventually) and Hooker; opposition from many churchmen and some scientists; some Christians reconciled faith and evolution.
- [ ] D7 The Descent of Man (1871): human evolution, the most offensive point to critics.
- [ ] D8 Participant or witness: a witness of nature on the voyage and a careful theorist; a participant in the debate through his books, but he avoided public controversy, leaving that to Huxley.
- [ ] D9 How historians judge him: the founder of modern evolutionary biology; his theory was completed by genetics (Mendel's laws rediscovered 1900); misuse of his ideas as "Social Darwinism" was not his doing; burial in Westminster Abbey (1882) showed his standing.

Biology, medicine and technology
- [ ] S1 Pasteur: fermentation and decay caused by micro-organisms (late 1850s); disproving spontaneous generation with swan-neck flasks (early 1860s); pasteurisation (1860s); this work led to the germ theory of disease.
- [ ] S2 Mendel: pea-plant experiments, published 1866; patterns of inheritance; ignored until 1900; Darwin did not know of it.
- [ ] S3 Cell theory: Schleiden and Schwann (1838–39), Virchow's every cell from a cell (1858) (optional, one bullet).
- [ ] S4 Medicine: anaesthesia (ether 1846, Simpson's chloroform 1847); Lister's antiseptic surgery with carbolic acid (1867); Snow and cholera (1854) as one clause (public-health detail belongs elsewhere).
- [ ] S5 Steam technology: railways (Stephenson's Rocket, 1829; Liverpool and Manchester Railway, 1830) and steamships (SS Great Britain, 1843: iron hull and screw propeller); the Suez Canal (1869). Here as technology and communication; their economic effects belong elsewhere.
- [ ] S6 Photography: Niépce's first photograph (about 1826); Daguerre's daguerreotype (announced 1839); Talbot's calotype negative–positive process (patented 1841); uses in portraits and in recording war and cities.
- [ ] S7 Bessemer's converter (1856) for cheap steel (optional, one clause).
- [ ] S8 How science and technology changed life: faster communication and travel, safer surgery, a new view of nature; science challenged religious authority.

Assessment (paired points)
- [ ] X1 Cultural nationalism, achievement vs limit: gave nations symbols, languages and a shared past, and prepared the ground for political unity (Italy, Germany) vs it reached mainly the educated minority, idealised the past, and could not by itself win independence (Poland, Czechs, Hungary by 1871).
- [ ] X2 The churches, achievement vs limit: charity, schools, temperance and moral reform for the poor vs they treated symptoms rather than causes, lost much of the urban working class, and in France were tied to conservatism.
- [ ] X3 Science, achievement vs limit: Faraday, Darwin, Pasteur and Mendel transformed knowledge and technology changed daily life vs many benefits (electric power, germ theory in medicine, genetics) came only after 1871, and Darwin's ideas divided society.
- [ ] X4 Contemporary vs later views: contemporaries saw Darwin as a threat to religion and Hugo as a controversial Romantic; later historians rank both among the greatest figures of the century.

## 3. Traps

Do not use (wrong or doubtful facts in the current notes)
- Do not use: Pasteur's swan-neck experiments "published in 1861", because his experiments ran about 1859–62 and his memoir appeared in 1861–62. Write "in the early 1860s".
- Do not use: Pasteur's late-1850s work "developed the germ theory of disease", because the fermentation work only led towards it; the germ theory of disease was established in the 1860s–80s (much of it after this period). Write that his work led to the germ theory.
- Do not use: Herder "developed the Volksgeist", because Herder wrote of a people's spirit or soul; the word Volksgeist was popularised by later writers. Write "Herder's idea of a national spirit (later called the Volksgeist)".
- Do not use: Italian audiences hearing "Va, pensiero" as a call for liberation at its 1842 premiere as plain fact, because historians doubt how political its first reception was; the patriotic reading grew later and was built up after unification. Write that the chorus "came to be heard" as patriotic. The "Viva VERDI" slogan (about 1859) is safe.
- Do not use: Jungmann's dictionary "1835–39", because sources give 1834–39 or 1835–39. Write "the 1830s".
- Do not use: Siemens's dynamo "of 1866" alone; write "1866–67" (the discovery late 1866, announced early 1867).
- Do not use: Ozanam "founded" the Society alone; he co-founded it with fellow students (with Emmanuel Bailly as first president). Write "co-founded" or "led the founding".
- Do not use: the Salvation Army "began as the Christian Mission of William Booth in 1865", because the name Christian Mission came a few years later. Write that Booth began his East London mission in 1865, renamed the Salvation Army in 1878.
- Do not present the Ring (1876) or Má vlast (1874–79) as achievements within 1815–1871 without saying they came after it. Prefer Wagner's earlier operas and The Bartered Bride (1866).
- Do not say Darwin "delayed publication for about twenty years" without the dates; write "he worked on the theory from 1838 and published only in 1859".
- Do not invent a quotation for Wilberforce (the "grandmother an ape" exchange exists only in later and differing accounts). Describe the exchange without quotation marks.
- Do not give figures for the 1851 religious census or for the share of Italians speaking Italian at unification: sources differ widely.
- Do not say Darwin coined "survival of the fittest": it was Herbert Spencer's phrase.
- Do not say Mendel influenced Darwin: Darwin never knew his work.
- Do not say Edison or electric light belongs here: the electric light bulb came in 1879, after the period.

- Mirari vos (1832, against Lamennais's liberal Catholicism) vs the Syllabus of Errors (1864, against liberalism in general).
- Romanticism (an artistic movement) vs liberalism and nationalism (political ideas): Romantics could be conservative (the early Hugo was a royalist) or revolutionary (Wagner in 1849).

## 4. What exams ask

- Higher 100-mark essay on how far music, art, literature and language contributed to cultural nationalism (the commonest question): examiners want all four strands, each with named figures, works and dates (Verdi, Wagner, Chopin, Liszt, Smetana; Delacroix; Grimm, Manzoni, Mickiewicz, Pushkin; language standardisation). Marks go to explaining how each work built national feeling (folk sources, national legends, a standard language, symbols), not to biography. A judgement on "how far" is needed: strongest where there was no state; it reached the educated more than the masses; it prepared rather than achieved political unity.
- Higher essays pairing Darwin with Hugo, Marx or Haussmann: each half needs the person's main work with dates and an assessment of impact. For Darwin: the Beagle, the theory, the Origin, the controversy, the Descent of Man, his lasting standing. For Hugo here: the literary half (Romanticism, Hernani, Notre-Dame de Paris, Les Misérables); a pairing with Haussmann or a "French affairs" question also needs his political half from the notes on the State in France. Compare the two people explicitly at the end.
- Higher essay on developments in science (electricity, biology, technology, sometimes medicine): cover each area named with at least two developments, each dated, and explain its effect on society (communication, industry, health, religious belief). Students lose marks by writing only on Darwin; spread the answer. A "what did you learn" or "how important" wording needs a clear judgement on which development mattered most.
- Higher essay on the churches and social change in France and Great Britain: both countries needed, roughly equally. France: the Catholic revival, Lamennais and liberal Catholicism, Ozanam's charity, the Falloux Law, Pius IX and anticlericalism. Britain: Methodism, the Oxford Movement, Christian Socialism, schools, temperance, evangelical reform, the 1851 census. Reward goes to linking each to a social problem (poverty, drink, ignorance, factory conditions) and to a judgement of achievements vs limits, including the loss of the urban working class.
- Higher essay agreeing that France was politically unstable but culturally productive: this subsection supplies the cultural half (Hugo, Delacroix, the Catholic revival, Pasteur, Daguerre's photography). Make the agreement explicit.
- Ordinary Level 30/40-mark paragraphs on Darwin (very common): each correct dated statement earns marks: birth and education, the Beagle and its route, the Galápagos, natural selection explained simply, the Origin (1859), Wallace, the Oxford debate, the Descent of Man, burial in Westminster Abbey.
- Ordinary Level paragraphs on Hugo: the preface to Cromwell, Hernani, Notre-Dame de Paris, Les Misérables, exile on Guernsey; some versions also expect his opposition to Napoleon III (from the notes on the State in France).
- Ordinary Level paragraphs on science (Faraday's induction, Darwin's Origin, Pasteur, the telegraph): short, accurate, dated statements about what each discovered and why it mattered.
- Contemporary vs later views earn marks in personality answers: Darwin attacked by churchmen, defended by Huxley, now the founder of evolutionary biology; Hugo attacked by classical critics, now a national figure buried in the Panthéon.
