## 1. Must cover (one bullet or block for every item; tick each off)

Background (assumed)
- [ ] A1 (assumed) The Third Home Rule Bill (1912) and the unionist response: the Ulster Covenant (September 1912), the UVF (1913) and the Larne gun-running (April 1914), led by Carson and Craig.
- [ ] A2 (assumed) World War I and the 1916 Rising changed nationalist politics; Sinn Féin overtook the Irish Parliamentary Party in 1918 (detail belongs to the notes on the Rising and Sinn Féin).
- [ ] A3 (assumed) The War of Independence (1919–21), the truce and the Treaty negotiations ran alongside the creation of Northern Ireland (detail belongs elsewhere).

Key concepts (each defined as used in this topic; also in keyTerms)
- [ ] K1 Partition: the division of Ireland under the Government of Ireland Act 1920 into Northern Ireland (six counties) and Southern Ireland.
- [ ] K2 Ulster Unionism: the movement that opposed Home Rule and then defended Northern Ireland's place in the United Kingdom; its party was the Ulster Unionist Party.
- [ ] K3 Discrimination: unequal treatment of Catholics in jobs, housing, policing and local government, as alleged by nationalists and disputed by unionists.
- [ ] K4 (essential terms) Gerrymandering, proportional representation, Special Powers Act, Ulster Special Constabulary, Boundary Commission, Stormont, Government of Ireland Act.

Why partition happened, 1912–1920
- [ ] P1 Unionist resistance: Covenant and UVF showed Home Rule for all Ireland could be imposed only by force.
- [ ] P2 Exclusion debates: county option (March 1914), Buckingham Palace Conference (July 1914), Lloyd George proposals (1916); all assumed some Ulster counties would be excluded.
- [ ] P3 Why the 1916 plan collapsed (permanent vs temporary exclusion).
- [ ] P4 War, the Rising and the Irish Convention (1917–18).
- [ ] P5 The 1918 election and the geography of the vote.
- [ ] P6 Why six counties, not nine (Tyrone and Fermanagh; a safer majority); population and religious balance.
- [ ] P7 British policy: Lloyd George's coalition, Conservative influence, unwillingness to coerce Ulster; the Government of Ireland Act as the response.

The Government of Ireland Act and the new state, 1920–1925
- [ ] G1 The Act (December 1920): two parliaments and a Council of Ireland.
- [ ] G2 The elections of 24 May 1921 in both parts; abstention of Sinn Féin and the Nationalists.
- [ ] G3 Craig becomes Prime Minister (June 1921); George V opens the parliament (22 June 1921).
- [ ] G4 Southern Ireland's parliament never worked.
- [ ] G5 Northern Ireland opts out of the Free State (7 December 1922).
- [ ] G6 The Boundary Commission: members, Craig's refusal to nominate, the leak, the outcome.
- [ ] G7 The Tripartite Agreement (3 December 1925): border unchanged, debt released, Council of Ireland ended.

Violence and security, 1920–1922
- [ ] V1 Expulsions from the Belfast shipyards (July 1920) and riots in Derry and Belfast; deaths and displacement.
- [ ] V2 The Belfast boycott (1920) and its effect.
- [ ] V3 The Ulster Special Constabulary (A, B and C classes) and its Protestant makeup.
- [ ] V4 The RUC (June 1922) and the unfilled Catholic places.
- [ ] V5 The Craig–Collins pacts (1922), their failure, and the May 1922 border offensive.
- [ ] V6 The Special Powers Act (April 1922): powers, renewal, permanence from 1933; internment and the Argenta.

The Unionist government in power: control of the system
- [ ] C1 Nationalist abstention and the 1925 entry of the Nationalist Party under Devlin; Sinn Féin's continued abstention.
- [ ] C2 Dissolution of nationalist-controlled councils (Tyrone, Fermanagh) and commissioners.
- [ ] C3 Abolition of PR for local elections (1922) and redrawing of areas.
- [ ] C4 Abolition of PR for Stormont (1929) and single-member constituencies.
- [ ] C5 Derry Corporation as the best-known gerrymander.
- [ ] C6 One-party dominance 1921–1969 and the border bloc vote.
- [ ] C7 Stormont Parliament Buildings (1932) as a symbol.

Discrimination in jobs, housing and public life
- [ ] D1 Senior posts, the shipyards and heavy industry; Orange Order networks.
- [ ] D2 Housing allocation by unionist councils.
- [ ] D3 Basil Brooke's calls not to employ Catholics.
- [ ] D4 Craig's "Protestant parliament and a Protestant state" remark (1934) and its context.
- [ ] D5 Historians' disagreement: systematic discrimination (Farrell) vs uneven and not centrally planned; the unionist defence (nationalist non-participation).
- [ ] D6 Church schools and abstention left the minority with a limited voice.

The economy and society in the North, 1921–1939
- [ ] E1 Shipbuilding and linen, and their post-1920 decline.
- [ ] E2 High unemployment in the 1920s and early 1930s.
- [ ] E3 Agriculture (small farms, cattle and eggs to Britain).
- [ ] E4 The "step by step" parity policy on welfare and the Imperial Contribution.
- [ ] E5 Poor housing in Belfast; little building until late 1930s.
- [ ] E6 The Outdoor Relief strike and riots (October 1932): cross-community action; two deaths; relief increased.

The impact of partition on the island's economy and society
- [ ] I1 Customs controls from 1923; the Economic War and smuggling.
- [ ] I2 Belfast industries lost the southern market.
- [ ] I3 The South lost the industrial north-east and depended on agriculture.
- [ ] I4 Broken all-island links (the Great Northern Railway as example).
- [ ] I5 Social division: Catholics in the North cut off; Protestants in the South a smaller minority.
- [ ] I6 Politics: the border as a lasting issue; Articles 2 and 3 (1937).

Key personality: James Craig (one block, every point)
- [ ] J1 Who he was: born Belfast 1871 into a wealthy whiskey family; stockbroker; Boer War service; MP for East Down from 1906.
- [ ] J2 Before partition: UVF and Larne gun-running with Carson; British junior posts 1916–21 (ending at the Admiralty).
- [ ] J3 Leader and Prime Minister: succeeded Carson (February 1921), Prime Minister June 1921 until death (24 November 1940); Viscount Craigavon 1927.
- [ ] J4 Dealings: Collins and Lloyd George, border "settled", refusal to nominate to the Boundary Commission.
- [ ] J5 His government: RUC, Special Powers Act, abolition of PR, parity policy; the 1934 speech.
- [ ] J6 Shaped by events: violence 1920–22, the Boundary Commission, the Depression.
- [ ] J7 Contemporaries: revered by unionists as founder; nationalists blamed him for exclusion and sectarian rule.
- [ ] J8 Historians: Buckland's biography (cautious, conservative, no outreach to Catholics) and the wider debate; strengths (stability, organiser) vs weaknesses (no attempt to win minority consent).

Key personality: Richard Dawson Bates (one block, every point)
- [ ] B1 Who he was: Belfast solicitor; secretary of the Ulster Unionist Council from 1906 to 1921; helped organise the UVF; born 1876, died 1949.
- [ ] B2 Office: Minister of Home Affairs 1921–43; responsible for the RUC, Special Constabulary, Special Powers Act, local government and elections.
- [ ] B3 What he did: Local Government Act 1922, the 1929 electoral change, bans on republican parades and organisations, internment, censorship.
- [ ] B4 Shaped by events: security crisis of 1921–22 created the powers he used for over twenty years; left office in 1943 in ill health.
- [ ] B5 Contemporaries: nationalists as architect of control; unionists praised his efficiency.
- [ ] B6 Historians: Buckland, The Factory of Grievances (1979); legacy of grievances.

Assessment (paired judgement points, final block)
- [ ] S1 Achievement: survival of the state through 1920–22 and the Depression; Northern Ireland's place in the UK secured.
- [ ] S2 Achievement: British standards in education, health and welfare through parity.
- [ ] S3 Limit: reliance on emergency powers, a mainly Protestant police and manipulated electoral rules; the minority never accepted the state.
- [ ] S4 Limit: no real opposition within a one-party state; timid economic and housing policy.
- [ ] S5 Contemporary view (unionist bulwark vs nationalist Protestant state) and later view (debate over how planned discrimination was; legacy for the Troubles).

## 4. What exams ask

- Higher 100-mark essay on factors in partition: the commonest question. Strong answers name three linked factors (unionist resistance and the threat of force; repeated British failure to find an all-Ireland settlement, with the 1914 and 1916 proposals; the divided result of 1918 and the logic of six counties) and judge which mattered most, with dates. Narrating the Home Rule crisis without a judgement scores poorly.
- Higher 100-mark essay on how the Unionist government dealt with its challenges, usually grouped as security and violence, the nationalist minority, the economy and unemployment. Marks go to paired points: success in security and survival, failure in fairness and in economic and housing policy. Give dated evidence for each group (Special Powers Act, B Specials, PR abolition, Outdoor Relief riots, parity).
- Higher essay on Craig: strengths and weaknesses. Use J1–J8; contrast organiser and builder of a stable state with the leader of a Protestant state who made no outreach to Catholics. A named historian (Buckland) adds weight.
- Higher essay on discrimination: use specific evidence (Derry gerrymander, housing, the shipyards, Brooke's and Craig's speeches) and show the historians' disagreement; unsupported claims of "discrimination" earn little.
- Ordinary Level 30-mark paragraph on Dawson Bates: who he was (solicitor, UUC secretary, UVF), his office and dates (Home Affairs 1921–43), what he did (RUC, Special Powers Act, local government and elections), and one judgement. Each correct, dated statement earns marks.
- Ordinary Level 40-mark paragraph on Craig: Larne, succession in 1921, the opening of parliament, the Boundary Commission refusal, Viscount Craigavon, death in 1940, and at least one achievement and one criticism.
- Ordinary Level 30-mark paragraph on how the Unionist Party kept power: PR abolished (local 1922, Stormont 1929), single-member constituencies, Derry, unbroken Stormont victories 1921–69.
- Ordinary Level 30-mark paragraph on the economic effects of partition: border customs controls, the South's dependence on agriculture, Belfast industries cut off, the Economic War.
- Short answers (6 marks): identify an official or an act (the Special Powers Act, Bates), a term (gerrymandering, PR) or a party (Unionist, Nationalist); answers are single facts, so learn names and dates exactly.
- Cause and consequence: why the Boundary Commission failed to move the border; why Northern Ireland chose six counties; why the Special Powers Act was passed and why it was made permanent.
