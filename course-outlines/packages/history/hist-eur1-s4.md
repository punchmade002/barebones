## 1. Must cover (one bullet or block for every item; tick each off)

Background (assumed)
- [ ] A1 (assumed) Napoleon's rule in Italy: he redrew the map, created a Kingdom of Italy in the north, and brought the Code Napoléon, a single centralised administration and the idea of a united Italy, which the restored rulers could not entirely undo.
- [ ] A2 (assumed) The Vienna settlement restored the legitimate rulers in Italy and made Austria the dominant power there; Metternich saw Italy as "a geographical expression", not a nation.
- [ ] A3 (assumed) The Austrian (Habsburg) Empire was multinational, so Italian nationalism threatened it directly; Austria used its army to put down Italian revolts.
- [ ] A4 (assumed) Napoleon III, Emperor of the French from 1852, wanted to undo the 1815 settlement and posed as a friend of nationalities, but also needed Catholic support at home, so he protected the Pope.
- [ ] A5 (assumed) The Crimean War (1853–56): Britain, France and the Ottoman Empire against Russia; Austria's unfriendly neutrality cost it Russia's friendship.
- [ ] A6 (assumed) Prussia's wars against Austria (1866) and France (1870) gave Italy Venetia and Rome; only the Italian side belongs here.

Key concepts and essential terms (each defined as used in this topic; also in keyTerms)
- [ ] K1 Nationalism: people sharing language, culture and history should form one state; in Italy it meant expelling Austria and uniting the separate states.
- [ ] K2 Liberalism: constitutional government, an elected parliament and civil liberties; in Italy represented by Piedmont's Statuto and by Cavour.
- [ ] K3 Risorgimento ("resurgence"): the movement for Italian unity and independence, 1815–1870.
- [ ] K4 The competing programmes: a unitary republic (Mazzini), a federation under the Pope (the neo-Guelphs), and a constitutional monarchy led by Piedmont (the moderates, later Cavour).

Italy after 1815
- [ ] I1 The states: Piedmont-Sardinia (House of Savoy, the only major state ruled by a native Italian dynasty, enlarged by Genoa); Lombardy and Venetia ruled directly by Austria; Tuscany, Modena and Parma under Habsburg relatives; the Papal States across central Italy from Rome to Bologna; the Kingdom of the Two Sicilies (Bourbons, Naples and Sicily).
- [ ] I2 Austrian dominance: direct rule of Lombardy-Venetia, family links with the duchies, the fortresses of the Quadrilateral, and armed intervention against revolts.
- [ ] I3 The Papacy: the Pope was a temporal ruler (an absolute monarch with clergy running the administration) as well as head of the Church, so unity threatened his state.
- [ ] I4 Social and economic division: a mainly agricultural economy, poor communications, regional loyalties, dialects instead of a common language, widespread illiteracy, a poor south.

Secret societies and early risings
- [ ] C1 The Carbonari: secret society spreading liberal and nationalist ideas among army officers, students and professional men.
- [ ] C2 The 1820 rising in Naples and the 1821 rising in Piedmont, crushed by Austrian troops; leaders imprisoned or exiled.
- [ ] C3 The 1831 risings in Modena, Parma and the Papal Legations, crushed with Austrian help; Ciro Menotti executed.
- [ ] C4 Why the early risings failed: small secret groups, no mass support, local rather than national aims, no foreign help, Austrian military power. Lesson drawn by Mazzini: a national movement with popular support was needed.

Key personality: Giuseppe Mazzini (one block, every point)
- [ ] M1 Who he was: born Genoa 1805, law graduate, joined the Carbonari, arrested 1830, exiled 1831.
- [ ] M2 Young Italy (Marseille, 1831): a secret society for young men (under 40) who swore to work for an independent, united republic; it spread through branches across Italy.
- [ ] M3 His ideas: a unitary republic with the people as sovereign; "God and the People"; unity through education and popular insurrection, not through kings or foreign help; The Duties of Man (1860) put duty to the nation above individual rights.
- [ ] M4 Failed risings: the Piedmont army plot (1833) with executions; the invasion of Savoy (1834); the Bandiera brothers (1844); Milan (1853); Pisacane's Sapri expedition (1857). Each failed for lack of popular support.
- [ ] M5 Young Europe (1834) and exile in Switzerland, France and, from 1837, London, where he ran his network of exiles.
- [ ] M6 1848–49: returned to Italy; one of the three triumvirs of the Roman Republic (1849), with Garibaldi commanding its defence.
- [ ] M7 After 1849: his reputation suffered as risings failed and moderates turned to Piedmont; his supporters helped prepare the Sicilian rising and Garibaldi's expedition (1860).
- [ ] M8 Participant, not witness: he planned risings and sent agents, and his writings inspired followers, including Garibaldi.
- [ ] M9 Contemporaries: radicals and republicans admired him; Cavour and the monarchists distrusted him; Austria and the Pope saw a dangerous conspirator; to many moderates his risings wasted lives.
- [ ] M10 His disappointment: he rejected the monarchical Italy of 1861; arrested in 1870 while trying to start a republican rising in Sicily; died at Pisa in 1872.
- [ ] M11 Historians: the prophet and ideologue of the Risorgimento who kept the idea of unity alive and inspired a generation, but whose methods failed and whose republic was not achieved.

Moderate alternatives and Pius IX
- [ ] N1 The neo-Guelphs: Gioberti proposed a federation of Italian states under the Pope.
- [ ] N2 Balbo and the moderates: reform and independence led by Piedmont's monarchy, not revolution.
- [ ] N3 Pius IX's election (1846) and early reforms raised hopes of a liberal Pope.

1848–49: revolution and war
- [ ] R1 Causes: economic distress, hopes raised by Pius IX, and news of the February revolution in Paris; the first rising was at Palermo in January 1848; rulers granted constitutions (Naples, Tuscany, Piedmont, the Papal States).
- [ ] R2 The Five Days of Milan (18–22 March 1848): Radetzky withdrew to the Quadrilateral. Venice rose under Daniele Manin and declared the Republic of San Marco.
- [ ] R3 Charles Albert declared war on Austria (March 1848), the First War of Independence, with the slogan that Italy would do it by herself; defeated at Custoza (July 1848).
- [ ] R4 Pius IX withdrew from the war (April 1848); after the murder of his minister Pellegrino Rossi he fled to Gaeta (November 1848).
- [ ] R5 The Roman Republic (February 1849): Mazzini, Saffi and Armellini as triumvirs; Garibaldi's defence; French troops sent by Louis-Napoleon took Rome (July 1849), restored the Pope and kept a garrison there.
- [ ] R6 Novara (March 1849): Charles Albert abdicated; Victor Emmanuel II made peace with Austria.
- [ ] R7 Venice held out until August 1849.
- [ ] R8 Why 1848–49 failed and what it taught: Austria's army, divisions between republicans and monarchists and between states, the Pope's withdrawal, no foreign ally. Lessons: Italy could not do it alone; Piedmont, which kept its constitution and its flag, became the hope of moderates.

Piedmont under Victor Emmanuel II and Cavour
- [ ] P1 The Statuto Albertino (1848): kept after 1849, with an elected chamber; Piedmont the only constitutional state in Italy after 1849, a refuge for exiles.
- [ ] P2 The Siccardi Laws (1850): church courts and some clerical privileges abolished; conflict with the Papacy.
- [ ] P3 Cavour: landowner and journalist (the newspaper Il Risorgimento), minister of agriculture and then finance, Prime Minister from November 1852 through the connubio (alliance with Rattazzi's centre-left).
- [ ] P4 Economic modernisation: free trade treaties, railway building, banking and credit, agricultural improvement, a reformed army. Piedmont became the most advanced Italian state.
- [ ] P5 The Crimean War: about 15,000 troops (1855) won Piedmont a seat at the Congress of Paris (1856), where Cavour raised the Italian question before the Great Powers.
- [ ] P6 The Italian National Society (1857): backed unity under Victor Emmanuel and Piedmont instead of Mazzini's republic; former republicans such as Manin and Garibaldi supported it.
- [ ] P7 Orsini's attempt on Napoleon III's life (January 1858) pushed Napoleon III towards action in Italy.
- [ ] P8 Plombières (July 1858): France to fight Austria if Austria appeared the aggressor; a kingdom of Upper Italy for Piedmont; Savoy (and, less firmly, Nice) for France; the marriage of Princess Clotilde to Prince Napoleon.
- [ ] P9 The price: the cession of Nice and Savoy angered nationalists, above all Garibaldi, born in Nice.

The war of 1859 and the annexation of central Italy
- [ ] W1 Austria's ultimatum and war (April 1859), provoked by Piedmont's mobilisation; France joined Piedmont.
- [ ] W2 Magenta (4 June 1859) and Solferino (24 June 1859).
- [ ] W3 Why Napoleon III stopped: heavy losses, Prussian mobilisation on the Rhine, Catholic anger in France, and fear of a too-strong Piedmont.
- [ ] W4 Villafranca (11 July 1859): agreed without Cavour, who resigned; Lombardy to Piedmont, Venetia kept by Austria. Confirmed by the Treaty of Zürich (November 1859).
- [ ] W5 Central Italy: the rulers of Tuscany, Modena and Parma fled and the Romagna rose against the Pope; Cavour returned to office (January 1860); plebiscites (March 1860) voted to join Piedmont.
- [ ] W6 The Treaty of Turin (March 1860): Nice and Savoy ceded to France in return for Napoleon III's acceptance.

Garibaldi, the Thousand and the Kingdom of Italy
- [ ] G1 Garibaldi: born in Nice (1807), a Mazzinian condemned to death after the 1834 plot, exile and guerrilla fighting in South America (the red shirts), defender of the Roman Republic (1849).
- [ ] G2 The Thousand sailed from Quarto near Genoa in May 1860 after a rising in Sicily; Cavour neither stopped nor openly backed them.
- [ ] G3 Sicily: landing at Marsala, victory at Calatafimi, capture of Palermo; Garibaldi ruled as dictator in the name of Victor Emmanuel II.
- [ ] G4 The mainland: Garibaldi crossed the Strait of Messina and entered Naples (7 September 1860) as Francis II withdrew; the battle of the Volturno.
- [ ] G5 Cavour's response: fearing a march on Rome and French or Austrian intervention, he sent the Piedmontese army through the Papal Marches and Umbria (Castelfidardo), avoiding Rome itself.
- [ ] G6 Teano (October 1860): Garibaldi handed his conquests to Victor Emmanuel II and retired to Caprera; plebiscites approved annexation in the south, the Marches and Umbria.
- [ ] G7 The Kingdom of Italy proclaimed on 17 March 1861 (capital Turin); Cavour died on 6 June 1861.
- [ ] G8 Garibaldi's marches on Rome: stopped by Italian troops at Aspromonte (1862); defeated by French and papal troops at Mentana (1867).

Venetia and Rome, 1864–1871
- [ ] V1 The September Convention (1864): France to withdraw from Rome; Italy's capital moved to Florence (1865).
- [ ] V2 1866: alliance with Prussia; Italian defeats at Custoza (on land) and Lissa (at sea); Prussia's victory forced Austria to give up Venetia, handed through Napoleon III by the Treaty of Vienna (1866) and approved by plebiscite.
- [ ] V3 1870: the French garrison left Rome on the outbreak of the Franco-Prussian War; after Sedan, Italian troops entered Rome (20 September 1870); plebiscite (October 1870).
- [ ] V4 Rome the capital (1871); the Law of Guarantees (1871) offered the Pope privileges; Pius IX rejected it and called himself a prisoner of the Vatican.

Obstacles, and why attempts after 1852 succeeded
- [ ] O1 Obstacle: Austria's military power and its position in Lombardy-Venetia and the Quadrilateral, until 1866.
- [ ] O2 Obstacle: the Papacy and its French protectors, until 1870.
- [ ] O3 Obstacle: regionalism, local loyalties, no common spoken language, illiteracy and peasant indifference.
- [ ] O4 Obstacle: divisions among nationalists (republicans vs monarchists, unitary vs federal) and reliance on small conspiracies.
- [ ] S1 Success factor: Piedmont as a strong leading state, constitutional, modernised and with a regular army.
- [ ] S2 Success factor: Cavour's diplomacy (the Crimean War, Paris 1856, Plombières) isolated Austria and won French help.
- [ ] S3 Success factor: foreign help and Great Power circumstances: France in 1859, Prussia in 1866 and 1870, Britain's goodwill, Austria's isolation after the Crimean War.
- [ ] S4 Success factor: Garibaldi's volunteers and popular enthusiasm in 1860, which moved faster than a regular army could.
- [ ] S5 Success factor: the National Society brought many former republicans behind Piedmont, ending the 1848 divisions.
- [ ] S6 The roles compared: Mazzini the idea and inspiration, Cavour the diplomacy and the Piedmontese state, Garibaldi the military daring, Victor Emmanuel II the monarchy all could accept.

The new Kingdom's problems
- [ ] E1 A narrow franchise: about 2 per cent of the population could vote.
- [ ] E2 "Piedmontisation": Piedmont's constitution, laws, officials and taxes extended to the whole kingdom; a centralised state, not a federation.
- [ ] E3 The south: peasants gained little; brigandage was put down by the army in a war lasting several years after 1861 (the southern question).
- [ ] E4 Illiteracy (about three-quarters of Italians in 1861) and few speakers of standard Italian.
- [ ] E5 The Pope's hostility kept many Catholics apart from the new state.

Assessment (paired judgement points, final block)
- [ ] H1 Achievement: by 1871 the peninsula was united under one constitutional monarchy with Rome as its capital, and Austria was expelled.
- [ ] H2 Limit: unity came largely through French and Prussian victories and Piedmontese expansion, not a popular national rising; the state was narrow, centralised and poorly integrated.
- [ ] H3 Achievement vs limit for Mazzini: the idea of a united Italy won, his republican, popular Italy did not.
- [ ] H4 Historians: Denis Mack Smith on the rivalry between Cavour and Garibaldi; Antonio Gramsci's "passive revolution" that left out the peasants; Lucy Riall on how the Garibaldi legend was built.
- [ ] H5 Italy vs Germany (for comparison questions): both led by one strong kingdom using war and diplomacy, but Italy relied more on foreign help, ended weaker and less united, and was a constitutional monarchy rather than a Prussian-dominated empire. Keep to two or three points; detail of Germany belongs elsewhere.

## 3. Traps

Do not use (wrong or doubtful facts in the current notes):
- Do not use: Mazzini's "arrest and exile in 1830", because he was arrested in 1830 and went into exile early in 1831. Give both dates.
- Do not use: Habsburg "princes" ruling Parma, because Parma went to Marie-Louise, Napoleon's Habsburg widow, until 1847. Say Tuscany and Modena had Habsburg rulers and Parma went to Marie-Louise.
- Do not use: the Thousand sailing on "5 May 1860" as a firm date, because they left on the night of 5–6 May. Say the night of 5–6 May, or May 1860.
- Do not use: the National Society "founded by Manin, La Farina and Garibaldi", because Garibaldi joined as vice-president; the founders were Manin, Pallavicino and La Farina.
- Do not use: Plombières as giving France "Nice and Savoy" without qualification, because Savoy was agreed and Nice was left less firmly settled; both were in the January 1859 treaty.
- Do not use: Mentana "by French troops" alone, because Garibaldi was beaten by French and papal troops together.
- Do not use: "the Piedmontese model of ... a modern economy was adopted in the new Kingdom" as an achievement, because the new kingdom stayed mostly agricultural and the south poor. What was extended was Piedmont's constitution, laws and administration (Piedmontisation).
- Do not use: "arrested in Sicily" for Mazzini in 1870 without detail; say he was arrested on arrival at Palermo and imprisoned at Gaeta.

Doubtful quotations: do not use
- Massimo d'Azeglio's "We have made Italy; now we must make Italians" is widely quoted but its wording is not well attested. Give the idea without quotation marks, without naming him as the speaker.
- Metternich calling Mazzini "the most dangerous man in Europe" is not well attested. Do not use it.
- Do not invent a Cavour quotation; none is checked here.
- "The soul, the brain and the sword" (Mazzini, Cavour, Garibaldi) is a textbook summary, not a quotation. It may be used as a summary without quotation marks.

Confusable people, terms and dates
- Custoza was fought twice: July 1848 (Piedmont defeated) and June 1866 (Italy defeated). Novara is March 1849.
- Charles Albert (king to 1849, granted the Statuto, fought 1848–49) vs Victor Emmanuel II (his son, king of Piedmont from 1849, King of Italy from 1861).
- Villafranca (armistice, July 1859) vs the Treaty of Zürich (November 1859) vs the Treaty of Turin (March 1860, Nice and Savoy) vs the Treaty of Vienna (October 1866, Venetia; not the 1815 Congress of Vienna).
- The Roman Republic (1849, Mazzini) is not the Republic of San Marco (Venice, 1848–49, Manin).
- Young Italy (1831, Marseille) vs Young Europe (1834, Bern); the Carbonari came before both.
- Aspromonte (1862, stopped by Italian troops) vs Mentana (1867, French and papal troops).
- Pius IX is the Pope throughout 1846–1878: the liberal hope of 1846–48, then the enemy of unification.
- Napoleon III appears twice in the Roman story: as President Louis-Napoleon he sent troops to crush the Roman Republic (1849); as Emperor he kept the garrison that blocked unity until 1870.
- Capitals: Turin (1861), Florence (1865), Rome (1871).
- Calatafimi (Sicily, May 1860) vs Castelfidardo (Marches, September 1860, the Piedmontese army) vs the Volturno (near Naples, October 1860, Garibaldi).
- The Kingdom of Italy (1861) is not Napoleon's Kingdom of Italy (1805).

## 4. What exams ask

- Higher 100-mark essay on the stages of progress towards unity, 1815–1871: marks go to a clear structure by stages (secret societies, Mazzini, 1848–49, Piedmont and Cavour, 1859–60, 1866, 1870) with dated evidence for each stage and a judgement on which factors mattered most. A plain chronological story earns less than an answer that shows how each stage moved things on.
- Obstacles and how they were overcome: name each obstacle (Austria, the Papacy and France, division and regionalism, weak and divided nationalists) and pair it with how it was removed (1859 and 1866 wars, 1870, Piedmont's leadership and the plebiscites, the National Society). Pairing obstacle with solution is what earns credit.
- Why attempts after 1852 succeeded when earlier ones failed: a cause question. Contrast 1820–49 (conspiracies, no strong state, no ally, divisions) with after 1852 (Piedmont, Cavour's diplomacy, France and Prussia, Garibaldi). Each factor needs a concrete example and date.
- Mazzini's contribution: judge it, do not just narrate his life. Strong answers balance ideas, inspiration and Young Italy against failed risings, rejection of monarchy and disappointment in 1861, and state a verdict.
- Comparison essays (Mazzini vs Bismarck; Italy vs Germany): need a clear verdict and point-by-point comparison (methods, results, how far unity was complete). For Italy students need Mazzini's ideas and achievements and the role of foreign help stated explicitly.
- Ordinary Level 30/40-mark paragraphs on Mazzini (the commonest) or on Cavour and Piedmont: each correct, relevant, dated statement earns marks. Mazzini: birth, Carbonari, exile, Young Italy and its aims, motto, failed risings, Young Europe, Roman Republic, attitude to 1861, death. Cavour: Prime Minister 1852, economic reform, Crimean War and Paris, Plombières, 1859, central Italy, Nice and Savoy, attitude to Garibaldi.
- Ordinary Level document questions on the new Kingdom: who ruled it, what its economy was based on, literacy, the war on brigandage in the south, why Pius IX opposed unity. Students need the problems of united Italy (E1–E5), not only the story of unification.
- Achievements vs limits: rewarded as paired points (united and Austria expelled vs a narrow, centralised, Piedmont-dominated state with a neglected south and a hostile Pope). Naming a historian with a view (Mack Smith, Gramsci) adds weight.
