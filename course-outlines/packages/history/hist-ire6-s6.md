## 1. Must cover (one bullet or block for every item; tick each off)

Background (assumed)
- [ ] A1 (assumed) Ireland had been a member of the League of Nations (1923–46) and was neutral in the Second World War ("the Emergency"); neutrality shaped how other states saw it in 1946.
- [ ] A2 (assumed) The Cold War: the United States-led West and the Soviet bloc; NATO (1949); the UN Security Council with five permanent members who hold a veto.
- [ ] A3 (assumed) Partition: Ireland's stated reason for refusing to join NATO in 1949.
- [ ] A4 (assumed) Britain was Ireland's main market in the 1950s, so a British decision on the EEC forced an Irish one; the economic turn from protection to free trade after 1958 (the First Programme; see the notes on economic planning).
- [ ] A5 (assumed) The Belgian Congo became independent on 30 June 1960 and descended into disorder, with Katanga province seceding; this is the setting for the UN force.

Key concept (also in keyTerms)
- [ ] K1 Common market: members trade freely without tariffs among themselves, apply a common tariff to outsiders, and allow free movement of goods, workers and capital.
- [ ] K2 Essential terms (not outline key concepts): veto; peacekeeping; neutrality; non-proliferation; Single European Act; Crotty case; ONUC; Common Agricultural Policy.

The UN, 1946–1955
- [ ] U1 Ireland applied in 1946 and was vetoed by the Soviet Union: reasons given were Ireland's wartime neutrality and the lack of diplomatic relations.
- [ ] U2 The deadlock was broken by a package deal in 1955: sixteen states admitted together; Ireland admitted 14 December 1955.
- [ ] U3 Ireland's position in the Cold War: military neutrality (no bloc), but a Western Christian democracy broadly sympathetic to the West on communism.
- [ ] U4 What membership gave a small state: a voice, recognition, a platform for independent policy.

Frank Aiken and the independent Irish line
- [ ] N1 Who he was: Minister for External Affairs 1951–54 and 1957–69; veteran of the War of Independence and Civil War; in de Valera's governments from 1932.
- [ ] N2 China: from 1957 Ireland would not back the American-led move to block debate on the seat of the People's Republic of China; this irritated Washington.
- [ ] N3 Tibet: Ireland and Malaya sponsored a 1959 resolution criticising the suppression of human rights in Tibet.
- [ ] N4 Non-proliferation: Irish resolutions from 1958; Resolution 1665 (4 December 1961) called on states not to transfer or acquire nuclear weapons.
- [ ] N5 The result: the Treaty on the Non-Proliferation of Nuclear Weapons, opened for signature in 1968; Ireland was the first state to sign.
- [ ] N6 Views of the Irish line: critics (naive, inconsistent with Ireland's Western position) vs supporters (a small state could influence world opinion).

Peacekeeping
- [ ] P1 Irish officers as UN observers in Lebanon from 1958.
- [ ] P2 The Congo (ONUC) from July 1960: why the UN went in, and the Irish contribution (about 6,000 served to 1964; twenty-six died).
- [ ] P3 Niemba, 8 November 1960: an Irish patrol ambushed in Katanga; nine killed.
- [ ] P4 Jadotville, September 1961: A Company, 35th Battalion, under Commandant Pat Quinlan, besieged five days, surrendered, held for weeks, released (brief; detail belongs with the notes on decolonisation in Africa).
- [ ] P5 Cyprus (UNFICYP) from 1964: keeping the Greek and Turkish Cypriot communities apart.
- [ ] P6 Lebanon (UNIFIL) from 1978: set up after Israel's invasion of southern Lebanon.
- [ ] P7 Legal basis: the Defence (Amendment) Act 1960 allowed Irish contingents to serve abroad under the UN.
- [ ] P8 Significance: neutral state takes part in world affairs without joining an alliance; a source of national pride; the Defence Forces' reputation.

Frederick Boland and the General Assembly, 1960
- [ ] B1 Who he was: Secretary of the Department of External Affairs, ambassador in London, then Ireland's permanent representative at the UN from 1956.
- [ ] B2 Elected President of the fifteenth session of the General Assembly (September 1960): an honour for a state that had joined only five years before.
- [ ] B3 The session: dominated by the Congo and Cold War tension; Boland broke his gavel (October 1960) in a heated exchange involving Khrushchev.
- [ ] B4 Significance: respect won by Ireland; many newly independent African states admitted that session. Ireland on the Security Council in 1962.

The EEC, 1957–1973
- [ ] E1 The EEC: Treaty of Rome 1957; six founding states; customs union and common external tariff.
- [ ] E2 Why Ireland applied: fear of being outside a market that Britain joined; hope for farm-export access and investment; Lemass's modernisation aim.
- [ ] E3 First application: 31 July 1961, backed by the Dáil, main parties and farm groups.
- [ ] E4 De Gaulle's veto of British entry, 14 January 1963: negotiations stopped; the Irish application stayed on the table.
- [ ] E5 Preparation: bodies to improve competitiveness; Free Trade Agreement with Britain (1965) (one line; see the notes on economic planning).
- [ ] E6 Second application, 1967, with Britain, Denmark and Norway; second veto in November 1967.
- [ ] E7 De Gaulle's resignation (1969); the Hague summit (December 1969) agreed to enlarge; negotiations opened in Luxembourg in 1970.
- [ ] E8 Hillery led the Irish negotiators; the five-year transition for industry; terms on farm prices (fisheries detail belongs with the notes on the EEC's impact).
- [ ] E9 Treaty of Accession signed in Brussels, 22 January 1972.
- [ ] E10 The referendum, 10 May 1972 (the Third Amendment): about 83 per cent yes on a turnout of about 71 per cent.
- [ ] E11 Who was for (Fianna Fáil, Fine Gael, farm and employers' bodies) and against (Labour, Sinn Féin, some trade unionists, the Common Market Defence Campaign) and why: sovereignty, neutrality, small firms and farms, foreign ownership.
- [ ] E12 Entry on 1 January 1973 with Britain and Denmark; Norway's voters had said no (September 1972); Hillery became the first Irish member of the European Commission.
- [ ] E13 Motives, economic and political; the CAP as the main attraction for farmers; the strong yes vote showed the link between membership and economic hopes.

Neutrality
- [ ] T1 Irish refusal of NATO in 1949 (partition given as the reason) and the policy of military neutrality.
- [ ] T2 In 1972 opponents asked whether membership affected neutrality; the government said it did not require joining a military alliance.

The Single European Act and the Crotty case
- [ ] C1 The Single European Act (signed February 1986): single market by end of 1992; more qualified majority voting; a legal basis for European political cooperation (foreign policy coordination).
- [ ] C2 Raymond Crotty, an economist and critic of the EEC, challenged ratification.
- [ ] C3 The Supreme Court, 9 April 1987: the political cooperation provisions limited the state's freedom in foreign policy, so ratification needed a constitutional amendment.
- [ ] C4 The Tenth Amendment, referendum 26 May 1987: about 70 per cent yes, turnout about 44 per cent.
- [ ] C5 Significance: major transfers of power to the EEC/EU would need a referendum.

Assessment: UN or EEC (paired judgement points, final block)
- [ ] S1 UN: recognition, independent policies (China, Tibet, nuclear weapons), respected peacekeeping, a shaper of Irish identity and foreign policy.
- [ ] S2 EEC: guaranteed farm prices, a large market, regional and social funds, foreign investment, less dependence on Britain.
- [ ] S3 Contemporary views: UN supporters (best guarantee of independence and neutrality) vs EEC supporters (the only way to modernise and end dependence on Britain).
- [ ] S4 Later view: historians generally judge the EEC the deeper influence on economy and society, the UN on self-image and foreign policy. A clear judgement is needed.

## 4. What exams ask

- Higher 100-mark essay, UN and/or EEC: which affected the Republic more, or the effects of each. Marks go to a stated judgement, balanced points on each, and dated detail. A narrative of one organisation with a sentence on the other scores poorly. Most strong answers judge that the EEC had the deeper effect and give a reason (farm incomes, funds, investment, less dependence on Britain), while crediting the UN for independence and reputation.
- Higher essay on "isolation to international involvement": show the sequence 1946 veto, 1955 entry, 1960 Congo, 1961 EEC application, 1973 entry. Link neutrality to peacekeeping.
- Ordinary Level 30-mark paragraph on the UN: the 1946 application and Soviet veto, 1955 admission (14 December), Aiken's independent line with one or two examples, peacekeeping (Congo, Cyprus, Lebanon with dates), Boland in 1960. Each correct, dated, relevant statement earns marks, so list four or five, not one long story.
- Ordinary Level short answers (6 marks) on a document: the year Ireland joined the EEC and with whom, the Irish condition for supporting British entry, farm benefits; give one fact or one reason, in a sentence.
- Cause and consequence: why Ireland applied in 1961 (British entry, markets, modernisation); why the applications failed twice (de Gaulle); why the 1972 vote was so large; why the Single European Act needed a referendum.
- Judgement on peacekeeping: pride and reputation against cost in lives (Niemba, the Congo); name the missions and dates.
- Comparison of views: supporters and opponents of entry in 1972, with the opponents' reasons (sovereignty, neutrality, foreign ownership).
- Examiners credit precise figures (nine killed at Niemba, about 83 per cent yes) when correct; they penalise confusing the 1972 and 1987 referendums.
