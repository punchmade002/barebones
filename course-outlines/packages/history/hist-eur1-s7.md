## 1. Must cover (one bullet or block for every item; tick each off)

Background
- [ ] B1 (assumed) What "Industrial Revolution" means: the shift from hand production at home or in small workshops (the domestic system) to machine production in factories powered by water and then steam, beginning in Britain in the later eighteenth century.
- [ ] B2 (assumed) Why Britain industrialised first: coal and iron ore close together; capital from trade and farming; colonial and overseas markets; a large merchant fleet and navy; rivers, canals and ports; political stability; a growing population fed by improved farming.
- [ ] B3 (assumed) Population growth: Britain's population roughly doubled between 1801 and 1851, giving both workers and customers; growth across Europe put pressure on land and food.
- [ ] B4 (assumed) Before 1815 most Europeans lived on the land and worked in farming; western Europe was ahead of central and eastern Europe in both farming and industry.

Agriculture in Britain
- [ ] A1 Enclosure: open fields and common land consolidated into compact, hedged farms, mostly by private Acts of Parliament (thousands of them, most between 1750 and 1850); the General Enclosure Acts of 1801 and 1845 simplified the procedure.
- [ ] A2 Effects of enclosure: larger, more efficient farms and higher output, but cottagers and labourers lost common rights and access to land, so many became wage labourers, moved to towns or emigrated.
- [ ] A3 New crops and rotation: the Norfolk four-course rotation (wheat, turnips, barley, clover) ended fallow years; turnips and clover fed livestock through winter, so more animals and more manure.
- [ ] A4 Earlier improvers as background (one bullet): Jethro Tull's seed drill (early 1700s); Robert Bakewell's selective breeding in Leicestershire.
- [ ] A5 Fertilisers and science from the 1840s: guano imported from Peru; John Bennet Lawes's superphosphate (from 1842) and the Rothamsted experiments; Justus von Liebig's agricultural chemistry (1840); field drainage.
- [ ] A6 Machinery: the threshing machine; reapers (the McCormick reaper shown at the Great Exhibition, 1851); steam power on farms later in the period. Mechanisation cut the need for labour, and anger over threshing machines fed the Swing Riots (1830) in southern England.
- [ ] A7 The Corn Laws (1815) protected British grain growers by keeping out cheap foreign grain until prices were high; their repeal in 1846 is one clause here (Peel's role belongs to the notes on responses to industrial society).

Agriculture in France and the German lands
- [ ] A8 France: the Revolution of 1789 abolished feudal dues and left a large class of peasant owners; farms stayed small, methods slow to change, and many peasants stayed on the land, which slowed migration to industry.
- [ ] A9 Prussia: the Stein–Hardenberg reforms (October Edict 1807 abolished personal serfdom; the 1811 edict let peasants become owners in return for surrendering part of their land to the lord); the Junker estates of the east grew large, modernised and produced grain for export and the cities.
- [ ] A10 New crops on the continent: the potato as the staple of the poor; sugar beet in France and the German lands.
- [ ] A11 The 1840s crisis: potato blight from 1845 and bad grain harvests in 1846 and 1847 raised food prices and caused hunger and food riots across western and central Europe; the crisis, with the industrial depression of 1847, helped create the conditions for the revolutions of 1848 (one clause; the 1848 story belongs to its own case study).
- [ ] A12 Emigration: from Ireland after the Famine (one clause) and from the German lands to the United States, which grew strongly in the late 1840s and 1850s; rural people and artisans seeking land and work.

Industrialisation in Britain
- [ ] I1 The leading industries: coal, iron and cotton textiles, with the factory and steam power at the centre.
- [ ] I2 Steam: James Watt's improved steam engine (1760s–1780s, including rotary motion) used to pump mines and drive factory machinery, so factories no longer had to be built by rivers.
- [ ] I3 Textiles: Richard Arkwright's water frame and Samuel Crompton's spinning mule mechanised spinning; power looms spread weaving into factories; Lancashire cotton mills used raw cotton mostly from the southern United States; Britain became the leading exporter of cotton goods.
- [ ] I4 Iron and steel: Abraham Darby's coke smelting at Coalbrookdale (background); iron for rails, bridges and machines; Henry Bessemer's converter (1856) made cheap steel.
- [ ] I5 Railways: the Stockton and Darlington Railway (1825); the Stephensons' Rocket at the Rainhill Trials (1829); the Liverpool and Manchester Railway (1830), the first inter-city line worked by steam locomotives with timetabled passenger trains; the railway boom of the 1840s ("railway mania").
- [ ] I6 Effects of railways: cheaper, faster transport of goods and people; demand for coal, iron and engineering; national markets; employment in construction.
- [ ] I7 The Great Exhibition (1851) at the Crystal Palace showed Britain as the "workshop of the world".

The spread of industrialisation to the continent
- [ ] I8 Why the continent was slower: the French wars, poorer transport, internal tariffs and many states (German lands), less accessible coal, smaller markets, a peasantry that stayed on the land; continental states copied British machines and hired British engineers.
- [ ] I9 Belgium, first on the continent: coal in Hainaut and Liège; John Cockerill's ironworks and engineering at Seraing; Belgium's first railway, the steam-worked Brussels–Mechelen line (1835), part of a state-planned network.
- [ ] I10 France: slower, mixed growth; textiles in Alsace (Mulhouse) and Normandy (Rouen); the Schneider works at Le Creusot; the Saint-Étienne–Andrézieux line for coal (1827); the Railway Law of 1842 (state builds the track bed, companies run the lines); faster growth in the 1850s and 1860s under the Second Empire (one clause; Napoleon III's economic policy belongs to the notes on France).
- [ ] I11 The German lands: the Zollverein (1834) removed internal tariffs (one clause; its founding belongs to the Metternich and unification notes); the first German railway, Nuremberg–Fürth (1835); coal and iron in the Ruhr and Silesia; Krupp at Essen expanding from the 1850s; Friedrich List's case for railways and protective tariffs (1841); by 1871 Germany was becoming Europe's leading industrial power on the continent.
- [ ] I12 Banks and the state: continental industry relied more on investment banks and state help (railways, tariffs) than Britain, where firms grew largely from their own profits and private capital.

The factory system and working conditions
- [ ] W1 The factory system: workers concentrated under supervision, working fixed hours to the pace of machines for wages, with fines and discipline; the decline of hand workers such as handloom weavers.
- [ ] W2 Conditions: long hours (often 12 to 14 a day in cotton mills); child and female labour; accidents with unfenced machinery; poor ventilation, dust and heat; low wages; little or no compensation for injury.
- [ ] W3 Mines: children and women underground, dangerous work; the Mines Act (1842).
- [ ] W4 Resistance and inquiry: the Luddites (1811–16) broke machines; the Sadler Committee (1832) gathered evidence of child labour; Lord Ashley (later Earl of Shaftesbury) campaigned for factory reform.
- [ ] W5 The Factory Act of 1833: no children under nine in most textile mills; shorter hours for children 9–13 and young persons 13–18; some schooling for child workers; inspectors to enforce it.
- [ ] W6 The Ten Hours Act (1847): ten hours a day for women and young persons in textile mills. (The 1844 Factory Act is one clause here; it belongs with Peel.)
- [ ] W7 Life for agricultural workers was also harsh: low wages, insecure seasonal work, loss of common rights, and poor cottages; exams ask about both groups.

Laissez-faire and the role of the state
- [ ] L1 Laissez-faire defined: the belief that the state should leave the economy, prices, wages and trade to the free market; Adam Smith's The Wealth of Nations (1776); Thomas Malthus and David Ricardo as later economists in this tradition.
- [ ] L2 Laissez-faire in practice in Britain: free trade (repeal of the Corn Laws, 1846, one clause); the Poor Law Amendment Act (1834) and the workhouse "less eligibility" principle for the able-bodied poor.
- [ ] L3 The limits of laissez-faire: the state intervened in child and female labour (Factory Acts, Mines Act) and public health, but not in men's wages or hours.
- [ ] L4 The continent was more interventionist: Prussia and France promoted railways, tariffs and banks; List argued that young German industry needed protection.

Urban growth and migration
- [ ] U1 Manchester ("Cottonopolis"): from about 75,000 people in 1801 to about 300,000 in 1851; other fast-growing towns (Liverpool, Leeds, Birmingham, Glasgow).
- [ ] U2 The 1851 census: for the first time more than half the people of England and Wales lived in towns.
- [ ] U3 Migration from the countryside: push (enclosure, mechanisation, rural poverty, the 1840s crisis) and pull (factory work, wages); Irish migrants to British cities, especially in the 1840s.
- [ ] U4 Paris: grew mainly by migration from the provinces, roughly doubling to about a million in the first half of the century; overcrowding in the old centre.
- [ ] U5 The Ruhr: Essen, Bochum and Dortmund grew fast in the 1850s and 1860s, drawing workers from rural Prussia; mass Polish-speaking migration came later (one clause).

Slums, cholera and public health
- [ ] H1 Slum conditions: back-to-back houses, cellar dwellings (Little Ireland in Manchester), overcrowding, no drains, shared privies, polluted water, smoke; high death rates, especially among infants.
- [ ] H2 Witnesses: Friedrich Engels, The Condition of the Working Class in England (1845), on Manchester.
- [ ] H3 Cholera: epidemics in Britain in 1831–32 and 1848–49 (and 1853–54), and in Paris in 1832 and 1849; also typhus and tuberculosis.
- [ ] H4 Edwin Chadwick's Report on the Sanitary Condition of the Labouring Population (1842) linked dirt and poor drainage to disease and early death.
- [ ] H5 The Public Health Act (1848): a General Board of Health and local boards of health, but it was permissive (towns were not forced to act).
- [ ] H6 John Snow traced the Broad Street (Soho) cholera outbreak to a contaminated pump (1854), showing that cholera spread through water.
- [ ] H7 London's sewers: after the "Great Stink" (1858), Joseph Bazalgette built London's main drainage system from 1859.
- [ ] H8 Housing: the Peabody Trust (1862) built model dwellings for London's poor; the Public Health Act of 1875 made sanitary provision compulsory (one clause, after the period). Haussmann's rebuilding of Paris is one clause only (own case study).

Primary education and rising literacy
- [ ] E1 Why states expanded schooling: to produce disciplined, literate workers and soldiers, loyal citizens and subjects; religious and moral aims of the churches; fear of disorder; later, the need for voters who could read.
- [ ] E2 Prussia: compulsory schooling from the eighteenth century (the General School Regulations, 1763); state-supervised village schools and trained teachers; Prussia was seen as Europe's model and had among the highest literacy rates.
- [ ] E3 France: the Guizot Law (1833) required each commune to maintain a primary school (in practice for boys) and each department a teacher-training college (école normale); schooling was not free or compulsory for all. Victor Duruy's law (1867) required larger communes to provide girls' schools. (The Falloux Law of 1850 and church schools belong to the notes on the churches.)
- [ ] E4 Britain before 1870: schools run by voluntary religious societies, the Anglican National Society and the Nonconformist British and Foreign School Society, using the monitorial system; Sunday schools (one clause; they belong with the churches).
- [ ] E5 British state grants: the first grant (1833, £20,000) to the two societies; the Committee of the Privy Council on Education (1839) set up to supervise a larger grant, with James Kay-Shuttleworth as its secretary; school inspectors.
- [ ] E6 The Newcastle Commission (report 1861) found many children were poorly taught; Robert Lowe's Revised Code (1862) brought "payment by results": grants depended on pupils' results in reading, writing and arithmetic.
- [ ] E7 The Elementary Education Act (1870), introduced by W. E. Forster: elected school boards to build schools where voluntary provision was inadequate; fees still charged and attendance not yet compulsory everywhere.
- [ ] E8 Rising literacy and its effects: more people could read newspapers, books and pamphlets; the growth of a cheap popular press; spread of political ideas, nationalism and mass politics; literacy higher in Protestant northern Europe and lower in the south and east.

Assessment (end with this block, headed "Assessment: …")
- [ ] J1 Gains: rising output and national wealth; more food from improved farming; railways and cheaper goods; by the end of the period, factory, public health and education reform.
- [ ] J2 Costs: loss of land and common rights; child labour and dangerous work; slums and epidemics; the 1840s hunger; slow and uneven government response because of laissez-faire.
- [ ] J3 Uneven change: Britain first and furthest; Belgium early; France slower with its peasant farms; the German lands rapid after 1850; eastern and southern Europe largely agricultural in 1871.
- [ ] J4 Contemporary vs later views: critics at the time (Engels, Chadwick, the factory reformers) saw misery; supporters saw progress (the Great Exhibition); historians still debate whether living standards for workers rose or fell in the early decades (the "standard of living debate"), with broad agreement that they rose after about 1850.

## 3. Traps

Do not use (doubtful or wrong in the current notes):
- Do not use "Bakewell ... producing sheep and cattle with more meat and wool": his New Leicester sheep were bred for meat, and their wool was coarse. Say "for meat" (A4).
- Do not use "George Stephenson's locomotive Rocket" alone: it was built by George and his son Robert, with Robert doing much of the design. Say "the Stephensons' Rocket" or "built by George and Robert Stephenson" (I5).
- Do not use "the first steam-hauled passenger railway with a timetable" as a bare claim: the Stockton and Darlington (1825) used steam locomotives earlier. Say "the first inter-city railway worked by steam locomotives, with timetabled passenger trains" (I5).
- Do not use "the 1851 census showed that ... more than half of the people of Britain lived in towns": the figure is for England and Wales (U2).
- Do not use "Cholera ... killing thousands in each city" with Britain listed as a city; say thousands died in each epidemic, and give no exact toll (H3).
- Do not use "The Poor Law Amendment Act of 1834 required the poor to enter workhouses to receive relief" without "able-bodied": the sick and old, and in practice many able-bodied in the North, still received outdoor relief (L2).
- Do not use "The Guizot Law ... required each commune to maintain a boys' primary school": the law required a primary school; that these were in practice for boys is the point. Use the E3 wording.
- Do not use "the Public Health Act of 1848 ... with powers over drainage" without saying it was largely permissive (H5).
- Do not use "Prussia had required children to attend school since ... a decree of 1763" as the first such law: there were earlier decrees; say "compulsory schooling from the eighteenth century, confirmed by the General School Regulations of 1763" (E2).
- Do not use "the first railway on the European continent" (or "first on the continent") for Brussels–Mechelen, 1835: the Saint-Étienne–Andrézieux line (1827, horse-drawn at first) is earlier, and other claims (e.g. Saint-Étienne–Lyon) are contested. Say "Belgium's first railway, steam-worked, opened 5 May 1835" (I9).
- Do not use "Industrial growth on the continent was often financed by banks ..., while in Britain it was financed mainly from profits" as absolute: say "more" and "largely" (I12).

## 4. What exams ask (topics only)

- Higher 100-mark essay on the main changes in agriculture and industry, 1815–1871 (the most frequent question for this subsection). Marks go for a clear structure (agriculture, then industry), several countries rather than Britain only, and specific dated evidence: enclosure and the Norfolk rotation, fertilisers and machinery, the Stein–Hardenberg reforms and French peasant farms, the 1840s crisis; steam, cotton, iron and steel (Bessemer 1856), railways (1825, 1830, the 1840s boom, Belgium and Germany 1835, the French law of 1842), Belgium as first on the continent, the Ruhr and Krupp. The best answers explain why change happened and how fast in each country, not a list of inventions.
- Higher essay on why life was harsh for agricultural and industrial workers: both groups needed. Farm workers: enclosure, loss of common rights, mechanisation and the Swing Riots, the 1840s hunger and emigration. Industrial workers: hours, child labour, accidents, slums and cholera, the workhouse. Marks for explaining causes (laissez-faire, the speed of urban growth) and for naming the reforms and their limits.
- Higher essay on the main social and economic developments: agriculture, industry, urban growth and public health, and education with literacy. Students should be able to link them (industry pulls migrants to towns; towns breed disease; reform follows inquiry).
- Higher essays may pair industrialisation with trade unionism and socialism: here the student supplies the industrial cause (the factory working class and its conditions); the unions and socialism come from the notes on responses to industrial society.
- Ordinary Level 30-mark paragraphs on urban growth and migration (Manchester's growth with its two figures, other cities, Irish migrants, Paris, the 1851 census, slums and cholera) and on industrialisation in Britain or on the continent (Watt, Arkwright, railways 1830, Belgium first, Nuremberg–Fürth 1835). Each SRP is one specific fact, so many short dated facts score best.
- Ordinary Level document question on primary education in Britain: students should recognise the two religious societies, the government grant and the committee set up to supervise it, the Newcastle Commission's finding, Lowe's payment by results, and explain why literacy mattered (newspapers, books, nationalism).
- Judgement material (Assessment block): gains vs costs of economic change; how far governments responded (laissez-faire vs Factory Acts and public health); Britain vs the continent (uneven and later change). Marks go for paired points with evidence and a final verdict.
