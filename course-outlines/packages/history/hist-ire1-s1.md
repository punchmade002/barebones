## 2. Checked facts and examples (the only names, dates, figures and quotations you may use)

Do not name any person, place, date or figure that is not on this list. No quotations are cleared for use.

| Item | Checked facts |
|---|---|
| A1 | Act of Union passed 1800; the United Kingdom of Great Britain and Ireland began 1 January 1801. Pitt (prime minister) hoped to follow the Union with Emancipation; George III refused; Pitt resigned in 1801. |
| A2 | Relief Act of 1793: Catholics got the parliamentary (county) vote on the same terms as Protestants, entry to the bar, the right to sit on juries and some army commissions (not the senior ranks). |
| A3 | The Union joined the Church of England and Church of Ireland as one established church. Catholics were about four-fifths of Ireland's population. |
| A4 | The rebellion of 1798. |
| G1 | 100 Irish MPs in the Commons (a small minority of the roughly 650 members). In the Lords: 28 representative peers elected for life by the Irish peerage, and four Church of Ireland bishops sitting in rotation. |
| G2 | The Lord Lieutenant, usually a British peer, lived in Dublin Castle and the Viceregal Lodge in the Phoenix Park. Marquess Wellesley held the post 1821–28 and favoured concession; the Marquess of Anglesey held it 1828–29 and was recalled early in 1829 after openly favouring concession. |
| G3 | The Chief Secretary sat in the Commons. Robert Peel was Chief Secretary 1812–18. |
| G4 | The Under-Secretary was the senior permanent official. "Dublin Castle" was the name for the whole Irish administration, separate from Whitehall. |
| G5 | Grand juries were chosen by the county sheriff from leading landowners (nearly all Protestant); they levied the county cess (a local tax on land) and paid for roads, bridges, jails and courthouses. |
| G6 | Unpaid magistrates, nearly all Protestant landowners, tried minor cases at petty and quarter sessions. |
| G7 | Dublin Corporation and other corporations were closed, self-electing Protestant bodies; Catholics were admitted to municipal office only after the Municipal Corporations (Ireland) Act of 1840 (a later subsection; mention only as the end of the exclusion). |
| G8 | Peace Preservation Force, 1814, created by Peel: a paid force sent into disturbed districts, at local expense. |
| G9 | Rural unrest by agrarian secret societies: Whiteboys, Ribbonmen, and the "Rockites" of 1821–24 (followers of "Captain Rock"). Constabulary Act 1822: a county constabulary under provincial inspectors-general, Ireland's first regular rural police. |
| G10 | Constabulary Act 1836: Irish Constabulary, armed and centrally controlled from Dublin under an inspector-general; named the Royal Irish Constabulary in 1867. |
| Q1 | Catholics could not sit in Parliament, because MPs took the oath of supremacy and declared against transubstantiation. Also excluded from the senior judiciary, the rank of King's Counsel, high government office and senior military ranks. |
| Q2 | County vote held by forty-shilling freeholders (life lease on land worth at least 40 shillings a year); landlords created many such tenancies to control votes. |
| Q3 | Voting was open (spoken aloud at the poll), so a tenant who defied his landlord could be identified and evicted. |
| Q4 | Veto controversy from 1808: some supporters of relief offered the Crown a veto on the appointment of Catholic bishops. The Irish bishops rejected it; in 1814 the Quarantotti rescript from Rome, which accepted it, was repudiated in Ireland. O'Connell led the anti-veto opposition; the dispute split the Catholic leadership. |
| C1 | Catholic Association founded in Dublin in May 1823 by Daniel O'Connell and Richard Lalor Sheil; at first a body of lawyers and gentry with an annual membership fee of one guinea. |
| C2 | 1824: associate membership at a penny a month, the Catholic Rent, which brought in tenant farmers, labourers and townspeople in very large numbers (hundreds of thousands; do not give a total). |
| C3 | The Rent was collected through the parishes by collectors under the supervision of the local priest. |
| C4 | Priests read the Association's addresses from the altar and chaired local meetings. James Doyle, Bishop of Kildare and Leighlin (he signed himself "JKL"), was its most influential clerical supporter. |
| C5 | Funds paid for lawyers to defend Catholics in the courts, for petitions to Parliament, for newspaper publicity and, later, for election campaigns. Demands widened to tithes, the grand jury system and treatment of Catholics in the courts. |
| C7 | Unlawful Societies (Ireland) Act, 1825. O'Connell re-formed the body as the New Catholic Association in July 1825, restricted to what the law allowed. |
| C8 | Sir Francis Burdett's relief bill of 1825 passed the Commons and was rejected by the Lords. |
| C9 | General election of June 1826, County Waterford: the pro-Emancipation Protestant Henry Villiers-Stuart defeated Lord George Beresford, whose family dominated the county. Forty-shilling freeholders were led to the poll by their priests; Thomas Wyse was a leading organiser. The Association's Rent organisation acted as election committees. Pro-Emancipation candidates also won seats in other counties (name none). |
| C10 | In 1828 William Vesey-FitzGerald, MP for County Clare, a popular landlord who supported Emancipation, was appointed President of the Board of Trade in the Wellington government and so had to seek re-election. The Association opposed him as a member of a government that resisted Emancipation. O'Connell, a Catholic, stood although he could not take the oath needed to sit. |
| C11 | Polling at Ennis ended on 5 July 1828: O'Connell 2,057 votes, Vesey-FitzGerald 982. Priests led their parishioners to the poll in orderly columns; the campaign passed without violence. |
| C12 | The Duke of Wellington was prime minister from January 1828; Robert Peel was Home Secretary; both had opposed Emancipation. The Test and Corporation Acts (which excluded Protestant dissenters) were repealed in 1828. Anglesey warned of rebellion. Wellington overcame George IV's resistance in March 1829 by threatening the government's resignation. The Commons had voted for relief several times. The Association dissolved itself in February 1829 as the government moved to suppress it. |
| C13 | Roman Catholic Relief Act, royal assent 13 April 1829: Catholics could sit in both Houses under a new oath of allegiance and hold almost all civil and military offices, including judgeships. |
| C14 | Withheld: the offices of Lord Lieutenant of Ireland and Lord Chancellor, and the regency; Catholics could not advise the Crown on appointments in the established church. Bishops could not take the titles of existing Church of Ireland sees; religious orders, especially the Jesuits, were restricted. |
| C15 | Parliamentary Elections (Ireland) Act 1829 raised the county franchise from forty shillings to £10, abolishing the forty-shilling freeholders' vote and disfranchising most small tenants. The electorate fell sharply (to a fraction of its former size; give no figures). |
| C16 | The Act was not retrospective. O'Connell was refused his seat in 1829 and returned unopposed for Clare in July 1829; he took his seat in 1830. |
| C17 | Brunswick Clubs formed in 1828 to defend the Protestant constitution. Peel resigned his Oxford University seat in 1829 to seek his constituents' approval, lost the by-election and sat for Westbury. Wellington fought a duel with the Earl of Winchilsea in March 1829 after Winchilsea accused him of plotting against Protestantism. Ultra-Tory anger split the Tory party; Wellington's government fell in 1830. |
| P1 | Born 6 August 1775 at Carhen near Cahersiveen, Co. Kerry, into a Catholic gentry family; raised by his uncle Maurice O'Connell of Derrynane, whose estate he later inherited. |
| P2 | Educated at St Omer and Douai in France; left in 1793. Called to the Irish bar in 1798; became the most successful barrister in Ireland, known for defending poor Catholics. |
| P3 | His first public speech, in January 1800, was against the Union. He led the anti-veto party in the Catholic Committee and Board in the 1810s. In 1815 he killed John D'Esterre in a duel arising from his attack on Dublin Corporation and regretted it all his life. |
| P6 | Catholics called him "the Liberator". |
| P7 | Oliver MacDonagh presents him as the founder of modern mass democratic politics. Fergus O'Ferrall (*Catholic Emancipation: Daniel O'Connell and the Birth of Irish Democracy*, 1985) stresses the Association's organisation and the role of the clergy and local leaders. |

## 3. Traps

Do not use (wrong or doubtful facts in the current notes):
- Do not use: "Parliament first passed an act in February 1829 suppressing the Association, which O'Connell then dissolved", because the Association dissolved itself in February 1829 in anticipation of the bill. Say the government moved to suppress it and it dissolved itself.
- Do not use: the electorate falling "from about 216,000 to about 37,000", because estimates of the pre-1829 and post-1829 Irish county electorate vary widely between sources. Say it fell sharply, to a small fraction.
- Do not use: Louth, Monaghan and Westmeath as the other 1826 wins, because the county list is not checked. Say "other counties".
- Do not use: "O'Connell was not allowed to take his seat in 1828". He was elected in 1828, but was refused the seat in May 1829, after the Act, because it was not retrospective.
- Do not use: "limits on public processions" as a term of the Relief Act, because it is not checked.
- Do not use: the nickname "Orange Peel" as a reaction to the 1829 Act; it dates from Peel's years as Chief Secretary and is not needed.
- Do not use: Catholic Rent or Association money totals, membership figures or franchise totals; none are checked. Do not give the 1826 or 1828 votes except the Clare result in section 2.
- Do not use: "first mass political movement in Irish or British history" as a bare fact; say "an early example of mass political organisation" or attribute the claim to historians.

## 4. What exams ask

- Higher 100-mark essay, very common: why Emancipation was won in 1829 and Repeal was not (2006, 2010, 2021), or how far O'Connell's leadership explains success and failure (2019); or the political and religious dimensions of the campaign (2020). Marks go to a judgement stated at the start and kept up: O'Connell's mass movement and the Clare crisis against ministers' own fear of civil war, and the point that the demand was limited and could be granted within the Union. Narrating the events without a judgement scores poorly.
- For "political and religious dimensions", students need both sides named explicitly: political (seats, office, the franchise, the use of the vote against landlords) and religious (the clergy as organisers, Catholic identity, the Church of Ireland left established and tithes untouched).
- Changes in political structures, 1815–1870 (2011): use this subsection for what did not change under the Union (Westminster, Dublin Castle) and what changed in 1829 (Catholics in Parliament, the £10 franchise).
- O'Connell's methods and achievements (2008, 2009, 2013, 2016, 2024 style): explain the Catholic Association, the Rent, the clergy and Clare as methods, each with a year and an effect, then judge them.
- Ordinary Level 40-mark paragraph on O'Connell: his background, the Catholic Association (1823, with Sheil), the Rent at a penny a month (1824), collection by the parishes and priests, Clare (1828) and the Relief Act (1829). Each correct, dated statement earns marks, so give founders, dates and what each step achieved.
- Ordinary Level 30-mark questions: the Catholic Association or its achievements. Name the Association, its founders, the Rent, the Clare election and what Catholics gained in 1829.
- Document questions have been set on O'Connell and the oath barring Catholics from Parliament, and on a diary of the Rent and the Clare victory. Students should know why a Catholic could not take the oath, how the Rent was collected, and what Emancipation changed.
- Examiners reward cause and result: why the Association became a mass movement (the penny Rent and the parishes), why Clare forced the issue (Catholics could win seats they could not take), why Wellington and Peel conceded, and what the Act withheld.
- Paired judgement points score highest: gains vs limits of the Act (seats and office, but the £10 franchise and the Church of Ireland), contemporary Catholic triumph vs Protestant and Tory anger.
