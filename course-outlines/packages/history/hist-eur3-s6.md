## 1. Must cover (one bullet or block for every item; tick each off)

Background (assumed)
- [ ] A1 (assumed) How the Third Republic worked: founded 1870 after defeat by Prussia, constitutional laws of 1875; an elected Chamber of Deputies (men only) and an indirectly elected Senate that could block laws; a President elected by both houses with a largely ceremonial role; governments (the Prime Minister, "President of the Council", and ministers) needed a majority in the Chamber and fell when they lost a vote. Women had no vote until 1944–45.
- [ ] A2 (assumed) The party system: many parties and no single majority, so every government was a coalition. Right: conservative and Catholic groups; centre-left: the Radical Party (anticlerical, strong among small-town and rural voters, the "hinge" of most coalitions); left: the Socialists (SFIO) and, from 1920, the Communists (PCF).
- [ ] A3 (assumed) The legacy of the First World War: victory, but huge losses, the devastated north-east, heavy war debts, Alsace-Lorraine regained, a falling birth rate and a deep fear of Germany; France wanted the Versailles Treaty enforced in full.
- [ ] A4 (assumed) Deep divisions in French society: left vs right, Catholic vs secular (anticlerical) France, workers vs employers, Paris vs the provinces; these shaped every crisis of the period.

Key concepts (each defined as used in this topic; also in keyTerms)
- [ ] K1 Democracy: government by elected representatives answerable to the voters, with free elections, parties and civil rights; the Third Republic as a parliamentary democracy that survived the 1930s but ended by its own parliament's vote in 1940.
- [ ] K2 Collaboration: cooperation with the occupying power; in France both state collaboration by the Vichy government and collaborationism (open support for Nazism) by Paris-based fascist groups.
- [ ] K3 Resistance: organised opposition to the occupier and to Vichy, from outside (de Gaulle's Free French) and inside France (underground newspapers, intelligence, escape lines, sabotage, armed maquis).

Post-war France, 1919–1929
- [ ] P1 War losses and reconstruction: about 1.4 million soldiers dead; the north-east devastated; reconstruction financed by borrowing on the expectation that German reparations would pay ("Germany will pay").
- [ ] P2 The Bloc National (1919–1924): right and centre coalition, won the November 1919 election; Clemenceau left politics after losing the presidential election of January 1920; firm line on Germany and against the left.
- [ ] P3 Poincaré and the Ruhr: Prime Minister 1922–1924; with Belgium occupied the Ruhr in January 1923 when Germany defaulted on reparations; German passive resistance; British disapproval; the Dawes Plan (1924) and withdrawal (completed 1925). Results: some payments, but isolation, cost and a weaker franc. The German side (hyperinflation) is in the notes on Weimar Germany.
- [ ] P4 Financial weakness: budget deficits, large debts and a falling franc in the early and mid 1920s.
- [ ] P5 The Cartel des Gauches (1924–1926): Radicals and Socialists won the May 1924 election; Herriot Prime Minister; brought down in April 1925 by a financial crisis (the "wall of money": banks and investors against the left); a run of short governments and the franc crisis of July 1926.
- [ ] P6 Poincaré's Government of National Union (July 1926–1929): tax rises and spending cuts restored confidence; the franc stabilised in 1926 and fixed by law in 1928 at about a fifth of its pre-war value (the "Poincaré franc"); prosperity in the late 1920s; Poincaré retired in 1929.
- [ ] P7 Security and foreign policy in the 1920s: alliances with Poland (1921) and Czechoslovakia (1924); Briand as Foreign Minister (Locarno 1925, the Kellogg–Briand Pact 1928); the decision to build a fortified line (see M1).
- [ ] P8 Why governments changed so often: many parties and fragile coalitions; Radicals switching between left and right partners; financial crises; a Senate that blocked reforms; the President's power to dissolve the Chamber unused after 1877, so deputies could bring down governments without facing an election. Ministers often served only months. Give no total number of governments (see Traps).

The Depression and the crisis of 1934
- [ ] D1 The Depression's late arrival: a structure of small farms and small family firms and large gold reserves cushioned France at first; the slump hit from 1931, then lasted longer than in Britain or Germany; farmers and small businesses suffered most; official unemployment stayed lower than in Britain or Germany, partly because foreign workers left and some unemployed returned to the land.
- [ ] D2 Deflation: governments kept the franc at its 1928 gold value (France led the "gold bloc") and cut spending, wages and prices, which deepened the slump; Laval's decree-laws of 1935 cut public spending and salaries; devaluation came only under the Popular Front in 1936.
- [ ] D3 Political instability in the early 1930s: the left (Radicals and Socialists) won the 1932 election but governments fell in quick succession; growing contempt for parliament and "the politicians".
- [ ] D4 The leagues: anti-parliamentary, nationalist, mostly right-wing movements. Action française (Charles Maurras; monarchist and anti-Semitic, active since before 1914); the Jeunesses Patriotes (Pierre Taittinger); the Solidarité française (funded by François Coty); the Croix-de-Feu (veterans' league, Colonel François de La Rocque, the largest); smaller openly fascist groups such as Francisme (Marcel Bucard).
- [ ] D5 The Stavisky affair: Alexandre Stavisky, a swindler with political protection, sold worthless bonds (through the municipal pawnshop of Bayonne); found shot dead in January 1934, officially suicide but widely believed silenced; it suggested corruption reaching into the Radical Party; Prime Minister Chautemps resigned in January 1934.
- [ ] D6 The riots of 6 February 1934: the new Daladier government dismissed the Paris Prefect of Police, Jean Chiappe (favoured by the right); leagues and veterans' groups marched on the Chamber of Deputies; police opened fire; about 15 killed and over a thousand injured; Daladier resigned the next day, the first government brought down by street violence; Doumergue's government of national union followed (Pétain was its War Minister).
- [ ] D7 The left's response: general strike and demonstration on 12 February 1934; the left read 6 February as an attempted fascist coup, which led to left unity.

The Popular Front, 1934–1938
- [ ] F1 Formation: Socialist–Communist unity pact (July 1934), made possible by the Comintern's turn to anti-fascist alliances; the Radicals joined in 1935 (the mass rally of 14 July 1935); a common programme in January 1936; slogan "Bread, Peace and Liberty".
- [ ] F2 The 1936 election (26 April and 3 May) and the Blum government (June 1936): Blum the first Socialist and first Jewish Prime Minister; Socialists and Radicals in the cabinet; the Communists supported it in parliament but took no ministries; three women became under-secretaries although women could not vote; Blum attacked by the anti-Semitic right (he had been beaten up by Action française supporters in February 1936).
- [ ] F3 The strike wave of May–June 1936: sit-down strikes and factory occupations by about two million workers, before Blum took office.
- [ ] F4 The Matignon Agreements (7–8 June 1936) and the June laws: wage rises of about 7 to 15 per cent, union rights, collective agreements and elected workers' delegates; laws for the 40-hour week and two weeks' paid holiday.
- [ ] F5 Other reforms: the leagues dissolved (June 1936; the Croix-de-Feu reformed as the Parti Social Français, which grew into a large party); the Wheat Office to stabilise farm prices; greater state control of the Bank of France; part-nationalisation of the arms industry; school-leaving age raised to 14; Léo Lagrange's leisure and sport policy with cheap rail tickets for holidays; devaluation of the franc (autumn 1936); rearmament programme from 1936.
- [ ] F6 Problems: flight of capital, rising prices that ate up wage gains, industrial output slow to recover (the 40-hour week limited production, including arms); the Spanish Civil War split the coalition (Blum chose non-intervention in August 1936, angering the Communists and much of his own party; Britain backed non-intervention; the Radicals opposed intervention); the right-wing press campaign that drove the Interior Minister Roger Salengro to suicide (November 1936). The Spanish war itself is not part of this subsection.
- [ ] F7 Decline and end: Blum's "pause" in reform (February 1937); resignation (June 1937) when the Senate refused him emergency financial powers; Chautemps governments; Blum's brief second government (March–April 1938); Daladier Prime Minister (April 1938); finance minister Reynaud's decrees (November 1938) undid the 40-hour week; the failed general strike of 30 November 1938 ended the Popular Front era.

Defence policy and the approach of war
- [ ] M1 The Maginot Line: named after War Minister André Maginot; funded from 1930 and built mainly in the 1930s; concrete forts and underground works along the Franco-German border; reflected defensive thinking after Verdun and the shortage of manpower; did not extend along the Belgian border (Belgium was an ally until it returned to neutrality in 1936) and was weak opposite the Ardennes, thought unsuitable for tanks.
- [ ] M2 Defensive military doctrine: a slow, methodical, defensive army; tanks spread among infantry units; de Gaulle's call for a professional armoured force (1934) ignored.
- [ ] M3 Foreign policy in the 1930s in brief: the Franco-Soviet Pact (1935); no action when Hitler remilitarised the Rhineland (7 March 1936); growing reliance on Britain; Daladier signed the Munich Agreement (September 1938). One block; Hitler's side and appeasement are in the notes on the Nazi state and Hitler's foreign policy.
- [ ] M4 War declared on 3 September 1939; the Phoney War (drôle de guerre): little fighting, the army waiting behind the Maginot Line; the Communist Party banned (September 1939) after the Nazi-Soviet Pact; Reynaud replaced Daladier as Prime Minister (March 1940).

The fall of France, 1940
- [ ] W1 The German attack (10 May 1940): Allied armies moved into Belgium on the Dyle plan; German armour came through the Ardennes and crossed the Meuse at Sedan (13 May); reached the Channel coast (20 May), cutting off the northern armies; Gamelin replaced by Weygand (May 1940); Dunkirk evacuation (26 May–4 June). One block of military narrative; the general course of the war and blitzkrieg are in the notes on the Second World War.
- [ ] W2 Collapse: Italy declared war (10 June); Paris declared an open city and occupied (14 June); millions of civilians fled south in the "exodus"; the government moved to Bordeaux; Reynaud resigned (16 June); Pétain became Prime Minister and sought an armistice (17 June).
- [ ] W3 The armistice of 22 June 1940 at Compiègne, in the railway carriage of the 1918 armistice: occupied zone (north and the Atlantic coast, about three-fifths of the country) and unoccupied zone in the south; France to pay the costs of occupation; an armistice army of 100,000; the fleet to be disarmed but kept by France; prisoners of war to stay in Germany; Alsace-Lorraine in practice annexed by Germany.
- [ ] W4 Losses: about 1.8 million French soldiers taken prisoner; most held in Germany for years. Give no figure for French dead (see Traps).
- [ ] W5 Why France fell, two schools: military explanations (doctrine, dispersed tanks, slow command and communications, the Ardennes gamble, German air power and surprise; Ernest May) vs political and moral explanations (instability, division, defeatism, "decadence"; the contemporary witness Marc Bloch's Strange Defeat; Jean-Baptiste Duroselle). Today most historians stress the military failures of May 1940.
- [ ] W6 The end of the Third Republic: on 10 July 1940 the National Assembly (Chamber and Senate together) at Vichy voted full powers to Pétain to draw up a new constitution, 569 to 80; the next day Pétain made himself Head of the French State. Note: democracy ended by parliament's own vote.

The Vichy state and the National Revolution
- [ ] V1 The French State (État français): Pétain (aged 84) held executive and legislative power; no parliament or elections; capital at the spa town of Vichy; the Legion of French Combatants (veterans) instead of a single party; a cult of Pétain (portraits, the song "Maréchal, nous voilà!").
- [ ] V2 The National Revolution: blamed the defeat on the Republic, the left, the Popular Front and "anti-French" groups; motto "Travail, Famille, Patrie" replaced "Liberté, Égalité, Fraternité"; Catholic Church favoured; family, large families and women's place in the home; return to the land; youth camps (Chantiers de la jeunesse).
- [ ] V3 Workers and repression: trade union confederations dissolved, strikes banned, the Labour Charter (1941); Freemasons, Communists and Jews excluded or persecuted; de Gaulle sentenced to death in absence; the Riom trial (1942) of Blum, Daladier and Gamelin, suspended when it embarrassed the regime.
- [ ] V4 State collaboration: Pétain met Hitler at Montoire (24 October 1940) and announced a policy of collaboration; Laval dismissed (December 1940); Admiral Darlan headed the government (February 1941–April 1942); Laval returned as head of government (April 1942) and in June 1942 publicly wished for a German victory.
- [ ] V5 Why Vichy collaborated: hope of a better place in a German-dominated Europe, release of prisoners, the chance to carry out the National Revolution, and anti-communism; the "shield" defence (Pétain protected the French) vs the view that Vichy collaborated willingly (see H2).
- [ ] V6 The burden of occupation: occupation costs (400 million francs a day at first); food, raw materials and industrial output sent to Germany; rationing, queues, shortages, the black market; the prisoners of war; the "relève" (1942: skilled workers to Germany in return for releasing prisoners, a failure) and the STO compulsory labour service (February 1943).
- [ ] V7 Mers-el-Kébir (3 July 1940): the British attacked the French fleet in Algeria to stop it falling to Germany, killing about 1,300 French sailors; it fed anti-British feeling and helped Vichy.
- [ ] V8 November 1942: after the Allied landings in French North Africa (8 November) Germany occupied the southern zone (11 November); the fleet was scuttled at Toulon (27 November); Vichy lost what independence it had left.
- [ ] V9 Collaborationists: Paris-based fascist parties more pro-Nazi than Vichy, Jacques Doriot's Parti Populaire Français and Marcel Déat's Rassemblement National Populaire; the Legion of French Volunteers against Bolshevism (1941) fought on the Eastern Front.
- [ ] V10 Anti-Jewish laws: the first Statute on Jews (October 1940), issued without German demand, excluded Jews from public service and many professions; a second statute (June 1941); the Commissariat-General for Jewish Questions (1941, Xavier Vallat); "aryanisation" of Jewish businesses; foreign Jews interned in camps.
- [ ] V11 Deportations: from 1942 the Vichy police, under René Bousquet's agreements with the SS, carried out arrests in both zones; foreign Jews in the southern zone handed over (August 1942); the Drancy transit camp near Paris; about 76,000 Jews deported from France, about 2,500 survived; about three-quarters of the Jews in France survived the war, many helped by ordinary people and churches. The wider Holocaust is in the notes on anti-Semitism and the Holocaust.
- [ ] V12 The Vel' d'Hiv round-up (16–17 July 1942): about 13,000 Jews, including about 4,000 children, arrested by French police in Paris; families held in the Vélodrome d'Hiver cycle stadium, then sent through camps such as Drancy to Auschwitz.
- [ ] V13 The Milice (January 1943, Joseph Darnand): Vichy's paramilitary police that hunted resisters and Jews and fought the maquis; a sign of Vichy becoming a police state by 1943–44.

De Gaulle, the Free French and the Resistance
- [ ] R1 De Gaulle's appeal (18 June 1940, BBC, London) to fight on; Churchill recognised him as leader of the Free French (28 June 1940); French Equatorial Africa, led by Chad, rallied to him (August 1940); his Free French forces fought alongside the British.
- [ ] R2 De Gaulle and the Allies: difficult relations with Churchill and especially Roosevelt, who preferred General Giraud; de Gaulle won control of the French Committee of National Liberation in Algiers (formed June 1943), which became the Provisional Government (June 1944).
- [ ] R3 The early internal Resistance (1940–41): small groups of volunteers; underground newspapers; escape lines for Allied airmen; intelligence for Britain; in the south the movements Combat (Henri Frenay), Libération and Franc-Tireur.
- [ ] R4 The Communists: the PCF joined armed resistance after Germany invaded the USSR (June 1941); the Francs-Tireurs et Partisans (1942) became one of the largest armed groups; German hostage shootings in reprisal, such as at Châteaubriant (October 1941).
- [ ] R5 Unification under Jean Moulin: de Gaulle's representative, parachuted into France (January 1942); chaired the first meeting of the National Council of the Resistance (CNR) in Paris (27 May 1943); arrested near Lyon (June 1943) and died after torture (July 1943); the CNR programme (March 1944) of post-war social reform.
- [ ] R6 The maquis and armed struggle: young men fleeing the STO in 1943–44 swelled rural guerrilla groups; united as the French Forces of the Interior (FFI) in 1944; sabotage of railways and telephone lines aided the Normandy landings; the Vercors maquis crushed in July 1944.
- [ ] R7 Repression: the Gestapo and Milice; torture and executions; hostages shot; massacres in June 1944 by the SS Das Reich division: 99 men hanged at Tulle (9 June) and more than 600 villagers killed at Oradour-sur-Glane (10 June).
- [ ] R8 Rescue: Le Chambon-sur-Lignon, where Protestant villagers led by Pastor André Trocmé sheltered thousands of Jews and refugees; the Resistance was always a minority, as was active collaboration; most people tried to survive (attentisme, "wait and see").

Liberation and its aftermath, 1944–1946
- [ ] L1 Liberation: D-Day (6 June 1944); landings in the south (15 August 1944) with a large French army; the Paris rising (from 19 August); Leclerc's Free French 2nd Armoured Division entered; Paris liberated (25 August); de Gaulle walked down the Champs-Élysées (26 August).
- [ ] L2 The Provisional Government under de Gaulle: recognised by the Allies (October 1944); prevented civil war between Gaullists and Communists; restored the Republic's authority.
- [ ] L3 The purge (épuration): summary executions of collaborators, women accused of relations with Germans publicly shaved; then trials. Pétain (taken to Sigmaringen, Germany, in 1944) sentenced to death in August 1945, commuted to life imprisonment by de Gaulle, died 1951; Laval shot (October 1945).
- [ ] L4 The new Republic: women's right to vote (ordinance of April 1944, first used in 1945); de Gaulle resigned (January 1946); the Fourth Republic's constitution approved (October 1946).

Leading figures (profile blocks; none is a key personality, so do not head them "Key personality:")
- [ ] N1 Philippe Pétain: born 1856; "the victor of Verdun" (1916); War Minister 1934; ambassador to Franco's Spain (1939); Prime Minister June 1940, then Head of the French State 1940–1944; trial and fate (L3). Seen in 1940 as a saviour by many; now judged a collaborator responsible for persecution.
- [ ] N2 Charles de Gaulle: born 1890; argued for armoured warfare in the 1930s; commanded an armoured division (May 1940); Under-Secretary for War (June 1940); 18 June appeal, Free French, Provisional Government; resigned January 1946. Built the post-war image of a France united in resistance.
- [ ] N3 Léon Blum: born 1872; Socialist leader; Prime Minister 1936–37 and briefly 1938; tried at Riom; deported to Germany (held near Buchenwald) and survived.
- [ ] N4 Pierre Laval: born 1883; Prime Minister in 1931–32 and 1935–36 (deflation decrees; the Hoare–Laval Pact of December 1935 over Abyssinia); Pétain's deputy (1940) and head of government (1942–44); executed 1945.
- [ ] N5 Other names in one line each where they occur: Poincaré, Herriot, Briand, Daladier, Reynaud, Darlan, Moulin.

Historians and memory
- [ ] H1 The interwar Republic: "decadence" (Duroselle) and the view that the system was too weak, vs the point that the Republic survived the Depression and the leagues and fell only to military defeat; were the leagues fascist? René Rémond (no: a traditional French right) vs Zeev Sternhell (fascist ideas had deep French roots).
- [ ] H2 Vichy: the post-war "sword and shield" view (de Gaulle the sword, Pétain the shield; Robert Aron, 1954) vs Robert Paxton (Vichy France, 1972): collaboration was Vichy's own choice and its anti-Jewish laws were home-grown.
- [ ] H3 Memory: the Gaullist myth of a nation united in resistance; challenged by Marcel Ophuls's film The Sorrow and the Pity (1969) and by Paxton; Henry Rousso's "Vichy syndrome" (1987); the trial of Maurice Papon (convicted 1998); President Chirac's acknowledgment of the French state's responsibility for the Vel' d'Hiv round-up (16 July 1995).

Assessment
- [ ] AS1 Assessment block: achievements vs failures of the Third Republic (stabilised the franc, survived the leagues, social reform in 1936; but chronic instability, slow response to the Depression, division, unprepared army); contemporary vs later views of Vichy; how far France's problems were overcome, 1920–1945 (a stated judgement).

## 3. Traps (known errors and boundaries)

- Tanks in 1940: say France had roughly as many tanks as Germany but spread many of them among infantry units. Do not claim France had more tanks or better tanks; neither is checked here.
- Do not use: "France lost about 90,000 killed in six weeks" (current notes), because estimates range from about 60,000 to about 90,000 and recent French research favours the lower end. Give no figure for the dead; use the 1.8 million prisoners (W4).
- Do not use: "a chain of fortifications ... built between 1930 and 1938" and "built between 1929 and 1938" (current notes and keyTerm; they contradict each other). Say "funded from 1930 and built mainly in the 1930s".
- Do not use: "Heavy spending on the Maginot Line ... was one of the largest state investments of the period" (current notes), because it is unchecked. Say only that it was very expensive.
- Do not use: "The Ruhr occupation damaged French relations with Britain and the United States, which opposed it" as written; Britain disapproved openly and refused to take part. Say "Britain disapproved".
- Do not use: "The franc was devalued ... in October 1936" as a precise month; the devaluation was decided in late September and enacted at the start of October. Say "autumn 1936".
- Do not use: "in July 1935 Socialists, Communists and Radicals united in the Popular Front" as the founding date; the Socialist–Communist pact was July 1934, the Radicals joined in 1935 and the programme was January 1936. Use F1.
- Do not use: "The Popular Front's programme of nationalising ... the railways" (current notes); the railways were merged into the state-controlled SNCF only from 1938, after Blum's first government. Use "part-nationalisation of the arms industry".
- Do not use: "Laval ... returned as head of government in April 1942 ... Vichy's sense of 'neutral' independence ended in 1942" as two separate points; they are one development (V4, V8). Each fact once.
- Blum was the first Socialist and the first Jewish Prime Minister, but not the first left-of-centre one: Herriot, a Radical, led the Cartel in 1924.
- Do not use: "Better Hitler than Blum" ("Plutôt Hitler que Blum") as a quotation; it is often quoted but its origin is unclear. If used, say it was a phrase attributed to parts of the right, without quotation marks as anyone's words.
- Do not use: Pétain's description of the Ardennes as "impenetrable" as a quotation. Say generals believed the Ardennes unsuitable for tanks.
- Do not use: a total number of governments between 1920 and 1940 (counts range from the mid-30s to over 40 depending on what is counted). Say "governments changed very often, many lasting only months".
- Do not use: unemployment figures for France in the 1930s or a membership figure for any league; none is checked here.
- Do not use: a number for members of the Resistance or for the women shaved at the liberation.
- The Communists supported the Blum government but did not join it. Do not list them as ministers.
- The Vichy regime governed all of France in law (except Alsace-Lorraine) and was based in the unoccupied zone; do not say it governed "only the south". The Germans ruled the occupied zone directly through their military administration.
- The vote of 10 July 1940 gave full powers to Pétain's government to write a new constitution. The vote itself did not create the "Vichy regime"; Pétain's constitutional acts of 11 July did. Keep the order: armistice (22 June), vote (10 July), Head of State (11 July).
- Compiègne: the armistice of 22 June 1940 was with Germany; a separate armistice with Italy followed (24 June 1940). Do not confuse the two.
- Laval was Prime Minister twice under the Third Republic (deflation, Hoare–Laval) as well as under Vichy. Darlan, not Laval, led the Vichy government in 1941.
- The Milice (1943) is not the Legion of French Combatants (1940) and not the LVF (1941, fought in Russia).
- STO (compulsory, February 1943) vs the relève (voluntary exchange, 1942).
- Free French (de Gaulle's forces outside France) vs the internal Resistance vs the FFI (the 1944 union of armed resistance inside France).
- Action française (monarchist, Maurras) vs the Croix-de-Feu (veterans, La Rocque, became the PSF) vs Doriot's PPF (ex-Communist turned fascist, collaborationist in the war).
- Belongs elsewhere (one cross-reference line at most): German hyperinflation and Stresemann (notes on Weimar Germany); Hitler's foreign policy, appeasement and the causes of war (notes on the Nazi state); blitzkrieg and the general course of the war, the Battle of Britain, D-Day in detail (notes on the Second World War); civilian life, rationing and collaboration in other occupied countries such as Quisling's Norway (notes on society during the Second World War); the Holocaust across Europe, the death camps and Wannsee (notes on anti-Semitism and the Holocaust); the Spanish Civil War itself; the Depression in Britain and Keynes (notes on inter-war Britain).
- No case study and no key personality: write no "Key personality:" block; use plain headings for the profile blocks (N1–N4).

## 4. What exams ask (topics only)

- Higher essay, the most common France question: the problems or challenges France faced between 1920 and 1940 or 1945, and how far they were overcome. Rewarded answers organise by problem (war legacy and finance; political instability; the Depression; the leagues and division; defence and defeat; occupation and Vichy), give each one named evidence (Ruhr, Cartel, Poincaré franc, Stavisky, 6 February, Matignon, Maginot, Sedan, 10 July vote) and say for each whether it was overcome. A clear overall verdict in the introduction and conclusion earns the overall evaluation marks.
- Higher essay: why the Third Republic was unstable. Expected causes: the multi-party system and coalitions, the Radical "hinge", no use of dissolution, the Senate's veto, financial crises (1924–26), the Depression and deflation, left–right polarisation and the leagues, and pressure from the German threat. Linking cause to result (for example, Senate refusal of powers to Blum ending his government) scores higher than listing.
- Higher essay: the fortunes of France 1920–1945, or its challenges in peace and in war. Needs both halves: interwar problems, then defeat, Vichy, collaboration, resistance and liberation; examiners expect a judgement on Vichy (willing collaboration, Paxton) and on the Resistance (minority but important).
- Higher, civilians in Britain and France in the war: the French side needs occupation, zones, rationing and shortages, the prisoners of war, STO, persecution of Jews (Vel' d'Hiv), the Milice, resistance and reprisals (Oradour). The British side belongs to the notes on wartime society.
- Ordinary Level short account: the Vichy state (how it was created, the National Revolution and motto, collaboration, the Labour Charter and Legion, anti-Jewish statute and deportations, the Milice, the fall of Vichy at liberation). Students should give the dates of 22 June and 10 July 1940 and at least three named policies.
- Ordinary Level short account: the fall of France in 1940 (Ardennes, Sedan, Channel coast, Dunkirk, Paris, Pétain, armistice terms and the zones), and why France fell (Maginot Line gap, tank tactics, command).
- Ordinary Level short account: politics of the Third Republic (constitution, frequent changes of government, Bloc National, Cartel, Poincaré) or the Popular Front (formation after 6 February, Blum, Matignon gains, why it failed).
- Judgement throughout: achievements vs limits (Popular Front: lasting social gains vs economic failure and division over Spain); contemporary vs later views (Vichy as a shield in 1940 vs Paxton's verdict; "decadence" vs military explanation of 1940); "how far" questions need a stated verdict.
