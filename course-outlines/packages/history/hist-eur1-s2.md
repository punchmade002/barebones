## 1. Must cover (one bullet or block for every item; tick each off)

Background (assumed knowledge)
- [ ] X1 (assumed) The German Confederation of 1815: a loose league of German states (39 at its foundation) under Austrian presidency, with a Diet at Frankfurt; Austria and Prussia the two great powers inside it. One or two bullets only; the detail is in the notes on the Vienna settlement.
- [ ] X2 (assumed) Metternich's system of repression (censorship, police, the Carlsbad Decrees) had kept liberal and national movements down since 1819; his fall in March 1848 is mentioned in one clause only.
- [ ] X3 (assumed) The Zollverein (1834) had already linked most German states economically under Prussia, without Austria. One clause only.

1848 across Europe
- [ ] E1 "The year of revolutions": the sequence and speed (Palermo January, Paris February, then Vienna 13 March, Budapest 15 March, Venice 17 March, Milan and Berlin 18 March; the Prague rising was in June, see R1).
- [ ] E2 Common causes across Europe: economic crisis of 1846–47; liberal demands for constitutions; nationalist demands; the example of Paris spread by newspapers, railways and the telegraph.
- [ ] E3 Britain and Russia escaped revolution; by 1849 nearly every rising had been defeated.

Causes in Germany
- [ ] C1 Crop failures: potato blight from 1845, poor grain harvests 1846 and 1847, high food prices, hunger riots.
- [ ] C2 The 1847 commercial and industrial depression; artisans hit by factory competition; the Silesian weavers' rising of 1844 as an earlier sign.
- [ ] C3 Liberal demands of the educated middle class: constitutions, freedom of press and assembly, trial by jury, a national parliament.
- [ ] C4 Nationalism: the wish for a German nation state, with disagreement about the Habsburg lands.
- [ ] C5 Peasant grievances: remaining feudal dues and services, especially in the south and west.
- [ ] C6 Growing liberal opposition in the southern state parliaments and in Prussia's United Diet of 1847, which refused a railway loan without constitutional concessions.
- [ ] C7 The trigger: news of the February revolution in Paris.

The March days
- [ ] M1 Baden: the Mannheim meeting (27 February 1848) and its "March demands" spreading across the states.
- [ ] M2 March ministries, promised constitutions and the end of censorship in the smaller and middle states; Ludwig I of Bavaria abdicates.
- [ ] M3 Berlin: troops fire on 18 March, barricade fighting and the dead.
- [ ] M4 Frederick William IV's concessions: troops withdrawn (19 March), honouring the dead, the ride in the black, red and gold colours (21 March), "Prussia merges into Germany".
- [ ] M5 The Camphausen–Hansemann liberal ministry and the Prussian National Assembly elected in May 1848.
- [ ] M6 Peasant risings in the south-west and the start of the abolition of feudal dues.

The Vorparlament and the Frankfurt National Assembly
- [ ] F1 The Vorparlament (31 March–3 April 1848): self-appointed, rejected a republic, arranged elections by broad manhood suffrage with state-by-state rules.
- [ ] F2 Hecker and Struve's republican rising in Baden, April 1848, defeated at Kandern.
- [ ] F3 The Frankfurt National Assembly opens on 18 May 1848 in the Paulskirche.
- [ ] F4 Its members: lawyers, judges, officials, teachers and professors ("the professors' parliament"); almost no peasants or workers.
- [ ] F5 Heinrich von Gagern as president.
- [ ] F6 Archduke John as Reichsverweser (29 June 1848) at the head of a Provisional Central Power with no army, civil service or taxes of its own.
- [ ] F7 Political groupings: deputies formed clubs named after their meeting places, the forerunners of German parties (right, liberal centre, democratic left). One bullet; name no club.
- [ ] F8 The Basic Rights of the German People (December 1848): what they guaranteed and abolished.
- [ ] F9 Grossdeutsch vs Kleindeutsch: who favoured each and why.
- [ ] F10 Schwarzenberg's insistence that the whole Habsburg Empire enter or none of it, which killed the Grossdeutsch plan (late 1848 to March 1849).
- [ ] F11 Liberal nationalism against other nations: the claim to parts of Prussian Poland (Posen) and the hostility of Czechs to inclusion.

Schleswig-Holstein
- [ ] S1 The duchies: Holstein in the Confederation and German; Schleswig mixed and outside it; both ruled by the Danish king.
- [ ] S2 Frederick VII's move to incorporate Schleswig (March 1848), the German rising and provisional government at Kiel.
- [ ] S3 Prussian troops under Wrangel invade; the Assembly calls it a national war.
- [ ] S4 The Malmö armistice (August 1848), signed by Prussia without consulting Frankfurt; rejected and then accepted (16 September).
- [ ] S5 The Frankfurt riots of 18 September; the murder of Lichnowsky and Auerswald; the Assembly rescued by Prussian and Austrian troops.
- [ ] S6 Outcome: Prussia's peace with Denmark (1850), Idstedt (1850), the London Protocol (1852).

The recovery of Austria and Prussia, late 1848
- [ ] R1 Austria: Windischgrätz takes Prague (June); Radetzky's victory at Custoza (July) in one clause.
- [ ] R2 Vienna's October rising crushed (31 October) by Windischgrätz and Jelačić.
- [ ] R3 Robert Blum shot (9 November 1848) despite his immunity as a Frankfurt deputy.
- [ ] R4 Schwarzenberg chief minister (November); Franz Joseph emperor (2 December 1848).
- [ ] R5 Prussia: the Brandenburg ministry; Wrangel's troops enter Berlin (10 November).
- [ ] R6 The Prussian Assembly dissolved and a constitution imposed by the king (5 December 1848).
- [ ] R7 The three-class franchise (1849): how it worked and its effect.

The constitution and the crown refused
- [ ] K1 The Frankfurt imperial constitution (March 1849): federal empire, hereditary emperor, lower house by manhood suffrage, upper house of states.
- [ ] K2 The election of Frederick William IV as Emperor of the Germans (28 March 1849): 290 votes, 248 abstentions.
- [ ] K3 His refusal (evasive reply 3 April; formal refusal 28 April 1849) and why: a crown from an elected assembly, not the princes; fear of Austria; his private contempt for it.
- [ ] K4 Austria rejects the constitution and recalls its deputies (April 1849).
- [ ] K5 Twenty-eight smaller states accept, but Prussia, Bavaria, Saxony and Hanover reject; the constitution never takes effect.

The final phase, 1849–1851
- [ ] P1 The May 1849 risings in Saxony (Dresden), the Palatinate and Baden in support of the constitution; Wagner and Bakunin at Dresden.
- [ ] P2 Their defeat by Prussian troops (Rastatt surrenders, July 1849); executions, imprisonment and flight.
- [ ] P3 The rump parliament at Stuttgart, dispersed 18 June 1849.
- [ ] P4 Emigration: the Forty-Eighters (Hecker, Carl Schurz) in the United States.
- [ ] P5 The Erfurt Union (Radowitz's plan, 1849–50): a Kleindeutsch union by agreement of the princes; the Erfurt Parliament (1850).
- [ ] P6 The Punctation of Olmütz (29 November 1850): Prussia gives way to Austria backed by Russia; "the humiliation of Olmütz".
- [ ] P7 The old Confederation and Diet restored (1851); the Basic Rights repealed (1851); Bismarck Prussia's delegate at the Diet from 1851.

Why the revolution failed
- [ ] W1 The Assembly had no army, money or administration and depended on the governments it sought to limit.
- [ ] W2 The armies and bureaucracies stayed loyal to the rulers and were used once the shock of March passed.
- [ ] W3 Liberals divided from radicals and workers; fear of social revolution and a republic.
- [ ] W4 Peasants satisfied once feudal dues were abolished (for example Austria, September 1848).
- [ ] W5 Long debates (Basic Rights, Grossdeutsch–Kleindeutsch) gave the princes time to recover.
- [ ] W6 National rivalries (Danes, Poles, Czechs, Croats) split the revolutionaries and helped the Habsburg army.
- [ ] W7 Frederick William IV's refusal left the constitution without the one state able to enforce it.
- [ ] W8 Austria's recovery and Russian backing of the old order (Russia also crushed Hungary in 1849: one clause).

Assessment and results (end with this block, headed "Assessment: …")
- [ ] A1 Failure: no united Germany; Basic Rights repealed; Confederation restored.
- [ ] A2 Achievement: feudal dues and remaining serfdom abolished; Prussia kept a constitution and parliament, however limited.
- [ ] A3 Lesson for liberals and for Bismarck: unity would come by Prussian power ("blood and iron" in 1862, one clause only), not by speeches and majority votes; liberals discredited as impractical.
- [ ] A4 Achievement: first all-German elected parliament; the start of political parties; a model for the constitutions of 1919 and 1949.
- [ ] A5 Contemporary views: conservatives welcomed order restored; Marx and Engels blamed the cowardice of the liberal middle class.
- [ ] A6 Later views: A. J. P. Taylor's "turning point" verdict and the Sonderweg idea, vs recent historians who stress lasting gains in political culture and participation.

Key concepts and terms (keyTerms)
- [ ] T1 Liberalism, as used in 1848.
- [ ] T2 Nationalism, as used in 1848.
- [ ] T3 Democracy, as demanded by the radicals of 1848 (manhood suffrage, often a republic).
- [ ] T4 Other terms: Vorparlament; Frankfurt National Assembly; Basic Rights; Grossdeutsch; Kleindeutsch; Reichsverweser; Malmö armistice; three-class franchise; Erfurt Union; Punctation of Olmütz; March ministries; Forty-Eighters.

## 3. Traps

Do not use (doubtful or wrong in the current notes):
- Do not use "about 585 deputies were elected": the number of seats and of deputies present varied and sources give different figures. Use only "about 800 men sat at some point, including substitutes", or give no number.
- Do not use "Prague in March" as the date of the Prague rising: it was June 1848 (R1). Do not list the March revolutions with Berlin before Budapest or Venice: Vienna 13, Budapest 15, Venice 17, Milan and Berlin 18 March.
- Do not use "about 300 civilians dead" in Berlin as a single figure: sources differ (roughly 200 to 300). Use the range.
- Do not use "many rebels were executed" after Rastatt: executions were in the dozens, not the hundreds. Say "some rebels were executed and many imprisoned".
- Do not call the Confederation "the 39 states" in 1848 as an exact figure: 39 was the number in 1815 and it changed slightly. Say "39 states at its foundation" or "nearly forty states".
- Do not put the "crown from the gutter" words in quotation marks as a direct quotation of a single document; give it as his private description, as in section 2. Do not invent other quotations for him (for example about a "dog collar" or "stink of revolution").

Confusables:
- Two assemblies met in 1848: the Frankfurt National Assembly (all-German) and the Prussian National Assembly in Berlin. Keep them separate. The Prussian one was dissolved on 5 December 1848; the Frankfurt one ended as the Stuttgart rump in June 1849.
- The Vorparlament (pre-parliament, March–April 1848) is not the Frankfurt National Assembly (from May 1848), and neither is the Diet of the Confederation.
- Grossdeutsch = with Austria (Greater Germany); Kleindeutsch = without Austria, under Prussia (Lesser Germany). Students often swap them.
- Schwarzenberg (Austrian chief minister) vs Windischgrätz (general) vs Radetzky (general in Italy) vs Jelačić (Croatian commander).
- Wrangel appears twice: commanding in Schleswig-Holstein (1848) and entering Berlin (November 1848). Same man.
- Two Felixes: Prince Felix Schwarzenberg (Austrian minister) and Prince Felix Lichnowsky (deputy murdered 18 September 1848).
- Frederick William IV (king in 1848) vs his brother Prince William (commanded in Baden 1849; later William I, under whom Bismarck served).
- Frederick William IV refused the crown, not "the Frankfurt Parliament's offer was withdrawn"; the refusal was formal on 28 April 1849, not 3 April.
- The Erfurt Union (1849–50, Prussia's union of princes) is not the Zollverein (1834, customs union).
- Olmütz (1850) was a defeat for Prussia, not for Austria.
- The 1848 Basic Rights were passed by the Frankfurt Assembly; the 1850 Prussian constitution was imposed by the king. Do not credit the king's constitution to Frankfurt.

## 4. What exams ask (topics only)

- Higher 100-mark essays: why there was revolution in Germany in 1848, and why it failed (often both in one question). Marks go for a clear argument stated at the start, then separate paragraphs on economic, liberal, national and trigger causes, each tied to a specific German event (hunger and depression 1846–47, the United Diet of 1847, the Paris news, the Mannheim demands). Narrative of events without explanation earns little.
- Why it failed: examiners reward ranking or weighing reasons rather than a list. The strongest answers argue that the Assembly lacked force (no army, money or administration) and that the rulers kept their armies, then link the other reasons to that (divisions, delay, the crown refused). Each reason needs a supporting event: Malmö and the September riots for powerlessness; Prague, Vienna and Berlin retaken for loyal armies; the refused crown for dependence on Prussia.
- "Year of revolutions": why 1848 saw risings across Europe; the common causes and the speed of their spread. Use Germany as the main example with brief comparison to Paris and Vienna.
- Results and judgement: achievements vs failures (Basic Rights repealed vs feudal dues abolished; no unity vs first national parliament and parties; Prussian constitution kept). The Assessment block should give these as paired points, then a verdict.
- Contemporary vs later views: Marx and Engels' blame of the middle class; Taylor's turning-point verdict; recent emphasis on lasting gains. A named historian's view earns credit when used to support a judgement, not just quoted.
- Link to Bismarck: the lesson that unity would come through Prussian power, Olmütz as the humiliation Prussia later reversed. Useful as a concluding point in failure essays.
- Ordinary Level: short paragraphs on the March days in Germany (concessions in the states, Berlin 18–21 March); why the Frankfurt Assembly failed (the reasons in W1–W7); success or failure of the revolutionaries, with Frederick William IV's refusal as the standard example of failure.
- Ordinary Level documents: extracts describing sudden risings, Metternich's flight, troops firing on crowds and a king's concessions (a constitution, a German parliament). Students must recognise the events and say whether the revolutionaries achieved their aims; the answer that earns marks is "generally no, because the old rulers regained control by 1849", with one supporting fact.
