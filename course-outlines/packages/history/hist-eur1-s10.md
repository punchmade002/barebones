## 1. Must cover (one bullet or block for every item; tick each off)

Background (assumed knowledge)
- [ ] K1 (assumed) Urban growth: industrialisation and migration from the countryside made cities grow far faster than their streets, housing, water and drainage; one or two bullets only, since urban growth in general belongs to the notes on the changing economy and society.
- [ ] K2 (assumed) Cholera and the miasma idea: cholera struck Paris in 1832 and 1849; most people blamed foul air ("miasma") from dirt and stagnant water, so clearing slums, letting in light and air, and carrying away sewage seemed the cure even before germ theory.
- [ ] K3 (assumed) Napoleon III's regime: Louis-Napoleon took power by the coup of December 1851 and became Emperor in 1852; an authoritarian ruler who needed prestige, prosperity and order, and could override local opposition. One or two bullets; his reign belongs to the notes on France.
- [ ] K4 (assumed) Paris and revolution: the Paris crowd had overthrown governments in 1830 and February 1848, and the June Days (1848) were fought behind barricades in narrow streets.

Why cities needed planning
- [ ] B1 Overcrowding: old centres packed with tall, decaying houses; people crowded into the centre to be near work.
- [ ] B2 Disease: no proper drainage, polluted water, epidemics (links to K2).
- [ ] B3 Traffic: narrow, winding medieval streets could not carry growing traffic or link the new railway stations.
- [ ] B4 Revolution and order: barricades were easy to build in narrow streets; wide streets let troops move.
- [ ] B5 Prestige and the economy: grand streets and buildings displayed the power of the state; public works gave employment and drew investment.

Paris before 1853
- [ ] P1 Size and growth: about one million people around 1850, mostly crowded into the old centre.
- [ ] P2 The centre: the Île de la Cité and the area around Les Halles a maze of narrow, dark streets, poor drainage and old houses; the Hôtel-Dieu hospital overcrowded.
- [ ] P3 Water: drawn largely from the polluted Seine and the Ourcq canal; many homes had no piped water; sewage ran into the Seine.
- [ ] P4 Social geography: richer districts to the west, poorer districts to the east and in the centre.
- [ ] P5 Earlier attempts: Rambuteau, Prefect of the Seine under Louis-Philippe, cut one new street and began improvements, but on a small scale (one bullet).

Napoleon III's aims and Haussmann's appointment
- [ ] A1 Napoleon III's aims: health (light, air, clean water, drainage); traffic and links between railway stations; order (room for troops, fewer barricades); employment through public works; prestige, making Paris the showcase of the Empire (the Exhibitions of 1855 and 1867).
- [ ] A2 London as a model: during his exile in London Louis-Napoleon admired its parks and squares; he gave Haussmann a map with the new streets he wanted marked in colour.
- [ ] A3 Haussmann's background: born in Paris in 1809, of a Protestant family of Alsatian origin; a career prefect in the provinces, last at Bordeaux (Gironde); chosen by the Interior Minister Persigny as a forceful administrator.
- [ ] A4 The appointment: Prefect of the Seine (the official in charge of Paris and its department) from June 1853 to January 1870, working directly with the Emperor; made a senator in 1857; known as "Baron" Haussmann (see Traps).
- [ ] A5 The legal tools: the expropriation law of 1841, widened by a decree of 1852, let the city buy property compulsorily, including whole plots beside a new street, so whole blocks could be cleared.
- [ ] A6 Methods: centralised control by the Prefect and the Emperor, a large team of engineers and architects (Belgrand, Alphand), building regulations on facades and heights.

The boulevards and the grands travaux
- [ ] W1 The grands travaux: the "great works" programme of demolition and rebuilding, carried out in stages from 1853.
- [ ] W2 The "great crossing" (grande croisée): an east–west axis (the extended Rue de Rivoli) and a north–south axis (Boulevard de Strasbourg, Boulevard de Sébastopol, then Boulevard Saint-Michel on the Left Bank), linking the Seine to the northern railway stations.
- [ ] W3 Other new streets: the Boulevard Saint-Germain on the Left Bank; the Avenue de l'Opéra (finished after 1870); the twelve avenues radiating from the Place de l'Étoile; the Avenue de l'Impératrice (now Avenue Foch) to the Bois de Boulogne.
- [ ] W4 Design: long straight streets with vistas ending on a monument; trees, pavements, gas lamps, benches and kiosks; uniform cream stone apartment facades of controlled height (the "Haussmann building").
- [ ] W5 The Île de la Cité largely cleared of housing and rebuilt with public buildings (the Hôtel-Dieu rebuilt, law courts, police headquarters).
- [ ] W6 Purposes served: traffic and access to stations; light and air; the military argument (troops and artillery could move along wide straight streets; barricades harder to hold). Note that Haussmann himself stressed health and traffic over the military motive.

Water supply and sewers
- [ ] H1 Eugène Belgrand, the engineer in charge of water and sewers.
- [ ] H2 Water: aqueducts brought spring water from the Dhuis valley (completed 1865) and the Vanne valley (completed 1874, after the Empire) to covered reservoirs; Seine water kept for street cleaning and fountains; a double system of drinking and non-drinking water.
- [ ] H3 Sewers: a large underground network of tunnels, big enough to walk through, carrying waste water away from the centre and out of the city downstream; it became a sight for visitors.
- [ ] H4 Effects: cleaner streets and water and better sanitation; but many houses were not yet connected, and household sewage was not fully carried away until later in the century.

Parks and open spaces
- [ ] G1 Jean-Charles Alphand, the engineer in charge of the parks and promenades.
- [ ] G2 Bois de Boulogne (west): given to the city in 1852 and laid out as an English-style park with lakes and paths; the Longchamp racecourse opened in 1857.
- [ ] G3 Bois de Vincennes (east): laid out in the 1860s as a counterpart for the eastern districts.
- [ ] G4 Buttes-Chaumont (north-east): a former quarry and rubbish dump turned into a park with a lake, cliffs and a temple on a rocky island; opened in 1867.
- [ ] G5 Smaller parks and squares: the Parc Monceau redesigned, the Parc Montsouris begun in the south, and many small neighbourhood squares.

New buildings, markets and stations
- [ ] U1 The Opéra: Charles Garnier won the design competition in 1861; building began in 1862; it opened in 1875, after the fall of the Empire; a symbol of Second Empire display.
- [ ] U2 Les Halles: the central food market rebuilt as iron-and-glass pavilions by Victor Baltard, from the 1850s.
- [ ] U3 Railway stations: the Gare du Nord rebuilt in the 1860s; new boulevards linked the stations to the centre and to each other.
- [ ] U4 Street furniture and fountains: Gabriel Davioud designed kiosks, lamps, benches and the Saint-Michel fountain (1860).
- [ ] U5 Other buildings: churches, town halls for the arrondissements, schools, barracks and the new Hôtel-Dieu (one bullet; no further names needed).

Annexation of the suburbs (1860)
- [ ] N1 On 1 January 1860 the communes between the old customs wall and the fortifications were annexed; the arrondissements increased from twelve to twenty.
- [ ] N2 Annexed areas included Belleville, Montmartre and Passy; the city grew to about 1.7 million people (1861 census).
- [ ] N3 Why: to plan the whole urban area, extend the boulevards and services outward, and bring the growing suburbs under the city's control and taxes.

Financing and Haussmann's fall
- [ ] F1 Financing: state subsidies, city revenue (mainly the octroi, a tax on goods entering the city), loans, and the hope that rising property values would pay for the works ("productive expenditure").
- [ ] F2 Hidden borrowing: the Caisse des travaux de Paris (1858) and advance payments to contractors (bons de délégation) let Haussmann borrow without the approval of the Legislative Body; the city's debt grew greatly in the 1860s.
- [ ] F3 Court decisions in the late 1850s and 1860s made the city pay more compensation to expropriated owners, which raised costs and helped speculators.
- [ ] F4 Jules Ferry's articles of 1867–68, collected as Les Comptes fantastiques d'Haussmann (1868), a pun on The Tales of Hoffmann, attacked the cost and the irregular borrowing.
- [ ] F5 The fall: with the Empire turning liberal and the opposition strong in Paris after the 1869 elections, the new ministry of Émile Ollivier dismissed Haussmann in January 1870.
- [ ] F6 Afterwards: the Third Republic finished many of his projects (the Avenue de l'Opéra, the Boulevard Saint-Germain); Haussmann published his Mémoires (1890–93) defending his work; he died in 1891.

Social effects
- [ ] S1 Displacement: demolition destroyed many cheap central houses; working-class families were pushed out to the east, the north and the annexed suburbs, often further from their work.
- [ ] S2 Rents rose in the rebuilt centre; the new apartment blocks suited the middle classes.
- [ ] S3 The east–west divide sharpened: the wealthy west (with the Bois de Boulogne) and the working-class east (Belleville, Ménilmontant).
- [ ] S4 Speculation: property developers and financiers made fortunes from rising land values; Émile Zola's novel La Curée (1871–72) portrayed this speculation.
- [ ] S5 Employment: the works employed large numbers of building workers, many migrants from the provinces, though much work was seasonal.
- [ ] S6 Political effect: the displaced workers of the east became a base for radical and republican politics (Belleville); the Commune of 1871 showed barricades were still possible, though the army retook the city in the Bloody Week (one clause; the Commune belongs to the notes on France).

Criticisms (contemporary)
- [ ] C1 Victor Hugo: long before Haussmann he had campaigned against the destruction of old buildings (his pamphlet "War on the demolishers", and Notre-Dame de Paris, 1831, which celebrated medieval Paris); he spent the whole period of the works in exile, opposing Napoleon III; his writing stood for the old Paris that Haussmann destroyed. Do not claim he wrote a specific attack on Haussmann's works (see Traps).
- [ ] C2 Writers who mourned the old Paris: Charles Baudelaire's poem "Le Cygne" (The Swan), published 1860; the Goncourt brothers' journal.
- [ ] C3 Political critics: republicans and liberals (Jules Ferry, Thiers) attacked the cost, the debt and the lack of control by elected bodies.
- [ ] C4 Other criticisms: monotony of the uniform facades and straight lines; destruction of historic buildings and neighbourhoods; the poor excluded from the new city; the boulevards seen as built for troops.
- [ ] C5 Defenders: Haussmann and the Emperor pointed to health, traffic, beauty, jobs and prestige; visitors to the 1867 Exhibition admired the new city.

City planning elsewhere
- [ ] O1 Vienna's Ringstrasse: Emperor Franz Joseph's decree of December 1857 ordered the old city walls demolished; a broad ring boulevard lined with public buildings (parliament, town hall, university, opera, museums) and apartment blocks, built from the late 1850s, opened in 1865, mostly finished later; plots sold to private builders paid for much of it.
- [ ] O2 Barcelona: Ildefons Cerdà's plan for the extension of the city (the Eixample), 1859, a regular grid of blocks with cut-off corners.
- [ ] O3 Brussels: the River Senne was covered over and central boulevards built (1867–71) under the burgomaster Jules Anspach, partly on the Paris model.
- [ ] O4 Berlin: James Hobrecht's plan for the city's expansion, 1862 (one clause).
- [ ] O5 British sanitary reform: Edwin Chadwick's sanitary report (1842) and the Public Health Act (1848) as background (one clause each; detail is in the notes on the changing economy and society); London's "Great Stink" of 1858 led Joseph Bazalgette of the Metropolitan Board of Works to build main sewers from 1859, with the Victoria Embankment (1870) along the Thames.
- [ ] O6 Contrast: Paris was planned from above by an authoritarian state with power over property; British cities relied mainly on local authorities and permissive laws, improving drains and water more than street plans.

Legacy and differing views
- [ ] L1 Haussmann's Paris became a model: "Haussmannisation" became a term for large-scale clearance and boulevard planning, copied in French cities and abroad.
- [ ] L2 Later views: admirers see the creator of modern Paris and a pioneer of modern city planning and public health; critics see a destroyer of old Paris and a planner who served the regime, the wealthy and speculators and pushed the poor to the edges.
- [ ] L3 Lasting features: most of central Paris's street plan, apartment blocks, parks and sewers still date from his works; Baltard's Les Halles pavilions were demolished in the early 1970s.

Assessment (end with this block, headed "Assessment: …")
- [ ] J1 Success vs failure, by aim: health (water, sewers, light and air: largely achieved, though not complete by 1870); traffic and stations (achieved); order (barricades harder but not prevented: 1871); beauty and prestige (achieved: the 1867 Exhibition); jobs (many, but seasonal).
- [ ] J2 Costs and limits: displacement, higher rents and the east–west divide; destruction of historic Paris; huge debt and irregular finance that brought his dismissal; speculation.
- [ ] J3 Contemporary vs later views (paired points), and a verdict: largely successful in physical and sanitary terms, less so socially and financially.
- [ ] J4 Comparison material for paired essays (Haussmann with Hugo, Darwin or Napoleon III; city planning with science or cultural nationalism): Haussmann's achievement was practical and physical; one or two points only, leaving the other person to their own notes.

## 3. Traps

Do not use (doubtful or wrong in the current notes):
- Do not use "The Paris Opéra ... was begun in 1861", because 1861 was the design competition; building began in 1862. Use U1.
- Do not use "The Buttes-Chaumont ... a park with a lake and a cliff-top waterfall", because the cliff-top feature is a small temple; the waterfall is in a grotto. Say a lake, cliffs and a temple (G4).
- Do not use "Among the annexed areas were Belleville, Montmartre and Passy, which brought large working-class populations into the enlarged city", because Passy was a well-off western district. Say the annexed communes included both working-class districts (Belleville) and richer ones (Passy).
- Do not use "the population of the enlarged city ... about 1.8 million by 1872", because the 1872 census figure is higher and the date is after the period; use N2.
- Do not use "an effect that Zola satirised in his novel La Curée" for the east–west divide, because La Curée is about property speculation and the rich. Use S4.
- Do not use "The Rue de Rivoli was extended eastward from the 1850s" as Haussmann's own start, because the extension was decided under the Second Republic before he arrived; say it was extended eastwards in the 1850s (W2).
- Do not use "The Gare de l'Est was enlarged" with no date; the enlargement date is not checked. Name only the Gare du Nord (U3).
- Do not use "Berlin's Hobrecht Plan ... and in the 1870s Joseph Chamberlain, mayor of Birmingham, cleared slums and built a new street, Corporation Street", because Chamberlain's work (from 1875) is outside the period. Keep Hobrecht (O4); leave Chamberlain out.
- Do not use "Napoleon III had admired London's parks ... and he drew up a plan marking the new streets he wanted" as if the Emperor planned everything himself; say he gave Haussmann a map with the streets he wanted marked (A2), and that Haussmann worked out and greatly extended the plan.
- Do not use "the works made Paris a safer, brighter city ... and raised property values in the central districts" as a pure success, because rising property values also meant higher rents and speculation (S2, S4). Pair it with the cost.
- Do not use "Haussmann's methods were centralised, and he gave the Prefecture of the Seine large budgets" without the point that much of the money was borrowed outside parliamentary control (F2).
- Do not use "The Hôtel-Dieu ... held large numbers of sick patients" with any figure; give none.
- Do not use "Critics such as the republican journalist Jules Ferry": Ferry was a republican lawyer and journalist who became a leading minister later; "republican" is enough, with the 1868 date (F4).

Figures and quotations not to use:
- No totals for people displaced, houses demolished, kilometres of new streets or sewers, trees planted, workers employed, or the cost and debt in francs; sources give widely different figures. Say "many", "large" or "huge".
- Do not copy figures that appear only in past-paper documents or model answers (for example a population of the Île de la Cité falling from one figure to another, or "about seventeen years"; give 1853–1870 instead).
- No quotations except the label "artiste démolisseur" (F6) and titles of works. Do not quote Baudelaire, the Goncourts, Hugo, Napoleon III or Haussmann, and do not quote the past-paper documents (for example the complaint about Haussmann's "love of straight lines").

Confusables:
- "Baron" Haussmann: he used the title, but Napoleon III never formally created him a baron; he was made a senator (1857). Call him Baron Haussmann or Haussmann; do not say the Emperor made him a baron.
- Haussmann (the Prefect who ran the works) vs Belgrand (water and sewers) vs Alphand (parks) vs Garnier (the Opéra) vs Baltard (Les Halles) vs Davioud (street furniture).
- Bois de Boulogne (west, wealthy, racecourse) vs Bois de Vincennes (east, working-class districts) vs Buttes-Chaumont (north-east, former quarry).
- The Opéra was begun under the Empire but opened in 1875 under the Third Republic; likewise the Vanne aqueduct (1874), the Avenue de l'Opéra and the Boulevard Saint-Germain were finished after 1870.
- Haussmann's dismissal (January 1870, by Ollivier's liberal ministry) vs the fall of the Empire (September 1870, after Sedan).
- The annexation of 1860 (twelve to twenty arrondissements) vs the 1841 expropriation law vs the 1852 decree.
- Ferry's Comptes fantastiques (1868, finance) vs Zola's La Curée (1871–72, speculation) vs Baudelaire's "Le Cygne" (1860, loss of old Paris) vs Hugo's "War on the demolishers" (1825/1832, before Haussmann).
- Victor Hugo was abroad for the whole period of the works (1851–70), so he did not campaign against them on the spot; his criticism is his long defence of old Paris and his opposition to the regime that rebuilt it.
- London's main drainage (Bazalgette, from 1859) vs Paris sewers (Belgrand): parallel sanitary engineering in the same years.
- The Ringstrasse (Vienna, replaced walls, 1857) vs the Eixample (Barcelona, a grid extension, 1859) vs Haussmann's Paris (cutting through an existing city).

## 4. What exams ask (topics only)

- Higher 100-mark essay on how successful Haussmann was in re-planning Paris (the most frequent): marks go for judging each aim against evidence (health: Belgrand's water and sewers; traffic: the boulevards and stations; order: military value but the 1871 barricades; beauty and prestige: parks, the Opéra, uniform facades, the 1867 Exhibition) and then for the limits (displacement, rents, the east–west divide, destruction of old Paris, cost and hidden debt, dismissal). A clear overall verdict is rewarded; a description of the works without judgement is not.
- Higher essay on developments in city planning (sometimes paired with science and technology): needs why planning was needed (overcrowding, disease, traffic, revolution), Haussmann's Paris as the main example, and at least one other example (Vienna's Ringstrasse, British sanitary reform, Barcelona). Answers that discuss only Paris lose marks for range.
- Higher paired essays: Haussmann with Victor Hugo, with Charles Darwin, or with Napoleon III; city planning with cultural nationalism. The Haussmann half needs specific works with dates and a judgement of his contribution; the best answers compare the kind of achievement (practical and physical vs literary or scientific) and say which was more lasting.
- Ordinary Level 30-mark and 40-mark paragraphs on Haussmann's rebuilding of Paris: each SRP is one specific fact, so many short dated facts score best (Prefect of the Seine June 1853, the 1841 expropriation law, the Boulevard de Sébastopol, Belgrand and the Dhuis and Vanne aqueducts, sewers, Bois de Boulogne, Buttes-Chaumont 1867, Garnier's Opéra, Les Halles, twenty arrondissements in 1860, dismissal 1870).
- Ordinary Level document questions (accounts of the rebuilding): students should recognise the title Prefect of the Seine, the arrondissements and their number, Belgrand's role, the sewers, the Opéra, the Longchamp racecourse in the Bois de Boulogne, Les Halles as iron and glass, why old streets were demolished, and whether a writer favours Napoleon III or Haussmann. Answers use the document's own evidence plus context from the notes.
- Judgement material (Assessment block): success vs failure by aim; physical success vs social cost; contemporary critics (Ferry, Hugo's defence of old Paris, Baudelaire) vs later admirers; Paris vs other cities. Marks go for paired points with evidence and a final verdict.
