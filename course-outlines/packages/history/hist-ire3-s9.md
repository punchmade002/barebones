## 1. Must cover (one bullet or block for every item; tick each off)

Background (assumed)
- [ ] A1 (assumed) Partition and the Government of Ireland Act (1920): Northern Ireland a self-governing part of the United Kingdom with a Unionist government at Stormont since 1921, a Protestant majority and a Catholic nationalist minority.
- [ ] A2 (assumed) The 1930s: Northern Ireland's export industries (shipbuilding, linen) were hit by the Depression, leaving heavy unemployment.
- [ ] A3 (assumed) The South stayed neutral in the war (the "Emergency"); Britain was at war from 3 September 1939 (the subsection on the South covers neutrality in detail).
- [ ] A4 (assumed) Germany's conquest of France in 1940 brought Belfast within range of German bombers; the Blitz on British cities began in 1940.

Key concepts (also in keyTerms)
- [ ] K1 Neutrality as seen from the North: the South's neutrality set it apart from the British war effort and strengthened unionist arguments against unity; nationalists in the North were divided.
- [ ] K2 Ulster Unionism: the political tradition that presented the war effort as proof of Northern Ireland's British loyalty.

Politics and war
- [ ] P1 Northern Ireland at war from 3 September 1939; unionists contrasted its loyalty with the South's neutrality.
- [ ] P2 Nationalists in the North divided; many Catholics volunteered for the forces; others resented unionist rule.
- [ ] P3 Conscription not applied to Northern Ireland; the idea was raised in May 1941 and dropped after opposition.
- [ ] P4 IRA activity and the execution of Tom Williams (September 1942), deepening nationalist resentment.
- [ ] P5 The Andrews government (November 1940 to April 1943): Craig's death, Andrews's age and slowness, criticism, resignation.
- [ ] P6 The Brooke government (from April 1943 to 1963): energetic, concentrated on war production and farming.

Industry, agriculture, employment
- [ ] E1 Harland and Wolff: warships, merchant ships and repairs for the Admiralty.
- [ ] E2 Short Brothers: Stirling bombers and other aircraft.
- [ ] E3 Engineering firms (munitions and other war equipment); linen and rope works (parachutes, uniforms, tents, rope).
- [ ] E4 Agriculture: compulsory tillage; Northern Ireland a food supplier to Britain.
- [ ] E5 Unemployment fell sharply; women entered factories; wages rose.
- [ ] E6 Industrial unrest over pay and conditions despite full employment.
- [ ] E7 Close co-operation with Whitehall ministries (Admiralty, Aircraft Production).

Case study: Belfast during World War II
- [ ] C1 Background: why Belfast was a target (shipyard, aircraft factory, docks, engineering works).
- [ ] C2 Unpreparedness: the assumption that Belfast was out of range; few public shelters; weak anti-aircraft defences and no night fighters; small fire services; overcrowded slum housing.
- [ ] C3 The raid of 7–8 April 1941 (smaller, aimed at the shipyard).
- [ ] C4 The Easter Tuesday raid, 15–16 April 1941: about 180 bombers; about 900 dead; north and east Belfast, working-class districts, worst hit.
- [ ] C5 The raid of 4–5 May 1941: incendiary attack on the shipyard, aircraft factory and docks; about 150 dead.
- [ ] C6 Total toll: about 1,000 dead; more than half of the city's houses damaged; tens of thousands homeless; deadliest night of the Blitz outside London.
- [ ] C7 The ditchers: people leaving the city nightly to sleep in the countryside.
- [ ] C8 Dublin's fire brigades (and those of Dún Laoghaire, Dundalk and Drogheda) crossing the border on 15–16 April with de Valera's approval: significance as help from a neutral state.
- [ ] C9 The dead and the mortuaries: public buildings used, mortuaries overwhelmed.
- [ ] C10 Evacuation of mothers and children to rural areas; refuge in halls and churches; emergency rest centres.
- [ ] C11 What evacuation revealed: poverty, poor health and hygiene among slum children; the effect on debates about housing and public health.
- [ ] C12 Political fallout: criticism of the Unionist government's preparation and response; Andrews's fall (1943).
- [ ] C13 American troops: first landing 26 January 1942 at Dufferin Quay; over 100,000 by 1944; effects on daily life; segregated units.
- [ ] C14 Northern Ireland's ports and airfields in the Atlantic convoy escort and D-Day preparations.
- [ ] C15 Social and political effects: housing urgent; the Beveridge Report (1942) and parity; Beattie's by-election win (February 1943); Labour's rise; the 1947 Education Act and 1948 Health Services Act.
- [ ] C16 Belfast in 1945 vs 1939: unemployment, housing, industry dependent on war orders, Unionist control, stronger British links, sectarian division unchanged.

Assessment (paired judgement points, final block)
- [ ] S1 Achievement: full employment and better wages.
- [ ] S2 Achievement: stronger place in the United Kingdom; entry to the post-war welfare state.
- [ ] S3 Limit: the Blitz exposed poor preparation and housing; the government had to answer for it.
- [ ] S4 Limit: Unionist control and the exclusion of the Catholic minority unchanged; sectarian division and the border entrenched.
- [ ] S5 Contemporary views: unionists praised the contribution; nationalists were critical.
- [ ] S6 Overall: economic and social change, political continuity.

## 3. Traps

## 4. What exams ask

- Higher 100-mark essay, the commonest: how far Belfast in 1945 differed from 1939, or the effect of the war on Northern Ireland. Marks go to a clear judgement (economy and housing changed; politics and religious division did not) supported by paired dated points: full employment vs the Blitz damage; stronger British links vs unchanged Unionist control.
- Comparison of North and South in the war (2013, 2017, 2026 style): needs Northern points kept distinct from the South (at war vs neutral; bombing vs shortages). Give each side at least three specific, dated points.
- Ordinary 40-mark paragraph on the Belfast Blitz: the raids of April and May 1941, the main raid's date, bombers and deaths, the damage, the ditchers, the help from Dublin. Each correct, dated fact earns marks.
- Ordinary 40-mark paragraph on Northern Ireland at war: at war from September 1939; Harland and Wolff, Short Brothers, linen, farming; full employment; conscription not applied; American troops from January 1942.
- Short answers (6 marks) ask a single fact: what the raids showed (Northern Ireland was not safe), which government helped (Dublin), the figure killed.
- Source questions (2014 style) on evacuees: compare how two writers view the evacuees (sympathy vs blame), say what a source leaves out (no statistics), and explain how far a source is objective; the evacuees' poverty links to housing and public health.
- Explanations of why Belfast was unprepared: assumption of safety, few shelters, small anti-aircraft defences, no night fighters, small fire service.
