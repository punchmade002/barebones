## 1. Must cover (one bullet or block for every item; tick each off)

Background (assumed)
- [ ] A1 (assumed) Home Rule: the demand for an Irish parliament in Dublin for domestic affairs, within the Union of 1800; Butt's Home Rule League (1873) had made it a parliamentary movement.
- [ ] A2 (assumed) Parnell's rise before 1885: Land League president (1879), chairman of the Irish Parliamentary Party (1880), Kilmainham (1881–82), the Irish National League (1882).
- [ ] A3 (assumed) The Land War and coercion under Gladstone's Liberal government (1880–85), and the Phoenix Park murders (May 1882), which left Liberals and nationalists with a bitter legacy.
- [ ] A4 (assumed) British politics: the Liberals (Gladstone) and Conservatives (Salisbury) alternate in power; the Commons and the Lords; a majority of seats is needed to govern.
- [ ] A5 (assumed) Ulster: a Protestant majority in the north-east (above all Belfast and east Ulster) and a Catholic nationalist majority in the south and west of the province; "unionist" and "nationalist" defined.

Key concepts and essential terms (each defined as used here; also in keyTerms)
- [ ] K1 Home Rule (the central question of both elections).
- [ ] K2 Democracy: government through elected representatives; the 1884 Act widened it (men only).
- [ ] K3 Balance of power: a smaller party whose votes decide which larger party governs.
- [ ] K4 Hung Parliament: no party has an overall majority.
- [ ] K5 Pledge: undertaking by Irish Party candidates to sit, act and vote with the party.
- [ ] K6 Liberal Unionists; Orange card; Hawarden Kite (each a keyTerm).
- [ ] K7 Third Reform Act (1884) and the Redistribution of Seats Act (1885) (each a keyTerm).

Background: the new electorate and constituencies, 1884–85
- [ ] B1 The Representation of the People Act 1884 (Third Reform Act): vote to male householders and lodgers in the counties on the borough terms; applied to Ireland.
- [ ] B2 Effect on the Irish electorate: more than tripled, mostly small farmers and labourers; women excluded.
- [ ] B3 The Redistribution of Seats Act 1885: small boroughs abolished, mostly single-member constituencies; Ireland kept 103 seats.
- [ ] B4 Why this favoured an organised party: National League branches in every district, run by local committees and often by priests.
- [ ] B5 Candidate selection: constituency conventions, with clergy influential; the pledge (1884).
- [ ] B6 Secret ballot (Ballot Act 1872) let tenants vote against landlords.
- [ ] B7 The Catholic hierarchy (Archbishops Walsh and Croke) backed the party in return for support on Catholic education.

Parnell's balance-of-power strategy, 1884–85
- [ ] C1 Parnell at Cork, January 1885: no limit to the march of a nation; Home Rule as a national demand.
- [ ] C2 The "Maamtrasna" alliance: Conservatives (Lord Randolph Churchill) joined Irish MPs in 1884 to attack Lord Lieutenant Spencer and Liberal coercion over the Maamtrasna murders and the hanging of Myles Joyce.
- [ ] C3 9 June 1885: Irish Party votes with the Conservatives; Gladstone's government falls; Salisbury forms a minority ministry.
- [ ] C4 Carnarvon's secret meeting with Parnell (August 1885); Crimes Act allowed to lapse; Ashbourne Act (land purchase).
- [ ] C5 Chamberlain's Central Board scheme (through O'Shea) rejected, leaving Home Rule as the demand.
- [ ] C6 Parnell's Arklow speech (October 1885): demand for a legislature controlling Irish domestic affairs.
- [ ] C7 Parnell's manifesto (November 1885): Irish voters in Britain to vote against Liberals.

The general election of November–December 1885
- [ ] E1 Irish result: 85 of 103 Irish seats plus Liverpool Scotland (86 MPs).
- [ ] E2 Outside Ulster only the two Dublin University seats escaped the party; the south and west swept.
- [ ] E3 Ulster: 17 of 33 seats, in the Catholic-majority districts; unionists took the rest, strongest in the east.
- [ ] E4 Great Britain: Liberals about 335, Conservatives about 249; the Liberal lead was about the size of the Irish Party, so Parnell held the balance; hung Parliament.
- [ ] E5 The Irish vote in British cities, and its (uncertain) effect.
- [ ] E6 Meaning of the result: nationalists claimed a national mandate; unionists said two Irelands had voted.

The Hawarden Kite, Gladstone's conversion and the First Home Rule Bill
- [ ] G1 The Hawarden Kite (17 December 1885): leaked by Herbert Gladstone.
- [ ] G2 Salisbury defeated (January 1886, Collings amendment); Gladstone's third ministry (February 1886); John Morley as Chief Secretary.
- [ ] G3 Chamberlain and Trevelyan resign (March 1886); Hartington declines to serve.
- [ ] G4 The Government of Ireland Bill introduced, 8 April 1886: terms (Irish legislature and executive; Crown, defence, foreign policy, trade reserved; no Irish MPs at Westminster; a fixed Irish contribution to imperial costs; two orders; no endowment of religion).
- [ ] G5 The Land Purchase Bill: companion measure, unpopular, not proceeded with.
- [ ] G6 Parnell's response: accepted as a basis, with objections on finance.
- [ ] G7 Arguments for (coercion had failed; Ireland's clear verdict; reconciliation) and against (break-up of the empire; Protestants and Ulster; separation).

The defeat of the bill, June 1886
- [ ] D1 Second reading defeated in the early hours of 8 June 1886: 341 to 311, majority of 30.
- [ ] D2 About 93 Liberals voted against: the Liberal Unionists led by Hartington and Chamberlain; John Bright also opposed.
- [ ] D3 Gladstone dissolved Parliament.

Unionist mobilisation
- [ ] U1 The Orange card: Churchill's letter (February 1886) and Ulster Hall speech (22 February).
- [ ] U2 "Ulster will fight and Ulster will be right" (attributed to Churchill, 1886).
- [ ] U3 The Irish Loyal and Patriotic Union (1885); Ulster Loyalist Anti-Repeal organisation (1886); Orange Order growth.
- [ ] U4 The Belfast riots (June to September 1886): scale and interpretation.

The July 1886 election and outcomes
- [ ] O1 Result: Conservatives and Liberal Unionists a combined majority of over 100; Irish Party again about 85 seats.
- [ ] O2 Salisbury's second ministry; Liberal Unionists in coalition from 1895.
- [ ] O3 Liberal–Nationalist alliance; Home Rule now Liberal policy.
- [ ] O4 Home Rule dependent on a Liberal majority able to overcome the Lords (not achieved until the Parliament Act of 1911).
- [ ] O5 Organised unionism and the origin of the Ulster question.
- [ ] O6 Parnell at his peak, with his later fall named as the next stage only.

Key personality: Charles Stewart Parnell, role in 1885–86 (one block, every point)
- [ ] P1 Who he was: Protestant landowner from Avondale, Co. Wicklow; Irish Party leader.
- [ ] P2 What he did: built the party machine, pledge and conventions; bargained with Carnarvon; manifesto; Galway by-election (February 1886).
- [ ] P3 Participant: led the campaigns and the bargaining.
- [ ] P4 Shaped by events: depended on Gladstone surviving; the Liberal split and unionist backlash limited him.
- [ ] P5 Contemporaries: nationalists (national triumph) vs unionists (agitator linked to violence).
- [ ] P6 Historians: Lyons on the zenith; strengths and weaknesses as examiners ask.

Views and documents
- [ ] V1 Nationalist press and speeches; unionist press and organisations.
- [ ] V2 Gladstone's speeches; Liberal Unionist speeches (Chamberlain, Hartington).
- [ ] V3 Cartoons (Punch, Tenniel) and their symbols; anti-Irish caricature.
- [ ] V4 Historians' debate on why Gladstone embraced Home Rule: conviction vs tactics.

Assessment (final block, paired points)
- [ ] X1 Achievement: a national mandate, Home Rule on the British agenda, a British party won.
- [ ] X2 Limit: no majority in the Commons; the Liberal split put the Conservatives in power.
- [ ] X3 Achievement: narrow defeat and a realistic route via the Liberals.
- [ ] X4 Limit: Ulster Protestant opposition, never answered.
- [ ] X5 Contemporary views (nationalist vs unionist) and later views (a turning point).

## 3. Traps

Do not use (wrong or doubtful facts in the current notes):
- Do not use: "except the two for Dublin University and a few in Dublin suburbs", because the sums show only the two Dublin University seats were outside Ulster and not won (85 = 17 + 68).
- Do not use: "unionists held the Belfast seats", because I am not certain of the 1885 result in every Belfast seat. Say instead that unionists won most of the east of Ulster.
- Do not use: "the Liberals' lead over the Conservatives was almost exactly the Irish total" as a fact; say about 335 to about 249 and that Irish votes could decide who governed.
- Do not use: "(some sources give 343 to 313)", because the divisions I am sure of are 341 to 311 (majority 30). Give majority 30 and 341 to 311.
- Do not use: Parnell objected to "its control of the police"; say only that he objected to its financial terms.
- Do not use: "stands at your bar, expectant, hopeful, almost suppliant" as part of the 8 April 1886 introduction speech; it comes from Gladstone's closing speech on the second reading, 7 June 1886, before the defeat.
- Do not use: the date "4 June 1886" for the first riot, because sources vary; say early June 1886.
- Do not use: "at least 30 killed, and hundreds injured" without hedging; say "about 30 or more killed, estimates vary".
- Do not use: "sitting together" for the two orders; say the legislature had two orders and leave it there.
- Do not use: the phrase "Ulster will fight and Ulster will be right" dated to a month; say 1886.

Confusable people, terms and dates
- Third Reform Act (1884, the vote) vs Redistribution of Seats Act (1885, the constituencies).
- Salisbury's first government (June 1885–January 1886) vs his second (July 1886 onwards); Gladstone's second ministry (1880–85) fell in June 1885; his third ran February to July 1886.
- Chamberlain (radical, resigned March 1886, Liberal Unionist) vs Hartington (Whig, never joined). Randolph Churchill (Conservative) vs Winston Churchill (his son, not here).
- Hawarden Kite (December 1885, leak) vs the First Home Rule Bill (April 1886).
- First Home Rule Bill (1886, defeated in the Commons) vs the Second (1893, passed the Commons, rejected by the Lords) vs the Third (1912).
- Carnarvon (Lord Lieutenant, Conservative) vs Spencer (Lord Lieutenant, Liberal) vs Morley (Chief Secretary).
- The Irish Loyal and Patriotic Union (southern unionists, 1885) vs the Ulster Unionist Council (1905) and the UVF (1913).
- The November–December 1885 election vs the July 1886 election. Parnell's manifesto of November 1885 told Irish voters in Britain to vote against Liberals; this was reversed after the Kite.
- Captain O'Shea (the Galway candidate; Chamberlain's go-between) is the same man whose wife's affair with Parnell led to the 1890 divorce (not here).
- Home Rule (a parliament within the Union) vs separatism (full independence).

## 4. What exams ask

- The 2006 documents-based question used Gladstone's speech on the bill and an anti-Home Rule cartoon. Parts (a)-(c) rewarded comprehension (what each document says), comparison (a specific point of agreement or difference), and criticism (primary source, persuasive speech, edited extract, cartoon that attacks Home Rule and the Irish). Notes should explain how to place a speech by speaker, date and purpose, and how to read the symbols in a cartoon.
- Part (d), 40 marks: Parnell's strengths and weaknesses, using the whole topic. Strengths: discipline and the pledge, tactics with both parties, balance of power, appeal to Fenians and constitutionalists. Weaknesses: reliance on a British ally, secrecy about his private life, imposing O'Shea, aloofness. Each point needs a date or event.
- Higher 100-mark essay on the issues and outcomes of the 1885 and 1886 elections: strong answers name the issue (Home Rule) and give causes (the 1884 Act, the National League machine, the balance of power), the events with dates, and outcomes (Liberal split, Conservative government, Liberal–Nationalist alliance, organised unionism) with a judgement.
- Cause and effect: why the Irish Party won 85 seats (new voters, organisation, clergy, pledge); why the bill failed (the Liberal split rather than the Conservatives alone); why Gladstone changed (conviction vs tactics).
- Ordinary Level 40-mark question: the 1884 Act and 1885 Act (what each did, the electorate more than tripled), the seats won in 1885, the balance of power; marks per correct, dated statement.
- Ordinary Level short answers on a document: who introduced the bill, what its result was, who voted against (Liberals led by Hartington and Chamberlain), why the Liberals could not bring in a new bill (out of power 1886 to 1892).
- Examiners reward paired judgement: achievements vs limits, contemporary vs later views, and naming a historian.
