## 1. Must cover (one bullet or block for every item; tick each off)

Background (assumed)
- [ ] A1 (assumed) Northern Ireland was created in 1921; unionists (mostly Protestant) wanted to stay in the United Kingdom, nationalists (mostly Catholic) wanted a united Ireland.
- [ ] A2 (assumed) The Troubles began in 1968-69; the period of this topic is 1949-1993.
- [ ] A3 (assumed) The Reformation split Christianity into Catholic and Protestant churches; the 1690 Boyne and 1688-89 Derry events are the historical memory behind Protestant tradition.
- [ ] A4 (assumed) Sectarianism: hostility between Catholic and Protestant communities (the outline's concept for neighbouring subsections; use it here only as context).

Key concepts (defined as used in this topic; also in keyTerms)
- [ ] K1 Cultural traditions: the distinct heritage, customs and practices of the unionist and nationalist communities.
- [ ] K2 Cultural identity: a sense of belonging through shared religion, language, history and customs, tied in Northern Ireland to allegiance.
- [ ] K3 Ecumenism: dialogue and cooperation between Christian churches.
- [ ] K4 Bigotry: obstinate intolerance of people with different beliefs or identities.
- [ ] K5 Tolerance and intolerance: acceptance versus refusal to accept differing beliefs and identities.

Religion and identity
- [ ] R1 Religion and political allegiance overlapped (unionist/Protestant/British; nationalist/Catholic/Irish), but not absolutely.
- [ ] R2 Population: Catholics about one third, Protestants the majority; the largest Protestant churches.
- [ ] R3 Separate schooling, social life and marriage patterns kept the communities apart; identity passed on across generations.
- [ ] R4 Not every nationalist was a practising Catholic; the overlap was one of identity, not belief.

The Orange Order and Protestant tradition
- [ ] O1 Founded 1795 after the Battle of the Diamond (Armagh).
- [ ] O2 The Twelfth of July parades (the Boyne, 1690); the eleventh night bonfires; bands and banners; the Twelfth a public holiday.
- [ ] O3 Links with the Ulster Unionist Party and Council; Protestant-only membership, local lodges.
- [ ] O4 Parades through or near Catholic areas as a source of tension.
- [ ] O5 Related bodies: the Black Preceptory and the Apprentice Boys (the Apprentice Boys have their own case-study subsection; mention only).

Catholic tradition, the GAA and the Irish language
- [ ] G1 The GAA, founded 1884; Gaelic games as an expression of Irish identity.
- [ ] G2 Rule 21 banning members of the RUC and British army; removed in 2001; a unionist grievance and a nationalist symbol.
- [ ] G3 The Ancient Order of Hibernians as a Catholic parallel to the Orange Order.
- [ ] G4 The Irish language in the North: a small, active community; the Irish-medium school at Shaw's Road, Belfast (1971).
- [ ] G5 Church, parish halls and GAA clubs as the centres of social life in nationalist areas; the Church's role in education.
- [ ] G6 Nationalist grievance that the Stormont state did not respect Irish identity (the Flags and Emblems Act 1954).

Ecumenism: opportunities
- [ ] E1 Vatican II (1962-65) encouraged dialogue between the churches.
- [ ] E2 Corrymeela (1965): Ray Davey, Ballycastle, reconciliation centre.
- [ ] E3 Irish School of Ecumenics (1970): Michael Hurley, Dublin.
- [ ] E4 The Ballymascanlon meeting of church leaders (1973).
- [ ] E5 Clergy peace initiatives: the Feakle meeting with the IRA (December 1974) and the IRA's Christmas ceasefire.
- [ ] E6 Pope John Paul II's appeal at Drogheda (September 1979).

Ecumenism: problems and limits
- [ ] L1 Hardline Protestant hostility to ecumenism, led by Paisley and the Free Presbyterian Church.
- [ ] L2 Clergy and lay people who joined joint initiatives could be criticised by their own side; mixed marriage and joint worship disapproved of in both communities (the Catholic mixed-marriage rules until 1970).
- [ ] L3 Violence and sectarian killing undermined trust; clergy were also targets.
- [ ] L4 Ecumenism built contacts but did not change the political division.

Key personality: Ian Paisley (part 2: religion and politics)
- [ ] P1 Who he was: Armagh-born preacher; founder (1951) and leader of the Free Presbyterian Church.
- [ ] P2 What he did, with dates: protest against ecumenism (June 1966); Bannside (April 1970) and North Antrim (June 1970); founded the DUP (1971), leader to 2008; European Parliament (1979).
- [ ] P3 Opposition to ecumenism and the Catholic Church; the Pope incident in Strasbourg (October 1988).
- [ ] P4 Shaped by and shaping events: his religion drove his politics (opposing Sunningdale and the Anglo-Irish Agreement are covered in their own subsections; mention only).
- [ ] P5 As a participant: leader and preacher at the centre of the events.
- [ ] P6 Contemporaries: admired by supporters, condemned by opponents for his rhetoric.
- [ ] P7 Historians: Steve Bruce on the centrality of evangelical belief to his politics; ecumenism made harder by his religious politics.

Key personality: Seamus Heaney
- [ ] H1 Who he was: born 1939, Mossbawn, Co. Derry; Catholic farming family; St Columb's and Queen's.
- [ ] H2 Early collections (1966, 1969, 1972) and North (1975); bog bodies, "Punishment".
- [ ] H3 Later career: moved to Wicklow (1972); Field Work (1979), Station Island (1984); Harvard and Oxford; Nobel Prize (1995).
- [ ] H4 His role as a witness to the Troubles from within the Catholic community, and as a director of Field Day.
- [ ] H5 Contemporaries and critics: Ciaran Carson on North; praise from others.
- [ ] H6 Historians: a key cultural response to the Troubles; grief, guilt and identity beyond sectarianism.
- [ ] H7 "An Open Letter" (1983) and the green passport.

Cultural responses
- [ ] F1 Field Day Theatre Company (1980): founders, directors, purpose.
- [ ] F2 Translations (Guildhall, Derry, September 1980): theme.
- [ ] F3 Other poets: Mahon, Longley, Montague.
- [ ] F4 Novels, plays and music: Cal, Lies of Silence, Observe the Sons of Ulster Marching Towards the Somme, Alternative Ulster.
- [ ] F5 Murals (including Free Derry Corner) and gable-end art in both communities.
- [ ] F6 Critics of Field Day (Edna Longley); cultural production both expressed identity and reinforced separate traditions.
- [ ] F7 Film and television as outlets for response (name no titles; see Traps).

The Cultural Traditions Group, 1988
- [ ] C1 Set up in 1988 under the Central Community Relations Unit; later within the Community Relations Council (1990).
- [ ] C2 Aims: recognise both traditions and encourage sharing across the divide; its work in events and education.
- [ ] C3 Education Reform Order 1989: EMU and Cultural Heritage as cross-curricular themes.
- [ ] C4 Criticism: could equal recognition replace political agreement?

Assessment (paired judgement points, final block)
- [ ] S1 Ecumenism: built dialogue and gave moderates a voice (Corrymeela, Ballymascanlon, Feakle) vs limited by hardline opposition and violence.
- [ ] S2 Culture: Heaney and Field Day gave a language to the conflict vs they did not change the political divide.
- [ ] S3 Contemporary views: unionists and nationalists each saw their religion and culture as central to identity.
- [ ] S4 Later view: cultural responses were genuine and productive but not sufficient alone to resolve the conflict.

## 3. Traps

## 4. What exams ask

- Higher 100-mark essay on religion, cultural identity and responses (2013, 2014, 2017, 2019, 2024, 2026): how religion and politics overlapped, with the Orange Order, the GAA and the language as expressions of identity, and then at least one response (ecumenism or Heaney). Marks go to showing the overlap with named examples, not a general description.
- Ecumenism essay (2007, 2024): opportunities (Vatican II, Corrymeela, the Irish School of Ecumenics, Ballymascanlon, Feakle) set against problems (Paisley, violence, the mixed-marriage question, no political change). A reasoned verdict earns more than a list.
- Cultural responses essay (2015, 2016, 2020, 2021): Heaney, Field Day, other poets, murals, drama; use "divided societies are culturally productive" as the judgement. Strong answers quote the title and year of each work.
- Personality questions: Paisley as preacher, protest leader and politician; Heaney as poet. Paired with Devlin, Hume or O'Neill in 100-mark essays: give dates, a significant achievement, and a verdict on how far they changed the course of events.
- Ordinary Level 30/40-mark paragraphs: Heaney (birth 1939, Mossbawn, collections, North 1975, Nobel 1995), Paisley (1951, DUP 1971) and Field Day (1980, Friel and Rea, Translations). Each correct, dated statement earns marks, so give four or five.
- Document questions: a sermon or newspaper extract on religion and identity; students need to compare the two communities' attitudes and judge the usefulness of the source for a historian.
