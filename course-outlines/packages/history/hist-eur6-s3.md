## 1. Must cover (one bullet or block for every item; tick each off)

Background (assumed)
- [ ] A1 (assumed) The Cold War policy of containment and the domino theory; US aid to France and then to South Vietnam; the 1954 division of Vietnam at the 17th parallel (detail belongs to the foreign policy notes).
- [ ] A2 (assumed) North Vietnam (Ho Chi Minh, communist) and South Vietnam (Diem, US-backed); the Viet Cong as the communist guerrillas in the South.
- [ ] A3 (assumed) Kennedy's record in Vietnam: a rise in military advisers, no combat troops.
- [ ] A4 (assumed) The Great Society as Johnson's domestic programme (civil rights, War on Poverty, health, education).

Key concepts (defined as used in this case study; also in keyTerms)
- [ ] K1 Imperialism: the critics' charge that a great power was imposing its will on a small nation.
- [ ] K2 Public opinion: the views of ordinary Americans, measured by polls and shaped by television and the press; it turned against the war and Johnson from 1967.
- [ ] K3 Military-industrial complex: defence contractors, the armed forces and government sharing an interest in high defence spending; Eisenhower's 1961 warning; critics linked it to the war.
- [ ] K4 Credibility gap: the gap between official statements and what reporters found.
- [ ] K5 Other essential terms: Gulf of Tonkin Resolution, Rolling Thunder, Tet Offensive, Great Society, search and destroy, attrition, guns and butter, the draft.

Johnson's background and the inheritance
- [ ] B1 Johnson's life to 1963: Texas origins, New Deal work, the House, the 87-vote Senate win of 1948, Senate leadership, vice president.
- [ ] B2 His political skills: persuasion and pressure in the Senate ("the Johnson treatment"); why they did not work in Vietnam.
- [ ] B3 He became president on 22 November 1963 after Kennedy's assassination.
- [ ] B4 What he inherited: about 16,000 advisers, an unstable Saigon government after Diem's overthrow, Viet Cong control of much of the countryside.
- [ ] B5 Continuity: NSAM 273; Kennedy's advisers (McNamara, Rusk, Bundy) kept.
- [ ] B6 His motives: fear of "losing" Vietnam as China had been "lost", of right-wing attack, and of damage to the Great Society; credibility of US commitments.

Gulf of Tonkin and 1964 election
- [ ] T1 The 2 August 1964 attack on USS Maddox and the context (patrol, South Vietnamese raids).
- [ ] T2 The doubtful 4 August report; retaliatory air strikes.
- [ ] T3 The Tonkin Gulf Resolution: date, votes, wording in outline, the two opponents; later criticism.
- [ ] T4 The 1964 election: Johnson against Goldwater; Johnson as the candidate of restraint; the gap between campaign promises and later action.

Escalation, 1965
- [ ] E1 Pleiku and Flaming Dart (February 1965).
- [ ] E2 Rolling Thunder from 2 March 1965; its aims (pressure on Hanoi, support Saigon) and its limits.
- [ ] E3 The first Marine combat troops at Da Nang, 8 March 1965.
- [ ] E4 The Johns Hopkins speech (7 April 1965): talks offered, development plan.
- [ ] E5 The July 1965 decision and the 28 July announcement: no declaration of war, no reserve call-up.
- [ ] E6 Troop numbers by year, 1965–1968.
- [ ] E7 The advisers' debate: George Ball dissented; McNamara, Rusk and Bundy backed escalation.
- [ ] E8 Why Johnson kept the escalation low-key (Great Society legislation).

Westmoreland's strategy and the war on the ground
- [ ] W1 Attrition and search and destroy; the body count.
- [ ] W2 Why attrition failed (enemy's choice of battle, manpower, Ho Chi Minh Trail).
- [ ] W3 Firepower and civilian suffering (napalm, Agent Orange); pacification lagging; the Saigon regime (Thieu, 1967 election).
- [ ] W4 Westmoreland's optimism of November 1967.
- [ ] W5 My Lai (March 1968; exposed 1969) and growing casualties; the draft.

Credibility gap and the anti-war movement
- [ ] D1 The credibility gap.
- [ ] D2 Fulbright's televised hearings (1966) and The Arrogance of Power.
- [ ] D3 The teach-ins (March 1965) and student protest; SDS march (April 1965); the Pentagon march (October 1967).
- [ ] D4 Martin Luther King's Beyond Vietnam speech (April 1967).
- [ ] D5 Muhammad Ali's refusal of induction (April 1967).
- [ ] D6 Opposing arguments: critics (cost, morality, Great Society) vs supporters (credibility, domino theory); how far opinion had turned by late 1967.

Costs to the Great Society
- [ ] G1 Guns and butter; the delayed tax request and the 1968 surtax.
- [ ] G2 Deficits and inflation; War on Poverty cut or underfunded.
- [ ] G3 Great Society achievements that came before the costs bit: Civil Rights Act 1964, Voting Rights Act 1965, Medicare and Medicaid.
- [ ] G4 Urban riots and loss of public support for domestic spending.

Tet and Johnson's withdrawal
- [ ] J1 The Tet Offensive from 30 January 1968: scale, targets (Saigon, Hue).
- [ ] J2 The embassy attack and television coverage.
- [ ] J3 Military outcome (heavy communist losses) vs political outcome (credibility shattered).
- [ ] J4 Cronkite's broadcast, 27 February 1968.
- [ ] J5 The 206,000-troop request; Clifford; the Wise Men.
- [ ] J6 The New Hampshire primary and Robert Kennedy's entry.
- [ ] J7 The 31 March 1968 speech: partial bombing halt, talks, withdrawal from the race.
- [ ] J8 Consequences: Paris talks (May 1968), King and Robert Kennedy killed, Chicago convention, Nixon's win in November 1968.

Why Johnson could not win
- [ ] L1 Limited war: no invasion of the North, no mining of Haiphong, no nuclear weapons.
- [ ] L2 Enemy sanctuaries in Laos and Cambodia; the Ho Chi Minh Trail; enemy determination.
- [ ] L3 A weak, corrupt Saigon government and army.
- [ ] L4 Flawed strategy and measures (attrition, body count).
- [ ] L5 Gradual escalation and lack of a clear public explanation; loss of home support.
- [ ] L6 Differing explanations: Cold War assumptions vs avoidable choices.

Key personality: Lyndon Johnson (one block, every point)
- [ ] P1 Who he was: career, dates and offices.
- [ ] P2 What he did: Great Society and Vietnam decisions, with dates.
- [ ] P3 Shaped by events and shaping them: made the key decisions of 1964–65; forced out by Tet and opinion in 1968.
- [ ] P4 Participant: the decision-maker, not a witness.
- [ ] P5 How contemporaries saw him: admired as a legislator, distrusted over Vietnam, protest chants.
- [ ] P6 How historians judge him: Halberstam; Dallek and Logevall; domestic achievement against Vietnam failure.

Assessment (paired judgement points, final block)
- [ ] S1 Success: civil rights and social legislation.
- [ ] S2 Failure: Vietnam, about 58,000 American deaths over the whole war, damage to the Great Society.
- [ ] S3 Contemporary vs later views (approval in 1964–65 against about 40 per cent in late 1967; historians' debate).
- [ ] S4 Overall judgement: a great domestic president whose war undermined his presidency; the credibility gap as lasting legacy.

## 3. Traps

Do not use (wrong or doubtful facts in the current notes):
- Do not use: "Robert Kennedy was assassinated on 5 June", because he was shot on 5 June and died on 6 June 1968.
- Do not use: Diem's overthrow "on 2 November 1963" as a single date, because the coup began on 1 November and he was killed on 2 November. Write "1–2 November".
- Do not use: Johnson "won his Senate seat in 1948 by 87 votes" without saying it was the Democratic runoff, because the 87 votes decided the primary runoff (the fact card is right; the notes should be consistent).
- Do not use: the claim that the Tonkin resolution was "prepared before the incidents were fully understood" as settled fact. Say a draft existed beforehand and that the second incident was doubtful.
- Do not use: the claim that the war "ended without victory in 1973" without care. The US withdrew in 1973 and Saigon fell in 1975; that belongs to the notes on the retreat from Vietnam.
- Do not use: "Johnson's reputation has risen since the 1990s ... most historians regard him as a tragic figure" as fact; give named historians' views instead.
- Do not use: Westmoreland's "light at the end of the tunnel" or Nixon's "secret plan" to end the war, because neither is well attested as stated.
- Do not use: Johnson's Akron "American boys" remark as a quotation; paraphrase (restraint in 1964).
- Do not use: "tens of thousands killed by Agent Orange" or any casualty figures for Vietnamese or for My Lai beyond "several hundred".

## 4. What exams ask

- Higher 100-mark essays: Johnson's presidency and leadership, how effective or successful he was (typically strong at home, failing abroad); how Vietnam affected his presidency; why the United States lost the war; the importance of Vietnam to both the United States and Vietnam. Marks go to a clear judgement stated at the start, balanced achievements and failures with dates, and a stated overall verdict.
- DBQ-style questions on Johnson and Vietnam (cartoons, speeches, critics' accounts, Fulbright): students need enough context to place a document (date, speaker, purpose), judge whether a source is primary or secondary, assess a cartoon's effectiveness and bias, and say whether a speaker exaggerates. Part (d) style: why Johnson could not win; how Vietnam affected his presidency.
- "Why Johnson could not win" earns marks for four or five distinct reasons, each with evidence: limited war, enemy sanctuaries and determination, weak Saigon, flawed strategy (attrition, body count), loss of support at home.
- Effects on his presidency: the Great Society damaged by costs; the credibility gap; protest and the Tet shock; his 31 March 1968 withdrawal. Link cause to effect, not just list.
- Cause questions: why Johnson escalated (inherited commitment, fear of a "loss", Cold War credibility, protecting the Great Society), and why the escalation was gradual and low-key.
- Ordinary Level paragraphs (30/40 marks): Johnson's inheritance, the Tonkin Gulf Resolution with its date and votes, Rolling Thunder and troop numbers, the credibility gap, the Great Society and Vietnam, Tet. Each correct, dated and relevant statement earns marks; vague statements ("the war went badly") do not.
- Short-answer questions on an image or text: date and place of events, who a figure was, what Johnson announced, which president followed Johnson.
- Assessment: paired points (domestic success against Vietnam failure; contemporary against later views); naming Halberstam, Dallek or Logevall adds weight.
