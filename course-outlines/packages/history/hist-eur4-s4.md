## 1. Must cover (one bullet or block for every item; tick each off)

Background (assumed)
- [ ] A1 (assumed) The Soviet bloc after 1945: one-party communist "satellite" states in Poland, Czechoslovakia, Hungary, Romania, Bulgaria and East Germany, tied to Moscow by the Warsaw Pact (1955) and Comecon (1949), with Soviet troops stationed in several of them. Details are in the notes on the origins of the Cold War.
- [ ] A2 (assumed) Hungary 1956 as the standing warning: a reform movement that left the Warsaw Pact was crushed by Soviet tanks. One sentence only; the detail is in the notes on the Hungarian Uprising.
- [ ] A3 (assumed) Soviet leaders in order: Stalin (died 1953), Khrushchev (to 1964), Brezhnev (1964–82), Andropov (1982–84), Chernenko (1984–85), Gorbachev (1985–91).
- [ ] A4 (assumed) The command economy and its weaknesses by the 1970s–80s: central planning, shortages, low living standards, stagnation, foreign debt (Poland). One bullet; detail is in the notes on the communist economies.
- [ ] A5 (assumed) The Helsinki Final Act (1975): the Eastern bloc signed human-rights commitments that dissidents later used against their governments.
- [ ] A6 (assumed) The "second Cold War" of the early 1980s: the Soviet invasion of Afghanistan (1979), Reagan's arms build-up and the Strategic Defense Initiative. One bullet; detail is in the notes on the crises of the Cold War.

Key concepts and essential terms (each defined as used in this topic; also in keyTerms)
- [ ] K1 Glasnost: openness; freer discussion, less censorship, criticism of officials and of the Soviet past.
- [ ] K2 Communism: as practised in the Soviet bloc, rule by a single communist party, with a state-owned, centrally planned economy; in this subsection, the system that reformers tried to change and that collapsed in 1989–91.
- [ ] K3 Perestroika: restructuring of the Soviet economy (and later the political system), adding limited market elements to planning.
- [ ] K4 Uskorenie: acceleration, Gorbachev's first economic slogan.
- [ ] K5 Democratisation (demokratizatsiya): partly free elections and the end of the party's monopoly.
- [ ] K6 Brezhnev Doctrine: the Soviet claim to intervene wherever socialism was threatened in a socialist state.
- [ ] K7 Sinatra Doctrine: the joking name for Gorbachev's policy of letting Eastern European states go "their way".
- [ ] K8 Socialism with a human face; Action Programme; normalisation (Czechoslovakia).
- [ ] K9 Solidarity; the Gdańsk Agreement; martial law; the Round Table talks (Poland).
- [ ] K10 Dissident; Charter 77; Velvet Revolution.
- [ ] K11 Two Plus Four Treaty; Commonwealth of Independent States (CIS); Visegrád group.

Reform movements before 1968
- [ ] R1 East Germany, 17 June 1953: causes (raised work norms, shortages, Stalin's death), the rising in East Berlin and many other towns, Soviet tanks, results (Ulbricht kept power, emigration westward continued until the Wall).
- [ ] R2 Poland 1956: the Poznań protest (June), the October crisis, Gomułka's return after a showdown with Khrushchev, why Moscow did not invade (Poland stayed in the Warsaw Pact and the party kept control), the concessions (end of forced collectivisation, Wyszyński released, more room for the Church), and Gomułka's later return to tight control.
- [ ] R3 The pattern: Poland 1956 (concession), Hungary 1956 (crushed), Czechoslovakia 1968 (invaded): reform was tolerated only if the party kept its monopoly and the country stayed in the Pact.

The Prague Spring and the Brezhnev Doctrine
- [ ] P1 Background: Novotný's hardline rule, a stagnant economy, Slovak grievances; Dubček First Secretary (January 1968).
- [ ] P2 The reforms: "socialism with a human face", the Action Programme (April 1968): freedom of speech and press, end of censorship in practice, rehabilitation of victims of the 1950s, curbs on the secret police, economic reform with market elements, a federal settlement for Czechs and Slovaks; the "Two Thousand Words" manifesto (June 1968). Dubček did not propose leaving the Warsaw Pact.
- [ ] P3 Soviet fears and pressure: the danger of spreading reform to Poland, East Germany and Ukraine; meetings and warnings in the summer of 1968.
- [ ] P4 The invasion, 20–21 August 1968: which armies, Dubček taken to Moscow, mostly non-violent resistance, Romania's refusal, Albania's exit from the Pact.
- [ ] P5 The Brezhnev Doctrine (September and November 1968).
- [ ] P6 Normalisation: Husák replaces Dubček (April 1969), purges; Jan Palach (January 1969).
- [ ] P7 Charter 77 (January 1977) and Václav Havel; dissidents using the Helsinki commitments.

Pope John Paul II (political role)
- [ ] J1 Key personality block: who he was (Karol Wojtyła, Wadowice, Archbishop of Kraków, cardinal), his election (16 October 1978) as the first non-Italian pope in over 450 years.
- [ ] J2 The 1979 visit to Poland (2–10 June): huge crowds, the Victory Square Mass in Warsaw, the sense that Poles were many and could organise without the state.
- [ ] J3 Support for Solidarity: the pope's picture at the Gdańsk shipyard gate (1980); visits in 1983 (meeting Wałęsa) and 1987; defending workers' rights and human dignity without calling for revolt.
- [ ] J4 The assassination attempt (13 May 1981, Mehmet Ali Ağca): one bullet; who was behind it was never proven.
- [ ] J5 How the communist authorities saw him (a threat; the Church monitored by the secret police) and his meeting with Gorbachev in the Vatican (1 December 1989).
- [ ] J6 Historians' views: how far he caused 1989 (moral leadership and Polish civil society) vs economic and Soviet factors; Gorbachev's own later tribute.

Poland: Solidarity and martial law
- [ ] S1 Background: price-rise protests and the fall of Gomułka (December 1970), Gierek's borrowing from the West and the debt crisis; meat-price rises in July 1980. One bullet; detail is in the notes on the communist economies.
- [ ] S2 The Gdańsk strikes (August 1980) at the Lenin Shipyard, the 21 demands, the Gdańsk Agreement (31 August 1980): the right to free trade unions and to strike.
- [ ] S3 Solidarity: founded September 1980, led by Lech Wałęsa, about ten million members, a union that became a national movement with Church and intellectual support.
- [ ] S4 Martial law (13 December 1981): Jaruzelski, arrests, the union banned; the "lesser evil" argument and the debate over whether Moscow would have invaded.
- [ ] S5 Solidarity underground, 1982–88: Wałęsa's Nobel Peace Prize (1983), the murder of Father Jerzy Popiełuszko (1984), strikes in 1988.
- [ ] S6 The Round Table talks (February–April 1989) and Solidarity legalised again (April 1989).

Mikhail Gorbachev
- [ ] G1 Key personality block: background (born 1931, Stavropol, law at Moscow State University, party career in Stavropol, Politburo 1980, Andropov's protégé); General Secretary 11 March 1985; head of state from 1988; President of the USSR 1990–91.
- [ ] G2 Why reform was needed in 1985: stagnation, the cost of the arms race and of Afghanistan, low oil prices, an ageing leadership; Gorbachev's aim was to save socialism, not to end it.
- [ ] G3 Uskorenie and the anti-alcohol campaign (1985); the move to perestroika: the Law on State Enterprise (1987), the Law on Cooperatives (1988); why it failed (half-measures, shortages, inflation, falling output by 1990–91).
- [ ] G4 Glasnost: launched 1986; Chernobyl (26 April 1986) as the case that exposed secrecy; freer press, publication of banned writers, criticism of Stalin, release of dissidents such as Andrei Sakharov (December 1986).
- [ ] G5 Democratisation: the partly free elections to the Congress of People's Deputies (March 1989), the end of the party's constitutional monopoly (1990), the presidency (1990).
- [ ] G6 "New thinking" in foreign policy and arms control: Shevardnadze; summits with Reagan at Geneva (1985), Reykjavik (1986) and Washington (1987); the INF Treaty (8 December 1987); the UN speech (December 1988) announcing troop cuts; withdrawal from Afghanistan (completed February 1989); the Malta summit with Bush (December 1989); START I (1991).
- [ ] G7 The Sinatra Doctrine (Gerasimov, October 1989): non-intervention in Eastern Europe.
- [ ] G8 How contemporaries saw him: Thatcher's verdict (1984), "Gorbymania" in the West, the Nobel Peace Prize (1990); at home, blamed by conservatives for destroying the system and by radicals such as Yeltsin for being too slow; unpopular because of shortages.
- [ ] G9 How historians judge him: reformer who wanted to save communism and set off forces he could not control; the man who ended the Cold War peacefully; named views from section 2.

The revolutions of 1989
- [ ] E1 Poland: the June 1989 elections, Solidarity's sweep of the contested seats, Mazowiecki prime minister (August 1989), Jaruzelski president.
- [ ] E2 Hungary: Kádár removed (1988); the border fence with Austria taken down (from May 1989); Nagy reburied (16 June 1989); round-table talks and multi-party elections agreed; the border opened to East Germans (September 1989); the republic proclaimed (23 October 1989); free elections (1990).
- [ ] E3 East Germany: the exodus via Hungary and the West German embassy in Prague; New Forum; Gorbachev at the GDR's 40th anniversary (October 1989); the Leipzig Monday demonstrations; Honecker resigns (18 October 1989), Krenz.
- [ ] E4 The fall of the Berlin Wall (9 November 1989): Schabowski's confused announcement and the crowds at the crossings.
- [ ] E5 Czechoslovakia: the Velvet Revolution (from 17 November 1989), Civic Forum, Havel president (29 December 1989), Dubček chairman of the parliament.
- [ ] E6 Bulgaria: Zhivkov removed (10 November 1989).
- [ ] E7 Romania: Timișoara and Pastor László Tőkés (December 1989), the Bucharest rally that turned against Ceaușescu, the fighting, the National Salvation Front (Iliescu), the Ceaușescus executed (25 December 1989); the only violent revolution of 1989.
- [ ] E8 Why the revolutions of 1989 happened together and mostly peacefully: Soviet non-intervention, economic failure, the example of each neighbour, television and travel, the loss of the regimes' will to shoot.

The collapse of the Soviet Union
- [ ] U1 Nationalism in the republics: the Baltic Way (August 1989), Tbilisi (April 1989), Nagorno-Karabakh (from 1988); Lithuania's declaration of independence (11 March 1990) and the Vilnius killings (January 1991).
- [ ] U2 Yeltsin's rise: elected President of Russia (June 1991), Russian sovereignty as a rival to Soviet authority.
- [ ] U3 The end of the bloc's institutions: Comecon (June 1991) and the Warsaw Pact (1 July 1991) dissolved.
- [ ] U4 The August coup (19–21 August 1991): the State Committee for the State of Emergency (eight hardliners, including the vice-president, the KGB chief and the defence minister), Gorbachev held in the Crimea, Yeltsin on a tank at the Russian parliament, collapse of the coup; the Communist Party suspended and banned; the republics declare independence; the Baltic states recognised (September 1991).
- [ ] U5 The end of the USSR: the Ukrainian referendum (1 December 1991), the Belovezha agreement creating the CIS (8 December 1991), Gorbachev resigns (25 December 1991), the USSR ceases to exist (26 December 1991), fifteen independent states.

Fragmentation and realignment
- [ ] F1 German reunification: Kohl's Ten-Point Plan (November 1989), the first free East German election (March 1990), currency union (1 July 1990), the Two Plus Four Treaty (September 1990) with united Germany in NATO, unity on 3 October 1990.
- [ ] F2 The break-up of Yugoslavia: after Tito's death (1980), economic crisis and rising nationalism; Milošević's Serbia; Slovenia and Croatia declare independence (25 June 1991); the brief war in Slovenia and the war in Croatia (Vukovar, 1991); EC recognition of Slovenia and Croatia (January 1992); Bosnia's independence and the start of war there (1992).
- [ ] F3 Czechoslovakia's peaceful split, agreed after the 1992 elections and effective 1 January 1993 (one bullet; just past the period).
- [ ] F4 Realignment: the Visegrád group (February 1991); the former satellites and the Baltic states turning to Western institutions (Council of Europe, the EC, NATO), with later membership as one phrase only; the CIS in the east.

Assessment
- [ ] X1 Gorbachev's successes vs failures, as paired points: foreign policy (end of the arms race, Afghanistan, peaceful freeing of Eastern Europe, German unity) vs home (economy worse, nationalism, the coup, loss of the Union he meant to save).
- [ ] X2 Why communism collapsed: long-term (economic failure, lack of legitimacy, nationalism, the arms burden, Western prosperity seen on television) vs short-term (Gorbachev's reforms and non-intervention, opposition movements such as Solidarity and Charter 77, the Church, the domino effect of 1989); weighting of internal vs external (Reagan) causes.
- [ ] X3 How successful Soviet control of Eastern Europe was, 1953–1991: control kept by force in 1953, 1956 and 1968 and by proxy in 1981, but never by consent, and lost within months once Moscow would not use force.

## 3. Traps

Do not use (wrong or doubtful facts in the current notes):
- Do not use: "workers protested against wage increases" (factCard on 17 June 1953), because the protest was against raised work norms, which in effect cut wages. Use the section 2 wording.
- Do not use: "forces from five Warsaw Pact states invaded" (factCard), because the notes themselves say East German units did not cross; and do not use "five" anywhere. Name the four states and say East German troops were kept back apart from a token presence.
- Do not use: "Charter 77 … led by Václav Havel", because it had three first spokesmen (Havel, Patočka, Hájek) and no single leader. Say Havel was one of its spokesmen and its best-known figure.
- Do not use: "the first non-communist government in the Soviet bloc since 1948" for Mazowiecki, because the "1948" is doubtful (Poland's communists dominated government from 1945–47); say "the first non-communist-led government in the Soviet bloc in over forty years".
- Do not use: "in 1988 he became chairman of the Supreme Soviet", because in 1988 he became chairman of the Presidium of the Supreme Soviet (head of state); the chairmanship of the new Supreme Soviet came in 1989. Say "head of state from 1988".
- Do not use: "a Mass attended by hundreds of thousands" or any crowd figure for the 1979 visit, because estimates vary widely; say "huge crowds".
- Do not use: "it spread to hundreds of towns before it was crushed" alongside "suppressed in East Berlin and other towns" as two separate facts; say it once.
- Do not use: "Soviet archives show the Soviet leaders did not plan to send troops in" as settled fact, because historians still debate how to read the December 1981 records and whether the threat was real; present both views.
- Do not use: "he attributed his survival to divine intervention" as a political point; it adds nothing to his political role. Leave it out.
- Do not use: "the eight leaders who staged the coup included … " with a number unless you also name the committee (State Committee for the State of Emergency); name only the three leaders in section 2.
- Do not use: "the war in Bosnia was the most savage conflict in Europe since 1945" with any death toll or with Srebrenica (1995), because those are outside the period; "the bloodiest war in Europe since 1945" with no figure is enough.

Other errors and doubtful claims to avoid:
- Don't give death tolls for 1953, Poznań 1956 or the 1968 invasion: figures vary. Romania's "about 1,100" is the only revolution figure to use.
- Don't say Dubček wanted to leave the Warsaw Pact or end communism: he insisted Czechoslovakia stayed in the Pact and the party kept the leading role. Leaving the Pact was Imre Nagy's step in 1956, which is the key difference.
- Don't say Gorbachev meant to end communism or to free Eastern Europe as a goal: he meant to save and renew socialism; non-intervention was a choice whose consequences he did not foresee.
- Don't say the Berlin Wall was "knocked down" on 9 November 1989: it was opened; demolition came in 1990.
- Don't say Gorbachev was elected by the people: he was chosen by the party (1985) and by the Congress of People's Deputies (1990). Yeltsin was the one with a popular mandate (June 1991).
- Don't say the USSR ended on 25 December 1991 without care: Gorbachev resigned on the 25th; the formal dissolution was the 26th.
- Don't call the 1989 Polish elections fully free: a share of the Sejm seats was reserved for the communists and their allies.
- Don't say Reagan's "tear down this wall" speech or SDI caused the collapse as fact: present it as one view.
- Don't call John Paul II's role the main cause of 1989 as fact: it is a debated judgement.
- Don't invent quotations from Gorbachev (e.g. "Life punishes those who come too late" is a paraphrase of what he said in East Berlin in 1989; do not use it in quotation marks).

## 4. What exams ask

- Gorbachev (Higher, 100 marks, frequent): his contribution, his effectiveness, or his successes and failures. Top marks go to a clear judgement held throughout (e.g. highly effective abroad, much less so at home), with each paragraph pairing a success with its limit: arms control and the INF Treaty vs economic collapse; glasnost freeing speech vs glasnost releasing nationalism; non-intervention freeing Eastern Europe vs losing the Union he meant to save. Give dates for each step (1985, 1986, 1987, 1988, 1989, 1991).
- Gorbachev (Ordinary, 30 and 40 marks, very frequent): a run of plain facts in order: General Secretary March 1985; glasnost and perestroika defined; Chernobyl; elections 1989; INF 1987; Afghanistan 1989; non-intervention and 1989; the August coup; resignation December 1991. Defining glasnost and perestroika correctly earns marks on its own.
- Khrushchev and Gorbachev compared as reformers (Higher): parallel points (both reformed from above, both met resistance, both fell); Gorbachev's side comes from here: unlike Khrushchev he refused to use force and his reforms ended the bloc and the Union.
- Why communism collapsed (Higher): rank the causes, don't just list them. Long-term economic failure and lack of legitimacy, the arms burden, nationalism, then the trigger: Gorbachev's reforms and the Sinatra Doctrine; plus opposition movements (Solidarity, Charter 77) and the Church. Examiners reward weighing internal against external (Reagan) causes and naming historians' views.
- How the Cold War ended (Higher, as the last part of a Cold War question): the summits, INF, the UN speech, Afghanistan, 1989, Malta, German unity in NATO, the end of the Warsaw Pact and the USSR. Link each to the easing of tension.
- How successful Soviet control of Eastern Europe was (Higher, often 1945–1992): this subsection supplies the second half: control kept by force (1953, 1968) and by proxy (1981), so a success in holding power but a failure in winning consent; the speed of 1989 proves the point. A balanced judgement with each crisis dated earns the top band.
- Pope John Paul II (Higher with Gorbachev, Monnet and Delors, or Thatcher; Ordinary 30 and 40 marks): his election, the 1979 visit and its effect on Solidarity, support for Wałęsa, visits of 1983 and 1987, the meeting with Gorbachev. Higher answers need a judgement on how far he caused the fall of communism, set against economic and Soviet factors. Keep his political and religious roles distinct unless the question pairs him with Vatican II.
- Ordinary Level source and short questions: why Hungary's opening of its border mattered (East Germans escaped west); what the 1979 visit did for Solidarity; recognising the Berlin Wall's opening, Solidarity or Gorbachev in a photograph or cartoon. Short, exact answers score.
- Turning points of the Cold War (Higher): Prague 1968 (the Brezhnev Doctrine), Gorbachev's arrival in 1985 and 1989 as turning points; explain why each changed the direction of events, not just what happened.
