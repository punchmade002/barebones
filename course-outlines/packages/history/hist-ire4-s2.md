- [ ] E5 Origin: west and south-west highest; poorest counties highest rate.

Restriction and regulation after 1920
- [ ] R1 Emergency Quota Act 1921: rule.
- [ ] R2 Immigration Act 1924 (Johnson-Reed): rule and purpose; Free State quota 28,567; cut to 17,853 from 1929; Northern Ireland under the British quota.
- [ ] R3 Effect: US emigration fell sharply, Britain became the main destination (the shift to Britain from the 1930s).
- [ ] R4 Why Britain: open entry, jobs, short journey, low fare, family and friends.
- [ ] R5 Great Depression: American demand fell, some returned; Britain still drew Irish in the 1930s.
- [ ] R6 Irish governments did not restrict emigration.
- [ ] R7 Wartime travel permits (the Emergency, 1939–45), British recruitment of Irish workers, Irish in the British forces.
- [ ] R8 Ireland Act 1949 and the Common Travel Area; exemption from the Commonwealth Immigrants Act 1962.
- [ ] R9 US Immigration and Nationality Act 1965 (national-origins quotas abolished from 1968).

Post-war emigration and the 1950s
- [ ] W1 Post-war reconstruction work: men (navvies: roads, building sites, power stations), women (nursing in the NHS, factories, offices).

Welfare, community and assimilation
- [ ] L1 Conditions for young emigrants in Britain: lodgings, navvy camps, subcontractors, no insurance, drink.
- [ ] L2 Prejudice: "No Irish" notices, with the historians' doubt about how common they were.

## 4. What exams ask

- Higher 100-mark essay, very common: the main trends in emigration 1920–1966 (shift from the USA to Britain, 1950s peak, fall in the 1960s). Marks go for the reasons for the shift (US quotas, British demand, proximity), dates and acts, and a clear sequence (1920s, 1930s, war, post-war, 1950s, 1960s).
- Why Britain became the main destination and how far the Irish assimilated there: marks for naming the pull factors (work, fares, open entry, Ireland Act, Common Travel Area) and a balanced judgement (integrated into working life, but a visible Catholic minority with Irish social life and prejudice).
- Challenges and opportunities for the Irish in Britain: work and housing and prejudice against jobs, wages, community; give examples (navvies, nurses, dance halls, chaplaincies).
- How emigration changed lives (those who left and those who stayed): remittances, depopulation, loneliness, better wages, return or not.
- Mac Amhlaigh at Higher Level: his life and work with dates, that he was a participant, and what the diary shows about the 1950s; a judgement of how typical he was. Ordinary Level paragraph (30/40 marks): birthplace, Irish language, 1951, labourer, Northampton, diary dates, translation title. Each correct fact earns a mark.
- Ordinary Level short answers: quota acts and their rules; the Free State quota; why Britain; Commission on Emigration finding (economic causes) and dates; why emigration continued after US restriction.
- Strong answers separate causes at home (economic weakness, small farms) from pulls abroad (jobs, wages, family), and show the effect of each act with a date and a rule.
- Document or source questions: use the diary or a historian's account to identify a source's viewpoint and its limits (single author, one occupation).
