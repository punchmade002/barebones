## 1. Must cover (one bullet or block for every item; tick each off)

Background (assumed)
- [ ] A1 (assumed) The 1916 Rising and its aftermath: de Valera's part, the death sentence commuted; the rise of Sinn Féin.
- [ ] A2 (assumed) The December 1918 general election: Sinn Féin's landslide; the first Dáil (January 1919) declared an Irish Republic.
- [ ] A3 (assumed) Self-determination and the Paris Peace Conference: Wilson's principle, and why Irish republicans appealed to it.
- [ ] A4 (assumed) Irish America's earlier role: Clan na Gael, Devoy and Cohalan, the Friends of Irish Freedom (founded 1916). Detail on Devoy belongs to the notes on nationalism as an international force.
- [ ] A5 (assumed) Why he was in prison: the "German Plot" arrests of May 1918; held at Lincoln.

Why he went
- [ ] W1 Aim: US recognition of the Republic.
- [ ] W2 Aim: raise funds for the Dáil government.
- [ ] W3 Aim: influence the Paris Peace Conference and the 1920 presidential election, and put pressure on Britain.
- [ ] W4 Preceding events: the Irish Race Convention in Philadelphia (February 1919) and its delegation to Paris; the Dáil envoys at Paris not heard; his election as President of the Dáil ministry (April 1919).

Escape and arrival
- [ ] E1 Escape from Lincoln Gaol, 3 February 1919, arranged by Collins and Boland.
- [ ] E2 Secret passage from Liverpool and arrival in the USA in June 1919; Boland had gone ahead.
- [ ] E3 First public appearance in New York (Waldorf-Astoria), late June 1919; the US Senate resolution of 6 June 1919.

The tour
- [ ] T1 Cities with large Irish populations: Boston (Fenway Park rally, 29 June 1919), Chicago, New York; western states; huge crowds, receptions, honours.
- [ ] T2 The Chippewa honorary title and gifts (document-question detail).
- [ ] T3 Reception: enthusiastic among Irish Americans; cautious American government and press; British objections.
- [ ] T4 His message: recognition, support for the Dáil, funds.

The Dáil bond drive
- [ ] B1 Bond certificates for the Republic, launched in the USA in January 1920; target five million dollars; about that sum raised by 1921.
- [ ] B2 How it was organised (Irish-American organisations, parishes, clubs); Collins as Minister for Finance and the National Loan at home.
- [ ] B3 Disputes over control and spending of the money; Carroll's study.

The conflict with Irish-American leaders
- [ ] C1 The Friends of Irish Freedom led by Cohalan and Devoy: who they were.
- [ ] C2 The quarrel: control of funds and of the American movement; Dáil authority vs American independence; Gaelic American and the press.
- [ ] C3 Boland's role on de Valera's side; the split and its effect on Irish America.

The Westminster Gazette interview
- [ ] G1 February 1920: the Cuba analogy as a way to meet Britain's security fears.
- [ ] G2 Reaction: controversy among republicans; Cohalan and Devoy attack; de Valera's defence.

The 1920 conventions and election
- [ ] V1 Republican convention, Chicago, June 1920; Democratic convention, San Francisco, June–July 1920.
- [ ] V2 He sought a plank recognising the Republic; neither convention gave it (Republicans ignored it; Democrats offered sympathy only).
- [ ] V3 Harding's election, November 1920; no recognition; anger at Wilson among Irish Americans.

The AARIR
- [ ] R1 Founded November 1920 as de Valera's own campaigning body; rival to the Friends of Irish Freedom; rapid growth.
- [ ] R2 Related events lifting American sympathy: Terence MacSwiney's death on hunger strike (25 October 1920) and the American Commission on Conditions in Ireland (late 1920).

Events in Ireland while he was away
- [ ] I1 Soloheadbeg (21 January 1919) and the start of the Anglo-Irish War; the Dáil banned (12 September 1919).
- [ ] I2 Collins as Minister for Finance and director of intelligence.
- [ ] I3 Black and Tans (from March 1920) and Auxiliaries (from July 1920); reprisals reported in the American press.
- [ ] I4 Kevin Barry (1 November 1920), Bloody Sunday and Croke Park (21 November 1920), the burning of Cork (December 1920).
- [ ] I5 Government of Ireland Act (December 1920); his return in December 1920.

Outcomes and assessment (paired judgement points, final block)
- [ ] S1 Success: publicity, large audiences, about five million dollars, a rallied Irish America.
- [ ] S2 Failure: no recognition from either party or Wilson or Harding; Irish America divided.
- [ ] S3 Other costs: his long absence from Ireland and strained relations with Collins.
- [ ] S4 Views then: supporters, Irish-American critics, British government, nationalists at home.
- [ ] S5 Views since: propaganda and fundraising success but diplomatic failure; Carroll on the money.
- [ ] S6 Aims achieved vs not achieved, in the form examiners ask.

## 3. Traps

Do not use (wrong or doubtful facts in the current notes):
- Do not use: "gave an interview to the Westminster Gazette in London", because he was in the USA; say the interview appeared in the Westminster Gazette.
- Do not use: "left Ireland secretly in May 1919", because the route was by ship from Liverpool; give only the Liverpool stowaway passage and June arrival.
- Do not use: the AARIR "replaced the Friends of Irish Freedom", because the Friends continued; say it became a rival body for de Valera's supporters.
- Do not use: any exact membership figure for the AARIR, or any exact crowd size for Boston, because sources vary.
- Do not use: "Many Irish Americans voted Republican" with any percentage; say only that Irish-American anger at the Wilson administration hurt the Democrats.
- Do not use: that the bond drive raised exactly five million dollars; say "about".
- Do not use: that Wilson and Harding "would not offend Britain" as certain; say they did not recognise the Republic and neither party backed it.
- Do not use: that the American Commission on Conditions in Ireland was run by the AARIR; it was a separate body.
- Do not use: a date for his departure from the USA beyond "December 1920".

Confusable terms and dates
- Friends of Irish Freedom (1916, Devoy and Cohalan) vs AARIR (November 1920, de Valera).
- Bond drive in the USA (launched January 1920) vs the National Loan in Ireland (Collins).
- Anglo-Irish War began January 1919 (Soloheadbeg); Bloody Sunday is 21 November 1920, the day of the Croke Park shooting, not the 1972 event.
- Republican convention (Chicago) vs Democratic convention (San Francisco).
- "Success or failure" depends on the aim: money and publicity won, recognition lost.

## 4. What exams ask

- Higher 100-mark essay, the commonest form: how successful was de Valera's mission, how important or controversial, were his aims achieved. Marks go to a stated judgement, supported by paired points: money and publicity vs no recognition; large crowds vs a split Irish America.
- Aims are asked directly: list recognition, funds, influence on Paris and the election, pressure on Britain. A clear list of three or four aims earns easy marks.
- Strong answers judge each aim in turn (recognition not achieved; funds achieved; election influence not achieved; publicity achieved) and give dates (June 1919, January 1920, February 1920, June 1920, November 1920).
- Controversy questions need the Devoy/Cohalan quarrel, the money disputes and the Cuba interview, each with a short explanation of why it was controversial.
- Ordinary Level 40-mark questions ask for aims, the tour with Fenway Park, the bond drive and its outcome; each correct dated detail earns marks.
- Document questions use extracts about his reception (a hotel, crowds, a parade, honours such as the Chippewa title), the bond drive, and the founding of the AARIR; answers need only what the document shows plus the one-line context (Dáil bonds, Republic).
- Extracts may also cover the Lincoln escape and Collins's part in it.
- A combined question can set him beside Devoy and Mannix as a way nationalism became international: name what each did abroad.
