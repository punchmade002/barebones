## 1. Must cover (one bullet or block for every item; tick each off)

Background (assumed)
- [ ] A1 (assumed) The Free State (from December 1922) was a new state with a small, mainly agricultural economy; British rule had left the civil service, taxation and banking system in place.
- [ ] A2 (assumed) Partition (1920–22) had separated the industrial North-East from the rest of Ireland (detail belongs to the partition notes).
- [ ] A3 (assumed) The two main parties: Cumann na nGaedheal (government 1922–32, W.T. Cosgrave) and Fianna Fáil (government from 1932, de Valera).
- [ ] A4 (assumed) The land annuities: repayments by farmers who had bought their holdings under the Land Acts (the dispute itself is covered in the Anglo-Irish relations notes).

Key concepts (each defined as used in this topic; also in keyTerms)
- [ ] K1 Free trade: low or no tariffs; suited an economy selling farm produce to Britain.
- [ ] K2 Protectionism: tariffs, quotas and licences to shield home industry from imports; aimed at self-sufficiency and jobs.
- [ ] K3 Semi-state body: state-owned company run commercially (ESB, Irish Sugar Company, Aer Lingus).
- [ ] K4 Land annuities and Economic War (defined briefly; essential terms for the cattle farmers block).

The Free State's economic inheritance and the impact of partition
- [ ] E1 Small, mainly agricultural economy; agriculture the main employer; exports overwhelmingly to Britain (cattle, butter, bacon, eggs).
- [ ] E2 Partition left most heavy industry (Belfast shipyards, linen, engineering) in Northern Ireland; the Free State had little industry beyond Dublin and Cork food, drink and some other firms.
- [ ] E3 The border as a customs line from 1923; disruption of trade and smuggling.
- [ ] E4 Currency Act 1927: the Saorstát pound at parity with sterling; monetary policy followed Britain.
- [ ] E5 Small population and home market; limits on industrial growth.
- [ ] E6 Inherited institutions; the cautious Department of Finance.

Cumann na nGaedheal: free trade and agriculture first, 1922–1932
- [ ] C1 Policy: farm exports of quality produce, low taxes, balanced budgets, free trade.
- [ ] C2 Patrick Hogan, Minister for Agriculture 1922–32: quality control of beef, butter and bacon.
- [ ] C3 The 1924–25 farm Acts and the Agricultural Credit Corporation (1927).
- [ ] C4 Ernest Blythe, Minister for Finance from 1923: low spending; the 1924 pension cut (10s to 9s); public-sector pay cuts.
- [ ] C5 The Tariff Commission (1926) and its limited recommendations.
- [ ] C6 The Shannon Scheme and the ESB (1927) as the main exception to low state spending.
- [ ] C7 Criticism: the policy favoured large farmers; little for labourers, small farmers, the cities.

Key personality: James J. McElligott (one block, every point)
- [ ] M1 Who he was: a civil servant, not a politician; Secretary of the Department of Finance for a quarter of a century.
- [ ] M2 Career with dates: joined Finance 1923; Secretary 1927–53; Governor of the Central Bank 1953–60; life dates 1893–1974.
- [ ] M3 What he did: advised Blythe, then MacEntee, on budgets, spending and taxation; controlled other departments' spending.
- [ ] M4 Outlook: financial orthodoxy (balanced budgets, low taxes, limited borrowing).
- [ ] M5 Shaped by events or shaping them: Finance resisted costly schemes, including aspects of Lemass's industrial policy, through the 1930s.
- [ ] M6 Contemporaries: other departments (especially Industry and Commerce) saw Finance as a brake on development.
- [ ] M7 Historians: Ronan Fanning's study of Finance (1978) argues its caution shaped policy for decades and held back growth until the late 1950s.

The world economic crisis, 1929–1933
- [ ] W1 The Wall Street Crash (October 1929) and the world slump; falling farm prices.
- [ ] W2 Effects: lower farm incomes; emigration routes (especially to the USA) closed; unemployment in towns.
- [ ] W3 Britain left the gold standard (September 1931); the Free State stayed tied to sterling; Britain turned to protection in 1931–32.
- [ ] W4 Cumann na nGaedheal's response: spending cuts and few new tariffs.
- [ ] W5 Link to the 1932 election: Fianna Fáil's promise of protection and self-sufficiency.

From free trade to protectionism, 1932–1939
- [ ] P1 Fianna Fáil's aims: self-sufficiency, industrial jobs; Seán Lemass, Minister for Industry and Commerce from 1932.
- [ ] P2 Tariffs and quotas on a wide range of imports.
- [ ] P3 The Control of Manufactures Acts 1932 and 1934 (Irish majority ownership).
- [ ] P4 The Industrial Credit Company (1933).
- [ ] P5 Semi-state bodies: Irish Sugar Company (1933), Turf Development Board (1934), Aer Lingus (1936).
- [ ] P6 Tillage: wheat and beet encouraged by subsidy.
- [ ] P7 Results by 1939: more industrial jobs but small, home-market firms; higher prices; few exports; supporters v critics.

The Economic War and cattle farmers, 1932–1938
- [ ] W6 Link only: the annuities dispute led to British special duties on Irish cattle, beef and butter from 1932 (causes belong to the Anglo-Irish relations notes).
- [ ] W7 Effect on cattle farmers and cattle exports; falling incomes.
- [ ] W8 Government response: export bounties, wheat and beet subsidies, higher taxes.
- [ ] W9 The Coal–Cattle Pact (1935).
- [ ] W10 The 1938 agreement ended the dispute; exports did not return to earlier levels before the war.

Housing, social policy and emigration
- [ ] S1 Housing Acts 1932 and 1936; slum clearance and local authority building.
- [ ] S2 Unemployment Assistance Act 1933; Widows' and Orphans' Pensions Act 1935.
- [ ] S3 Persistent poverty: Dublin tenements, tuberculosis.
- [ ] S4 Emigration: low in the early 1930s (Depression), higher again from the late 1930s and in wartime; remittances.
- [ ] S5 Marriage bar (key term): married women barred from certain public posts; mention only with the approved wording in section 2.

Assessment (paired judgement points, final block)
- [ ] J1 Cumann na nGaedheal's achievements: stable finances, sound currency, efficient farm exports, Shannon Scheme.
- [ ] J2 Cumann na nGaedheal's limits: dependence on Britain; little help for the poor in the Depression.
- [ ] J3 Fianna Fáil's achievements: new industries and jobs, semi-state companies, more housing and welfare.
- [ ] J4 Fianna Fáil's limits: the Economic War, a small protected market, continued emigration, low living standards.
- [ ] J5 Why success was hard: small home market, few resources, dependence on Britain, the Depression, then war.
- [ ] J6 Contemporary views (protection necessary for independence v harm to consumers and farmers) and later views (Daly, Lee; Finance's caution).

## 3. Traps

Do not use (wrong or doubtful facts in the current notes):
- Do not use: the ESB as a semi-state body created by Fianna Fáil in the 1930s, because the ESB was set up in 1927 under Cumann na nGaedheal, with the Shannon Scheme. The Irish Sugar Company, Turf Development Board and Aer Lingus are the 1930s examples.
- Do not use: "the wheat acreage rose roughly tenfold", because the figure is not checked. Say wheat and beet growing rose sharply.
- Do not use: "industrial employment grew by tens of thousands", because no checked figure. Say it rose.
- Do not use: "In 1932 Britain introduced general import duties and imperial preference, which hurt Irish exports", because Irish goods were at first exempt from the general duties and the main blow was the special Economic War duties. Say Britain moved away from free trade in 1931–32.
- Do not use: "farmers in Munster and Leinster were hit more than small western farmers", because small farmers selling young cattle were also hit; it oversimplifies. Say cattle farmers across the country suffered.
- Do not use: the marriage bar dated "1932" for teachers or "from the 1920s" for the civil service, because the exact years are uncertain.
- Do not use: "local authorities built tens of thousands of houses" as a figure; say "many".
- Do not use: the Agricultural Credit Corporation and co-operative creameries as a repeated point (the current notes list it twice); give it once.
- Do not use: "the Free State kept its currency tied to sterling" as a Fianna Fáil achievement; the link continued under both parties.

## 4. What exams ask

- Higher 100-mark essay, set often (the chapter's economy-and-society question): how effectively did Irish governments tackle economic and social problems from 1922 to 1939 or 1945, or why was economic success hard to achieve. Marks go to a stated judgement, a balanced comparison of Cumann na nGaedheal (free trade, stability, Shannon Scheme, little for the poor) with Fianna Fáil (protection, semi-state bodies, housing and welfare, Economic War costs), and dated policies, not narrative.
- Strong answers explain why the Depression and the Economic War made things harder (dependence on the British market), and separate the achievements (jobs, stability, power scheme) from the limits (emigration, poverty, a small protected market).
- Compare de Valera's handling of the economy with his handling of Anglo-Irish relations: students should say he achieved more in constitutional aims than in economic ones, using the Economic War and protection results.
- McElligott as a 30-mark Ordinary Level profile or a short Higher answer: his post, the dates (joined 1923, Secretary 1927–53, Central Bank Governor 1953–60), who he advised, his financial orthodoxy, and what that meant in practice (low spending, opposition to costly schemes). Each correct dated statement earns marks; naming a judgement on his influence adds weight.
- Short Ordinary Level questions: who Hogan, Blythe or Lemass was, what the 1924 budget did, what the Tariff Commission was, what free trade and protection mean, the Shannon Scheme, and why the Economic War hurt cattle farmers.
- Policy-change question: why Fianna Fáil moved from free trade to protection (the Depression, Britain's change, self-sufficiency, jobs, the 1932 election) and what it achieved by 1939.
- Social-policy points that earn credit: Housing Acts, Unemployment Assistance (1933), Widows' and Orphans' Pensions (1935), with the limit that poverty and emigration continued.
