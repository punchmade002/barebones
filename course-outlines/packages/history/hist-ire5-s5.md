## 1. Must cover (one bullet or block for every item; tick each off)

Background (assumed)
- [ ] A1 (assumed) Stormont was a devolved parliament dominated by the Unionist Party since 1921; nationalists felt excluded (discrimination, gerrymandering).
- [ ] A2 (assumed) Violence escalated from 1969; internment (August 1971) and Bloody Sunday (January 1972) led to the suspension of Stormont and Direct Rule from London (March 1972).
- [ ] A3 (assumed) The SDLP and the Alliance Party were founded in 1970; unionism was split between supporters and opponents of reform and power sharing.
- [ ] A4 (assumed) Direct Rule meant a Secretary of State for Northern Ireland answerable to Westminster; the British aim was a devolved government acceptable to both communities.

Key concept
- [ ] K1 Power sharing: unionists and nationalists both holding executive office, so neither community can rule alone. Define it as used here; also in keyTerms.
- [ ] K2 Other essential terms for keyTerms: Council of Ireland, single transferable vote, border poll, executive, UUUC, Ulster Workers' Council strike, Sunningdale Agreement, Boland case.

Background and the 1973 settlement plan
- [ ] B1 The border poll (8 March 1973): the question, the nationalist boycott, the result and turnout, what it did and did not settle.
- [ ] B2 The White Paper (March 1973): an Assembly elected by proportional representation, a power-sharing executive, and an Irish dimension (a Council of Ireland).
- [ ] B3 The Northern Ireland Constitution Act (July 1973): abolished the old Stormont parliament, provided for the Assembly, Secretary of State kept security.
- [ ] B4 The Assembly election (28 June 1973): single transferable vote; unionist split (Faulkner's supporters vs anti-agreement unionists, including Paisley's DUP and Craig's Vanguard); SDLP and Alliance results; 78 seats.
- [ ] B5 Forming the executive (agreed November 1973, in office 1 January 1974): Faulkner Chief Executive, Fitt Deputy Chief Executive, Hume, Napier, Currie, Devlin; its make-up (unionists, SDLP, Alliance).

The Sunningdale conference, December 1973
- [ ] S1 Place, dates, participants (governments and parties).
- [ ] S2 The Council of Ireland: council of ministers and consultative assembly, how composed.
- [ ] S3 Status of Northern Ireland: consent guarantee from both governments.
- [ ] S4 Policing and security: the Law Enforcement Commission, acceptable policing.
- [ ] S5 Why Sunningdale was controversial for unionists (the Irish dimension, Articles 2 and 3 not changed).
- [ ] S6 The Boland case: who, what, and why it damaged Faulkner.

Opposition and collapse
- [ ] O1 The Ulster Unionist Council vote (4 January 1974), Faulkner's resignation as party leader, Harry West; later the Unionist Party of Northern Ireland.
- [ ] O2 The UUUC (formed January 1974): who was in it, Paisley's role.
- [ ] O3 The February 1974 Westminster election: UUUC result, Fitt; change of government to Labour (Wilson, Rees).
- [ ] O4 The Assembly vote of 14 May 1974 supporting Sunningdale policy (44 to 28) and the start of the strike next day.
- [ ] O5 The Ulster Workers' Council strike (15–28 May 1974): leaders, support from loyalist paramilitaries, methods (power cuts, roadblocks, intimidation), effect.
- [ ] O6 The British response: Rees and the army did not confront the strikers; Wilson's "spongers" broadcast (25 May) and its effect on unionists.
- [ ] O7 Faulkner's attempt to delay the Council of Ireland, its rejection by SDLP and Alliance, and his resignation on 28 May 1974; the executive collapsed.
- [ ] O8 Executive's work (housing, health, education) overshadowed by the Council of Ireland dispute; the executive lasted about five months.

Why it failed, legacy and assessment
- [ ] W1 Causes of failure, each as a separate point: unionist opposition to the Council of Ireland; split in the Unionist Party and no majority unionist support; the UWC strike; British reluctance to confront the strikers; the Boland case; violence continuing; Labour government's weaker commitment.
- [ ] L1 Legacy to 1993: Constitutional Convention (1975–76), return to Direct Rule, Anglo-Irish Agreement (1985) and Downing Street Declaration (1993) as later attempts (one line each; detail belongs elsewhere); Mallon's remark linking 1998 to Sunningdale.
- [ ] L2 Assessment block, paired points: achievements (first power-sharing executive, consent and Irish dimension principles) vs failures (five months, no majority unionist consent); contemporary views (governments and SDLP vs UUUC and UWC) vs later views of historians.

Key personality: Brian Faulkner (part 2 and overall evaluation; one block, every point)
- [ ] F1 Who he was (brief): Unionist politician; his earlier career and premiership (1971–72) belong to the previous notes; here only a one-line recap.
- [ ] F2 What he did, as a direct participant: Chief Executive, the main unionist negotiator at Sunningdale, defended the agreement to his party.
- [ ] F3 Shaped by events: split his party, lost party leadership, resigned as Chief Executive; never returned to office.
- [ ] F4 Contemporaries: loyalists saw a traitor; nationalists and British government saw a leader who took a risk for peace.
- [ ] F5 Historians: credit for courage and realism; criticism that he misjudged unionist opinion and lacked sustained British support.
- [ ] F6 Later life: Unionist Party of Northern Ireland; memoirs (1978); peerage and death in 1977.

Key personality: John Hume (part 2; one block, every point)
- [ ] H1 Who he was (brief recap): Derry-born SDLP founder; his University for Derry campaign belongs to the Coleraine notes.
- [ ] H2 What he did: SDLP negotiator at Sunningdale; Minister of Commerce in the executive.
- [ ] H3 His ideas: power sharing and an Irish dimension; the Council of Ireland as a stake for nationalists.
- [ ] H4 Shaped by events: defended the executive in the strike; the collapse confirmed his belief in an agreed, cross-community settlement.
- [ ] H5 Contemporaries and historians: seen as a capable nationalist negotiator, attacked by republicans; Mallon's "Sunningdale for slow learners" and the link between 1973 and 1998.
- [ ] H6 Later: SDLP leader from 1979 (one line; detail in later notes).

## 3. Traps

Do not use (wrong or doubtful facts in the current notes):
- Do not use: "the Assembly began to operate in the same month" as the executive taking office (January 1974), because the Assembly first met in July 1973.
- Do not use: "anti-agreement unionists took about as many seats as Faulkner's supporters", because the unionist totals are disputed (depending on whether "unpledged" unionists are counted); give no seat totals.
- Do not use: "The Conservative government of Edward Heath lost the February 1974 election", because no party won a majority; say Wilson formed a Labour government in March.
- Do not use: Faulkner resigned "after the other unionist members of the executive were unwilling to negotiate with the strikers", because it contradicts the better-attested reason (rejection of his delay proposal by SDLP and Alliance); use the delay proposal.
- Do not use: the Irish government "did not formally change Articles 2 and 3" as a claim that it ever promised to; say only that they were unchanged.
- Do not use: Oliver Napier's exact ministerial title; say "in charge of legal affairs".
- Do not use: the Boland case's judgments or dates beyond early 1974.
- Do not use: "the Belfast Agreement" as part of the 1973–74 story; it is a pointer only.

- Border poll (March 1973) vs Assembly election (June 1973) vs Westminster election (February 1974).
- Sunningdale (1973) vs Anglo-Irish Agreement (1985) vs Belfast Agreement (1998): different settlements.

## 4. What exams ask

- Higher 100-mark essay: what was the Sunningdale Agreement and why did it collapse, or why was it important. Marks go to a clear thesis, the terms (executive, Council of Ireland, consent guarantee, policing) and a ranked set of causes of collapse: unionist rejection of the Council of Ireland, the UWC strike, British weakness. Narrative without ranking scores poorly.
- Importance or contribution essays compare Sunningdale with the Anglo-Irish Agreement or Downing Street Declaration. Students need to link Sunningdale's principles (consent, an Irish dimension) to those later documents.
- Faulkner evaluation: strengths (courage, realism) vs weaknesses (misjudged unionist opinion, party split), with dated points (4 January, 7 January, 28 May 1974). Contemporary vs later views earns credit.
- Hume: his role at Sunningdale and in the executive, his ideas, and his later career; short Ordinary Level paragraph questions reward four or five dated facts.
- Document questions (2010 and 2023 style): a memoir by a member of the executive or Faulkner describes the strike and roadblocks and feelings of betrayal by London. Marks go for placing the document (strike, May 1974), reliability (first-hand, written within a few years, but biased) and cross-referencing with the second document.
- Ordinary Level paragraph (30 or 40 marks): date and place of the conference, who attended, terms of the agreement, why unionists rejected it (Council of Ireland, 427 to 374 vote), how the strike ended the executive. Each correct, dated statement earns a mark.
- Short answers: dates of the executive (1 January to 28 May 1974), who was Chief Executive, and what caused the collapse (the strike).
