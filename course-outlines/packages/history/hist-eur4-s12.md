## 1. Must cover (one bullet or block for every item; tick each off)

Background (assumed)
- [ ] A1 (assumed) Post-war recovery and the "economic miracle": rising wages, full employment and consumer spending in Western Europe in the 1950s and 1960s.
- [ ] A2 (assumed) The Cold War division of Europe into West and East, which shaped what young people could hear, wear and watch.
- [ ] A3 (assumed) The USA's cultural influence on Europe after 1945 (films, music, jeans, consumer goods).
- [ ] A4 (assumed) The baby boom after 1945 and the expansion of secondary and university education.

Key concepts (also in keyTerms)
- [ ] K1 Teenager: a young person treated after the 1940s as a distinct group with its own spending power and culture.
- [ ] K2 Pop star: a performer of popular music made famous to a mass audience by records, television and press coverage.
- [ ] K3 Mass media: television, radio, cinema and the press, reaching large audiences.
- [ ] K4 Counterculture: a culture rejecting the dominant values of society (1960s).
- [ ] K5 Subculture: a youth group with its own style, music and values.
- [ ] K6 Public broadcasting vs commercial broadcasting: licence-fee/state funding with public-service duties versus advertising funding.
- [ ] K7 Beatlemania and Eurovision (essential terms).

The teenager and youth culture
- [ ] T1 The word "teenager" and its spread from the USA; why young people became a market (wages, spending money, longer schooling).
- [ ] T2 Youth media: records (the cheap 45 rpm single), radio (Radio Luxembourg), magazines, dances, cinema.
- [ ] T3 Rock 'n' roll and American influence: Bill Haley, Elvis Presley, films about rebellious youth; adult fears about delinquency.
- [ ] T4 Live Aid (1985) as a late example of youth culture used for a cause.

Key personality: John Lennon (one block, every point)
- [ ] L1 Who he was: born Liverpool, 9 October 1940; the Quarrymen (1957) becoming the Beatles with McCartney, Harrison and Starr.
- [ ] L2 Rise: Hamburg and the Cavern Club; Brian Epstein and George Martin; first number ones in 1963.
- [ ] L3 Beatlemania, the Ed Sullivan Show (9 February 1964) and the "British invasion" of the USA.
- [ ] L4 Songwriting with McCartney; Rubber Soul (1965), Sgt. Pepper (1967): pop as art.
- [ ] L5 All You Need Is Love on the Our World satellite broadcast (June 1967).
- [ ] L6 Controversy: the 1966 remark about Jesus and the US backlash.
- [ ] L7 Peace campaign: Amsterdam bed-in (March 1969), Give Peace a Chance, returning his MBE (November 1969), the War Is Over billboards (December 1969).
- [ ] L8 Break-up of the Beatles (1970); solo career: Imagine (1971); US deportation attempt (1972) and green card (1976).
- [ ] L9 Househusband years from 1975; Double Fantasy (1980); shot dead in New York, 8 December 1980.
- [ ] L10 Contemporaries' views: idol, rebel, danger to morals, hero of the peace movement.
- [ ] L11 Historians' views: first global pop stars, counterculture symbol; his radicalism contradictory and commercially successful.

Fashion and subcultures
- [ ] F1 Teddy boys (1950s) and the delinquency scare.
- [ ] F2 Mods and Rockers (1960s) and the seaside clashes of 1964; the press and "moral panic".
- [ ] F3 Fashion of the 1960s: Mary Quant, the miniskirt, Carnaby Street; hippies.
- [ ] F4 Jeans as a symbol of America and of freedom, including in the East.
- [ ] F5 Punk (1976–77): the Sex Pistols, The Clash, unemployment and anger.
- [ ] F6 Skinheads and the new romantics (briefly).

1968 and student protest
- [ ] P1 Causes: university overcrowding and rules, the Vietnam War, hostility to authority and consumer society.
- [ ] P2 Paris: Nanterre (March), the Sorbonne, the Night of the Barricades (10–11 May), the general strike.
- [ ] P3 De Gaulle's response: leaving France briefly, the June election, his resignation in 1969.
- [ ] P4 Elsewhere: West Germany (Dutschke, Vietnam, the Nazi past), Czechoslovakia, Poland, Italy.
- [ ] P5 Results: changed attitudes to authority, sexuality and universities; no government overthrown; influence on the women's, environmental and peace movements.

Sport
- [ ] S1 The European Cup (from 1955–56); Real Madrid's first five wins.
- [ ] S2 The World Cup: West Germany 1954 (Miracle of Bern); England 1966 and BBC coverage.
- [ ] S3 Television, money and star players: broadcasting rights, sponsorship, advertising; celebrity footballers.
- [ ] S4 The Olympics as Cold War contest: the Soviet entry (Helsinki 1952), the medal table, East German success and doping.
- [ ] S5 Melbourne 1956 water polo match after the invasion of Hungary.
- [ ] S6 Munich 1972 attack.
- [ ] S7 The boycotts of 1980 (Moscow) and 1984 (Los Angeles).

Mass media
- [ ] M1 The spread of television in the 1950s–70s; the Coronation of 1953 as a spur in Britain.
- [ ] M2 Public service broadcasting: BBC, RAI, ARD, ZDF; licence fees; duty to inform, educate and entertain.
- [ ] M3 Commercial broadcasting: ITV (September 1955), Radio Luxembourg, pirate radio (Radio Caroline, 1964).
- [ ] M4 Eurovision: the cross-border network and the Song Contest from 1956.
- [ ] M5 Television's effect on politics, advertising, news and sport.
- [ ] M6 Press: mass-circulation tabloids (Bild, Daily Mirror).
- [ ] M7 State control: governments in Italy and France; party control and propaganda in the East, though West German broadcasts were received in East Germany.

Youth culture in the East
- [ ] E1 Western music reached young people via Radio Luxembourg, Radio Free Europe and copied tapes; authorities tried to control rock.
- [ ] E2 Czechoslovakia: the Plastic People of the Universe trial (1976) and Charter 77.
- [ ] E3 East Germany: Free German Youth; the 1965 ban on beat music, later limited tolerance.
- [ ] E4 Soviet Union: Komsomol; stilyagi.

Assessment (final block, paired points)
- [ ] J1 Achievements: wider tastes, challenge to the authority of school, church and parents, a political voice, shared European culture.
- [ ] J2 Limits: commercial packaging by record companies and advertisers; effects differed by class, country and gender; the East's controlled version.
- [ ] J3 Contemporary views (moral danger) vs later historians (a cultural turning point, but uneven).

## 3. Traps

Do not use (wrong or doubtful facts in the current notes):
- Do not use: "From Me to You, April 1963" as the date of the first UK number one, because it was released in April and reached number one in May 1963, and different charts differ; give 1963 only. Do not use "Please Please Me reaching the top of some charts in early 1963" as a separate claim; leave it out.
- Do not use: Mods and Rockers clashing at Brighton, Margate and Clacton "at Easter 1964", because only Clacton was at Easter; Margate and Brighton followed at Whitsun (May 1964).
- Do not use: "The European Broadcasting Union's Eurovision network, founded in 1954", because the EBU was founded in 1950; the Eurovision network began transmitting in 1954. Say the EBU ran Eurovision.
- Do not use: a figure of "ten million" strikers in France 1968 as exact; estimates vary. Say "millions".
- Do not use: "Pelé, Johan Cruyff and George Best" as European television-era examples without noting Pelé is Brazilian.
- Do not use: Mary Quant "popularised the miniskirt in 1960s" as an exact invention claim; say she helped make it famous.
- Do not use: the Beatles "broke up after the death of Epstein"; say disputes over business and music contributed (Epstein's death in 1967 weakened them).
- Do not use: a global audience figure for Live Aid or for Our World; none is checked.

Doubtful quotations: do not use any song lyric or any quotation of Lennon's remark about Jesus in quotation marks; give it as reported speech.

## 4. What exams ask

- Higher 100-mark essay, the commonest form: assess the impact of youth and popular culture, sport and the mass media on European society, 1945–1992. Marks go to a clear thesis, balance of achievements and limits, and specific dated evidence (the teenager, rock 'n' roll, the Beatles, 1968, television). Strong answers separate West from East and compare liberation with commercial manipulation.
- Essays often pair youth culture with another social change (religion) or ask how far youth became a "distinct group": define the teenager, then explain spending, schooling and media as causes.
- Questions on television/media: cause (technology, rising incomes), effect (advertising, politics, sport), and the public vs commercial contrast with named examples (BBC, ITV, Radio Luxembourg).
- Sport questions reward Cold War links (the Olympics, boycotts, East German success, Munich) and television/money, not a list of results.
- Ordinary Level Lennon question (30 or 40 marks, repeated almost every year): birth date and city, Quarrymen/Beatles, Hamburg/Cavern, Ed Sullivan Show, songwriting with McCartney, peace campaign (bed-in), Imagine, death in 1980, why he was a symbol of youth culture. Each correct dated statement earns a mark; give four to six short, dated points, not a life story.
- Lennon on the Higher paper: appears as one of several personalities; credit for impact (global pop star, peace message), controversy and historians' views.
- 1968 appears as an example of youth political protest: causes, Paris events, de Gaulle, outcome, and a judgement that attitudes changed more than governments.
- Marks are lost for unsupported generalisation ("youth rebelled") with no named group, date or event, and for treating all of Europe alike.
