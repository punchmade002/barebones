## 1. Must cover (one bullet or block for every item; tick each off)

Background (assumed)
- [ ] A1 (assumed) The wartime boom ended the Depression; Americans had high wartime savings and pent-up demand in 1945 (detail belongs to the notes on the postwar boom).
- [ ] A2 (assumed) The Cold War setting: Americans were told that abundance showed the superiority of capitalism over communism.
- [ ] A3 (assumed) The GI Bill (1944) and federal housing agencies helped veterans buy homes (the Bill itself is covered in the notes on the postwar boom).

Key concepts (defined as used in this topic; also in keyTerms)
- [ ] K1 Consumerism: a culture in which buying goods, encouraged by advertising and credit, is central to life and identity.
- [ ] K2 Feminism: the movement for equal rights for women, revived in the 1960s (second wave).
- [ ] K3 American Dream: the belief that hard work brings a home, prosperity and upward mobility.
- [ ] K4 Suburbia, Sun Belt, baby boom, white flight (essential terms).

Demographic growth
- [ ] D1 The baby boom: about 1946 to 1964, peak in 1957; causes (end of war, prosperity, early marriage).
- [ ] D2 Larger families, earlier marriage; Dr Spock's childcare book (1946).
- [ ] D3 Move to the suburbs, and its effect on older city centres.
- [ ] D4 The Sun Belt: rapid growth of the South and Southwest; air conditioning and defence spending.
- [ ] D5 California overtakes New York as the most populous state (early 1960s).
- [ ] D6 The Great Migration of black Americans continues, while white families leave cities (white flight).

Suburbia and housing
- [ ] S1 Levittown: William Levitt, Long Island, begun 1947, mass-production building methods, about 17,000 houses.
- [ ] S2 FHA and VA mortgage guarantees: low deposits, long terms.
- [ ] S3 Race: Levittown and many estates barred black buyers; covenants and lending practice.
- [ ] S4 Car-based suburbs: highways, shopping centres.
- [ ] S5 The Kitchen Debate (July 1959): the suburban home as the proof of capitalism.

Consumerism, credit, advertising, television
- [ ] C1 Credit: Diners Club (1950), BankAmericard (1958), instalment buying.
- [ ] C2 Consumer goods: cars, appliances, supermarkets, malls.
- [ ] C3 Television: growth of ownership 1950 to 1960; advertising and sponsorship.
- [ ] C4 Advertising and market research; Vance Packard, The Hidden Persuaders (1957).
- [ ] C5 Critics of affluence: John Kenneth Galbraith, The Affluent Society (1958).
- [ ] C6 Leisure industries: Disneyland (1955), motels and the car holiday.

Leisure and the role of work
- [ ] L1 The forty-hour week; paid holidays; leisure centred on the home, television and the car.
- [ ] L2 Growth of white-collar jobs relative to manufacturing.
- [ ] L3 Corporate culture and conformity: William H. Whyte, The Organization Man (1956).
- [ ] L4 The male breadwinner ideal in suburban culture.

Women, work and the family
- [ ] W1 The post-war return to the home: wartime workers pushed out, the domestic ideal promoted by media.
- [ ] W2 Rising employment, especially of married women; share of the labour force about 30 per cent (1950) to about 38 per cent (1970); jobs concentrated in low-paid fields.
- [ ] W3 The pill (Enovid, 1960) and Griswold v. Connecticut (1965).
- [ ] W4 Equal Pay Act (1963); Title VII of the Civil Rights Act (1964); Title IX (1972); Equal Credit Opportunity Act (1974).
- [ ] W5 Roe v. Wade (22 January 1973).
- [ ] W6 The ERA: passed Congress March 1972, failed to be ratified by 1982; Phyllis Schlafly's STOP ERA and conservative opposition.
- [ ] W7 The changing family: rising divorce, more single-parent families, from the 1960s.

Key personality: Betty Friedan (one block, every point)
- [ ] F1 Who she was: born Peoria, Illinois, 1921; Smith College psychology; magazine writer and suburban housewife and mother.
- [ ] F2 What she did: the survey of Smith classmates; The Feminine Mystique (1963) and "the problem that has no name".
- [ ] F3 What she did next: founder and first president of NOW (June 1966); Women's Strike for Equality (26 August 1970); National Women's Political Caucus (1971); backing the ERA.
- [ ] F4 Participant: she was both a witness to and a victim of the domestic ideal she attacked.
- [ ] F5 Contemporaries: admired by supporters; attacked by conservatives; younger radicals found her too moderate.
- [ ] F6 Historians: a central figure who helped start the second wave; criticised for focusing on white middle-class women and for hostility to lesbians in NOW.

Key personality: Marilyn Monroe as an image of women (one block, only this aspect)
- [ ] M1 Who she was: born Norma Jeane Mortenson in Los Angeles, 1926; model turned film star.
- [ ] M2 Her screen image: films of 1953 and 1955; glamorous, sexually attractive and childlike blonde.
- [ ] M3 Link to consumer culture: image used in advertising and magazines.
- [ ] M4 Shaped by events: Hollywood marketed and typecast her; she resisted (Marilyn Monroe Productions, Actors Studio, 1955).
- [ ] M5 Contemporaries: admired as star; criticised as sex symbol rather than serious actress.
- [ ] M6 Historians and feminist critics: a symbol of both glamour and the constraints on women.
- [ ] M7 Pointer only: her later career and death (1962) are in the notes on culture and the collapse of consensus.

Assessment (paired judgement points, final block)
- [ ] AS1 Achievement: home ownership, rising incomes, consumer goods for millions of white middle-class families.
- [ ] AS2 Limit: exclusion of black Americans from suburbs; poverty left out (pointer to the notes on troubled affluence).
- [ ] AS3 Women: more jobs and new legal rights, but low pay, narrow job range, and Friedan's evidence of dissatisfaction.
- [ ] AS4 Critics: Galbraith, Packard and Whyte questioned the quality of affluent life.
- [ ] AS5 Overall: the Dream was real for many but unevenly shared by race, class and sex; by the 1970s the family and women's roles had changed.

## 3. Traps

Do not use (wrong or doubtful facts in the current notes):
- Do not use: "by 1960 more Americans lived in suburbs than in the central cities or on farms", because the 1960 census still had the central cities and suburbs roughly level; suburbs overtook cities only in the 1970 census. Say the suburbs were the fastest-growing area.
- Do not use: "between 1950 and 1970 the cities of the Northeast and Midwest lost population while the suburbs of the West and South expanded quickly", because it is muddled; say instead that older industrial cities such as Detroit began to lose people in the 1950s as the suburbs grew.
- Do not use: Levittown homes sold "with a washing machine and a television set included in some models", because this is uncertain. Leave out appliances.
- Do not use: Spock "sold tens of millions", because the figure is not checked. Say "millions".
- Do not use: Monroe's Fox contract "dropped and renewed in 1951" as stated, because the dates are uncertain (the 1946 contract was dropped in 1947 and she signed again around 1950–51). Give 1946 only, or leave the contract out.
- Do not use: "The Equal Pay Act required equal pay for equal work", stated without a limit. Say it applied to men and women doing equal work in the same establishment, and that enforcement was weak.
- Do not use: Friedan "became a magazine writer and suburban housewife" as a career order; she wrote magazine articles while a housewife and mother in the suburbs.
- Do not use: Roe v. Wade as covering abortion "in all circumstances"; say the early stages of pregnancy.

Confusable people, terms and dates
- The Feminine Mystique (1963, a book) vs NOW (1966, an organisation) vs the ERA (Congress 1972, never ratified). Friedan is not the author of the ERA.
- Title VII (1964, employment) vs Title IX (1972, education).
- Equal Pay Act (1963) vs Equal Credit Opportunity Act (1974).
- Griswold (1965, contraception for married couples) vs Roe (1973, abortion).
- Diners Club (1950, charge card) vs BankAmericard (1958, credit card).
- Levitt built the houses; the Kitchen Debate was in a model house in Moscow, not at Levittown.
- Whyte's The Organization Man (1956, about corporate conformity) is a book, not a person; here it supports only the discussion of work.
- Second-wave feminism (1960s onwards) vs the suffrage movement (the Nineteenth Amendment, 1920).

## 4. What exams ask

- Higher 100-mark essay on the American Dream: how far it was realised, 1945–1989. Marks go to a judgement stated at the start, with paired points: material gains and mass consumption for many against exclusion of black Americans, the poor and women. Use dated evidence (Levittown 1947, credit cards, television, the 1974 Credit Act).
- Higher essay on Friedan, often paired with another personality (Monroe, Mailer, Ali or Graham): the answer must say who she was, what The Feminine Mystique argued, what she did afterwards (NOW 1966, the strike, the ERA) and how contemporaries and historians judge her. A list of her book alone scores low.
- Higher essay pairing Friedan and Monroe on the changing role of women: Monroe as the 1950s ideal of glamour and its constraints, Friedan as the challenge to the domestic ideal. Link them through consumer culture and the domestic ideal.
- Ordinary Level 30-mark answers on Friedan: birth and background, the Smith survey, the 1963 book and its argument, the "problem that has no name", NOW 1966, and why the book is important. Each correct, dated point earns a mark.
- Ordinary Level answers on Monroe: birth name, star in 1953, the main films with years, the link to advertising and consumer culture, and how the press and later feminist critics saw her.
- Ordinary Level questions on women's changing role: the post-war return to the home, rising employment, and one or two laws with years (Equal Pay Act 1963, Title VII 1964).
- Short questions on suburbs and consumerism: Levittown (where, when, why cheap), why credit and television mattered, and who was excluded.
- Strong essays name a critic of affluence (Galbraith, Packard, Whyte) and say what he argued, rather than describing affluence alone.
- Weak answers copy a list of laws without saying what changed; strong answers say what each law did and its limits (low pay and narrow job range continued).
- Questions on the Organization Man ask for Whyte's picture of the salaried corporate employee, conformity and moves between suburbs; this belongs mainly to the notes on the postwar economy.
