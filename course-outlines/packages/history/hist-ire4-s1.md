## 1. Must cover (one bullet or block for every item; tick each off)

Background (assumed)
- [ ] A1 (assumed) Pre-Famine Ireland: a fast-growing, mostly rural population; the poorest depended on the potato; land held by landlords and let to tenants and cottiers; Ireland part of the United Kingdom.
- [ ] A2 (assumed) The Great Famine: potato blight (1845 onward), the death toll and the scale of the crisis, as the setting for emigration (the Famine itself is covered fully in other notes).
- [ ] A3 (assumed) Geography: British North America (Canada), the St Lawrence River, Quebec, Liverpool as the main emigration port.

Key concepts
- [ ] K1 Pre-paid passages: tickets bought abroad by relatives and sent home (also in keyTerms).
- [ ] K2 Chain-emigration: emigrants paying for and housing the next, so families and parishes moved in stages (also in keyTerms).
- [ ] K3 Other essential terms in keyTerms: assisted emigration, seasonal migration, coffin ship, ship fever, quarantine station, Gregory clause, push and pull factors, Encumbered Estates Act.

Pre-Famine emigration (to 1845)
- [ ] E1 Origins: Ulster Presbyterians the first large group, to the American colonies in the 18th century.
- [ ] E2 Scale, 1815–1845: about one million.
- [ ] E3 Type of emigrant: Protestant small farmers, weavers and tradesmen with some capital; from the 1830s a growing share of poorer Catholics from the south and west.
- [ ] E4 Destinations: British North America (cheap fares), the USA, Britain; many who landed in Canada went on to the USA.
- [ ] E5 Britain: the poorest went there because the crossing was cheap; Irish-born in Britain in 1841; where they settled.
- [ ] E6 Seasonal migration: western labourers to English and Scottish harvests.
- [ ] E7 Occupations: navvies, dockers, building labourers, mill workers in Britain; canal, road and railway labour and domestic service in North America.
- [ ] E8 Settlement: Irish districts in poor parts of British cities; Irish in North American towns and canal and railway works.
- [ ] E9 Push factors: subdivision of holdings, population growth, dependence on the potato, decline of domestic textile spinning, evictions.
- [ ] E10 Pull factors: wages and demand for labour, cheap land in Canada, letters from earlier emigrants.
- [ ] E11 Timber ships as cheap passage to British North America.

The Famine decade, 1845–1855
- [ ] F1 Blight and the crop failures of 1845, 1846, 1847 (small crop, "Black 47") and 1848.
- [ ] F2 Deaths and population fall (about one million dead; population 1841 to 1851).
- [ ] F3 Numbers emigrating 1845–1855 and where they went (USA, British North America, Britain).
- [ ] F4 Type of emigrant: Catholic, poor, unskilled, many Irish-speaking, from the west and south-west, whole families; panic flight rather than planned movement.
- [ ] F5 Liverpool in 1847 and the Irish in Britain by 1851.
- [ ] F6 Relief policy: public works (1846); soup kitchens (Temporary Relief Act, 1847); Poor Law Extension Act (June 1847) and the Gregory clause.
- [ ] F7 Evictions and clearances; the Encumbered Estates Act (1849).
- [ ] F8 Assisted emigration by landlords: why (cheaper than rates); Mahon (Strokestown), Palmerston (Sligo), Lansdowne (Kenmare).
- [ ] F9 The Earl Grey scheme (orphan girls to Australia).
- [ ] F10 Pre-paid passages and remittances as the main funding of Famine emigration; assisted schemes only a minority.
- [ ] F11 Chain-emigration: how it worked; settlement of kin together; letters as information.
- [ ] F12 Routes and ports of departure: Liverpool, Cork, Dublin, Limerick, Sligo, Derry, Galway and others.
- [ ] F13 Fares and why the poorest went to Quebec; the US Passenger Act of 1847.
- [ ] F14 Conditions on board: steerage, six weeks or more, poor ventilation, water and food; typhus (ship fever) and dysentery; coffin ships.
- [ ] F15 Ports of arrival: New York, Boston, Philadelphia, Quebec, Saint John; quarantine and fever sheds.

Case study: Grosse Isle
- [ ] G1 Location and role: island in the St Lawrence, about 48 km below Quebec City; quarantine station for the port of Quebec.
- [ ] G2 Origin: opened 1832 because of cholera.
- [ ] G3 Procedure: every Quebec-bound ship stopped; medical inspection; sick to the hospital, healthy held in sheds or on board.
- [ ] G4 Dr George Mellis Douglas, medical superintendent from 1836; his warnings and reports.
- [ ] G5 Station built for a normal season; unprepared for 1847.
- [ ] G6 The first fever ship, mid-May 1847; the hospital full within days.
- [ ] G7 The queue: about 40 ships and about 12,500 passengers waiting on 31 May 1847.
- [ ] G8 Conditions: delays, sick and dead among the healthy in the holds, tents and sheds, sick on the ground and beaches, healthy crowded into sheds and catching typhus.
- [ ] G9 Staff and clergy: doctors and Catholic (many French-Canadian) and Anglican clergy who caught typhus, some died.
- [ ] G10 Deaths on the island: more than 5,000 buried in 1847, mostly Irish.
- [ ] G11 Spread upriver: Montreal fever sheds, about 6,000 Irish dead; Mayor John Mills died of typhus.
- [ ] G12 Orphans adopted by French-Canadian families, often keeping Irish surnames; the generosity of Quebec.
- [ ] G13 Comparison: Partridge Island (Saint John) in 1847.
- [ ] G14 Comparison: New York (largest destination; Castle Garden depot, 1855) versus Quebec.
- [ ] G15 The Celtic cross of the Ancient Order of Hibernians, 15 August 1909; inscriptions in Irish, English and French; the Irish text is blunter in blaming British rule.
- [ ] G16 Closure of the station (1937) and heritage status (national historic site; Irish Memorial, 1990s).
- [ ] G17 Memory and interpretation: nationalist view (Mitchel onward) that British policy killed the poor; other emphasis on landlord clearances and limited colonial resources.
- [ ] G18 Significance: human cost of unregulated mass emigration; governments on both sides of the Atlantic unprepared; most Famine emigrants went to the USA and Britain, not Quebec.

Assessment (final block, paired points)
- [ ] S1 Relief versus loss: emigration saved many from starvation but emptied poor districts and began long-term population decline.
- [ ] S2 Choice versus force: pre-paid tickets and family decisions against clearances and assisted schemes.
- [ ] S3 Contemporary views: officials, landlords and many economists welcomed emigration as a cure for overpopulation; Mitchel and the Young Irelanders saw it as expulsion.
- [ ] S4 Later views: Kerby Miller (exiles); revisionist historians who played down British blame; Ó Gráda, Kinealy and others who stress failures of relief policy.
- [ ] S5 Grosse Isle's place in memory against the wider pattern of Famine emigration.

## 3. Traps

Do not use (wrong or doubtful facts in the current notes):
- Do not use: "British North America was the main transatlantic destination before 1845", because the USA took an increasing share from the 1830s; say Canada was cheapest and many went on to the USA.
- Do not use: "an estimated 17,000 or more died of about 100,000 who sailed for British North America in 1847", because the sources disagree and the 100,000 includes non-Irish emigrants. Use only the Grosse Isle and Montreal figures in section 2.
- Do not use: "a monument erected by the medical staff in 1853", because the date and sponsor are not certain.
- Do not use: "its hospital sheds held only a few hundred patients" with a figure, because capacity figures vary; say only that the station was built for a normal season.
- Do not use: estimates of "up to 1.5 million" for 1815–1845; the range is too uncertain.
- Do not use: "Palmerston sent about 2,000" or "Lansdowne several thousand" as exact figures, because sources vary; use the section 2 wording.
- Do not use: "Mitchel saw it as extermination" in quotation marks, or any quotation attributed to Mitchel; give his view as reported thought.
- Do not use: a death rate such as "one in nine from Cork" (a past-paper source claim, not a checked fact).

## 4. What exams ask

- Higher 100-mark essay on Grosse Isle (the commonest case-study question): why was it important or what did it show about Famine emigration. Marks go to a judgement stated at the start, then the quarantine role (opened 1832), the 1847 queue of ships, fever and sheds, deaths, Douglas and the clergy, the orphans, and its memory (cross of 1909), supported by dated facts. Cover both the suffering and the generosity of French-Canadian Quebec; an account of 1847 alone, without significance, scores low.
- Strong Grosse Isle answers connect it to the wider picture: panic flight, unregulated emigration, unprepared governments, and that most emigrants went elsewhere. Comparisons (Partridge Island, New York) and heritage (the 1909 cross, national historic site) add depth.
- Higher essay on push and pull factors in emigration 1840–1914: students need home factors (potato failure, evictions, land hunger, weak industry) and abroad factors (wages, cheap land, family networks, easier travel), with at least one fact each for this subsection's period. The Famine-era facts here (clearances, assisted passages, fares) are the evidence for the earlier years of that question.
- Higher essay on main trends in emigration 1840–1920: scale, change from comparatively well-off Protestants to the Catholic poor, destinations, and the settling into a permanent pattern. This subsection supplies the pre-Famine and Famine part: origin, destination, type, occupation and settlement, each with a date or number.
- Ordinary Level paragraph (30 marks) on Famine emigration: blight (1845–46), numbers emigrating, destinations (the USA, Canada, Britain), Liverpool, the reasons and the poor conditions. Each correct, dated or numbered point earns marks.
- Ordinary Level short answers on a document: what a coffin ship was and why the name was used, how conditions caused fever, what the document's source or view shows. Students should be ready to explain terms in their own words.
- Ordinary Level 40-mark Grosse Isle question: location, why it existed, what happened in May 1847, who helped, deaths, memorials. Marks go for each correct fact; unsupported statements earn little.
- Key concept questions: defining pre-paid passages and chain-emigration with an example (letters, tickets, kin living together abroad).
- Assessment questions: relief versus loss, choice versus force, contemporary versus later views. Naming Miller, Ó Gráda or Kinealy with their view adds weight.
