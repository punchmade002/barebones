# Package: hist-eur5-s6 — Case study: the secession of Katanga, 1960–1965

- **loId:** `hist-eur5-s6` · **chapterId:** `hist-eur5`
- **Syllabus:** Later Modern Europe, Topic 5 (European retreat from empire and the aftermath,
  1945–1990), case study: "the secession of Katanga, 1960–1965". Outline scope: Katanga's
  mineral wealth and the Union Minière; Moïse Tshombe and the secession (11 July 1960) with
  Belgian backing; Lumumba's appeal to the UN; ONUC; the Irish troops (Niemba, Jadotville);
  Lumumba's overthrow and murder; Conor Cruise O'Brien and Operation Morthor; Hammarskjöld's
  death; the end of secession (January 1963); Tshombe as prime minister (1964); Mobutu's career
  to 1990; national and international significance. Key personality: Sese Seko Mobutu. Key
  concepts: tribalism; transnational economy. Write in full Higher Level detail, including
  enough context to place any DBQ document.

## 1. Must cover (one bullet or block for every item; tick each off)

Background (assumed)
- [ ] A1 (assumed) Belgian rule of the Congo (Leopold II's Congo Free State, then the Belgian Congo from 1908): paternalistic, few educated Congolese, almost no African officers or professionals at independence.
- [ ] A2 (assumed) Independence on 30 June 1960: Kasa-Vubu president, Lumumba prime minister; the army (Force Publique) mutinied within days and Belgian troops returned. (Detail belongs to the notes on Nigeria, the Congo, Tanzania and Angola.)
- [ ] A3 (assumed) The Cold War context: United States and Soviet Union competing for influence in new states; the United Nations and its Secretary-General.

Key concepts (also in keyTerms)
- [ ] K1 Tribalism: the colonial and later term for ethnic loyalty; in Katanga, the Lunda/Conakat versus Baluba/Balubakat rivalry. Note that historians stress politics and economics as well as ethnicity.
- [ ] K2 Transnational economy: companies and capital operating across borders; the Belgian-controlled Union Minière as an economic power that shaped politics.
- [ ] K3 Secession (essential term, not an outline key concept).

Background: Katanga before 1960
- [ ] B1 Mineral wealth: copper above all, also cobalt and uranium; Katanga's share of the Congo's revenue.
- [ ] B2 Union Minière du Haut-Katanga: Belgian-controlled; ran the mines; its influence on the province's economy and administration.
- [ ] B3 European presence and settler/mining-town society (Élisabethville).
- [ ] B4 Conakat and Tshombe: autonomy (even federalism) for Katanga, support among the Lunda and Belgian/settler interests.
- [ ] B5 Balubakat and Sendwe: the Baluba of the north; ethnic and political rivalry within Katanga; links to Lumumba.
- [ ] B6 The May 1960 elections: Conakat leading the provincial government; Lumumba's allies strong in national results.

The secession, July 1960
- [ ] S1 Proclamation on 11 July 1960, eleven days after independence.
- [ ] S2 Tshombe's reasons: keep Katanga's revenue; fear of a centralising, "Communist" government in Léopoldville.
- [ ] S3 Belgian backing: troops intervened from 10 July; officers, advisers and mercenaries built the Katanga Gendarmerie; no state recognised Katanga.
- [ ] S4 Lumumba's appeal to the UN and the setting up of ONUC (July 1960); the UN demand that Belgium withdraw (August 1960).

The UN operation and the Irish troops
- [ ] U1 ONUC: aims, Hammarskjöld, the rule of not taking sides in internal disputes; Lumumba's anger that it would not use force against Katanga.
- [ ] U2 Niemba ambush, 8 November 1960: nine Irish soldiers killed by Baluba.
- [ ] U3 O'Brien (the UN representative in Katanga) and the push for a firmer policy; Operation Rumpunch (August 1961) and Operation Morthor (13 September 1961) and why Morthor failed.
- [ ] U4 Jadotville, 13–17 September 1961: A Company, 35th Battalion, Commandant Pat Quinlan; the siege, the surrender, no Irish dead, held prisoner for about a month.
- [ ] U5 Security Council resolutions: February 1961 (force as last resort; foreign officers and mercenaries to leave) and November 1961 (explicit action against secession and mercenaries).

Lumumba's overthrow and murder
- [ ] L1 Lumumba's turn to the Soviet Union (August 1960) and Western alarm.
- [ ] L2 Dismissal by Kasa-Vubu (5 September 1960) and Mobutu's coup (14 September 1960).
- [ ] L3 Lumumba's escape and arrest (late November–early December 1960).
- [ ] L4 Transfer to Katanga and murder, 17 January 1961; announced 13 February 1961; Belgian and Katangan involvement.
- [ ] L5 Reactions: Soviet and African protests; martyr of African nationalism.

Hammarskjöld and the end of the secession
- [ ] H1 Khrushchev's attack on Hammarskjöld (troika proposal, autumn 1960) as part of the Cold War dimension.
- [ ] H2 Hammarskjöld's death near Ndola, 17–18 September 1961, travelling to meet Tshombe; cause never settled.
- [ ] H3 Fighting in Élisabethville, December 1961, and the Kitona Declaration (21 December 1961), later repudiated by Tshombe.
- [ ] H4 Operation Grand Slam (December 1962–January 1963) under U Thant.
- [ ] H5 The end: Tshombe at Kolwezi, 14 January 1963; UN troops enter Kolwezi; Tshombe into exile.

After the secession
- [ ] T1 Tshombe recalled as prime minister, July 1964, to October 1965.
- [ ] T2 The Simba rebellion (1964): Stanleyville, the Belgian paratroop rescue with American aircraft.
- [ ] T3 The UN force leaves (1964); the army's growing role.
- [ ] T4 Mobutu's second coup, 24 November 1965; Tshombe's exile, hijacking and death in Algeria (1967, 1969).

Key personality: Sese Seko Mobutu (one block, every point)
- [ ] P1 Who he was: born Joseph-Désiré Mobutu, Lisala, 14 October 1930; Force Publique; journalist.
- [ ] P2 Career in the crisis: army chief of staff 1960; the coup of 14 September 1960; arrest of Lumumba; the return of power to Kasa-Vubu (1961); the 1965 coup.
- [ ] P3 What he did as a direct participant, with dates.
- [ ] P4 Shaped by events: the Congo crisis gave him the army, Western backing and the lessons of fragmentation; foreign support kept him in power.
- [ ] P5 The regime: one-party state (MPR, 1967), public hangings (1966), nationalisation of Union Minière (1966–67), Gécamines, authenticité, Zaire (1971), his own name (1972).
- [ ] P6 Contemporaries: Western governments saw an anti-communist bulwark; many Congolese saw a corrupt dictator.
- [ ] P7 Historians: personal dictatorship, kleptocracy, a weak state, dependence on Cold War patronage.
- [ ] P8 Within the topic's dates only: Shaba invasions 1977–78 briefly; April 1990 end of the one-party state; overthrow in 1997 only as a one-line pointer.

Significance and assessment (final block)
- [ ] G1 Causes of the secession: wealth, regional identity and politics, Belgian support, fear of Lumumba.
- [ ] G2 Why it drew in outsiders: Belgian self-interest, Cold War rivalry, the UN's duty to protect a member state's territory.
- [ ] G3 National significance: the Congo survived within its colonial borders, but dependent on foreign force, army rule followed.
- [ ] G4 International significance: UN peacekeeping precedent, UN financial crisis, Hammarskjöld's death, African unity debates.
- [ ] G5 Contemporary views: neo-colonial plot (central government, much of Africa and the Soviet bloc) versus self-determination (Tshombe and his supporters).
- [ ] G6 Later views: the 2001 Belgian parliamentary inquiry on Lumumba's murder; Union Minière and Belgium's role.

## 3. Traps

Do not use (wrong or doubtful facts in the current notes):
- Do not use: Operation Morthor "of 24 September 1961" (the factCard), because Morthor was launched on 13 September 1961; 24 September is not its date.
- Do not use: Conakat "founded by Moïse Tshombe in 1958", because it was a federation of regional associations formed in 1958 that Tshombe came to lead; say it was formed in 1958 and led by Tshombe.
- Do not use: "uranium used in the first atomic bombs", "Katanga contributed some 65% of national income" or any percentage, because sources differ; say copper, cobalt and uranium, and a very large share of the revenue.
- Do not use: the Monrovia group as "less hostile to Tshombe", because the line between the groups was not that clear; use only the Casablanca group backing Lumumba.
- Do not use: "UN-appointed panels in 2013 and 2017 concluded that an external attack was possible", or "a Rhodesian one in 1962 that favoured pilot error", because the findings are disputed and imprecise; say the cause remains disputed.
- Do not use: "cost about 400 million dollars" and "Britain and France criticised it for acting against Katanga", because the first figure is uncertain and the second overgeneralises (the Soviet Union and France refused to pay; that is the safe point).
- Do not use: the Friendship University renamed after Lumumba, because it is peripheral.
- Do not use: "Belgian regular troops left by September" as a firm date; say Belgian troops withdrew after the UN arrived but officers and advisers stayed.
- Do not use: "Tshombe accepted a settlement and Katanga was reintegrated" as a description of the end; he announced the end of the secession after Operation Grand Slam.

Confusable people, terms and dates
- Katanga's secession (11 July 1960) is separate from the South Kasai secession (August 1960) and from the army mutiny.
- Kasa-Vubu (president) vs Lumumba (prime minister) vs Tshombe (Katanga's leader) vs Mobutu (army chief). Tshombe was later prime minister of the whole Congo (1964–65).
- Mobutu's two coups: 14 September 1960 (neutralising Lumumba and Kasa-Vubu, power handed back in 1961) and 24 November 1965 (took the presidency).
- Hammarskjöld (killed September 1961) vs U Thant (successor).
- Operation Rumpunch (August 1961) vs Operation Morthor (13 September 1961) vs Operation Grand Slam (December 1962); Niemba (1960, ambush, nine dead) vs Jadotville (1961, siege, no Irish dead).
- Resolution 161 (February 1961) vs Resolution 169 (November 1961).
- Congo (Léopoldville) vs Congo (Brazzaville); Zaire is the Congo, renamed.
- Union Minière (Belgian company, before 1966) vs Gécamines (state company).

## 4. What exams ask

- Higher 100-mark essay (2006, 2007, 2011, 2016, 2019, 2024): why Katanga seceded, why it drew international involvement, and what its results and importance were. Marks go to a clear thesis stating the causes in order of weight, then development of each: wealth and Union Minière, Conakat and regional identity, Belgian backing, fear of Lumumba. A narrative of the UN's operations without analysis scores poorly.
- Strong answers link the secession to the Cold War: the US and USSR seeing the Congo as a prize; the Soviet aid to Lumumba; the CIA role; the Soviet attack on Hammarskjöld; the UN's role. Separate the Belgian motive (protecting investments) from the superpower motive.
- Compare with Suez (2020, 2024, 2014): both involved the UN, the superpowers and a former colonial power. Be specific about differences: at Suez the UN force separated combatants; in the Congo it used force on a member state's territory.
- National versus international significance (2016): national means the Congo kept its territory but remained weak and army-dominated; international means a UN precedent, a financial crisis and the death of a Secretary-General.
- DBQ (2013 type): documents on Belgian paratroopers, tribal division and the UN's limited success; students need to place them: the secession, Belgian troops, ONUC, tribal differences. Know what the sources do not show (who is responsible).
- Ordinary Level 30/40-mark answers (every year from 2006): the Union Minière, Tshombe and Conakat, the date 11 July 1960, Belgian help, the UN, Lumumba, the end in January 1963. Each correct dated point earns marks; give three or four causes and two or three results.
- Short questions: which province, what Belgium sent, why the UN came, who Mobutu was.
- Mobutu (2009 ordinary type): his career and why he is important: army chief, two coups, the one-party state, Zaire, corruption, the Western support behind him. Quote dates and say what an opponent and a supporter would say.
- Achievement versus limit, and contemporary versus later views: reward paired points and the named inquiry (Belgium 2001).
