## 1. Must cover (one bullet or block for every item; tick each off)

Background (assumed)
- [ ] A1 (assumed) The Cold War's two sides: the USA and its Western allies (capitalist democracies) against the Soviet Union and its Eastern European satellites (communist one-party states); the superpowers never fought each other directly.
- [ ] A2 (assumed) The two alliances: NATO (1949) and the Warsaw Pact (1955); Germany divided in 1949 into the Federal Republic (West) and the German Democratic Republic (East); Berlin, deep inside East Germany, split into four sectors, the three Western ones forming West Berlin. Details of their origins are in the notes on the origins of the Cold War.
- [ ] A3 (assumed) How the Soviet Union was run: the Communist Party ruled alone; its leader was the First Secretary of the Central Committee; the Presidium (later called the Politburo) was the inner leadership; the chairman of the Council of Ministers (premier) headed the government.
- [ ] A4 (assumed) Stalin's rule: terror, purges, the Gulag labour camps and the cult of personality; his death on 5 March 1953 opened a struggle for the succession.
- [ ] A5 (assumed) Nuclear weapons: the USA used atomic bombs in 1945, the Soviet Union tested its first in 1949, and both later built hydrogen bombs; medium-range missiles reach targets within a region, intercontinental missiles (ICBMs) reach the other superpower's homeland.
- [ ] A6 (assumed) Castro's revolution in Cuba (1959) and Cuba's move into the Soviet camp.

Key concepts (each defined as used in this topic; also in keyTerms)
- [ ] K1 Cold War: rivalry short of direct war, fought through alliances, the arms race, propaganda, espionage, the space race and crises; in Europe from about 1945 to 1991.
- [ ] K2 Nuclear deterrence: preventing attack by holding enough nuclear weapons to destroy an aggressor even after its first strike; mutually assured destruction (MAD); NATO's shift from "massive retaliation" to "flexible response".
- [ ] K3 Détente: the easing of tension from about 1969 to the late 1970s, through summits, arms-control treaties and treaties on European borders.

Key personality: Nikita Khrushchev (one block, plus the assessment)
- [ ] P1 Who he was: born 1894 in Kalinovka, Kursk province, of peasant stock; worked as a miner and metalworker; rose in the party under Stalin (first secretary in Moscow from 1935, in Ukraine from 1938).
- [ ] P2 Participant in Stalin's purges in Moscow and Ukraine in the late 1930s, which later made his attack on Stalin's terror open to challenge.
- [ ] P3 Rise to power, 1953–1958: First Secretary (September 1953); Beria arrested (June 1953) and executed (December 1953); Malenkov forced out as premier (1955); the "anti-party group" of Molotov, Malenkov and Kaganovich defeated (June 1957) with Zhukov's and the Central Committee's help; premier as well as First Secretary (March 1958).
- [ ] P4 How contemporaries saw him: in the West, blustering, unpredictable and dangerous but also a man who could be dealt with (the 1959 US visit, the "kitchen debate" with Nixon); in China, a "revisionist" who had betrayed Marxism-Leninism; to his Soviet colleagues by 1964, a reckless leader of "hare-brained schemes".
- [ ] P5 How historians judge him: a reformer who opened Soviet society after Stalin but whose policies were improvised and often failed, and who never built a lasting alternative to Stalinist planning (e.g. William Taubman's biography).
- [ ] P6 His fall: removed on 14 October 1964 by the Central Committee after being recalled from holiday on the Black Sea; reasons (the Cuban retreat, failing agriculture, repeated reorganisations that angered party officials, his erratic personal style); Brezhnev became First Secretary and Kosygin premier; he was pensioned off and lived in retirement until his death in 1971, the first Soviet leader removed without being killed or imprisoned.

Khrushchev's domestic policies
- [ ] D1 The secret speech to a closed session of the 20th Party Congress, 25 February 1956: denounced Stalin's cult of personality and terror against party members (not the system itself); its text reached the West within months; its shock in Eastern Europe fed the Polish October and the Hungarian Uprising (one line, cross-refer to the Hungarian Uprising notes).
- [ ] D2 De-Stalinisation in practice: release of many Gulag prisoners; the secret police curbed and placed under party control; a cultural "thaw" with limits (one line cross-referring to the notes on literature for Solzhenitsyn); at the 22nd Party Congress (October 1961) Stalin's body was removed from the Lenin Mausoleum.
- [ ] D3 Agriculture: the Virgin Lands campaign from 1954 in Kazakhstan and western Siberia; good early harvests; soil erosion, drought and poor management; the disastrous 1963 harvest and grain imports from the West; the maize (corn) campaign after his 1959 visit to the USA.
- [ ] D4 Administration: industry decentralised into regional economic councils (1957); the party split into industrial and agricultural branches (1962); both resented by officials and reversed by his successors.
- [ ] D5 Living standards: a large programme of new flats; promises of consumer goods and of overtaking the USA; the 1961 party programme promised that communism would be built "in the main" by 1980; the Novocherkassk strike (June 1962) over food prices and wages, crushed by troops who fired on the crowd.
- [ ] D6 Space and prestige: Sputnik 1 (4 October 1957), the first artificial satellite; Yuri Gagarin, first human in space (12 April 1961); Western fears of a "missile gap".

Khrushchev's foreign policy and peaceful coexistence
- [ ] F1 Peaceful coexistence (20th Congress, 1956): war with the capitalist states was not inevitable; competition would be economic and ideological, not military.
- [ ] F2 The thaw of 1955: the Austrian State Treaty (May 1955) and the withdrawal of Soviet troops from Austria; reconciliation visit to Tito's Yugoslavia (1955); the Geneva summit (July 1955), the first post-war summit of the four powers.
- [ ] F3 The limits of coexistence: the Soviet crushing of the Hungarian Uprising (November 1956) showed he would use force to keep the bloc together (one line, cross-refer to the Hungarian Uprising notes).
- [ ] F4 The US visit (September 1959): talks with Eisenhower at Camp David, the "spirit of Camp David"; and the kitchen debate with Vice-President Nixon in Moscow (July 1959).
- [ ] F5 The Sino-Soviet split: Mao rejected de-Stalinisation and peaceful coexistence; Soviet advisers withdrawn from China (1960); open polemics in the early 1960s; Albania sided with China and formally left the Warsaw Pact in 1968; the communist world was no longer united behind Moscow.

The Berlin crises, 1958–1963
- [ ] B1 Earlier crises as background (brief): the Berlin Blockade (1948–49) and the Korean War (1950–53), which led to the rearmament of NATO and the plan to rearm West Germany; the Korean armistice of July 1953 after Stalin's death. Cross-refer to the notes on the origins of the Cold War for the Blockade.
- [ ] B2 Why Berlin mattered: West Berlin was a Western enclave inside East Germany, a showcase of Western prosperity, and the main escape route for East Germans, mostly young and skilled, which drained the GDR's workforce.
- [ ] B3 The Berlin ultimatum (27 November 1958): Khrushchev gave the Western powers six months to end their occupation and make West Berlin a demilitarised "free city", or he would hand control of access to East Germany; the West refused and the deadline passed.
- [ ] B4 The U-2 incident: an American U-2 spy plane flown by Francis Gary Powers was shot down over the Soviet Union on 1 May 1960; Eisenhower would not apologise and the Paris summit (May 1960) collapsed; Powers was exchanged in 1962.
- [ ] B5 The Vienna summit (June 1961): Khrushchev pressed the new president, John F. Kennedy, to accept a Berlin settlement; Kennedy refused and strengthened US forces.
- [ ] B6 The Berlin Wall: building began on 13 August 1961; the Checkpoint Charlie tank stand-off (October 1961); the West protested but did not use force, accepting a divided city as better than war.
- [ ] B7 Results and significance: the refugee flow stopped and the GDR stabilised; people killed trying to cross; Kennedy's 1963 Berlin speech; the Wall became the symbol of the divided Europe until 1989.

The Cuban Missile Crisis, 1962, as seen from Europe
- [ ] C1 Khrushchev's motives: to protect Castro after the failed US-backed Bay of Pigs invasion (April 1961); to balance American missiles in Turkey and Italy; to make up for Soviet inferiority in long-range missiles; possibly to gain leverage over Berlin.
- [ ] C2 The events: U-2 photographs shown to Kennedy on 16 October 1962; his advisers (ExComm) weighed an air strike against a blockade; the naval "quarantine" announced on 22 October; the settlement of 28 October (missiles withdrawn in return for a US pledge not to invade Cuba, plus a secret promise to remove the Jupiter missiles from Turkey).
- [ ] C3 Seen from Europe: Europeans were informed rather than consulted (Dean Acheson briefed de Gaulle, who backed Kennedy; Macmillan was kept in touch by Kennedy); fear that the Soviets would answer a move on Cuba with a move on Berlin; the lesson that Europe's fate could be settled in Washington and Moscow, which strengthened de Gaulle's case for an independent French deterrent.
- [ ] C4 Results: Khrushchev's prestige damaged at home and in China; both sides sought safeguards: the Moscow–Washington hotline (1963) and the Partial Test Ban Treaty (5 August 1963).

Nuclear deterrence and arms control
- [ ] N1 The arms race under Khrushchev: the Tsar Bomba (30 October 1961), the largest nuclear explosion ever.
- [ ] N2 Western Europe and the bomb: reliance on the US "nuclear umbrella" through NATO; Britain's own deterrent; France's first test (1960) and its withdrawal from NATO's integrated military command (1966); doubts whether the USA would risk its own cities to defend Europe.
- [ ] N3 The Partial Test Ban Treaty (1963): USA, Britain and the Soviet Union; tests banned in the atmosphere, in space and under water (underground tests still allowed).
- [ ] N4 The Non-Proliferation Treaty (1968).
- [ ] N5 SALT I (26 May 1972, Nixon and Brezhnev): the ABM Treaty and an interim limit on strategic missiles; SALT II (signed June 1979) was never ratified by the US Senate.

Détente in Europe
- [ ] T1 Why détente came: the shock of Cuba; the cost of the arms race; rough nuclear parity by about 1970; the Soviet wish for Western trade, technology and recognition of the post-war borders; the US entanglement in Vietnam; the Sino-Soviet split; West German wishes to ease the division of Germany.
- [ ] T2 Ostpolitik: Willy Brandt (Chancellor from 1969); the Treaty of Moscow (12 August 1970, renouncing force); the Treaty of Warsaw (December 1970, accepting the Oder–Neisse border) and Brandt kneeling at the Warsaw Ghetto memorial; Nobel Peace Prize 1971.
- [ ] T3 The German settlement: the Four Power Agreement on Berlin (3 September 1971) guaranteeing access to West Berlin; the Basic Treaty between the two German states (December 1972); both joined the UN (1973).
- [ ] T4 The Helsinki Final Act (1 August 1975): 35 states, including the USA, Canada and the Soviet Union; post-war borders treated as inviolable (a Soviet gain); human rights commitments (a Western gain) that Eastern dissidents later used (cross-refer to the notes on reform movements in Eastern Europe).

The "second Cold War", 1979–1985
- [ ] S1 The Soviet invasion of Afghanistan (December 1979); US responses: a grain embargo, the boycott of the Moscow Olympics (1980), SALT II shelved.
- [ ] S2 The Euromissiles: Soviet SS-20 missiles aimed at Western Europe from the late 1970s; NATO's "double-track" decision (12 December 1979): deploy Pershing II and cruise missiles unless talks succeeded; deployment began late 1983 and the Soviets walked out of the Geneva talks (November 1983).
- [ ] S3 The peace movement: mass demonstrations in West Germany, Britain and the Netherlands, 1981–83 (e.g. the women's camp at Greenham Common in England).
- [ ] S4 Reagan's hard line: elected 1980; called the Soviet Union an "evil empire" (March 1983); announced the Strategic Defense Initiative ("Star Wars", March 1983); the NATO exercise Able Archer 83 (November 1983) and Soviet fears of a surprise attack.
- [ ] S5 The way out (brief, cross-refer to the notes on Gorbachev): Gorbachev leader from 1985; the Reykjavik summit (October 1986); the INF Treaty (December 1987) removed the Euromissiles.

Turning points to 1989
- [ ] TP1 The key turning points in order, each with what it changed: the Berlin Blockade (1948–49); Stalin's death (1953); Hungary (1956); the Berlin Wall (1961); Cuba (1962); the Prague Spring crushed (1968); Helsinki (1975); Afghanistan and the double-track decision (1979); Gorbachev and the INF Treaty (1985–87); the fall of the Wall (1989). Students need an argued choice of which mattered most.

Assessment (paired judgement points)
- [ ] J1 Khrushchev at home: de-Stalinisation, release of prisoners, housing and space successes vs the Virgin Lands crisis, reversed reorganisations, Novocherkassk and unkept promises.
- [ ] J2 Khrushchev abroad: peaceful coexistence, the Austrian treaty, the hotline and the Test Ban Treaty vs Hungary, the failed Berlin ultimatum, the Wall, the Cuban retreat and the split with China.
- [ ] J3 Khrushchev compared with Gorbachev (brief; exams pair them): both reformers from above who lost power; Khrushchev kept the one-party system and the bloc by force, Gorbachev let both go. Detail on Gorbachev is in the notes on reform movements and the collapse of the Soviet Union.
- [ ] J4 Détente: achievements (arms limits, the German treaties, Helsinki's human rights standard) vs limits (arms building continued, each side read it differently, it collapsed in 1979).
- [ ] J5 Contemporary vs later views of Khrushchev: the West's bully and gambler and his colleagues' "hare-brained" schemer vs historians' reformer who made the system less brutal.

## 3. Traps (known errors and boundaries)

- Do not use: "the German Democratic Republic, which had lost about 2.7 million people to the West since 1945" (current notes), because about 2.7 million is the usual figure for 1949–1961; the total from 1945 is higher. Say "about 2.7 million between 1949 and 1961".
- Do not use: Kennedy's private remark that a wall was better than a war, inside quotation marks or as "reported to have said" (current notes), because it is second-hand. Say without quotation that the West accepted the Wall as preferable to war.
- Do not use: "Albania ... became the only member state to leave the Warsaw Pact" (current notes) without qualification, because East Germany also left in 1990 on reunification. Say Albania formally withdrew in 1968, having stopped taking part in the early 1960s.
- Do not use: "On 24 December 1979" for the invasion of Afghanistan (current notes), because sources date it variously between 24 and 27 December. Say "December 1979".
- Do not use: "The Soviet leadership's fear of a Western first strike led to a serious misreading during ... Able Archer 83" as settled fact (current notes), because historians still debate how alarmed Soviet leaders really were. Say Soviet fears of a surprise attack were heightened, and that the danger is debated.
- Do not use: "The crisis was defused by arms negotiations" for the Euromissiles (current notes), because the talks broke down in November 1983 and the missiles were deployed; they were removed only by the INF Treaty of 1987 under Gorbachev.
- Do not use: "most European governments learned of the crisis only as it unfolded" (current notes) as stated, because Kennedy informed Macmillan, de Gaulle and Adenauer just before the quarantine was announced. Say the allies were informed, not consulted.
- Do not use: Khrushchev's "We will bury you" (1956) or the shoe-banging at the United Nations (1960), because the first is a mistranslated boast about outlasting capitalism, not a threat of war, and the details of the second are disputed.
- Do not use: any figure for the number of deaths at the Berlin Wall, Soviet missiles in Cuba, or Euromissile numbers, because none is checked here.
- Khrushchev's secret speech attacked Stalin's crimes against party members and the cult of personality; it did not condemn communism, collectivisation or the one-party state.
- Khrushchev was First Secretary from 1953 but became premier only in 1958; Malenkov was premier 1953–55, Bulganin after him (do not name Bulganin in the notes).
- The Berlin ultimatum was 1958; the Wall was 1961; the Berlin Blockade was 1948–49 (Stalin, not Khrushchev). The Wall was built by East Germany with Soviet approval.
- The U-2 incident (1960) wrecked the Paris summit; a U-2 also photographed the Cuban missiles (1962). Do not merge them.
- The Bay of Pigs (April 1961) was a failed US-backed invasion by Cuban exiles, not a US invasion by American troops.
- The "quarantine" was a naval blockade; Kennedy avoided the word blockade because a blockade is an act of war.
- Partial Test Ban Treaty (1963) banned tests in the atmosphere, space and under water, not underground tests; France and China did not sign. NPT (1968) is a different treaty.
- Détente is usually dated from about 1969 (Nixon, Brezhnev and Brandt), not from 1963; the hotline and Test Ban Treaty are its forerunners.
- Ostpolitik is Brandt's policy (Chancellor 1969–74), not Adenauer's. The Helsinki Final Act was not a peace treaty and was not legally binding.
- Brezhnev was First Secretary (later General Secretary) after 1964; Kosygin was premier. Do not reverse them.

  - Yalta, Potsdam, the Iron Curtain speech, Sovietisation, the Truman Doctrine, Marshall Plan, the Berlin Blockade in detail, the founding of NATO and the Warsaw Pact: the notes on the origins of the Cold War.

  - East Berlin 1953, the Prague Spring and Brezhnev Doctrine, Solidarity, Gorbachev's career, glasnost and perestroika, 1989 and the collapse of the USSR: the notes on reform movements and the collapse of the Soviet Union.
  - Soviet planning, Comecon, the Brezhnev stagnation and Kosygin's reforms: the notes on the communist economies.

## 4. What exams ask (topics only)

- Essay: how successful Khrushchev was (domestic and/or foreign policy). Reward goes to a stated verdict ("partly successful") argued through paired points: de-Stalinisation and released prisoners (success) vs the speech unsettling the bloc and leading to Hungary (cost); Virgin Lands early harvests vs the 1963 failure and grain imports; peaceful coexistence and the Test Ban Treaty vs the Berlin ultimatum failing, the Wall and the Cuban retreat. End with his fall in 1964 as evidence.
- Ordinary Level 30/40-mark paragraphs on Khrushchev: his rise (defeating Beria and the anti-party group), the secret speech, the Virgin Lands, Berlin, Cuba and his fall. Dates and names (25 February 1956, 1957, 1961, 1962, October 1964) earn marks; narrative in order is enough.
- Essay: Khrushchev and Gorbachev compared as reformers or as failures. Students need parallel points: both reformed from above, both met resistance, both lost power; Khrushchev kept the system and the bloc, Gorbachev's reforms ended both. Give Khrushchev's side here; Gorbachev's comes from the notes on him.
- Essay: the main crises of the Cold War affecting Europe. Group them (Berlin crises; crises in the Soviet bloc; Cuba and the nuclear danger; the Euromissiles) and for each give cause, events and result for East–West relations. A question asking "how they affected Europe" wants consequences: the Wall, the hotline, French independence in defence, the peace movements.
- Essay: the turning points of the Cold War. Choose a few, put them in order, and argue what each changed (hardened division, eased tension, began détente, ended it). Credit goes to judgement, e.g. Cuba as the turning point that made both sides seek arms control.
- Essay: the development of the Cold War in stages (1945 origins → crises 1958–62 → détente 1969–79 → second Cold War → end 1985–91). This subsection supplies the middle stages; students need dates for each stage's start and end.
- Short questions (Ordinary Level): define peaceful coexistence, détente, nuclear deterrence; identify the Berlin Wall's date and purpose.
- Judgement throughout: achievements vs limits (Khrushchev, détente); cause and result chains (refugee flow → Wall → stabilised GDR but propaganda defeat; Cuba → hotline and Test Ban Treaty). Paired points with dates earn more than narrative.
