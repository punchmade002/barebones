# Package: hom-fruit-vegetables-s1 — Classification, nutritional significance and contribution to the diet

- **loId:** `hom-fruit-vegetables-s1` · **chapterId:** `hom-fruit-vegetables` · **syllabusRef:** 1.3.2
- **Syllabus scope:** classes of vegetables and of fruit; nutritive and dietetic value; contribution to the diet and current healthy-eating guidance on servings. No [HL] marks on this subsection (the outline marks none).
- **Also examined (outline):** classes (2019O, 2016O, 2012O, 2004O); nutritional significance of vegetables (2026H, 2022H, 2019H, 2012H, 2008H); ways to eat more fruit and vegetables (2025O, 2019H, 2017O, 2004O); consumption data (2017O, 2011O).
- **Prepared by:** a higher model, 2026-11-23. Fruit and vegetables are one commodity group in the syllabus, so every block should say "fruit" or "vegetables" explicitly. Exams often ask "nutritive value" (what nutrients it supplies) and "dietetic value" (which diets and conditions it suits) as two parts; keep the two headings distinct. Selection, storage and spoilage, cooking and preparation, and processing are in the other three subsections of this chapter.

## 1. Must cover

Classes of vegetables (one block; the list is a fact card, examiners award a mark per class plus a mark per example)
- [ ] C1 Vegetables are classified by the part of the plant eaten. Roots (carrots, turnips, beetroot, parsnips); bulbs (onions, leeks); tubers (potatoes); leafy greens (cabbage, spinach, lettuce, kale); flowers (broccoli, cauliflower); pulses (peas, beans, lentils, chickpeas); fruit vegetables (tomatoes, peppers, cucumbers); stems (asparagus, celery); fungi (mushrooms).
- [ ] C2 Each class gets one nutritional note: roots and tubers store starch and some sugar (carrots and onions have small amounts of sugar); orange and yellow roots supply beta-carotene; green leafy vegetables supply folate, iron and calcium, plus vitamin C; pulses are the richest vegetable source of protein and fibre; fruit vegetables such as tomatoes and peppers supply vitamin C; mushrooms are fungi, not plants, and supply some B vitamins.
- [ ] C3 (assumed) Pulses are the seeds of leguminous plants; "pulse vegetables" is the name examiners use.

Classes of fruit (one block)
- [ ] F1 Citrus (oranges, lemons, limes, grapefruit, mandarins); soft and berry fruit (strawberries, raspberries, blackcurrants, blueberries, blackberries, grapes); stone fruit (plums, peaches, nectarines, cherries, apricots); pome or hard fruit (apples, pears); dried fruit (raisins, sultanas, currants, prunes, dates, figs, apricots); other, tropical or exotic (bananas, kiwi, pineapple, mango, melon, rhubarb).
- [ ] F2 One nutritional note per class: citrus, kiwi and blackcurrants are rich in vitamin C; yellow, orange and red fruit (apricots, mango) supply pro-vitamin A (beta-carotene); apples and pears supply pectin and fibre; dried fruit is concentrated in sugar, energy and fibre because the water has been removed.

Nutritive value of vegetables (one block; this is the "nutritional significance" answer, a 15 to 20 mark question at Higher Level)
- [ ] V1 Protein: small amounts, mainly low biological value (LBV); pulses are the best vegetable source; soya beans supply high biological value (HBV) protein. Protein content is a few per cent of the weight (marking schemes give about 1 to 8 per cent).
- [ ] V2 Fat: very little; the exceptions are olives, avocado and soya beans, which supply mainly unsaturated fat. Vegetables contain no cholesterol.
- [ ] V3 Carbohydrate: fibre (non-starch polysaccharide, NSP), starch in potatoes, root vegetables and pulses, and small amounts of sugar in carrots and onions.
- [ ] V4 Vitamins: beta-carotene (pro-vitamin A) in dark green, orange, yellow and red vegetables; vitamin C in green vegetables, peppers, tomatoes and potatoes; B vitamins (thiamine, riboflavin, B6) in pulses and green vegetables; folate in leafy green vegetables; vitamin K in green vegetables. Vitamins A, C and E act as antioxidants. Vegetables do not supply vitamin B12 and are poor in vitamin D.
- [ ] V5 Minerals: non-haem iron (dark green vegetables, pulses), calcium (green leafy vegetables), potassium and magnesium, with traces of zinc and iodine.
- [ ] V6 Water: 70 to 95 per cent of the weight (marking schemes state this range); contributes to hydration and low energy density.
- [ ] V7 Fibre function: adds bulk to the stool, stimulates peristalsis, prevents constipation, may reduce the risk of bowel disease (diverticular disease and cancer of the bowel appear in schemes), and promotes fullness.

Nutritive value of fruit (one block)
- [ ] R1 Carbohydrate: natural sugars (glucose, fructose, sucrose) give energy and sweetness; unripe fruit such as bananas contains starch that turns to sugar on ripening; fibre is cellulose (insoluble) and pectin (soluble).
- [ ] R2 Protein: almost none. Fat: almost none; avocado is the exception (mainly unsaturated fat).
- [ ] R3 Vitamins and minerals: vitamin C (citrus, kiwi, blackcurrants, strawberries), pro-vitamin A in coloured fruit; potassium (bananas), small amounts of calcium and iron. High water content.

Dietetic value and contribution to the diet (one block; "dietetic value" means which diets or conditions the food suits)
- [ ] D1 Low energy density, high fibre and high water make fruit and vegetables filling and suitable for weight control and low-calorie diets.
- [ ] D2 Low in fat, no cholesterol, and fibre: suit low-fat, low-cholesterol and heart-healthy diets.
- [ ] D3 Soya and pulses give protein for vegetarian and vegan diets; vegetables add iron and folate to those diets.
- [ ] D4 Antioxidant vitamins (A, C, E) protect cells; vitamin C supports the immune system and iron absorption. Fibre supports bowel health.
- [ ] D5 Limits: vegetables lack vitamin B12 and are poor in vitamin D, so they cannot meet all needs alone. Dried fruit, juice and fruit canned in syrup are high in sugar; people controlling blood glucose or weight should watch the amount.
- [ ] D6 Practical value: versatile, available all year (frozen, canned, dried), economical when in season, quick to prepare.

Healthy-eating guidance on servings (one block)
- [ ] G1 Current Irish guidance, from the Department of Health's Healthy Food for Life guidelines and Food Pyramid (2016): vegetables, salad and fruit form the base shelf, the largest of the Pyramid, with 5 to 7 servings a day for adults. Marking schemes phrase it as "five portions plus".
- [ ] G2 Fruit juice, if drunk, should be unsweetened and counts as at most one serving a day; whole fruit gives fibre that juice lacks. Foods high in fat, sugar and salt sit on the small top shelf.
- [ ] G3 Surveys of Irish people show many do not reach the recommended number of servings (examiners give a chart, so students must read patterns: share eating none, share reaching five, difference between adults and children or men and women). Teach how to comment on such a chart; give no survey figure.

Ways to eat more fruit and vegetables (one block; a very frequent "suggest ways" question for children, teenagers, men)
- [ ] W1 Vegetable soups, stews, sauces, curries and Bolognese; grating or blending vegetables into dishes.
- [ ] W2 Fruit on breakfast cereal, in yoghurt, in baking and desserts; fruit salads as fillings; smoothies.
- [ ] W3 Stir-fries; vegetable toppings on pizza; vegetable shapes cut with cutters for children; cherry tomatoes, carrot sticks and fruit in lunch boxes; fruit instead of sweet snacks; trying new vegetables and cooking methods; spiralised vegetable noodles and cauliflower rice in place of some starchy food; vegetarian main courses; growing vegetables at home.
- [ ] W4 Reasons women often eat more than men (examined 2017O): more health and weight conscious, aware of nutritional benefits, use salads in low-calorie diets. Present as reasons, not as a fact about all women.

## 3. Traps

- Do not use: the per-age serving split (5 for children, up to 7 for teenage boys and men), because it could not be confirmed against the 2016 Pyramid.
- Potatoes are tubers, not roots. Onions and leeks are bulbs. Tomatoes, peppers and cucumbers are botanically fruit; the schemes list them as "fruit" vegetables, so give them there. Avocado is listed under stone fruit in one scheme; if mentioned, mention it only as the fat-containing exception.
- Mushrooms are fungi, not plants; don't call them a plant class.
- Nutritive value (what is in it) is not dietetic value (who benefits). Keep the two blocks separate; the exam asks for both.
- Vegetables have LBV protein; only soya approaches HBV. Don't say all plant protein is HBV.
- Beta-carotene is pro-vitamin A, not vitamin A. Vegetables don't contain vitamin B12 or much vitamin D; don't claim they do.
- Do not use: "young people eat even fewer vegetables than adults", because it is a survey claim with no checked source and survey figures change.
- Do not use: "fats, oils and sugars at the top" of the Pyramid or "fruit and vegetables on the lower shelves", because the current Pyramid has vegetables, salad and fruit as the base (largest) shelf and the top shelf holds foods and drinks high in fat, sugar and salt.
- Do not use: "at least five portions a day" as the Department's figure on its own, because the Pyramid gives 5 to 7 servings; "five portions plus" is the schemes' wording and may be quoted as that.
- Do not use: "potatoes are an important source of vitamin C in the Irish diet" as a headline claim, because the amount varies with storage and cooking; say potatoes supply some vitamin C.
- Do not use: "calcium in root vegetables", because the amount is trivial; calcium comes from green leafy vegetables and the schemes credit that.
- Neighbouring subsections: how storage, cooking and processing change these nutrients (vitamin C loss, blanching, canning, freezing) is in Selection, storage and spoilage, Preparation and cooking, and Processing; here state only what is present in the fresh food. The detail on vitamins, minerals, fibre and protein quality belongs to the nutrient chapters; give one-line links and don't redefine LBV, HBV or NSP at length. Diabetic, vegetarian and weight-loss diets are in the modified diets chapter; give one-line fits only.

## 4. What exams ask

- Classify vegetables, or fruit, and give examples of each class (short 6-mark questions in Section A; twelve-mark in Ordinary B). Also name two or three fruits in a given class, and true/false statements such as "peas, beans and lentils are pulses", "fruit and vegetables have no fibre" (false), "vegetables have a high water content".
- Discuss the nutritional significance of vegetables (a 15 to 20 mark Higher Level answer): go through protein (LBV, pulses, soya), fat, carbohydrate (fibre, starch, sugar), vitamins (carotene, C, B group, folate, K, antioxidants), minerals (iron, calcium, potassium, trace elements), water, and the role of each in the body (growth, energy, bowel, immune, nerve, muscle, blood, bones, hydration). Also given as "fruit or vegetables" (choose one) and as "nutritive value and dietetic value".
- Dietetic value or contribution to the diet: low-calorie, low-cholesterol, high-fibre, vegetarian, versatile, economical, with the limits (lacks D and B12; sugar in dried and canned-in-syrup fruit).
- Four reasons it is important to include fruit and vegetables.
- Suggest ways to increase intake for a named group: young people, children, teenagers, men; usually two to four ways, each with a short reason it works.
- Interpret a bar chart or table of consumption against guidelines (five portions plus): comment on who is under or over, give reasons, suggest changes. Students need to write in sentences using the chart.
- Fill-in-the-gap items: citrus fruit as a source of vitamin C; vegetables blanched before freezing; in-season produce.
