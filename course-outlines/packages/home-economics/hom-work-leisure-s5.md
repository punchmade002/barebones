# Package: hom-work-leisure-s5 — Reconciling employment with family responsibilities

- **loId:** `hom-work-leisure-s5` · **chapterId:** `hom-work-leisure` · **syllabusRef:** 6.3.2
- **Syllabus scope:** "changing patterns in sex roles in the family; the impact of dual-earner families on family life: role overload, role conflict, distribution of parental and home-care responsibilities." Settings: a class survey and questionnaire on male and female roles in twenty home tasks. Common to both levels; nothing is [HL] (role conflict and the family's response to special needs in the family chapter are separate). Asked very often: 2005O, 2008O, 2014O, 2023H, 2024O.
- **Prepared by:** a higher model, 2026-11-23. Neighbours: reasons women entered paid work, and flexible working arrangements as a general trend, belong to the changing patterns subsection (the arrangements are listed here only as ways to reconcile work and family); child care options belong to the next subsection; the sociological concepts of roles (role conflict in general) and social change in marriage belong to the sociology chapter.

## 1. Must cover

Changing sex roles
- [ ] R1 A role is the pattern of behaviour expected of a person in a position (parent, partner, employee, homemaker). Sex or gender roles are the tasks and behaviour expected of men and women.
- [ ] R2 Traditional pattern: man as breadwinner in paid work; woman as homemaker responsible for child care and housework.
- [ ] R3 Changing pattern: more women in paid work (reasons are in another subsection); more men take part in child care and housework; roles are shared; fathers can take paternity and parent's leave; some families have a stay-at-home father; attitudes have changed but women still do more of the household and caring work on average.
- [ ] R4 Children learn sex roles from how tasks are shared at home, and from school, peers and media.
- [ ] R5 A dual-earner (dual-career) family is one in which both partners are in paid work.

Role overload
- [ ] O1 Definition: one person trying to perform several roles at once (employee, parent, homemaker, carer) and not being able to meet the demands of all.
- [ ] O2 Effects: less leisure and rest time, tiredness, stress, tension between partners, poorer health, less time with children, neglected household tasks, less energy for training or promotion, and a risk of burnout. It affects women more where they still carry most of the home duties.
- [ ] O3 Reduce it by sharing tasks, using labour-saving appliances, outside help (cleaner, child care), simpler routines and meal planning, leave and flexible work arrangements.

Role conflict
- [ ] K1 Definition: the expectations of one role clash with those of another so that both cannot be met, and a choice has to be made. Example: a sick child needs a parent at home while a deadline or meeting at work requires the parent to be there.
- [ ] K2 Other forms: guilt about priorities; employer expectations (late meetings, travel) against school hours; a partner who does not support the other's career; competing careers.
- [ ] K3 Effects: stress, guilt, tension and arguments between partners; children may feel neglected; employment may suffer (absence, missed promotion); the family may need outside help.
- [ ] K4 Do not mix with role overload: overload is too many roles to cope with at once; conflict is two roles pulling in different directions.

Distribution of parental and home-care responsibilities
- [ ] D1 Parental responsibilities: feeding, dressing, supervising, school runs and collections, homework, health care, emotional support, discipline, play. Home-care responsibilities: cooking, cleaning, laundry, shopping, budgeting, repairs, garden, bills.
- [ ] D2 Modern pattern: tasks are shared between partners by agreement; fathers more involved in child care; children take on age-appropriate jobs; outside help is bought in (cleaner, childminder); relatives such as grandparents help; labour-saving appliances (washing machine, dishwasher, microwave, slow cooker) cut the time needed.
- [ ] D3 Benefits of sharing: less overload, stronger parent-child bonds (including father-child), children learn that care and housework are shared and that men and women have equal roles, fairer family relationships, models gender equality.
- [ ] D4 Drawbacks if not shared or if poorly managed: resentment, arguments, one partner carrying a "double shift", children given too much responsibility.

Impact of dual-earner families on family life
- [ ] F1 Positive: higher family income, a better standard of living, less money worry and security if one job is lost, more independence and self-esteem for both partners, children learn independence and see parents working, a role model for work and equal roles, wider social contact for parents.
- [ ] F2 Negative: less time for the family together and for leisure; tiredness and stress; children may feel neglected, lonely or left out; older children may be given care duties for younger siblings; child care costs absorb part of the second income; less time for home tasks, meals eaten out or convenience foods; the couple may have less time for each other; role overload and role conflict.
- [ ] F3 Evaluate: examiners want both sides and a judgement (2024O, 20 marks). Say the impact depends on how well tasks are shared, on child care and on how flexible the employers are.

Ways to reconcile employment with family responsibilities
- [ ] W1 Flexitime, job-sharing, part-time and term-time work, compressed week, teleworking, career break (arrangements as defined in the changing patterns subsection).
- [ ] W2 Parental leave (unpaid, 26 weeks per child under 12), maternity leave, paternity leave, parent's leave, force majeure leave for family emergencies, carer's leave (names under the relevant Acts, no lengths other than the 26 weeks of parental leave).
- [ ] W3 Child care (see next subsection), after-school care, help from relatives; sharing housework; labour-saving appliances; planning (meal planning, shopping lists, online shopping); family meetings to share the load.
- [ ] W4 Employers can help: flexible hours, crèche facilities, understanding about family emergencies.

Investigation: survey on male and female roles in twenty home tasks (Higher and Ordinary)
- [ ] I1 Aim: find out how twenty home tasks are divided between males and females in a sample of households.
- [ ] I2 Method: list twenty tasks (cooking, ironing, grocery shopping, bills, car maintenance, minding children, gardening, cleaning the bathroom, DIY, etc.); design a questionnaire (who usually does each task: mainly male, mainly female, shared, other); a sample of students or families; anonymous answers.
- [ ] I3 Analysis: count and give as percentages, find which tasks are mostly male, mostly female or shared, compare with traditional roles, and link to dual-earner families; conclusions and limits (small sample, bias, honesty).

## 3. Traps

- Do not use: the 2005 marking scheme's point that child care has no tax relief, because tax treatment changes. Say only that child care costs can absorb part of the second income, with no figures.
- Do not use the Family Income Supplement (named in the 2005 and 2014 papers); it was replaced by the Working Family Payment and belongs to the poverty chapter.
- Do not use any amounts of Child Benefit, subsidies or leave payments.
- Role overload is not role conflict; keep the definitions apart and give each its own example.
- Do not claim that all families now share equally; say the pattern is changing and women still often carry more.
- "Sex roles" is the syllabus term; "gender roles" is acceptable as a synonym once.
- Child care options and their evaluation belong to the next subsection; mention child care here only as a way to reconcile work and home.
- Reasons for more women working (education, equality, cost of living) belong to the changing patterns subsection; do not repeat the list.
- Do not discuss lone-parent families or divorce here (sociology chapter).

## 4. What exams ask

- Discuss the impact of dual-earner families on family life, referring to role overload, role conflict and the distribution of parental and home-care responsibilities (2005O, 2008O, 2014O, 2023H; up to 26 marks): define each, give an effect on family members, and say how parents share duties; both positives and negatives.
- Evaluate the impact of dual-earner families (2024O): balanced judgement.
- Give reasons why there are more dual-earner households (2008O): costs, education, independence, job availability, flexible conditions.
- Effects on parental and home-care duties (2005O): children feel left out, unsupportive partner, costs.
- Practical question: survey on twenty home tasks (aim, method, results).
- Sex-role changes and how they affect a family (links to social change questions in the sociology chapter).
