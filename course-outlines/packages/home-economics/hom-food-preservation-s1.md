# Package: hom-food-preservation-s1 — Principles of food preservation

- **loId:** `hom-food-preservation-s1` · **chapterId:** `hom-food-preservation` · **syllabusRef:** 1.3.9
- **Syllabus scope:** why food is preserved; the principles that underlie all methods (removing the conditions micro-organisms and enzymes need). Also examined: causes of food spoilage (2025O, 2010O); advantages of preservation (2025O). No Higher Level only items in this subsection. Common to Ordinary and Higher Level.
- **Prepared by:** a higher model, 2026-11-23. This is the opening subsection of the chapter: it sets up the causes of spoilage and the handful of principles that every later subsection (freezing, heat, drying, chemicals, fermentation, irradiation) applies. It states each principle once, in general terms, and names the methods that use it. The detail of each method belongs to its own subsection. The detail of the organisms (structure, reproduction, named food poisoning bacteria) and of enzyme control belongs to the food safety chapter and is only touched here.

## 1. Must cover

Why food is preserved (one block)
- [ ] W1 Preservation means treating food so that spoilage is stopped or slowed and the food can be stored safely for longer. It aims to keep colour, flavour, texture and nutritive value as near as possible to fresh.
- [ ] W2 Advantages (examined 2025O, "discuss two advantages"): reduces food waste and so the environmental footprint; makes food safer (destroys or stops food poisoning organisms); lengthens shelf life; provides out-of-season foods all year; allows perishable foods to be transported and stored, including over long distances; increases variety and choice; improves quality or creates new products (jam, chutney, dried fruit, smoked fish); lets a household use a surplus such as a glut of home-grown fruit or vegetables.
- [ ] W3 Each advantage should be developed in a sentence or two when "discuss" is asked (what it means for the household or the industry), not just named.

Causes of food spoilage (one block; examined 2025O, 2010O, 2009H)
- [ ] C1 Enzymes: protein catalysts naturally present in food; after harvest or slaughter they carry on working, causing over-ripening, softening, browning (enzymic browning of cut apple, banana, potato) and changes in flavour and texture, even when no micro-organism is present.
- [ ] C2 Micro-organisms: bacteria, yeasts and moulds. They cause decay, rot, souring, off-odours, slime and mould growth, and some cause food poisoning. Name the three groups and the kind of spoilage each gives (moulds: fuzzy growth on bread, cheese, jam; yeasts: fermentation of fruit juice and jam, with bubbling and an alcoholic smell; bacteria: souring, putrefaction of protein foods, food poisoning).
- [ ] C3 Oxidation: oxygen reacts with fats (rancidity) and with cut fruit and vegetable surfaces (browning); vitamin C is also lost by oxidation.
- [ ] C4 Moisture loss: through the skins of fruit and vegetables, causing shrivelling, wilting, wrinkling and loss of weight; also drying and hardening of bread and cheese.
- [ ] C5 Incorrect storage temperature, poor hygiene and careless handling: warmth speeds micro-organisms and enzymes; unwashed hands, equipment, pests or raw food carry organisms onto food.
- [ ] C6 Chemical contamination (accepted in the 2009H scheme): insecticide or pesticide residues, antibiotics, metals, cleaning chemicals. Physical damage (bruising) also opens food to spoilage.
- [ ] C7 Putrefaction (assumed): the breakdown of proteins by bacteria, with foul-smelling products; one phrase, as the scheme lists it.

Conditions for growth of micro-organisms (one block; examined 2013O, 2026O; condition-by-condition detail is in the food safety chapter)
- [ ] G1 Micro-organisms need food (nutrients), moisture (available water), warmth (a suitable temperature), time (to multiply), a suitable pH (most bacteria prefer neutral or slightly acid; moulds and yeasts tolerate acid better) and, for aerobic ones, oxygen. Use "warmth or temperature" and "pH or acidity".
- [ ] G2 The danger zone is 5 to 63 °C, in which food poisoning bacteria multiply rapidly. Below 5 °C growth is slowed; above 63 °C it is stopped and most are killed by sustained heat.
- [ ] G3 Under good conditions one bacterial cell divides about every 20 minutes (binary fission), so numbers rise very fast. (Assumed; one clause.)
- [ ] G4 Spores (assumed): some bacteria and all moulds form dormant, resistant spores that survive ordinary heat and cold and start growing when conditions improve.
- [ ] G5 Enzymes work best at a moderate temperature, are slowed by cold, and are inactivated (denatured) by heat or strong acid.

The principles that underlie all methods (one block; the core of the syllabus line)
- [ ] P1 Every method works by removing or controlling one or more of the conditions that micro-organisms and enzymes need, and by preventing re-contamination.
- [ ] P2 Destroy micro-organisms and inactivate enzymes with heat: canning, bottling, jam and chutney making, pasteurisation, sterilisation, blanching. Irradiation also destroys organisms and some enzymes.
- [ ] P3 Slow or stop activity with low temperature: refrigeration slows growth and enzyme action; freezing makes organisms dormant (inactivated, not killed) and makes water unavailable as ice.
- [ ] P4 Remove available water: dehydration (drying, freeze drying) takes water away; sugar and salt tie it up and draw it out of organisms by osmosis (jam, salted fish, cured meat).
- [ ] P5 Alter the pH: vinegar (acetic acid) in pickles and chutney; acids produced in fermentation (lactic acid in yoghurt and sauerkraut, acetic acid in vinegar).
- [ ] P6 Use chemical preservatives that inhibit organisms: sugar, salt, vinegar, smoke, alcohol, permitted additives such as sulphur dioxide, sorbates, benzoates and nitrite.
- [ ] P7 Exclude oxygen and prevent re-entry of organisms: airtight packaging, vacuum sealing, sealed hot jars (a vacuum forms as the contents cool), moisture-proof wraps for the freezer.
- [ ] P8 Prevent re-contamination after treatment: sterilised jars and lids, clean utensils, good hygiene, sealing straight away.
- [ ] P9 Most methods combine principles: jam uses heat, sugar (osmosis), acid and pectin, and a sealed jar; chutney uses heat, vinegar and sugar; smoked fish uses salt, drying and smoke chemicals.
- [ ] P10 A summary table matching each spoilage cause to the principle and method that controls it (one line per cause) is a good revision device.
- [ ] P11 Osmosis (assumed; one sentence): the movement of water through a partially permeable membrane from a weaker solution to a stronger one. A strong sugar or salt solution outside a microbial cell draws water out, so the cell shrivels and cannot multiply.

Classification of the methods (one short block, signposting)
- [ ] M1 Name the methods and group them by principle (low temperature; heat; water removal; chemicals; fermentation; irradiation) so the student sees the structure of the chapter. One line each; no detail.

## 3. Traps

- Preservation does not always kill organisms. Freezing and drying only stop them growing; they restart after thawing or rehydration. Say "inactivated" or "dormant", never "killed", for freezing and drying.
- Spoilage is not only caused by micro-organisms: enzymes, oxidation and moisture loss act with no organisms present. Examiners ask for three or four distinct causes, so each cause is its own point.
- "Preservation" is not the same as "safe storage": refrigerator and cooked-food storage and reheating rules belong to the food safety chapter (safe food preparation). Here refrigeration appears only as a low-temperature principle.
- The structure and reproduction of moulds, yeasts and bacteria, the named food poisoning bacteria, toxic and infectious poisoning, and the control of enzymic spoilage belong to the food safety chapter (Higher Level). Keep them to one-line links here.
- Fermentation, irradiation and the comparative evaluation are Higher Level only and have their own subsections; name them in the classification block without detail and do not put an HL mark on this subsection.
- Packaging materials and labelling law belong to the food industry chapter (packaging and labelling). Mention only airtight sealing as an anti-contamination principle.
- Do not use: the "FAT TOM" name for the growth conditions, because it is a US mnemonic that no Irish marking scheme uses; list the conditions by name (food, moisture, warmth, time, pH, oxygen).
- Do not use: "produce can be bought when in season" as a way preservation reduces the environmental footprint, because buying in season is not preservation; say that preservation lets a surplus be kept instead of thrown away.
- Do not use: "preservatives are used at concentrations that stop growth; a low concentration may encourage growth" as a general statement, because it is unconfirmed outside sugar (a low-sugar jam can ferment); say it only in the sugar and jam context.

## 4. What exams ask

- Name and describe three or four causes of spoilage (15 marks in 2025O; 16 marks in 2010O; a 12-mark Higher question in 2009H). Expected: enzymes, micro-organisms (bacteria, yeasts, moulds), oxidation, moisture loss, incorrect storage temperature, poor hygiene or contamination, chemical contamination. Each cause comes with its visible effect.
- Discuss two advantages of preservation (10 marks, 2025O): each advantage named and developed in a sentence or two.
- True or false and matching items (2015O, 2009O, 2004O): "enzymes present in food cause it to decay" (true); "chutney making is a method of preventing food spoilage" (true); name three methods of preservation with an example food each (heat, freezing and a chemical method, with fruit, vegetables, meat or fish as the foods).
- Name two natural preservatives (2004O): sugar and vinegar; salt, smoking and alcohol are also accepted.
- Suggest a method for a surplus of home-grown fruit or vegetables and explain its principle (2008H): name the method, then the principle in terms of which condition is removed or which organism is destroyed.
- Control of enzymic spoilage (2009H, 2015H) is a Higher Level question on a neighbouring subject; here students need only the idea that heat, acid, cold, removing oxygen and certain chemicals stop enzymes.
- Tip content: always link a method to its principle (the condition removed) and to the cause of spoilage it controls; marks go to the principle, not to the recipe.
