# Package: hom-social-change-s1 — Changes in settlement patterns from rural to urban

- **loId:** `hom-social-change-s1` · **chapterId:** `hom-social-change` · **syllabusRef:** 6.1
- **Syllabus scope:** "The impact of social and economic change on family life", item 1: changes in settlement patterns from rural to urban [6.1]. Also examined: 2022O, 2018O, 2009O; initiatives to keep population in rural areas (2012O, 2006H). Settings: comparing family life a hundred years ago with today; interviewing an older relative or community member. No [HL] items in this subsection.
- **Prepared by:** a higher model, 2026-11-23. Common to Ordinary and Higher Level. The subsection has three jobs: why people moved from the country to towns and cities, what that did to family life in both places, and what the State and its agencies did to keep people and jobs in rural areas. No census figures: they change and the notes must not quote them.

## 1. Must cover

Settlement patterns and the vocabulary (one block)
- [ ] R1 Settlement pattern: the way a population is spread across the land, in scattered rural homes and farms, villages, towns and cities. Over the last century Ireland's pattern shifted towards towns and cities; this is urbanisation (the growing share of the population living in urban areas). Say "a rising share", never a figure.
- [ ] R2 Rural depopulation: a fall in the number of people living in the countryside, through movement to towns or emigration. It is most marked in remote areas and where farm employment has fallen.
- [ ] R3 The movement is not one-way or uniform. Towns, commuter belts around cities and some rural areas have grown while remote areas lost people. Past papers have asked why the number of people in rural areas increased (R14), so the notes say the picture is mixed.
- [ ] R4 (assumed) Rural means countryside, farms and small villages; urban means towns and cities, with a concentration of jobs, colleges and services. One sentence.

Why people moved to urban areas (one block; examiners ask for three or four reasons, each explained)
- [ ] R5 Employment: manufacturing, services and office work are concentrated in towns and cities. Larger, mechanised farms need fewer workers, so farm jobs fell, and the children of small farms could not all inherit land.
- [ ] R6 Education: third-level colleges and specialist courses are in urban centres, and many young people move for college and do not return.
- [ ] R7 Services and infrastructure: hospitals, shops, public transport, entertainment and leisure facilities and fast broadband are more available and better in towns and cities.
- [ ] R8 Housing: a wider choice of rented and bought housing, including apartments for people with no land.
- [ ] R9 Family and social ties: moving to be near friends, relatives, a partner or a partner's workplace; a bigger social life for young adults.
- [ ] R10 Young adults are the group most likely to leave, so rural areas are left with an older population and fewer children.

Effects on family life in urban areas (one block)
- [ ] R11 Positive: easier access to childcare, schools, health care, shops and entertainment; a wider choice of jobs so that both parents can find work; more leisure facilities. Negative: long commutes from suburbs reduce time at home and add pressure to family routines; high housing costs and high rents (heavy demand for limited housing) can delay marriage and children, squeeze family budgets and cause overcrowding; congestion, noise and air pollution; crime and antisocial behaviour in some areas; less contact with grandparents and the extended family, so less daily childcare and practical help; less community spirit, with neighbours who do not know each other.

Effects on family life and on life in rural areas (one block)
- [ ] R12 Older people left behind can be isolated and lonely, with fewer relatives nearby for daily support. Schools, shops, post offices, banks, public transport, Garda stations and clubs lose customers and close, which makes daily life harder for those who remain and makes the area less attractive to the next generation (a cycle of decline). Smaller schools mean fewer classmates, and teams and clubs struggle to field players. Whole families may leave, weakening neighbourly and family support networks. Family farms may have no heir.
- [ ] R13 Positive points for those who stay: lower housing costs, a strong sense of community, lower crime and pollution, more space and safer play for children, and a slower pace of life.
- [ ] R14 Why some rural areas have grown (past-paper question on rising rural numbers): better roads and transport let people live in the country and commute; cheaper housing and living costs; lower crime and pollution; improved local services, leisure and community facilities; broadband and working from home; rural resettlement and local-authority housing moves.

Initiatives to keep population and jobs in rural areas (one block; examiners ask "name and give details of one")
- [ ] R15 Redeployment (decentralisation) of government departments and state agencies to rural and regional towns, creating public-sector jobs outside Dublin.
- [ ] R16 Rural resettlement schemes that help families (including city families) move to and settle in the countryside, keeping schools and services viable.
- [ ] R17 IDA Ireland: the agency responsible for attracting investment from overseas companies, encouraging them to locate in regional towns as well as the cities, creating jobs.
- [ ] R18 Enterprise Ireland: helps Irish firms to start, grow and export, including firms outside Dublin, so jobs are created locally.
- [ ] R19 Local Enterprise Offices (LEOs): in each local authority area, supply advice, mentoring, training and small grants to micro and small businesses, so local people can create their own jobs (cottage industries, craft and food businesses, tourism).
- [ ] R20 Údarás na Gaeltachta: the regional authority responsible for economic, social and cultural development of the Gaeltacht areas, encouraging enterprise and jobs there.
- [ ] R21 LEADER: the EU-funded rural development programme that supports community-led local projects (village renewal, tourism, enterprise). Leave out amounts.
- [ ] R22 Investment in roads, public transport, electricity and telecommunications, especially broadband (the National Broadband Plan), which makes rural living and remote or hybrid working practical; remote-working hubs in towns.
- [ ] R23 Community employment and training programmes that give rural people skills and work experience (for example in childcare, horticulture, caring).
- [ ] R24 Answer format: name the initiative (about 4 marks), then give two details of what it does and how it keeps people or jobs in the area.

Settings (one short block)
- [ ] R25 Compare family life a hundred years ago and today (who lived on the land, size of family, school attendance, leisure); interview an older relative or community member about when people left the area, why, and what changed.

## 3. Traps

- Do not use: any census figure, percentage or phrase such as "almost two-thirds of the population live in urban areas" (it appears in a past-paper stem), because the urban share changes census to census.
- Do not use: County Enterprise Boards as a current body, because they were dissolved in 2014 and replaced by Local Enterprise Offices (the old notes say only "local enterprise supports"; name the LEOs).
- Do not use: dates, job totals, named locations or a named year of a decentralisation programme, because they are unconfirmed and the programme was scaled back.
- Do not use: a named rural resettlement scheme with dates, funding or a founding body, because only the type of initiative named in the marking scheme is confirmed.
- Do not use: FÁS as the body running training, because it was replaced by SOLAS and the Education and Training Boards (relevant if a community training programme is named).
- Rural depopulation and emigration are different: emigration means leaving the country; internal migration means moving within Ireland. Both can cause rural decline.
- Urbanisation is a rising share of people in towns and cities, not just population growth.
- Do not claim urban life is worse or rural life better; examiners reward effects on family life in both directions and in both places. Each point must be about family life, not just geography.
- Neighbouring subsections: the types of family and the decline of the extended family belong to the family structures chapter; attitudes, working hours, education and welfare belong to the other subsections of this chapter; poverty, unemployment and community co-operatives belong to the unemployment, poverty and social responses chapter; housing choice and finance belong to the housing and personal finance chapters. Mention them only as a cause or effect in one phrase.
- Do not describe the Gaeltacht or Irish-language policy beyond the one line in R20.

## 4. What exams ask

- Give three or four reasons more people live in towns and cities, explained: jobs, education, services, housing, family and friends. Ordinary Level asks for three; the marks go on the explanation, so each reason needs a "because".
- Explain the impact of movement from rural to urban areas on family life, and on life in rural areas (older people, services, schools). A scheme rewards effects on both.
- Discuss two effects of more people living in urban areas (more services and leisure, congestion, noise and air pollution, education access, crime) and compare with rural areas.
- Why did the number of people in some rural areas increase? Expected: transport, cheaper housing and living costs, lower crime and pollution, improved services, remote working, resettlement.
- Name and give details of one initiative or organisation that keeps population or jobs in rural areas: redeployment of government departments, rural resettlement, IDA Ireland, Enterprise Ireland, local enterprise support, with two details each.
- Higher Level analysis of social and economic change (20 marks): rural depopulation and urbanisation are one of several changes, alongside women's work, education and attitudes; each change must be tied to a family effect.
