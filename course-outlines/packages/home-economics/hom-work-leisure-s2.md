# Package: hom-work-leisure-s2 — Changes in patterns of work

- **loId:** `hom-work-leisure-s2` · **chapterId:** `hom-work-leisure` · **syllabusRef:** 6.3.1
- **Syllabus scope:** [HL] "the effect of developing technology on industry; the decline in primary and secondary industries and growth in services; increased educational requirements; increased participation by women; improved working conditions; increasing flexibility in working hours, e.g. job-sharing, flexible hours, parental leave." Settings: investigating Ireland's current occupational structure compared with fifty years ago. The whole subsection is Higher Level only (the outline marks it [HL]; Appendix C lists "Changes in patterns of work"). Ordinary Level papers still ask about the increased participation of women and about flexible working in a simple form (2005O, 2008O, 2014O, 2016O, 2024O, 2026O), so the women and flexibility blocks must be clear enough for those questions.
- **Prepared by:** a higher model, 2026-11-23. Neighbours: the definitions of work and satisfaction belong to the first subsection; the effect of two earners on family life (role overload, role conflict) belongs to the subsection on reconciling employment with family; unemployment, IDA and foreign investment belong to the unemployment chapter.

## 1. Must cover

The effect of developing technology on industry [HL]
- [ ] T1 Technology (machines, robots, computers, electronic communication) automates repetitive and manual tasks, so the same output needs fewer workers. Jobs shift to programming, supervising, maintaining and operating the technology.
- [ ] T2 Positive effects: boring repetitive tasks removed; interesting skilled jobs created; safer, cleaner, healthier workplaces (dangerous tasks done by machines); higher productivity, quality and consistency; shorter working hours and more leisure time; home working (teleworking) and electronic communication; more goods and services.
- [ ] T3 Negative effects: loss of unskilled and manual jobs; need for retraining; companies can relocate production to low-cost countries; difficulty switching off from work; less face-to-face contact; skills becoming out of date; some workers suffer stress or isolation.
- [ ] T4 Technology also changed office and service work: banking, shopping and booking online, so fewer counter and clerical jobs and more technical ones.

Decline of primary and secondary industries and growth in services [HL]
- [ ] I1 Primary industry takes raw materials from the land and sea: agriculture, fishing, forestry, mining/quarrying (peat). Secondary industry processes and manufactures those materials into finished goods: food processing, clothing, engineering, construction. Tertiary (service) industry provides services: retail, banking and finance, tourism and hospitality, health, education, transport, media, IT.
- [ ] I2 Trend: fewer people work in farming and traditional manufacturing; most workers now work in services. Reasons: mechanisation and automation of farming and factories, falling number of small farms, relocation of some manufacturing to lower-cost countries, rising demand for services as incomes rose, growth of tourism, finance and technology firms.
- [ ] I3 Effects on people: manual and unskilled workers displaced; rural areas lost jobs; urban areas gained service jobs; more part-time and shift work in services; new demand for qualified workers.
- [ ] I4 Setting: compare Ireland's occupational structure now with fifty years ago (farming and factory work then; services and professional work now). Use the pattern without figures.

Increased educational requirements [HL]
- [ ] E1 Service and technology jobs need literacy, numeracy, communication and computing skills. Employers use qualifications to select between applicants, and more applicants hold them. The Leaving Certificate is the minimum for many jobs; many need a further education or higher qualification; qualifications widen employment options and allow career advancement.
- [ ] E2 Early school leavers and unskilled workers find it difficult to acquire work and are most at risk of unemployment and low pay.
- [ ] E3 Continuing education: adults return to education to reskill after job loss, keep pace with technology, improve promotion prospects, change career. Distance learning, evening and part-time courses, and adult education supports exist. (The detail of adult education schemes belongs to the education chapter; one or two named examples are enough.)

Increased participation by women [HL; also OL]
- [ ] M1 Many more women, including married women and mothers, are now in paid work than in the middle of the twentieth century. Reasons (examiners ask for three or four): better education and qualifications; equality legislation and equal pay; a wish for independence, self-esteem, status and social contact; rising living, housing and child care costs, so a second income is needed; growth of service jobs and part-time work; better conditions and flexible arrangements (job-sharing, term-time work, maternity and parental leave, career breaks); more child care facilities and labour-saving appliances; smaller families; more marital breakdown or lone parenthood, which makes earning necessary; changing attitudes to women working.
- [ ] M2 Effects: women have financial independence; more dual-earner families (family effects are dealt with in another subsection).

Improved working conditions [HL]
- [ ] C1 Better pay and the introduction of the minimum wage; pensions; paid holidays and statutory leave; maternity leave, parental leave and force majeure leave; shorter hours; safer, cleaner workplaces; employment protected by legislation (the Acts are in the young persons subsection); facilities for working parents (crèches, breastfeeding breaks); equal pay and equality law.
- [ ] C2 Link: these improvements made paid work more attractive and enabled women to take part in the workforce more fully (2007H asked this directly).

Increasing flexibility in working hours [HL; flexible arrangements also OL]
- [ ] F1 Flexitime: the employee chooses start and finish times within agreed core hours.
- [ ] F2 Job-sharing: two people share one full-time job, dividing hours, pay and duties.
- [ ] F3 Part-time work and term-time working (work arranged around the school year); reduced or compressed working week (longer days, fewer days); teleworking or remote working from home.
- [ ] F4 Career break: an agreed period of unpaid leave after which the employee returns to the job.
- [ ] F5 Parental leave: under the Parental Leave Acts, parents are entitled to unpaid leave from work to look after a young child. Currently 26 weeks per child, for a child under 12 (longer if the child has a disability or long-term illness); it can be taken in one block or in agreed blocks. Related leaves exist under other Acts (maternity leave under the Maternity Protection Acts, paternity leave and parent's leave under their own Acts, carer's leave, force majeure leave) and can be named in one line each, without durations.
- [ ] F5a Flexible and remote working requests: under the Work Life Balance and Miscellaneous Provisions Act 2023 (the flexible and remote working provisions commenced on 6 March 2024), parents and carers of children up to 12 (or up to 16 if the child is disabled or long-term ill) and carers can request flexible working, and any employee can request remote working. The employer must consider the request and reply in writing within set time limits, giving reasons for any refusal. The Act also provides paid domestic-violence leave (5 days, from November 2023) and unpaid medical care leave (from 3 July 2023).
- [ ] F6 Effects on work-life balance: more time with children, less stress, easier to combine work and family, helps retain staff and widens the labour pool; against: lower income, slower promotion, work may spill into home life, difficulty switching off, resentment from colleagues, shortfall in pension.
- [ ] F7 Effects of all the changes together on individuals and families (the 2015H and 2023H questions): changing availability of work, retraining, dual-earner households, need for child care, work-life balance, more leisure for some and less for others.

## 3. Traps

- Do not use: "Parental leave is 14 weeks unpaid, up to the child's eighth birthday" (from the 2015 marking scheme), because it is superseded: the entitlement is now 26 weeks per child up to age 12.
- Do not use any statistic on women in the workforce (the past papers quoted 5% and 56%) or on the share of workers in agriculture, industry or services, because they change.
- "Tertiary" means services, not third-level education.
- Teleworking and job-sharing are working arrangements, not types of leave. Parental leave is not paid by the employer by right; it is unpaid.
- The shift from primary to tertiary is about where people work, not a claim that farming or manufacturing has stopped; both still exist and produce more with fewer workers.
- Do not turn this into unemployment, poverty or foreign investment (IDA, tax reliefs) content; those are in the unemployment and poverty chapter.
- Dual-earner family effects (role overload, role conflict, sharing of tasks) belong to the family-responsibilities subsection; one clause here only.
- Do not name Springboard+, SOLAS, FETAC or specific adult schemes beyond one example; education schemes are another chapter.

## 4. What exams ask

- Discuss how technology (and often increased educational requirements or flexible hours) has affected patterns of work and work availability (2007H, 2013H, 2019H, 2023H, 2025H; 15 to 30 marks): automation and fewer manual jobs, new skilled jobs, relocation to low-cost countries, home working, switching off, decline of primary and secondary industry and service growth, qualifications needed.
- Analyse positive and negative effects of technology on work and employment (2005H, 2019H): at least two of each.
- Reasons for more women in paid employment (2005O, 2014O, 2024O, 2026O): three or four reasons each explained: education, equality and equal pay, independence and self-esteem, cost of living, flexible and part-time work, better conditions, child care, social contact.
- How improved working conditions enabled women to participate (2007H): flexible hours, parental and maternity leave, job-sharing, teleworking, career break, term-time work, crèches, equal pay, minimum wage.
- Flexible working and work-life balance (2015H): give examples with explanation, and one clear benefit and one drawback.
- Discuss educational requirements and employment (2019H, 2025H, 2026O): qualifications widen options; early leavers struggle; reskilling; the Leaving Certificate as a minimum for many jobs.
- Changes in availability of work for individuals and families (2015H): one individual effect and one family effect plus others.
