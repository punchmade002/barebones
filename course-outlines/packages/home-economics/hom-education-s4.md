# Package: hom-education-s4 — Equality of opportunity in education

- **loId:** `hom-education-s4` · **chapterId:** `hom-education` · **syllabusRef:** 6.2
- **Syllabus scope:** "Equality of opportunity in education [6.2] **[HL]**: with reference to gender inequity, socio-economic status, disadvantaged students and early school leavers." No "Also examined" items listed, but past papers ask all four strands (2008H, 2018H, 2024H and an earlier HL question on gender and early school leavers). The whole subsection is Higher Level only (Appendix C lists "equality of opportunity" at 6.2). Every block heading ends " (Higher Level only)".
- **Prepared by:** a higher model, 2026-11-23. This subsection explains the unequal chances some students have and why, with a named scheme or two as evidence of the response. The initiatives themselves are described in the next subsection; give a scheme here as one clause (for example "DEIS gives extra supports") and no more. Factors in general are in the achievement-factors subsection; this subsection is about groups.

## 1. Must cover

Meaning of equality of opportunity (Higher Level only) (one block)
- [ ] Q1 [HL] Equality of opportunity in education means that every person has a fair chance to enter, take part in and progress through education, whatever their gender, family background, income, ability, disability, ethnicity or where they live; it is about access to opportunity, not guaranteeing the same result.
- [ ] Q2 [HL] Free education helps: the State funds primary and second-level education, and the Free Fees Initiative pays third-level tuition for eligible undergraduates; yet some groups still have lower participation and results, so the State adds targeted supports. Equity: extra help for those with greater need so that they can reach the same level (assumed contrast: equal access v equal outcomes).
- [ ] Q3 [HL] The Equal Status Acts 2000 to 2018 prohibit discrimination in education (in schools, colleges and in access to courses) on grounds that include gender, civil status, family status, age, disability, race, religion, sexual orientation and membership of the Traveller community; there is an exemption for single-sex schools; the Education (Admission to Schools) Act 2018 bars schools from using religion as a selection criterion when oversubscribed (the minority-faith-school exception remains). The Education Act 1998 sets out equality of access as a goal of the system. (Use year and what they provide; no section numbers.)

Gender inequity (Higher Level only) (one block)
- [ ] G1 [HL] Historical background: girls had fewer opportunities in the past (for example, fewer girls stayed on to Leaving Certificate or went to third level); today girls' participation and attainment at second and third level are, on average, higher than boys'.
- [ ] G2 [HL] Boys are more likely than girls to leave school early, to have lower literacy and to achieve lower grades on average; reasons include peer pressure against being seen to work hard, lower expectations and less engagement with some subjects and teaching methods.
- [ ] G3 [HL] Subject choice remains gendered: girls less often choose Physics, Engineering, Construction Studies, Technology and Design and Communication Graphics; boys less often choose Home Economics, Languages and Art. Single-sex schools can limit the subjects on offer (for example, a girls' school without technical subjects, a boys' school without Home Economics). This affects later course and career choices (fewer women in engineering and IT; fewer men in caring and teaching roles at primary level).
- [ ] G4 [HL] Influences: stereotypes held by parents, teachers and peers; textbooks and career guidance; role models. Responses: less stereotyped textbooks and resources, mixed-gender subject access, encouragement into non-traditional subjects (for example STEM promotion for girls), gender balance in career guidance, equality legislation (Q3).
- [ ] G5 [HL] Exam use (a past HL paper on gender equity): give gender inequity as a statement with evidence, then a response: both sexes are represented at third level, textbooks are less stereotyped, subject choice depends on whether the school is single-sex or co-educational, girls often attain more highly than boys, boys are more likely to leave early.

Socio-economic status (Higher Level only) (one block)
- [ ] SE1 [HL] Socio-economic status (SES) is a family's position based on income, occupation and education (social class). Students from higher groups (professional, managerial) are more likely to complete second level and go on to third level; those from lower groups are more likely to leave early and are under-represented at third level.
- [ ] SE2 [HL] Costs: books, equipment, uniforms, trips, subject charges, school bags and technology, transport, and in third level accommodation and living costs can exclude low-income families. Partial relief: back-to-school clothing and footwear allowance, book rental schemes and grants, school meals and school transport schemes, free fees and SUSI grants, and the Back to Education Allowance with its Cost of Education Allowance add-on for participants with dependent children (each one clause; detail in the next subsection). They help but do not remove the wider gap in resources.
- [ ] SE3 [HL] Higher-status families usually offer: higher expectations and aspiration, more help with homework and course choices, a quiet place to study, grinds and extra-curricular activities, social contacts and knowledge of the system. Lower-status families may face a cycle of poverty and low expectations, poor housing or crowding, and pressure to earn.
- [ ] SE4 [HL] School-related: location in a disadvantaged area (fewer resources, more early leaving); teacher expectations; streaming or banding into lower classes, which can limit subject level and grades.
- [ ] SE5 [HL] Responses: DEIS (Delivering Equality of Opportunity in Schools) gives extra support to schools in disadvantaged communities; HEAR gives reduced-points third-level entry to students from disadvantaged backgrounds; financial supports as in SE2.
- [ ] SE6 [HL] Exam use (2008H comment, 2024H analyse, both 15 marks): three points, each one linking cause and effect: for example costs exclude low-income families but allowances and grants offset them; higher status families give more opportunity and more third-level progression; DEIS and HEAR respond.

Disadvantaged students (Higher Level only) (one block)
- [ ] D1 [HL] Groups at risk of educational disadvantage: students from low-income and unemployed families, Travellers, Roma, students from migrant, asylum-seeking or refugee families, students with disabilities or special educational needs, homeless young people and young people in care, those in disadvantaged areas and lone-parent households.
- [ ] D2 [HL] Barriers: poverty, poor housing and health, lack of study space, language (English as an additional language), qualifications from abroad not recognised, discrimination, low expectations, lack of role models, and in some communities negative experiences of school, leading to low attendance and early leaving.
- [ ] D3 [HL] Responses to name: DEIS, language support, learning support and SNAs, Traveller and Roma education supports, grants and allowances, school meals and transport, the Home School Community Liaison scheme (clause only each; detail in the next subsection).
- [ ] D4 [HL] Exam use (2018H, 15 marks: "equality of opportunity at second level for students at risk of disadvantage and social exclusion"): three points with a scheme each: DEIS (school completion programme, homework clubs, mentoring, school meals, home school community liaison, enhanced book grants), learning support and SNAs, language assistance and equipment, exam accommodations, smaller pupil-teacher ratio.

Early school leavers (Higher Level only) (one block)
- [ ] L1 [HL] An early school leaver is a young person who leaves school before completing senior cycle (before the Leaving Certificate or an equivalent programme).
- [ ] L2 [HL] Causes: poor attendance and truancy, low literacy and numeracy, learning difficulties, bullying, family problems, poverty and the cost of staying on, negative peer pressure, a cycle of poverty and low parental expectation, living in a disadvantaged area, dislike of school or a lack of suitable programmes, pull of jobs, pregnancy, health or addiction problems (2015 OL, four reasons; 2018H).
- [ ] L3 [HL] Consequences: few or no qualifications, unemployment or low-paid unskilled work, welfare dependence, poverty, low self-esteem, poor health, social exclusion, crime risk, and less access to further education and training; the next generation is also affected (cycle of poverty); the State loses skills and pays more welfare.
- [ ] L4 [HL] Responses: School Completion Programme, Home School Community Liaison scheme, DEIS, the Educational Welfare Service of Tusla (school attendance), JCSP and LCA, Youthreach, Back to Education Allowance, VTOS (clause each).
- [ ] L5 [HL] Exam use: "evaluate two initiatives aimed at equality of opportunity with reference to early school leavers": name the scheme, what it does, whether it works.

## 3. Traps

- Do not use: any early school leaving rate, retention figure or number from a past paper's chart (for example the 2009/2010 figures), because they change each year.
- Do not use: "National Educational Welfare Board (NEWB)" as the current attendance body, because it is part of Tusla (the Child and Family Agency) since 2014; marking schemes before then name NEWB.
- Do not use: the EU/ESRI definition of early school leaving (age 18 to 24 with no more than lower secondary), because the syllabus and marking schemes use the simple "left before senior cycle" definition. Give the outline's definition.
- Do not use: "boys are less intelligent" or any ability claim; the gap is in engagement, expectation and literacy, and no reason given should be an ability claim.
- Do not say girls are disadvantaged overall: at second and third level girls on average achieve more; the inequity is in subject choice and later careers and at pay and promotion (neighbouring chapter on work).
- Confusable: equality of opportunity (fair chance) vs equality of outcome (same result); socio-economic status (family position) vs poverty (neighbouring chapter on poverty) vs disadvantage (a student at risk of low achievement through several causes).
- Detailed descriptions of DEIS, School Completion Programme, HEAR, DARE, JCSP, Youthreach and VTOS belong to the next subsection (contemporary initiatives); give only a clause here. Types of school, programmes and special needs supports belong to the provision subsection.
- Do not discuss unemployment or poverty in detail (their chapters).
- Do not assert that streaming is practised in all schools; say "some schools group students by ability, which can limit..." only.

## 4. What exams ask

- Equality of opportunity in education with reference to gender and early school leavers (HL, two parts): gender (both sexes at third level, less stereotyped textbooks, subject choice by single-sex or mixed school, girls often attain more, boys leave earlier) and early school leavers (consequences and the supports that keep or return them: School Completion Programme, DEIS, attendance tracking, BTEA, Youthreach, VTOS).
- Analyse or comment on how socio-economic status affects equality of opportunity (2008 HL 15 marks; 2024 HL 15 marks): cost barriers and partial relief, expectations and progression to third level, DEIS and HEAR.
- Evaluate equality of opportunity at second level for students at risk of disadvantage and social exclusion (2018 HL 15 marks): three points with named supports.
- Evaluate two initiatives aimed at equality for early school leavers (HL): DEIS, Youthreach, LCA, VTOS and others, each with what it does.
- Short answers: groups regarded as disadvantaged; two reasons students leave early; two effects of leaving early.
