# Package: hom-cooking-methods-s1 — Physical and chemical changes during preparation and cooking

- **loId:** `hom-cooking-methods-s1` · **chapterId:** `hom-cooking-methods` · **syllabusRef:** 1.3.4
- **Syllabus scope:** enzymic browning, non-enzymic browning, loss of nutrients (physical and chemical changes during preparation and cooking). Settings: an investigation to assess the control of enzymic browning, e.g. on a fresh apple. Also examined: 2023H, 2021H, 2019H, 2009O, 2009H. No Higher Level marks in this entry; the outline marks nothing [HL] here, so mark nothing.
- **Prepared by:** a higher model, 2026-11-23. Common to both levels. Exams ask for "a physical and a chemical change", "two chemical changes with an example", "enzymic and non-enzymic browning" and "two physical changes with an example". Keep the physical/chemical split clear, because the schemes accept several items under both headings (see Traps).

## 1. Must cover

Physical and chemical change (one block)
- [ ] A1 A physical change alters the size, shape, state, colour or texture of food without forming a new substance; a chemical change alters the molecular make-up of food and forms new substances (new colour, flavour, texture). Heat usually drives both during cooking.

Physical changes (one block, a list of named changes each with a food example)
- [ ] P1 Coagulation of protein: heat unfolds (denatures) egg, milk and fish proteins, which bond into a set structure; egg white turns from clear to white and opaque, fish flesh from translucent to opaque, custard and scrambled egg set. (Protein structure is in the protein chapter; use "denature" without redefining it.)
- [ ] P2 Gelatinisation: starch granules absorb water and swell when heated in liquid, thickening sauces, gravies, custard and porridge. Heat can make granules swell and burst.
- [ ] P3 Tenderising: long, moist cooking converts collagen in connective tissue to gelatine, so tough cuts become tender. (Cuts and tenderising methods belong to the meat chapter; one line here.)
- [ ] P4 Shrinkage: meat and fish lose water and melted fat, so they become smaller and lighter; fat melts out of meat.
- [ ] P5 Increase in size: dried peas, beans and fruit, rice and pasta absorb water and swell; whisked egg foam, sponge and bread rise and increase in volume.
- [ ] P6 Colour change: meat turns from red or pink to brown or grey as myoglobin is heated; green vegetables turn olive-brown with prolonged cooking or acid (chlorophyll is altered); carrots and other orange vegetables stay colourful.
- [ ] P7 Softening: heat softens the cellulose and breaks down pectin in plant cell walls, so vegetables and fruit become tender; cell water is lost.
- [ ] P8 Loss of nutrients (water-soluble vitamins dissolving in cooking water) is listed as a physical change by the marking scheme (2009H); it is taught in its own block below and cross-referred here in one phrase.
- [ ] P9 Increased digestibility and safety: heat destroys most micro-organisms and makes starch and protein easier to digest (one line only; the aims of cooking are in the next subsection).

Chemical changes (one block)
- [ ] C1 Maillard reaction: a chemical change in which amino acids from protein react with reducing sugars such as glucose under dry heat to give brown colour and savoury roasted flavour. The scheme calls it the non-enzymic browning of protein foods (e.g. roast potatoes, roast meat, bread crust, toast).
- [ ] C2 Caramelisation: sugar (sucrose) heated strongly melts and darkens to caramel, with a sweet slightly bitter flavour; no protein is needed (caramel, crème caramel, toffee).
- [ ] C3 Dextrinisation: dry heat breaks starch into shorter chains called dextrins, which brown and give a crisp surface (toast, the crust of cakes and bread).
- [ ] C4 Chemical raising agents: bicarbonate of soda reacts with an acid (e.g. buttermilk) and releases carbon dioxide, which raises soda bread; baking powder holds bicarbonate of soda plus an acid and releases carbon dioxide when wet and heated. The 2009 ordinary-level scheme accepts a chemical raising agent as a chemical change in bread and cakes.
- [ ] C5 Biological raising: yeast ferments sugar to carbon dioxide and alcohol, raising bread dough (the gas expands in the oven; the yeast is killed by baking). One line.
- [ ] C6 Tenderising by acid or enzymes (e.g. marinade, a meat tenderiser) is also accepted as a chemical change by the 2019H scheme; one line cross-reference to the meat notes.

Enzymic browning (one block)
- [ ] E1 What it is: browning of cut, bruised or peeled fruit and vegetables caused by an enzyme (polyphenol oxidase; the scheme says "oxidase") in the damaged cells reacting with phenolic compounds and oxygen from the air. It needs no heat. Examples: apple, banana, pear, avocado, potato, mushroom.
- [ ] E2 Why it happens: cutting breaks cell walls, so enzyme, phenolic compounds and oxygen meet and brown pigments form.
- [ ] E3 Control: stop oxygen reaching the surface (cover with water, sugar syrup, cling film or a coating of dressing); lower the pH with lemon juice or another acid; vitamin C (ascorbic acid) also prevents browning; heat inactivates the enzyme (blanching before freezing; cooking); cold slows it but does not stop it; work quickly and cut just before serving.
- [ ] E4 Disadvantage and use: browning spoils appearance, flavour and vitamin C content of fruit and vegetables; it is wanted in the making of black tea, which is a deliberate use. (One line; optional extra, not examined so far.)

Investigation (one block, "Investigation" type)
- [ ] V1 Investigation: controlling enzymic browning on a fresh apple. Aim, hypothesis, a variable table (independent: treatment such as untreated control, lemon juice, sugar syrup, salt water or cold water; dependent: degree of browning, rated on a colour scale at fixed times; controlled: same apple variety, ripeness, slice thickness, soaking time, temperature, timing), method steps, safety and hygiene, expected result and an evaluation that notes subjectivity and the benefit of repeating the test.

Non-enzymic browning (one block)
- [ ] N1 Non-enzymic browning needs heat and no enzymes. Three processes: Maillard reaction, caramelisation, dextrinisation (C1 to C3 used for the definitions; this block ties them together, states that the three together make up the "non-enzymic" side, and says which food molecule is involved in each: protein with sugar, sugar alone, starch).
- [ ] N2 Conditions: dry heat and a dry surface favour browning; boiled or steamed food stays pale because water holds the surface at about 100 °C. Roasting, grilling, baking and frying brown food.

Loss of nutrients (one block)
- [ ] L1 Water-soluble vitamins (C and the B group) dissolve into cooking water and are lost when it is discarded. Vitamin C is also destroyed by heat and by oxidation (exposure to air).
- [ ] L2 Fat-soluble vitamins (A, D, E, K) are more stable to heat but are lost in melted fat that is drained from meat or fried food.
- [ ] L3 Soluble minerals (e.g. potassium) and some sugars dissolve into cooking water too.
- [ ] L4 What increases loss: soaking and cutting into small pieces before cooking, cooking in a lot of water, cooking for a long time, keeping vegetables warm for a long time, peeling thickly, adding bicarbonate of soda (destroys vitamin C and thiamine).
- [ ] L5 How to reduce loss: prepare just before cooking; use a sharp knife; keep skins on or peel thinly; cook briefly in a little water with the lid on; steam, stir-fry or microwave; serve soon after cooking; use the cooking water in soups, sauces and gravy (a past scheme lists: little water, lid on, use the liquid, no alkalis or copper pans, cook al dente).
- [ ] L6 (assumed) The vitamins and minerals themselves, their solubility and functions are in the vitamin and mineral chapters; do not re-teach them.

## 3. Traps

- Physical versus chemical: the schemes accept tenderising, nutrient loss and colour change under physical, and tenderising and raised volume (chemical raising agents) under chemical, in different years. State the safest placement (coagulation, gelatinisation, shrinkage, increase in size, nutrient loss as physical; Maillard, caramelisation, dextrinisation, chemical raising agents as chemical) and mention that tenderising can be given for either when a question allows it.
- Enzymic browning needs no heat and involves an enzyme; the three non-enzymic processes need heat and no enzyme. Don't write "enzymic browning in toast".
- Maillard needs protein (amino acids) plus sugar; caramelisation is sugar only; dextrinisation is starch only. Don't swap them.
- Enzymic browning and enzymic spoilage in storage and freezing belong to the preservation chapter (blanching in detail); here one clause each.
- Marinades and tenderising methods belong to the meat chapter; raising agents in detail belong to the cereals chapter.
- Coagulation of egg in detail (egg structure, foams) belongs to the dairy and eggs chapter; here give the one-line example.
- Do not use: "iron leaches into cooking water", because iron is mostly insoluble and little is lost; name potassium (and other soluble minerals) only.
- Do not use: "biscuits" and "the golden top of a crumble" as dextrinisation examples, because those surfaces brown mainly by Maillard and caramelisation as well; the scheme's examples are toast and cakes.
- Don't give temperatures for browning, or a figure for percentage vitamin C lost.

## 4. What exams ask

- Describe one physical and one chemical change in cooking, or two physical, or two chemical (the commonest form, 6 marks): a named change plus an example food each, with a brief reason (e.g. Maillard: amino acids plus sugar under dry heat, roast potatoes browning).
- Explain enzymic and non-enzymic browning (6 marks): enzymic = oxidase in cut apple meets air and the flesh browns, no heat; non-enzymic = Maillard, caramelisation or dextrinisation under heat.
- Complete statements about heat on protein: coagulation of egg protein; Maillard as non-enzymic browning of protein foods; fish turning opaque.
- Ways to prevent enzymic browning in cut apple or banana (lemon juice, water, blanching, cold) and why each works.
- Ways of keeping vitamin C when preparing and cooking vegetables (little water, lid on, short time, use the liquid, steam or stir-fry, no bicarbonate, prepare just before cooking), the usual 10-mark list.
- Practical-style: the apple browning investigation, fair test, results and conclusion.
