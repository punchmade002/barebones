## 1. Must cover

Classification and shared features (one block)
- [ ] F1 Vitamins are organic compounds needed in very small amounts for normal growth, health and chemical reactions in the body; the body cannot make enough of most of them, so they must come from food (assumed; vitamins are first introduced as a nutrient group in the energy and nutrition work). Two classes: fat-soluble (A, D, E, K) and water-soluble (B group, C). A past paper asks students to name the two classes with one example of each.
- [ ] F2 Fat-soluble vitamins dissolve in fat and are absorbed in the small intestine with dietary fat (assumed digestion link). Diets very low in fat, or conditions that stop fat being absorbed, can therefore reduce vitamin intake.
- [ ] F3 They are stored in the liver and in fatty (adipose) tissue, so the body keeps a reserve and deficiency develops slowly. The same storage means excess (usually from supplements or very large amounts of liver) can build up to harmful levels (toxicity); water-soluble vitamins are mostly lost in urine instead.
- [ ] F4 Vitamin A in pregnancy: high intakes (supplements, large amounts of liver) are avoided because excess retinol can harm the developing baby.
- [ ] F5 RDA (recommended dietary allowance) is the daily amount that meets the needs of almost everyone in a group; it differs with age, sex, pregnancy and breastfeeding. State the idea; give no amounts (see Traps).

Vitamin A: retinol and beta-carotene
- [ ] A1 Two forms: retinol (preformed vitamin A, only in animal foods) and beta-carotene (a plant pigment and a provitamin: the body converts it to retinol).
- [ ] A2 Sources of retinol: liver, fish liver oils, oily fish, eggs, butter, full-fat milk and cheese, and margarine/spreads to which vitamin A is added (fortified). Sources of beta-carotene: carrots, sweet potato, red and orange peppers, tomatoes, apricots, mango, and dark green leafy vegetables such as spinach and kale (the green hides the orange pigment).
- [ ] A3 Functions: forms rhodopsin (visual purple), the light-sensitive pigment in the rod cells of the retina that allows vision in dim light (retinol combines with the protein opsin); keeps the surface tissues (skin and the mucous membranes lining the eyes, mouth, respiratory and digestive tracts) healthy; supports growth and the immune system; beta-carotene (and vitamin A) act as antioxidants.
- [ ] A4 Deficiency: night blindness (the first sign); xerophthalmia (dry, damaged cornea, can lead to blindness); dry rough skin; poor growth; lowered resistance to infection because the mucous membranes dry out and crack.
- [ ] A5 [HL] Properties: soluble in fat and in organic solvents, insoluble in water; stable to heat in ordinary cooking but destroyed by prolonged high temperatures; destroyed by oxygen (oxidised), so it is lost from foods kept exposed to air.

Vitamin D: cholecalciferol
- [ ] D1 Two forms: cholecalciferol (vitamin D3, made in human skin and found in animal foods) and ergocalciferol (D2, from plants and fungi, e.g. yeast and mushrooms exposed to UV light). The body can make vitamin D in the skin when ultraviolet B from sunlight falls on it, so it is sometimes called the "sunshine vitamin".
- [ ] D2 Sources: oily fish (salmon, mackerel, sardines, herring), egg yolk, liver, butter, and fortified foods (margarine/spreads, some breakfast cereals, some milks).
- [ ] D3 Functions: helps the small intestine absorb calcium and phosphorus; keeps blood calcium at the right level; so it builds and maintains strong bones and teeth. (Detail of absorption is in "Factors affecting the absorption of minerals".)
- [ ] D4 Deficiency: rickets in children (soft bones that bend, bow legs, delayed growth, poor teeth); osteomalacia in adults (soft, weak, painful bones; fractures); contributes to osteoporosis and to dental decay. Past papers accept all of these as "conditions linked to vitamin D deficiency".
- [ ] D5 Irish context: sunlight in Ireland is weak for part of the year, so dietary sources and fortified foods matter more then; people who cover up, stay indoors, or have dark skin make less. Do not give months or doses.
- [ ] D6 [HL] Properties: soluble in fat, insoluble in water; stable to heat and fairly stable in ordinary cooking and storage; not destroyed by moderate heat, so cooking oily fish loses little.

Vitamin E: tocopherols
- [ ] E1 Tocopherols are the active forms; alpha-tocopherol is the main form in the body.
- [ ] E2 Sources: vegetable oils (sunflower, wheatgerm, olive), nuts and seeds, wholegrain cereals, avocado, green leafy vegetables, egg yolk.
- [ ] E3 Functions: antioxidant: protects cell membranes and polyunsaturated fatty acids from damage by oxidation (free radicals); also helps protect the vitamin A and C in the body and supports healthy skin and immune function.
- [ ] E4 Deficiency: rare in healthy people; in people who cannot absorb fat and in premature babies it can damage nerves and muscles and cause red blood cells to break down early (a form of anaemia).
- [ ] E5 [HL] Properties: soluble in fat, insoluble in water; stable to heat in ordinary cooking, but destroyed by oxidation (rancid fats destroy it) and by frying at high temperature for long periods; light and air also destroy it.

Vitamin K: naphthoquinones
- [ ] K1 Two forms: K1 (phylloquinone) from plants, and K2 (menaquinones), made by bacteria living in the large intestine and found in some animal foods (e.g. dairy products, eggs, meat). The letter K is from the German word for coagulation.
- [ ] K2 Sources: green leafy vegetables (spinach, kale, cabbage, broccoli, sprouts), vegetable oils, liver; plus the bacteria in the gut.
- [ ] K3 Functions: the liver needs it to make prothrombin and other clotting factors, so it is needed for blood clotting (an OL exam item: "the primary function of vitamin K"); also supports bone formation.
- [ ] K4 Deficiency: slow blood clotting, bleeding and easy bruising. Newborn babies have little gut bacteria and low stores, so the HSE offers every baby a vitamin K injection soon after birth (within hours) to prevent bleeding; it is the parents' choice. Long courses of antibiotics or poor fat absorption can also reduce vitamin K.
- [ ] K5 [HL] Properties: soluble in fat, insoluble in water; stable to heat in ordinary cooking; destroyed by light (ultraviolet) and by alkalis.

Evaluating foods as vitamin providers (setting)
- [ ] V1 Food composition tables list the vitamin content of foods, normally per 100 g, so foods can be compared like for like; a processed or cooked food may differ from its fresh form (cooking, storage and processing lose some vitamins, fortification adds some).
- [ ] V2 Fortified foods have vitamins added that are not naturally present or that were lost in processing (e.g. vitamins A and D in margarine and spreads, fortified breakfast cereals, fortified milks).
- [ ] V3 On a food label, vitamins appear only when added or when a claim is made; the label then states the amount per 100 g (or per serving) and as a percentage of the reference intake. The mandatory nutrition declaration lists energy, fat, saturates, carbohydrate, sugars, protein and salt (Regulation (EU) No 1169/2011, given effect in Ireland by the European Union (Provision of Food Information to Consumers) Regulations 2014). Not listing vitamins is not a sign that the food has none.
- [ ] V4 Fresh and processed comparison: frozen vegetables (blanched, then frozen soon after harvest) can keep more vitamin C than fresh vegetables stored for days; canning and prolonged cooking lose heat-sensitive vitamins. (Fat-soluble vitamins are more stable in cooking than vitamin C, so the loss is usually from oxidation and very high temperatures.)

## 3. Traps

- Vitamin A ("retinol") and carotene are not the same: beta-carotene is a precursor that the body converts; it is not itself vitamin A.
- Night blindness and rickets are the most-asked deficiencies; do not mix them up (A vs D). Osteomalacia is the adult counterpart of rickets; it is not the same as osteoporosis (bone density falls but bone is properly mineralised); the marking scheme accepts osteoporosis as a consequence of low vitamin D only in the loose "linked to" sense.
- Fat-soluble does not mean "found only in fatty foods": carrots (beta-carotene) and leafy greens (K) are not fatty.
- Do not say fat-soluble vitamins are "lost in cooking water". They are not; the water-soluble ones are.
- Do not describe vitamin K only as an injection for babies; its general function is clotting and the gut bacteria are a second source.
- Do not use: that vitamin E "protects the arteries by stopping LDL cholesterol oxidising", because the claim is disputed in trials and is not asked in any scheme. Say only that it is an antioxidant that protects cell membranes and polyunsaturated fatty acids.
- Do not use: any RDA, supplement dose, "safe upper limit" or number of micrograms or IU, because these are changing figures and no marking scheme for this topic states them; teach that RDAs vary with age, sex and life stage.
- Do not use: "vitamin D is destroyed by sunlight or by cooking" or any specific temperature or month for vitamin D synthesis in Ireland, because they are not checked.
- Do not use: "vitamin A deficiency is the leading cause of preventable childhood blindness worldwide" as a bare statistic, because it is a global-health figure outside the syllabus; say deficiency causes night blindness and, in severe cases, blindness.
- Neighbouring subsections: vitamin C, B12 and folate (water-soluble); thiamine, riboflavin, niacin and B6; calcium and phosphorus functions and the detail of absorption (inhibitors, parathormone, lactose); cooking guidelines to retain vitamins are in the vegetables and fruit chapter and the cooking methods chapter.

## 4. What exams ask

- Name the two classes of vitamin with an example of each (2008 and 2014 Ordinary): fat-soluble A, D, E, K; water-soluble B group and C.
- State two functions of vitamin A and two dietary sources (2004, 2016 Ordinary; 2024 Higher); complete statements naming rhodopsin and night blindness (2013, 2019 Ordinary); give three properties of vitamin A (2011 Higher: fat-soluble, heat-stable but not at prolonged high temperature, destroyed by oxygen); give an account of vitamin A under sources, functions, deficiency (2012 Higher).
- Name the vitamin behind each deficiency disease: night blindness is A, rickets is D (2025 Higher); name a condition linked to vitamin D deficiency (2021 Higher): rickets, osteomalacia, osteoporosis or tooth decay; complete "deficiency of vitamin D causes rickets in children" (2019 Ordinary).
- State two functions of vitamin D and name one source (2026 Ordinary): helps absorb calcium and phosphorus and so builds bones and teeth; oily fish, egg yolk, fortified foods. Name the vitamin needed to absorb calcium (2009 Higher, 2004 Ordinary): vitamin D.
- State the primary function of vitamin K (2018 Ordinary): blood clotting.
- Comment on provision of vitamins in foods using a label or a table: use per-100 g figures, fortification, and fresh versus processed.
- Expected answer shape for a 6-mark part: two functions and one or two sources, in one line each; for a "give an account of" with properties (Higher), add the solubility and stability points above.
