# Package: hom-energy-nutrition-s3 — Energy value of the nutrients

- **loId:** `hom-energy-nutrition-s3` · **chapterId:** `hom-energy-nutrition` · **syllabusRef:** 1.1.2, 1.1.3, 1.1.4
- **Syllabus scope:** the energy value of protein, carbohydrate and lipid and the contribution of each to the total energy value of the average diet. Also examined: energy value of a food or menu calculated from composition data (Section B food-analysis data questions, for example the bread comparison in 2007H); energy value of alcohol (assumed).
  No item in this subsection is Higher Level only. The syllabus lines carry Higher Level only items elsewhere (amino acid structure, glucose structure, triglyceride structure), none of which belong here.
- **Prepared by:** a higher model, 2026-11-23. This subsection is short and quantitative: the values per gram, how to use them, and which nutrient supplies the most. The chemistry and detailed functions of protein, carbohydrate and fat belong to their own chapters (proteins, carbohydrates, lipids), and digestion to the digestion chapter. Energy requirements are in "Energy requirements and the role of energy"; energy balance in "Energy balance"; food composition tables as a planning tool in "Dietary guidelines".

## 1. Must cover

Energy value per gram (one block)
- [ ] V1 Three nutrients supply energy in food: protein, carbohydrate and lipid (fat). Vitamins, minerals and water supply none. (assumed: what each nutrient is; one clause only.)
- [ ] V2 Energy value per gram: protein 17 kJ (4 kcal); carbohydrate 16 kJ (3.75 kcal); fat (lipid) 37 kJ (9 kcal).
- [ ] V3 Alcohol is not a nutrient but supplies energy: 29 kJ (7 kcal) per gram, with no protein, vitamins or minerals; it is a source of empty kilocalories.
- [ ] V4 Fat is the most concentrated energy source: more than twice the energy per gram of protein or carbohydrate. Fat is stored in adipose tissue; this is why a high-fat diet is energy-dense and contributes to weight gain.
- [ ] V5 Units: 1 kcal is about 4.2 kJ, so kJ divided by 4.2 gives kcal. Check: 37 kJ is about 9 kcal; 17 kJ is about 4 kcal; 16 kJ is about 3.75 kcal; 29 kJ is about 7 kcal.
- [ ] V6 Dietary fibre is a carbohydrate the body cannot digest, so it supplies very little usable energy; do not count it with the starches and sugars.

Calculating energy value (one block, method)
- [ ] K1 Method for a food: (1) find the grams of protein, carbohydrate and fat in the amount eaten from the food label or composition table (given per 100 g); (2) multiply each by its kJ per gram; (3) add the three results; (4) give the unit (kJ), converting to kcal if asked.
- [ ] K2 Worked example with sample figures: 100 g of a food with 12 g protein, 60 g carbohydrate and 8 g fat: 12 x 17 = 204; 60 x 16 = 960; 8 x 37 = 296; total 1460 kJ. In kcal: about 348 (1460 divided by 4.2). Use the same invented foods only as sample data, never as facts about a named product.
- [ ] K3 Scaling to a portion: if the table gives values per 100 g and the portion is 150 g, multiply each per-100 g value by 150 divided by 100 (or 1.5).
- [ ] K4 Percentage contribution: energy from each nutrient divided by total energy, multiplied by 100. Example with the same sample: fat is 296 out of 1460, about 20 per cent of the energy.
- [ ] K5 Data questions: given a table of composition (protein, fat, carbohydrate, fibre, calcium, iron per 100 g) for several foods, rank them by energy value, say which has the highest and lowest, and comment on suitability for a diet (high fibre helps bowel health; high fat is a disadvantage on a low-fat diet; high carbohydrate gives energy). Interpretation matters as much as the sum (2007H bread example).
- [ ] K6 Diagram: none. A simple table of the four energy values is the useful visual.

The contribution of each nutrient to the average diet (one block)
- [ ] D1 Carbohydrate supplies the largest share of energy in the diet and is the body's main, quickly available source. Starches and sugars are digested to glucose; glucose is used for energy or stored as glycogen in the liver and muscles.
- [ ] D2 Fat supplies the next largest share. Each gram carries a lot of energy, so fat contributes strongly even when it makes a smaller part of the weight of food. Fat is also stored as adipose tissue as an energy reserve.
- [ ] D3 Protein supplies the smallest share of energy. Its main work is growth and repair, not energy. It is not stored as protein, so surplus protein is broken down and used for energy or converted to fat; when carbohydrate and fat are short, protein is used for energy at the expense of its building role.
- [ ] D4 Guidelines: the healthy eating guidelines aim for most energy to come from starchy foods (preferably wholegrain), with less from fat, especially saturated fat, and less from added sugars (see "Dietary guidelines").
- [ ] D5 Irish context: the typical Irish diet gets more of its energy from saturated fat and added sugar than the guidelines recommend (see "The Irish diet"); one sentence here.
- [ ] D6 Nutrient density: foods rich in fat and sugar are energy-dense; wholegrain starchy foods, fruit, vegetables and lean protein give nutrients with less energy per gram. Link to empty kilocalories (see "Energy balance"; term used here without redefining).
- [ ] D7 Source examples: carbohydrate from bread, potatoes, pasta, rice, cereals, fruit; protein from meat, fish, eggs, milk, cheese, beans; fat from butter, oils, fatty meat, nuts, cheese, oily fish.

## 3. Traps

- Do not mix up the values: fat 37 is the one that stands out; protein 17 is only slightly above carbohydrate 16.
- Carbohydrate is written 16 kJ (3.75 kcal); the syllabus-era schemes may also write 3.75 or 4 kcal; use 3.75 for consistency.
- Do not call vitamins, minerals or water "energy nutrients"; water is covered in its own subsection and supplies no energy.
- Protein is an energy source but not the preferred one; do not say protein "is for energy".
- Alcohol is not one of the three nutrients; do not include it in the "three energy nutrients".
- Fibre is carbohydrate but is not counted as 16 kJ per gram in these exam calculations.
- Using per 100 g values for a different portion without scaling is the usual calculation error.
- Detail on digestion (enzymes, absorption), glycogen formation, deamination and ketone bodies belongs to the digestion and nutrient chapters; do not add it.
- The functions of fat (insulation, protection, fat-soluble vitamins, essential fatty acids) belong to the lipids chapter; the functions of protein to the proteins chapter.
- Do not use: "the average Irish diet has historically drawn a higher share of its energy from fat than recommended", because total fat intake in Irish adults is near the recommended limit; the excess is saturated fat and added sugar.
- Do not use: any recommended percentage of energy from protein, carbohydrate or fat (such as "55 per cent carbohydrate"), because the figures change between guideline versions and no scheme states them.
- Do not use: any energy-density figure for foods (kJ per 100 g of a named food), because composition figures change by product.

## 4. What exams ask

- State or recall the energy value per gram of protein, carbohydrate and fat (and sometimes alcohol): 17, 16, 37 kJ; 4, 3.75, 9 kcal. Fat is the most concentrated source.
- Calculate the energy value of a food, recipe portion or day's menu from composition data, showing each multiplication, the total and the unit. The notes should carry one fully worked example and the scaling step.
- Data questions (Section B food data): compare foods or meals from a table of nutrient composition, identify the food with the highest energy value or fat content, and say whether each suits a given diet; link high-fibre foods to bowel health, high-sodium foods to blood pressure, high-fat foods to weight and heart disease.
- Explain the role of carbohydrate, protein or fat as an energy source, and which supplies most energy per gram, and the contribution to the average diet: carbohydrate main source, fat second, protein smallest; link to guidelines to reduce fat and sugar.
- Link to energy balance: over-consumption of fat and sugar leads to positive energy balance and obesity; empty kilocalories (see "Energy balance").
- Exam-tip material: always write the unit; show the multiplication for each nutrient, not only the answer; say "per gram" not "per portion"; note that fat gives more than double.
