## 1. Must cover

Melting, smoke and flash points (one block, with an Investigation block for the setting)
- [ ] M1 Melting point: the temperature at which a solid fat turns to liquid. Fats do not have one sharp melting point because they are mixtures of triglycerides; solid fats melt at roughly 30–40 °C (marking-scheme figure). Oils are already liquid at room temperature. The more unsaturated the fatty acids, the lower the melting point; the more saturated, the higher (link: straight saturated chains pack tightly, kinked unsaturated chains do not).
- [ ] M2 Smoke point: the temperature at which an overheated fat or oil starts to decompose and gives off a continuous blue haze (smoke). The fat breaks down into glycerol and fatty acids, and the glycerol breaks down further to give acrolein, which has an acrid, irritating smell. Marking schemes give about 200 °C for solid fats and about 250 °C for oils.
- [ ] M3 Smoke point is lowered by impurities, food particles and free fatty acids, which build up when fat is used repeatedly; so used or dirty fat smokes at a lower temperature. Matters for: choosing a fat for frying (a high smoke point suits frying; butter, with a lower smoke point, burns), straining and replacing frying fat, never heating fat to smoking.
- [ ] M4 Flash point: a higher temperature than the smoke point, at which the vapour from the overheated fat ignites and bursts into flame. Marking schemes give about 310 °C for solid fats and about 325 °C for oils. A fat reaching its flash point is a serious fire hazard (never leave a deep-fat fryer unattended; smother with a fire blanket or lid, never water).
- [ ] M5 Order of events on heating: melts, then smokes (smoke point), then flashes (flash point).
- [ ] M6 Investigation (setting): compare melting, smoke and flash points of common fats and oils (e.g. butter, lard, olive oil, sunflower oil). Class practical needs care: use a thermometer, small amounts, heat gently and do not go beyond the smoke point; a hot-plate demonstration is safer than a flame. Record the order of melting points and which fats smoke first; conclusion linked to saturation and impurities.

Formation of emulsions (one block, with an Investigation block)
- [ ] E1 An emulsion is a mixture of two liquids that do not normally mix (immiscible), such as oil and water, with one dispersed as tiny droplets in the other.
- [ ] E2 Formation: the liquids are forced to mix by vigorous shaking, whisking or blending, which breaks one liquid into droplets.
- [ ] E3 Temporary emulsion: separates on standing (French dressing, oil and vinegar). Permanent (stable) emulsion: stays mixed because an emulsifying agent is present (mayonnaise, hollandaise sauce, ice cream, cream, milk, butter).
- [ ] E4 Types: oil-in-water (oil droplets in water: mayonnaise, milk, cream) and water-in-oil (water droplets in fat: butter, margarine and spreads).
- [ ] E5 Investigation (setting): make mayonnaise (egg yolk, oil, vinegar or lemon juice) and a French dressing; show that the dressing separates and that the egg yolk makes the mayonnaise stable; add oil slowly so droplets are small and evenly spread. Compare with and without an emulsifier.
- [ ] E6 [HL] Emulsifying agents: a molecule with a hydrophilic (water-loving) end and a hydrophobic (water-hating, fat-loving) end. The hydrophilic end attaches to the water, the hydrophobic end to the oil, so the droplets are coated and cannot join together. Identification: lecithin (egg yolk, soya), mustard, mono- and diglycerides such as glycerol monostearate (GMS), the permitted food additive lecithin (E322) and mono- and diglycerides of fatty acids (E471). Function: make and keep the emulsion stable.
- [ ] E7 [HL] Stabilisers: substances that thicken the water phase and prevent droplets coalescing, without being emulsifiers themselves: starch, pectin, gelatine, gums such as xanthan gum, guar gum. Used in dressings, sauces, ice cream and low-fat spreads.
- [ ] E8 [HL] Difference to state: emulsifier = reduces the tension between oil and water so they mix; stabiliser = thickens and keeps the mixture stable.

Plasticity [HL] (one block)
- [ ] P1 [HL] Plasticity is the ability of a fat to be shaped, spread or worked (creamed, rubbed in) at a given temperature without being fully liquid or fully hard. It is due to a mixture of solid and liquid fat in the fat: the solid fat forms a network that holds the liquid fat. Butter, lard, margarine and shortening are plastic over a range of temperatures; oils are not plastic; hard fats are brittle when cold.
- [ ] P2 [HL] Importance: spreadability of butter and margarine; creaming for cakes (traps air); rubbing in and layering in pastry; (2019H asks hydrogenation and plasticity, 6 marks).

Hydrogenation [HL] (one block)
- [ ] H1 [HL] Hydrogenation is the addition of hydrogen to the double bonds of unsaturated fatty acids, using a nickel catalyst, under heat and pressure. It converts unsaturated fatty acids to saturated ones and so changes a liquid oil to a semi-solid or solid fat.
- [ ] H2 [HL] Use: margarine and spreads, shortenings and baking fats from liquid vegetable oils; makes fats solid, plastic and more stable against oxidative rancidity.
- [ ] H3 [HL] Disadvantage: partial hydrogenation forms trans fatty acids (health effects in "Fatty acids"), so the process is now controlled; full hydrogenation gives a hard saturated fat.

Rancidity [HL] (one block; examined 2021H, 2013H, 2008H)
- [ ] R1 [HL] Rancidity is the spoilage of fats and oils, giving an unpleasant smell and taste (off flavour).
- [ ] R2 [HL] Oxidative rancidity: oxygen from the air reacts at the double bonds of unsaturated fatty acids, forming aldehydes and ketones (and breaking the chain), which cause the rancid smell. Speeded by light, heat and traces of metals; affects polyunsaturated oils most because they have most double bonds.
- [ ] R3 [HL] Hydrolytic rancidity: fat is broken down by water and the enzyme lipase (from the food itself or from micro-organisms such as moulds and bacteria) into fatty acids and glycerol; the free fatty acids (for example butyric acid in butter) give the smell. Can occur in frozen foods because lipase is not destroyed by freezing (the 2008H scheme links it to a freezer; blanching vegetables before freezing destroys enzymes).
- [ ] R4 [HL] Prevention: store fats in airtight, opaque containers in a cool dark place; keep dry; buy in small amounts and use before the date; add antioxidants; use less-unsaturated fats; refrigerate or freeze; do not reuse frying oil repeatedly.

Antioxidants [HL] (one block)
- [ ] A1 [HL] An antioxidant is a substance that slows oxidation, so it delays oxidative rancidity and extends shelf life by reacting with oxygen or free radicals before they attack the double bonds.
- [ ] A2 [HL] Natural antioxidants: vitamin E (tocopherols, E306), vitamin C (ascorbic acid, E300). Synthetic antioxidants for fats and oils: BHA (butylated hydroxyanisole, E320) and BHT (butylated hydroxytoluene, E321). Used in oils, margarine, spreads, snack foods and fried foods.
- [ ] A3 [HL] Function stated in an exam answer: prevent or delay rancidity, keeping flavour and increasing shelf life; they appear in the ingredients list by name or E number under the additives rules (Regulation (EC) No 1333/2008).

## 3. Traps

- Melting, smoke and flash points are three different temperatures in increasing order. Flash point is not the same as smoke point; smoke is the warning stage.
- Smoke point is where the fat decomposes; melting point is just solid to liquid. Do not mix.
- Emulsion vs solution: oil and water do not dissolve; droplets of one are suspended in the other.
- Emulsifier vs stabiliser: emulsifier has the two ends and forms the emulsion; stabiliser thickens and prevents separation. Do not call mustard or lecithin a stabiliser.
- Oxidative rancidity needs oxygen and double bonds; hydrolytic needs water and lipase and does not depend on double bonds. Saturated fats can go rancid hydrolytically.
- Hydrogenation makes oils into fats by removing double bonds (adding hydrogen), not by adding water. The nickel is a catalyst and is not used up.
- Do not use: per-product smoke points (for example "butter smokes at x °C", "olive oil at y °C"), because actual values vary with refining and quality and the schemes only give the two rough class figures.
- Do not use: a flash point of 330 °C for oils, because the 2005 scheme says 330 °C and the 2015 and 2010 schemes say 325 °C; use 325 °C.
- Do not use: "Plasticity depends on the proportion of saturated fatty acids" as the whole explanation, because plasticity comes from the mixture of solid and liquid fat; saturated content only determines how much is solid.
- Do not use: "Fats with a higher proportion of unsaturated fatty acids ... too soft to hold its shape" as the only statement of the plastic range, because plastic fats are those with a balanced mixture; say very unsaturated fats are too liquid and very saturated fats too hard.

## 4. What exams ask

- Smoke and flash points (2026H, 2015H, 6 marks; 2010O choose two of melting, smoke, flash): definition of each with the decomposition products (glycerol and fatty acids, acrolein, blue haze), the approximate temperatures, and the fire-hazard consequence; impurities lower them. Ordinary level: melting 30–40 °C (solid fats), smoke blue haze and breakdown, flash vapour ignites.
- Emulsion formation (2025H, 10 marks): immiscible liquids forced together; temporary (French dressing) vs permanent; emulsifier with hydrophilic and hydrophobic ends; examples (mayonnaise, hollandaise, ice cream); oil-in-water vs water-in-oil. 2008H pairs it with rancidity (12 marks, 6 each).
- Rancidity (2021H, 2013H, 2008H): oxidative (oxygen, double bonds of unsaturated fatty acids, aldehydes and ketones, rancid smell) and hydrolytic (lipase from food or micro-organisms, water, free fatty acids, in a freezer); how to prevent each.
- Hydrogenation and plasticity (2019H, 6 marks): hydrogenation with nickel catalyst turning oil to solid fat, used in margarine; plasticity as ability to spread or be shaped, matters for spreading and pastry/cakes.
- Antioxidants: name and function (vitamin E, vitamin C, BHA, BHT; delay oxidative rancidity).
- Margarine production (2024H) draws on hydrogenation and emulsification (see "Fats and oils as a food commodity").
- Practical cues: choosing fat for frying; why oil is strained and replaced; why butter is covered in the fridge.
