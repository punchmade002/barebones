## 1. Must cover

Definition of the family (one block)
- [ ] D1 A family is a group of people related by blood (birth), marriage or adoption; the basic social unit of society. Past papers (2011H, 2021H) mark "related by blood, marriage or adoption" plus "the basic unit of society that supports its members and passes values from one generation to the next". Give a short, exam-ready definition first, then the refinements below.
- [ ] D2 Kinship: the ties of blood, marriage or adoption that link people; common ancestry or lineage. Kinship is the tie that makes a group a family. (Examined 2023H as a term to explain.)
- [ ] D3 Why defining it is hard: no single definition fits every society or every household. Criteria used in different definitions: blood or adoptive ties; marriage or a committed partnership; shared residence; shared economic life (pooling income, sharing expenses); reproduction and rearing of children; mutual support and obligation.
- [ ] D4 Murdock's definition (George Peter Murdock, *Social Structure*, 1949): a social group characterised by common residence, economic co-operation and reproduction. Use the short form. It is a classic sociological definition, one among several, and later sociologists criticise it as too narrow.
- [ ] D5 Legal definition in Ireland: Article 41 of the Constitution of Ireland (1937) recognises the family as the natural primary and fundamental unit group of society, and protects it. Article 41.3 pledges to guard the institution of marriage on which the family is founded. The Constitution's protection has traditionally been for the family founded on marriage. (Legal protection of the family is developed in the family law chapter; give only the Article 41 definition here.)
- [ ] D6 Sociological versus legal definitions: legal definitions are narrower and tied to marriage, blood and adoption (they decide rights such as inheritance and maintenance); sociological definitions look at relationships and functions and can include cohabiting couples, lone parents and same-sex parents.
- [ ] D7 Household versus family: a household is people living at the same address sharing living arrangements; a family may be spread over several households (grandparents living elsewhere, a parent working abroad, separated parents), and a household may contain unrelated people. Not every household is a family.
- [ ] D8 Limits of a single criterion (group definitions, a listed setting): a definition based only on marriage excludes cohabiting couples with children and lone-parent families; one based only on shared residence excludes family members who live apart; one based only on blood excludes adoptive and step relationships. Students should be able to build a working definition by combining criteria.

Universality of the family (one block)
- [ ] U1 Universality means that a family of some kind exists in every known human society, though its form (structure) varies from one society to another. Marked in 2011H, 2021H and 2023H as "exists in every (virtually every) known society; form varies".
- [ ] U2 What is universal and what varies. Universal: kinship ties, the rearing of children, the care of dependants, the passing on of values and culture. Variable: size, structure (nuclear, extended, polygamous), who lives together, where couples live after marriage, how descent and inheritance are traced, and the roles of members.
- [ ] U3 Why forms vary: economic conditions (farming versus industry), religion and culture, law and political conditions. One form is not "natural" everywhere.
- [ ] U4 The functions of the family (care, child rearing, socialisation) are found in all societies even where the structure differs. (The five functions are in "Family functions"; one sentence here only.)

Sociological terms examiners ask for (one block, each with a plain definition)
- [ ] T1 Monogamy (examined 2023H): marriage to one partner at a time. Serial monogamy (assumed): a succession of single marriages or partnerships, one after another (e.g. remarriage after divorce).
- [ ] T2 Polygamy (assumed): marriage to more than one spouse at the same time. Polygyny: one husband, several wives. Polyandry: one wife, several husbands.
- [ ] T3 Descent (assumed): patrilineal (traced through the father's line), matrilineal (through the mother's line), and bilateral (through both; the pattern in Ireland).
- [ ] T4 Residence after marriage (assumed): patrilocal (couple lives with or near the husband's family), matrilocal (with or near the wife's family), neolocal (couple sets up a new home of their own; the common modern pattern).
- [ ] T5 Consanguineal relatives are related by blood; affinal relatives are related by marriage (in-laws). Family of orientation (the family you are born into) and family of procreation (the family you form). (assumed)
- [ ] T6 Give each term a one-line definition and no more. Wider sociological concepts (norms, values, socialisation, primary and secondary groups) are in a separate chapter on sociological concepts.

Families in other societies: case studies (one block, settings)
- [ ] C1 Case study of a matrilineal society: the Minangkabau of West Sumatra, Indonesia, trace descent and pass family property through the female line. Shows that "family" can be organised around women's lines.
- [ ] C2 Case study of family functions carried out beyond the household: the Israeli kibbutz historically raised children in communal children's houses, with parents visiting; the practice has largely ended. Shows that some functions can be shared with the community while kinship remains.
- [ ] C3 General comparison: polygamous families in societies where polygyny is accepted; extended households of several generations in many societies; the nuclear family in industrial societies. Describe without naming further peoples.
- [ ] C4 The point of comparison: the structure varies with the society, but a family group is always present. This is the evidence for universality.
- [ ] C5 Literature or film extracts (listed setting): the notes need not name an extract; say that students may apply the terms to any case study they are given (identify the descent pattern, residence and form of marriage).

## 3. Traps

- Do not define the family only as "a mother, father and children". That is the nuclear family, one structure (see "Modern family structures"). The core definition is blood, marriage or adoption.
- Universality means every society has a family, not that every society has the same family. Students often write that the nuclear family is universal; that is Murdock's claim, which later studies dispute. Do not state it as fact.
- Monogamy is one partner at a time; it does not mean one partner for life. Do not confuse polygamy (more than one spouse) with polygyny (one husband, several wives) or polyandry (one wife, several husbands).
- Matrilineal is about descent and inheritance, not about who holds political power or who heads the household. Do not say the Minangkabau are ruled by women.
- Patrilocal and matrilocal describe where a couple lives, not who inherits.
- Consanguineal means by blood; affinal means by marriage.
- Do not treat a household and a family as the same thing (D7).
- Do not say the Constitution was changed in 2024 to include "durable relationships". The proposal was defeated; Article 41 still describes the family as founded on marriage.
- Neighbouring subsections: the types of family structure, the five family functions, the history of the Irish family and the changes affecting families each have their own subsection. Name nuclear, lone-parent, extended and blended families only as examples here. Marriage law (valid marriage, divorce) belongs to the marriage and family law chapters. Roles of members belong to the chapter on the family as a caring unit.
- Do not use: statistics on the number of families, average household size or percentages of family types, because they are changing census figures.
- Do not use: "Kibbutzim raise children communally today" (or any present tense), because the practice has largely ended and the current state is unconfirmed; use "historically".
- Do not use: Murdock's claim that the nuclear family is a universal unit, as a fact, because later anthropologists dispute it; state only his definition.
- Do not use: any definition that names a set number of generations or children, because no marking scheme sets one.

## 4. What exams ask

- "Define the term family and explain universality" (2011H, 2021H; 10 marks): expected, a group related by blood, marriage or adoption, the basic social unit that supports members and passes on values; universality, a family exists in every known society though the form varies. One extra sentence of explanation, such as an example of varying forms, secures full marks.
- "Explain the terms universality of the family, kinship and monogamy" (2023H, 15 marks, 5 each): the one-line definitions in D2, U1 and T1, each with a brief example or elaboration.
- A likely variation: explain polygamy, serial monogamy, matrilineal or patrilocal, or give two criteria used in defining a family. Expect a term to be given and defined in one or two lines.
- Case-study style: given a short description of a family in another society, identify its form (extended, polygamous, matrilineal) and say what remains universal.
- Tip for answers: start with the definition, then give a contrast or example. "Universality" is not "uniformity".
