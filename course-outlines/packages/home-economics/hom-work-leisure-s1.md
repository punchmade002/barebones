# Package: hom-work-leisure-s1 — Concepts of work and attitudes to work

- **loId:** `hom-work-leisure-s1` · **chapterId:** `hom-work-leisure` · **syllabusRef:** 6.3.1
- **Syllabus scope:** "defining work: paid employment, unpaid work in the home and home-related activities, voluntary work; attitudes to work and work attainment: extrinsic and intrinsic satisfaction, work ethic, variations according to occupation." Settings: case studies of job descriptions, judging the worker's satisfaction and how it could be improved. Also examined (from past papers): factors that affect attitudes to work (2005H, 2008O, 2013H, 2025H); the three types of work explained or differentiated (2013H, 2016O, 2024O). Common to Ordinary and Higher Level; nothing here is [HL].
- **Prepared by:** a higher model, 2026-11-23. Neighbours: the volunteer's benefits and the community's benefits belong to the unpaid and voluntary work subsection; changes in work patterns (technology, services, women, flexibility) belong to the changing patterns subsection. This subsection defines the three types of work in one place and then deals with satisfaction and attitudes.

## 1. Must cover

Defining work (one block)
- [ ] W1 Work is activity that produces goods or services, using time and effort. It is not only paid: the syllabus names three types: paid employment, unpaid work in the home and home-related activities, and voluntary work.
- [ ] W2 Paid employment: work done for financial gain (wage, salary, fees or profit). It may be permanent, temporary, full-time, part-time or contract; the worker may be employed by someone else (contract of employment) or self-employed (earns directly from their own trade, business or profession and carries the financial risk). It brings security and benefits in many jobs: PRSI contributions (social insurance cover), pension, paid holidays, sick pay.
- [ ] W3 Unpaid work in the home and home-related activities: work done without financial reward, mainly for the household: cooking, cleaning, laundry, shopping, child care, care of an older or disabled relative; also home-related jobs such as DIY, home maintenance, gardening, household budgeting. It is done out of duty or family commitment, not by choice from outside the home.
- [ ] W4 Voluntary work: unpaid work done freely, by choice, for people or causes outside the household, usually through a charity, community group or organisation, to help others and improve the community (e.g. charity shop, fundraising, helping at a local club). Volunteers may receive expenses but no wage.
- [ ] W5 The three types compared in one line each: paid = income; unpaid = household duty, no income; voluntary = chosen, for the wider community, no income. (Work placements and work experience are unpaid but are not the same as voluntary work; do not need extending.)
- [ ] W6 Why the distinction matters: paid work brings income and entitlements; unpaid and voluntary work are not counted in earnings, but they still use a person's time, which is also a resource. Work affects the time and money available for family life (the idea the 2016 paper used).

Satisfaction (one block each)
- [ ] S1 Job satisfaction: how fulfilled and content a person is in their work.
- [ ] S2 Extrinsic satisfaction: comes from the rewards that surround the job rather than the task itself: pay, bonuses, pension, paid holidays and leave, job security, promotion prospects, company car, health insurance, working conditions. Typical where the work is repetitive or uninteresting: the worker works "for the money". The rewards can be spent on goods, leisure and social life. They sustain a person in a job but alone give less pride and self-esteem.
- [ ] S3 Intrinsic satisfaction: comes from the work itself: challenge and variety, using skills, autonomy and responsibility, pride in a finished job, feeling useful, achievement. It builds self-esteem, confidence and independence, and gives status and identity (a "personal identity" as a nurse or teacher). Strongest when a job matches a person's skills and interests.
- [ ] S4 A worker usually gets a mix of both; occupations differ in the balance.
- [ ] S5 Work ethic: a person's commitment to working hard and doing a job well, shown by punctuality, good attendance, reliability, effort, honesty, respect for colleagues and employer, taking pride in the work. Employers value it; it helps a worker to keep a job, be promoted and get good references. Work ethic is influenced by upbringing, education and the example of family and peers.
- [ ] S6 Work attainment (the syllabus pairs it with attitudes): getting and achieving the kind of work a person wants. How far a person attains the work they aim for is affected by education and qualifications, social group, ambition and aspirations, family expectations, and the availability of jobs. People who have high aspirations and are encouraged at home are more likely to attain work in a desired field (the point made in the 2013 marking scheme).

Attitudes to work (one block)
- [ ] A1 Factors that shape a person's attitude to work (examiners accept any well-explained factors): upbringing and family attitudes; education and qualifications; social group (children from higher socio-economic groups tend to have higher educational aspirations and long-term goals); ambition; economic situation; financial reward; fulfilment and personal identity; self-esteem and status; social contact (the workplace meets social needs, friendships, peer influence); working environment and conditions (atmosphere, relationships with supervisor and colleagues, hours, safety); changing gender roles; earlier work experience.
- [ ] A2 Each factor should be explained, not just named: say how it raises or lowers a worker's attitude (e.g. good working conditions and a supportive supervisor produce a positive attitude; poor conditions, a long commute and unsociable hours produce a negative one).
- [ ] A3 Variation according to occupation: professional and creative jobs (teaching, medicine, design) tend to give high intrinsic satisfaction through responsibility, autonomy and recognised skill. Repetitive manual or production jobs (assembly-line work) depend more on pay, security and conditions. Shift work and unsocial hours (nursing, emergency services, hospitality) can lower satisfaction unless flexibility is offered. Self-employed workers often have high intrinsic satisfaction from controlling their own hours and methods but carry more financial risk and less security. Occupations that have status in the community encourage a positive attitude.

Case-study skills (one block, practical)
- [ ] C1 A job description sets out the job title, main duties, hours, place of work, pay and the skills or qualifications needed. Method for the case study: list the duties; mark each as offering intrinsic or extrinsic satisfaction; judge how satisfied the worker is likely to be; suggest one or two improvements.
- [ ] C2 Ways to improve satisfaction: variety and training, more responsibility and involvement in decisions, fair pay and secure employment, a clear route to promotion, recognition and feedback, a safe and pleasant workplace, teamwork, flexible hours or leave that help work-life balance, better relationships with management.
- [ ] C3 Judging satisfaction: ask whether the worker is paid fairly, is secure, is recognised, uses their skills and has social contact; then balance the intrinsic and extrinsic answers.

Assumed knowledge
- [ ] X1 (assumed) Wage, salary, PRSI, pension, social insurance as everyday terms; a family's resources include time and money (from the resource management chapter); the idea of a role (from social studies).

## 3. Traps

- Do not use: "work attainment reflects results such as promotion, added responsibility, qualifications gained and recognition from colleagues" (the current note), because it confuses attainment with career progress. Syllabus and past papers use attainment to mean getting the work a person is aiming for, shaped by education, social group, aspirations and opportunity. Promotion is a result of work ethic, not the definition of attainment.
- Do not use any pay, tax or PRSI rate, any average earnings or the number of people employed.
- Self-employed people are in paid work; do not list them as a separate fourth type of work.
- Extrinsic means rewards outside the task; intrinsic means from the task itself. Do not reverse them, and do not call pay "intrinsic".
- Unpaid work in the home is not voluntary work: it is a household duty. Voluntary work is a free choice for people outside the household. State this contrast clearly (the exam differentiates them).
- The benefits of volunteering to the volunteer and the community belong to the next-but-one subsection on unpaid and voluntary work; here give only the definition.
- Changes in work (technology, shift to services, women in work, flexible hours, parental leave) belong to the changing patterns subsection. Flexible hours may be mentioned in one clause as a way to improve satisfaction.
- Do not describe a manager's or employer's side (recruitment, payroll); this is the worker's viewpoint.
- Do not write about unemployment or poverty (another chapter).

## 4. What exams ask

- Explain or differentiate paid work, unpaid work and voluntary work (2013H, 2016O, 2024O; worth 10 to 18 marks): one clear point each, with an example of each (paid: wage, contract, benefits; unpaid: housework, care, DIY; voluntary: charity, free choice, community).
- Discuss or identify factors affecting attitudes to work (2005H, 2013H, 2025H): three or four factors each explained (family background and upbringing, education, social group, ambition, pay, conditions, personal identity, social contact, gender roles).
- List and explain three factors that contribute to job satisfaction (2008O): identity and self-esteem, pay and benefits, social contact, working conditions, promotion prospects.
- Analyse how intrinsic and extrinsic factors affect attitude to work (2025H): define each, give examples, say how each shapes motivation, pride and loyalty, and that a mix is typical.
- Case-study style: given a job description or short profile, say what satisfaction the worker gets and suggest improvements.
- Part-time work for students (2016O): an advantage and a disadvantage (earn money, gain experience and time-management skills against tiredness and less study time); one or two lines, because this links to the young persons legislation.
