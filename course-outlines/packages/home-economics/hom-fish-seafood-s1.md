# Package: hom-fish-seafood-s1 — Classification, nutritional significance and contribution to the diet

- **loId:** `hom-fish-seafood-s1` · **chapterId:** `hom-fish-seafood` · **syllabusRef:** 1.3.2
- **Syllabus scope:** white, oily and shellfish (crustaceans and molluscs); nutritive and dietetic value; omega-3 fatty acids and vitamin D in oily fish. No [HL] marks in this subsection.
- **Also examined:** 2023H, 2022O, 2016H, 2015O, 2010H, 2004H (nutritive and dietetic value, "contribution to the diet", nutritional significance); 2025O (true/false: omega-3 in oily fish, myomeres, white fish is low in fat); 2011O, 2006O, 2005O (classification with an example of each class; dietetic evaluation of cod and salmon); 2004H (reasons for trends in fish consumption; why oily fish is recommended in coronary heart disease; vitamin D account).
- **Prepared by:** a higher model, 2026-11-23. Nutritive value = what the fish contains. Dietetic value = how it suits different people and diets (the contribution to the diet). Examiners ask for both, usually as one 20-mark discussion. Content comes from the marking schemes, checked against the current notes.

## 1. Must cover

Classification (one block, with examples)
- [ ] C1 Fish is classified in three groups: white fish, oily fish and shellfish. Shellfish divide into crustaceans and molluscs. Examiners ask for the classes with one example of each.
- [ ] C2 White fish: little or no fat in the flesh (fat is stored in the liver, which is why cod liver oil exists). Examples: cod, haddock, whiting, plaice, sole.
- [ ] C3 Oily fish: fat spread through the flesh. Examples: salmon, trout, mackerel, herring, tuna (fresh).
- [ ] C4 Shellfish: crustaceans (hard jointed outer shell: lobster, crab, prawns) and molluscs (soft body in a hard shell with hinged halves: oysters, mussels, cockles).
- [ ] C5 Structure of the flesh: short muscle fibres (the scheme calls them myomeres) separated by thin layers of connective tissue. Less connective tissue than meat, so fish is tender, cooks quickly and is easy to digest.
- [ ] C6 Composition ranges (2023H scheme): water 65–80 per cent, fat 0–18 per cent, HBV protein 16–18 per cent. Water and fat are inversely related: the fattier the fish, the less water.

Nutritive value (one block, fact-card list)
- [ ] N1 Protein: high biological value (HBV), all the essential amino acids, for growth and repair.
- [ ] N2 Fat: none or very little in white fish; oily fish is high in polyunsaturated fat, including omega-3 fatty acids (EPA and DHA); fat-soluble vitamins are carried in the oil.
- [ ] N3 Carbohydrate: none (so serve fish with a starchy food).
- [ ] N4 Vitamins: B group in all fish; vitamins A and D in oily fish and in fish liver oils (cod liver oil); vitamin C in oysters (the only fish source the scheme names); little vitamin C otherwise.
- [ ] N5 Minerals: iodine and fluorine in sea fish; phosphorus, potassium and zinc in most fish; calcium in canned fish with soft edible bones (sardines, canned salmon) and in shellfish; iron in shellfish and sardines.
- [ ] N6 Cholesterol: shellfish contains cholesterol (relevant to low-cholesterol diets).
- [ ] N7 Water: 65–80 per cent (see C6).

Omega-3 fatty acids and vitamin D in oily fish (one block)
- [ ] O1 Essential fatty acids are polyunsaturated fatty acids the body cannot make in the amounts it needs, so they must come from food. Omega-3 fatty acids are the type found mainly in oily fish (EPA and DHA).
- [ ] O2 Benefits of omega-3: make blood platelets less sticky, so fewer blood clots; reduce the risk of heart attack, stroke and circulatory disease; reduce the build-up of cholesterol in the arteries; help lower high blood pressure. DHA also forms part of brain and eye tissue.
- [ ] O3 Why oily fish is recommended for someone with coronary heart disease: its polyunsaturated fats, including omega-3, are linked to lower blood cholesterol and a lower risk of heart disease.
- [ ] O4 Vitamin D in oily fish and fish liver oils: fat-soluble; helps the body absorb calcium (and phosphorus), giving healthy bones and teeth; deficiency causes rickets in children and osteomalacia (soft bones) in adults. The 2016H and 2004H questions ask for a full account (type, properties, functions, deficiency); the full account belongs to the vitamins chapter, so give the facts in the table below briefly here.
- [ ] O5 Vitamin D is heat-stable: not destroyed by normal cooking (scheme: heat stable, not affected by oxygen, acids or alkalis).

Dietetic value / contribution to the diet (one block, fact-card list; examiners ask for five or six points)
- [ ] D1 Easily digested, because of short fibres and little connective tissue: suits children, the elderly, convalescents and invalids.
- [ ] D2 HBV protein for growth in children and adolescents and repair in all ages; a good alternative to meat in the main meal.
- [ ] D3 White fish is low in fat and energy: suits low-calorie, low-fat and weight-reducing diets.
- [ ] D4 Oily fish suits people at risk of heart disease or with high cholesterol or blood pressure (O3).
- [ ] D5 Shellfish contains cholesterol, so those on low-cholesterol diets should take it with care.
- [ ] D6 No carbohydrate: serve with potatoes, rice, pasta or bread. Fish in breadcrumbs, batter or sauce does contain some carbohydrate.
- [ ] D7 Iodine in sea fish helps prevent goitre.
- [ ] D8 Versatile: many cooking methods; quick to cook, so it saves time and fuel; convenient as canned or frozen products, which make fish available all year.
- [ ] D9 The Irish healthy eating guidelines (Department of Health Food Pyramid; FSAI guidance) place fish on the shelf with meat, poultry, eggs, beans and nuts and advise eating fish regularly, including oily fish (no frequency given, see Traps).
- [ ] D10 Limitations to state in an evaluation: lacks carbohydrate; little iron (except shellfish and sardines) and little vitamin C. Fish, crustaceans and molluscs are each among the allergens that must be highlighted on food labels under Regulation (EU) No 1169/2011.
- [ ] D11 Consumer trends (2004H): salmon and trout consumption rose because of awareness of the health benefits of oily fish and the growth of fish farming; white fish was affected by over-fishing, depleted stocks and fishing laws, while still being part of a healthy diet; shellfish and tuna by foreign travel and eating abroad, trendy recipes, falling cost and farming.
- [ ] D12 (assumed) The nutrient roles of protein, fats, vitamins and minerals are taught in their own chapters; use the terms without redefining them.
- [ ] D13 Diagram: none required.

## 3. Traps

- Oily versus white: the dividing line is where the fat is stored (in the flesh versus the liver). Don't call white fish "fat-free".
- Canned tuna: the scheme lists tuna as an oily fish, but canned tuna does not count as oily fish for the omega-3 recommendation (FSAI). Give fresh tuna as the example and don't present canned tuna as an omega-3 source.
- Oysters, mussels and cockles are molluscs; prawns, crab and lobster are crustaceans.
- Omega-3 fatty acids are polyunsaturated, not saturated.
- Fish is not a source of vitamin C in general (oysters only), nor of carbohydrate, nor of much iron.
- Belongs to a neighbouring subsection: judging freshness, spoilage and storage (buying, causes of spoilage, freezing at home); cooking methods and the effects of heat; smoking, canning and processed products. The full account of vitamin D, essential fatty acids and HBV/LBV protein belongs to the vitamins and minerals, lipids and proteins chapters. The FSAI's role (2023H part c) belongs to the food safety chapter.
- Do not use: "fish is grouped by whether it has a backbone", because it muddles the exam classification (white, oily, shellfish).
- Do not use: "omega-3 supports concentration" or "reduces the risk of certain cancers (prostate)", because the evidence is weak or unconfirmed; the safe, examined benefits are clots, heart disease, stroke, cholesterol and blood pressure.
- Do not use: how many times a week to eat fish or oily fish, because Irish sources differ (FSAI guidance says oily fish once a week; older Food Pyramid and HSE wording says up to twice) and it may change. Say "regularly, including oily fish".
- Do not use: vitamin D RDA figures from the 2004H scheme (7–10 µg children, 15 µg adolescents, 10 µg adults), because reference intakes have been revised and the amounts are changing figures.
- Do not use: "haem" or "non-haem" iron for shellfish, because it is not on the syllabus and the type of iron is not examined for fish.
- Do not use: the canned tuna and other figures from the 2004 data table, because they come from one exam table and are out of date.

## 4. What exams ask

- Classify fish with one example per class (6 marks): white, oily, shellfish (crustacean and mollusc), with named examples.
- Nutritive value of fish (12–24 marks): protein, fat (white versus oily), carbohydrate, vitamins, minerals; four to six points, each with its food source. Often followed by dietetic value in the same question.
- Dietetic value or contribution to the diet: digestibility for children, elderly and invalids; meat alternative; low-fat white fish; oily fish for heart disease; no carbohydrate so serve with starch; cholesterol in shellfish; versatility and speed.
- Evaluate the dietetic value of two named fish from a table (2011O, cod and salmon): link each figure to a diet need (cod low in fat, salmon omega-3).
- Benefit of omega-3 (6 marks): clots, heart attack, stroke, circulation, cholesterol, blood pressure.
- Why oily fish is recommended in coronary heart disease; true or false items on omega-3, myomeres and fat in white fish.
- Reasons for trends in fish consumption from a table: health awareness, farming, over-fishing and stock depletion, travel, cost.
- Vitamin D linked to oily fish: type, properties, functions and deficiency.
