## 1. Must cover

Current dietary guidelines and the Food Pyramid (one block)
- [ ] G1 The Department of Health's Healthy Eating Guidelines and Food Pyramid (Healthy Ireland) are the national guide. A balanced diet follows the pyramid and contains all the nutrients in the correct proportions.
- [ ] G2 Shelves: vegetables, salad and fruit (five or more, up to seven, servings a day) and wholemeal cereals, breads, potatoes, pasta and rice form the foundation shelves; milk, yoghurt and cheese (three servings a day in menu answers); meat, poultry, fish, eggs, beans and nuts (two servings); fats, spreads and oils in small amounts; the top shelf of foods and drinks high in fat, sugar and salt, limited to once or twice a week at most and not needed for health.
- [ ] G3 Guidelines to apply in menus: choose wholegrain; at least two portions of fish a week, one of them oily; lower-fat dairy; lean meat; unsaturated rather than saturated fat; less salt and sugar; water as the main drink; vary foods; control portion sizes and balance energy with activity.

Planning a day's meals (one block)
- [ ] M1 Start with the people: ages, health needs, activity, special diets, likes and dislikes, number to feed.
- [ ] M2 Include in each meal a starchy food, fruit or vegetables and a protein food or dairy so that all pyramid groups are covered over the day; do not skip breakfast; include a packed lunch or snack where needed (2011O).
- [ ] M3 Vary colour, flavour, texture and cooking method so meals appeal; avoid repeating the same food in a day.
- [ ] M4 Set out a three-meal menu (breakfast, lunch, dinner, often with a drink or snack): name actual dishes, then show how they meet the person's needs (Ordinary Level asks this in most papers).
- [ ] M5 Steps in planning: decide menus for the week, check the food and equipment already at home, write a shopping list, buy, schedule the cooking order and timing (assumed).
- [ ] M6 Apply the life-cycle and special-diet requirements from other subsections when the question names a person (pregnant woman, teenager, vegetarian, coeliac, person with CHD).

Knowledge and skills (one block)
- [ ] K1 A skilled cook can adapt recipes (more fibre, less fat, special diets) and make dishes from raw ingredients; a less experienced cook needs simple recipes with few stages and may use convenience foods.
- [ ] K2 Using a tested recipe, accurate measuring and nutrition knowledge (labels, guidelines) gives consistent results and portion sizes; skills can be built by cooking with others.

Time (one block)
- [ ] T1 Choose dishes to suit the time available; quick dishes (stir-fries, omelettes, soups, grills) when short of time.
- [ ] T2 Save time by batch cooking and freezing portions, preparing ahead, using convenience foods sensibly, and cooking one meal in stages.
- [ ] T3 Irregular routines (2021H, 2016H): shopping lists, bulk and special offers, staple stocks, some prepared or frozen ingredients, reheatable and freezable batches, slow cooker or timer, packed meals, keep a balanced diet and portions.

Equipment (one block)
- [ ] E1 Equipment available decides what can be cooked: oven, hob, grill, microwave, pressure cooker, slow cooker, food processor, blender, freezer.
- [ ] E2 Appliances save time and energy (microwave reheating; pressure cooker and slow cooker; food processor for preparation); a freezer permits batch cooking and bulk buying; lack of equipment limits methods.
- [ ] E3 Frozen foods in meal planning (2021H): retain nutritional and sensory quality, save time and money, add variety and out-of-season foods, reduce waste, allow portion control and emergency meals.

Money (one block)
- [ ] Y1 Set a budget, plan menus before shopping, write a list and keep to it to avoid impulse buying; compare prices and unit prices; use special offers sensibly.
- [ ] Y2 Buy seasonal produce; own-brand products; bulk buying only for foods that will be used or frozen; cheaper protein such as pulses, eggs, tinned fish, less expensive cuts of meat; grow or batch cook.
- [ ] Y3 Cheaper processed foods can be high in salt, sugar and fat, so keep fresh produce, wholegrains and milk in the budget plan.
- [ ] Y4 Low-income families have limited choice (settings: case studies of menu planning on a low budget).

Choice of foods (one block)
- [ ] C1 Choose foods for nutritive value, cost, availability and season, suitability for the people (age, health), preparation time and storage; check labels and dates.
- [ ] C2 Food storage so purchased food stays safe and usable: fridge, freezer and cupboard storage, vegetable drawer, stock rotation (assumed; details in the food safety chapter).

Reducing food waste and the food bill (one block; 2024O, 2009O)
- [ ] W1 Plan meals and shop with a list; buy only what is needed; check what is at home.
- [ ] W2 Understand date marks: use-by is a safety date (do not eat after it); best-before is a quality date (food may still be safe after it).
- [ ] W3 Rotate stock so older items are used first; store food correctly; freeze food before its use-by date.
- [ ] W4 Use leftovers in later meals (roast meat into soup or pie); cook the right portions; compost scraps.
- [ ] W5 Reducing waste saves money and reduces environmental harm (landfill, greenhouse gas emissions).

## 3. Traps

- Do not use: "wholegrain bread, cereals, potatoes, pasta and rice sit at the base of the pyramid", because the current Department of Health pyramid has the vegetables, salad and fruit shelf at the base; older papers speak of starch at the bottom. Say only that these two groups make up the foundation shelves.
- Do not use: "own-brand products have the same ingredients as branded ones", because own-brand and branded recipes differ; say own-brand is often cheaper, and compare labels and unit prices.
- Do not use: serving counts for the other shelves of the pyramid beyond those in the Checked facts, and portion sizes in grams, because they are not needed and vary by age group.
- Do not use: any prices, budgets or welfare amounts.
- Do not set out a menu as a list of food groups; the examiner wants actual dishes and drinks.
- A menu must suit the stated person: a pregnant woman cannot have pâté or raw egg; a coeliac cannot have wheat; a lacto-vegetarian eats no meat, fish or eggs.
- Planning (before) is different from preparation (during cooking) and from storage (after); do not mix the factors with the food-choice factors in "Food choices".
- Belongs elsewhere: the nutrient needs of each life stage and special diets (their subsections); food hygiene and safe temperatures (food safety chapter); weekly household budgets and money management (resource management chapters); cooking methods (cooking methods chapter).

## 4. What exams ask

- Set out a menu for three meals for one day for a named person (teenager, pregnant woman, school child, vegetarian, person with CHD, family with teenagers) having regard to the healthy eating guidelines (Ordinary, 12 to 18 marks): three meals with named dishes, then a brief justification linked to the person's needs.
- Explain how money, time and equipment available influence family meal planning (6 marks): budget and cheaper protein, quick or convenience foods, appliances and freezer.
- Suggest ways to reduce food waste or the food bill; discuss benefits of frozen foods; give strategies for purchasing and planning meals for families with irregular routines (Higher, 20 marks).
- Apply guidelines for a stated group (for example three guidelines for pre-school children or for a convalescent) using the knowledge from other subsections.
- Comment on a food menu or a day's meals: strengths and weaknesses against the pyramid and suggestions for improvement.
