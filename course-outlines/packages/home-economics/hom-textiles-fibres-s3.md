## 1. Must cover

The classification (one block, with a diagram)
- [ ] C1 Fibre: a hair-like strand, the raw material of a yarn and so of a fabric. Staple fibres are short (cotton, wool, linen); filaments are continuous and very long (silk, all man-made fibres as extruded). (assumed: yarn and fabric.)
- [ ] C2 Three classes: natural, regenerated, synthetic. Regenerated and synthetic are together called man-made (manufactured) fibres.
- [ ] C3 Natural: from plants (cellulose) or animals (protein); needs only cleaning and processing before spinning. Plant (cellulosic): cotton, linen, also hemp and jute. Animal (protein): wool, silk.
- [ ] C4 Regenerated: made by dissolving a natural polymer (cellulose from wood pulp) and forcing the solution through a spinneret so that it reforms as a fibre: viscose (rayon), acetate, triacetate.
- [ ] C5 Synthetic: made entirely from chemicals (monomers from coal or petroleum, built into polymers): nylon (polyamide), polyester, acrylic, elastane (Lycra).
- [ ] C6 Diagram: classification tree: textile fibres → natural (plant, animal), regenerated, synthetic, one example at the end of each branch.

Natural fibres: source and uses (one block)
- [ ] N1 Cotton: plant, cellulose; seed hairs of the cotton plant (boll); shirts, jeans, T-shirts, towels, bed linen, curtains.
- [ ] N2 Linen: plant, cellulose; stem of the flax plant; tea towels, tablecloths, summer clothing, bed linen.
- [ ] N3 Wool: animal, protein; fleece of sheep; jumpers, tweed, suits, blankets, carpets.
- [ ] N4 Silk: animal, protein; cocoon of the silkworm (silk moth caterpillar, fed on mulberry leaves); a filament; scarves, ties, evening wear, lingerie, luxury bedding.
- [ ] N5 One property each, so a candidate can answer "state a property": cotton absorbent and cool; linen strong and absorbent; wool warm and resilient; silk smooth, drapes well, absorbent.
- [ ] N6 Why natural fibres are popular for household textiles (2014O): absorbent, comfortable, durable, accept dyes and finishes, washable, a personal preference for natural fabric; they come from renewable sources.

Regenerated fibres: source and uses (one block)
- [ ] G1 Viscose (rayon): cellulose from wood pulp, treated with chemicals, dissolved, forced through a spinneret into a chemical bath where fibre reforms. Absorbent, soft, drapes well, dyes well, weak when wet. Uses: dresses, blouses, linings, soft furnishings.
- [ ] G2 Acetate: cellulose chemically combined with acetic acid, dissolved and extruded; silky, lustrous, drapes well; linings, blouses, evening wear. Triacetate is a related fibre.
- [ ] G3 Lyocell: a newer regenerated cellulose fibre made by dissolving wood pulp in a solvent; absorbent and soft.
- [ ] G4 Why called regenerated: the cellulose already exists in the plant, is dissolved, and is re-formed as a long filament.

Synthetic fibres: source and uses (one block)
- [ ] Y1 Nylon (polyamide): from coal or petroleum chemicals; very strong, elastic, abrasion resistant, light, low absorbency; hosiery, swimwear, jackets, carpets, ropes.
- [ ] Y2 Polyester: petroleum-based; strong, crease resistant, quick drying; shirts, fleece, curtains, bed linen, often blended with cotton.
- [ ] Y3 Acrylic: polymer of acrylonitrile; soft, warm, light, wool-like; knitwear, socks, blankets.
- [ ] Y4 Elastane (Lycra is a brand name): a stretch fibre used in small amounts blended with others; sportswear, swimwear, hosiery.
- [ ] Y5 Man-made fibres can be made in different thicknesses and lusters by changing the spinneret holes and drawing.

Regenerated versus synthetic (one block; a 6-9 mark question)
- [ ] D1 Raw material: regenerated = natural cellulose (wood pulp); synthetic = chemicals from coal or petroleum.
- [ ] D2 Making: regenerated = cellulose dissolved and re-formed; synthetic = chemical monomers joined into polymer then extruded.
- [ ] D3 Properties: regenerated absorb moisture like cotton, drape well, burn and char like cotton; synthetic absorb little moisture, dry quickly, resist creasing and melt when heated.
- [ ] D4 Examples: regenerated viscose, acetate; synthetic nylon, polyester, acrylic.

Making man-made fibres (one block, with a diagram)
- [ ] M1 A thick liquid (melted polymer or polymer solution) is forced through a spinneret, a plate with many tiny holes.
- [ ] M2 The streams harden: cooling in air (melt spinning), or in a chemical bath (wet spinning), or by warm air evaporating the solvent (dry spinning).
- [ ] M3 The filaments are drawn (stretched) so the molecules line up, which makes them stronger.
- [ ] M4 Diagram: spinneret, polymer pumped in, filaments, drawing and winding onto a bobbin.

## 3. Traps

- Do not use: "regenerated fibres are not made with chemicals" or "regenerated fibres use no petroleum chemicals" as a selling point, because making viscose uses strong chemicals; say only that the raw material is plant cellulose.
- Do not use: "Lycra" as a generic name without noting it is a brand of elastane; the examination accepts Lycra.
- Acetate and triacetate are listed as regenerated in marking schemes (strictly, they are modified cellulose); follow the scheme and call them regenerated.
- Silk is a natural protein fibre but a filament, not staple; do not call it synthetic. Wool and silk are both protein (animal), not cellulose.
- Rayon and viscose are the same fibre family (viscose rayon); do not list them as two examples of different fibres.
- Regenerated is not "natural": it is man-made, but unlike synthetics it starts from a natural polymer.
- Fibre production for one named fabric (ginning, carding, shearing), yarn production, burning and microscope tests belong to the fabric-profiles subsection; weaves, finishes, dyeing to the last subsection. Give sources and uses only.
- No global production figures, prices or market shares.

## 4. What exams ask

- Classify natural fibres into plant and animal with an example and a use of each (2006H, 2017O, 2018H).
- Differentiate between regenerated and synthetic fibres with an example of each (2016H 6 marks, 2022H 6 marks, 2008H 9 marks): raw material, making, example.
- Name one natural and one synthetic fibre; state a use or property of each; explain why natural fibres suit household textiles (2014O, 2009O).
- List properties of one named fibre (2006H, three properties).
- A paragraph in a larger profile question often starts with naming the fibre class.
