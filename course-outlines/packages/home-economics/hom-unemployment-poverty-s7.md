## 1. Must cover

Voluntary organisations (one block)
- [ ] V1 A voluntary organisation is a non-profit group, run mainly by volunteers (often with some paid staff), set up to meet a social need. It is independent of the State, though many receive State funding. It is funded by donations, fundraising and charity shops, and State grants.
- [ ] V2 Society of St Vincent de Paul (SVP): national organisation working through local volunteer groups (conferences) that visit families in need in their own area, in confidence, and give practical help: food or fuel vouchers, help with bills, school books and uniforms, furniture, advice. It also runs charity shops, hostels and housing, homework clubs and education supports, and raises funds through church-gate collections and shops. SVP is the organisation named in the schemes of 2010H, 2016H, 2017O and 2022O.
- [ ] V3 Simon Community: supports homeless people and those at risk of homelessness with emergency accommodation, housing and outreach.
- [ ] V4 Focus Ireland: works to prevent and end homelessness, with housing, support and advice for people at risk and in homelessness.
- [ ] V5 Other named bodies the schemes accept for poverty or unemployment: Barnardos (children and families), Age Action (older people), Rotary and Lions clubs (local fundraising and practical support), and Men's Sheds. Give two or three; each with a one-line role.
- [ ] V6 Types of support to a family when the main earner is unemployed (2010O): financial or material help (vouchers, bills, clothing, food); advice, information and advocacy (helping people claim entitlements and use services such as Citizens Information and MABS); coping and skills classes; a friendly visit and someone to talk to.
- [ ] V7 Wider roles of voluntary organisations: they fill gaps statutory services leave, respond quickly and locally, campaign and lobby the Government on behalf of those affected (pressure group role) and gather evidence on need.
- [ ] V8 Benefits of voluntary work (short, 3-4 bullets): to the individual (purpose, skills, confidence, friendships, may lead to a job); to the community (quick, low-cost local services, complements State services, builds community spirit). Where these appear, the questions are asked in the leisure and work chapters; do not expand here.

Community-based education, training and employment (one block)
- [ ] E1 Community-based projects are locally run, often with State money, and respond to local unemployment and early school leaving. They build confidence, skills and contacts, which help people back to work.
- [ ] E2 Education: community education and adult literacy and numeracy classes, delivered through local centres and the Education and Training Boards (ETBs); Youthreach for early school leavers; Vocational Training Opportunities Scheme (VTOS) for unemployed adults.
- [ ] E3 Training: community training centres and local training projects for early school leavers and unemployed people, funded through SOLAS and the ETBs; courses are designed to meet local skills needs.
- [ ] E4 Employment: Community Employment (CE) gives long-term unemployed people part-time paid work on local community projects (environment, heritage, sport, tourism, community services). Tús gives temporary work placements in local communities through local development companies. The Rural Social Scheme gives income support to low-income farmers and fishers for community work.
- [ ] E5 Local development companies deliver the Social Inclusion and Community Activation Programme (SICAP 2024-2028), which is funded by the Department of Rural and Community Development and the Gaeltacht, with EU funding through the ESF+, and aims to reduce poverty and social exclusion by supporting communities and individuals (courses, job-readiness, starting a business).
- [ ] E6 How to answer "name and give details of one community-based initiative": name it, say who runs and funds it, and give two ways it creates employment or training.

Co-operatives (one block)
- [ ] C1 A co-operative is a business or organisation owned and run by its members, who pool resources, skills and knowledge to meet their needs; each member has one vote regardless of how much they invest; profit after costs and reserves is shared among members or reinvested.
- [ ] C2 Types: agricultural co-operatives (farming, dairy, fishing), credit unions (members save and borrow at fair cost), housing co-operatives (owned by their members, who build or manage homes), consumer and worker co-operatives, and community co-operatives that run a shop, childcare or transport where no private business will.
- [ ] C3 Why they respond to poverty and unemployment: they keep jobs and profits local, give low-income people access to services (credit, housing), and let a community create work for itself. Credit unions give savings accounts and affordable loans to members.
- [ ] C4 Co-operative principles (optional, international): voluntary and open membership, democratic member control, member economic participation, autonomy and independence, education and training, co-operation among co-operatives, and concern for community.

Cottage industries (one block)
- [ ] K1 A cottage industry is small-scale production by an individual or family, usually at home or in a small workshop, using traditional or craft skills. In Ireland: hand-knitting (Aran), hand-weaving (including Donegal tweed), lace (Carrickmacross) and pottery and crafts; also artisan food producers and farmers' markets (a marking scheme accepts these as community initiatives).
- [ ] K2 They give an extra or main income, especially in rural areas where jobs are scarce; need little capital; keep traditional skills alive; and sell to tourists and local markets.
- [ ] K3 Limits: small output, irregular or seasonal income, difficulty in marketing and competing with mass production, and no employment benefits. Local Enterprise Offices give training and grants to craft and small businesses.
- [ ] K4 Link to textiles: cottage firms supply handmade clothing and textiles (an Elective 2 question on the contribution of cottage industries to the Irish clothing and textile industry was set); give one link sentence.

The Combat Poverty Agency (one block)
- [ ] B1 The Combat Poverty Agency was a State body that advised the Government on poverty, carried out research and funded local anti-poverty projects (2007O asked about it).
- [ ] B2 It was dissolved on 1 July 2009 and its work merged into the Social Inclusion Division of the Department of Social Protection (then the Department of Social and Family Affairs). Say so, so a student asked about it can answer in the past tense, then name bodies that now do the work (the Department's Social Inclusion Division and the Roadmap for Social Inclusion, SICAP, and voluntary organisations such as SVP).

## 3. Traps

- Voluntary organisations are not State bodies; SOLAS, MABS, the HSE and the Department of Social Protection are statutory and belong to the statutory responses subsection. A student must not name SOLAS as a voluntary body. (Citizens Information services are delivered under the State's Citizens Information Board and are statutory; mention only as bodies SVP points people toward.)
- A co-operative is owned by its members, one member one vote; a credit union is a type of co-operative. Do not describe a co-operative as a charity.
- A cottage industry is small and home-based; a co-operative is a member-owned business. They are separate syllabus items; give each its own block.
- Benefits of voluntary work belong mainly to the leisure and community chapters; keep to the short link in V8.
- Do not use: "community training workshops" as a named programme (current notes), because it is not a confirmed scheme name; say "community training centres" or "local training projects".
- Do not use: "ICATA" or any named association for cottage industries (appears in an old scheme), because it is unconfirmed; say "support from Local Enterprise Offices".
- Do not use: "cottage firms form about 70% of Irish clothing businesses" (an old scheme figure), because it is a changing, unverified figure.
- Do not use: "FÁS" or "County Enterprise Boards" for supporting cottage industries or community employment (old schemes name them), because they are replaced; use SOLAS and the Local Enterprise Offices.
- Do not use: "the Combat Poverty Agency works to ..." in the present tense, because it was dissolved in 2009; and do not call it a voluntary body.
- Do not use: founding years, numbers of conferences or volunteers, or funding figures for SVP, Simon, Focus Ireland or credit unions, because none is checked.
- Do not use: "the seven co-operative principles are set by the International Co-operative Alliance" as a fact card required by exams, because exams do not ask for them; keep it as optional detail.

## 4. What exams ask

- Name and give details of one voluntary organisation that supports people at risk of poverty or unemployment (10-14 marks; 2022O, 2017O, 2016H, 2010H): SVP is the standard choice. A good answer gives the name, who it helps, the services (financial help, visits, vouchers, housing, education grants) and how it is funded.
- Two types of support voluntary bodies give a family when the main earner is unemployed (2010O): financial/material help and advice or advocacy.
- Name and give details of one community-based initiative that creates employment (12 marks, Higher): a co-operative, cottage industry, farmers' market, community cafe or a SICAP or Community Employment project.
- The Combat Poverty Agency (2007O): a State anti-poverty body (research, advice to government, funding of projects); note that it no longer exists.
- Cottage industries and co-operatives in Social Studies or textiles contexts: definition, examples, benefits, limits.
- Tips: name a real organisation; give two or three specific services; say who funds it; for co-operatives give "one member, one vote" and the profit being shared; for cottage industries give an Irish craft and the rural income benefit.
