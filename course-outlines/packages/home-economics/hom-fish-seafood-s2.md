## 1. Must cover

Selecting fresh fish (one block, fact-card list; examiners ask for four to six points)
- [ ] B1 Buy from a reliable, hygienic retailer where fish is displayed on ice or refrigerated; check the date on packaged fish; buy fish in season (better and cheaper); medium-size fish have the best flavour (2004H scheme).
- [ ] B2 Eyes: bright, clear and bulging (plump). Sunken, cloudy or dull eyes mean old fish.
- [ ] B3 Gills: bright red; grey or brown gills mean the fish is not fresh.
- [ ] B4 Flesh: firm and elastic, springs back when pressed; soft flesh that keeps the dent is stale.
- [ ] B5 Skin and scales: moist, shiny, scales tight and unbroken; no bruising or damage.
- [ ] B6 Smell: fresh and mild, like the sea; a strong or unpleasant smell shows spoilage.
- [ ] B7 Shellfish: alive and heavy for their size; mussels should be closed (an open one that does not close when tapped is dead and is discarded).
- [ ] B8 Fish products: smoked fish should look glossy; frozen fish should be solid with its packaging intact and no signs of thawing or freezer burn; canned fish in an undamaged, undented can within its date.
- [ ] B9 Get fish home quickly (cool bag or ice) and refrigerate straight away.

Causes of spoilage (one block, three causes with detail)
- [ ] S1 Fish spoils faster than meat, so it must be used or processed as soon as possible after it is caught. Its flesh is soft, high in water, with little connective tissue, and its fat and enzymes act quickly.
- [ ] S2 Oxidative rancidity: the fats in the fish, especially the polyunsaturated fat of oily fish, react with oxygen in the air, giving an unpleasant smell and taste.
- [ ] S3 Bacterial action: a fish caught in a net struggles and uses up its glycogen stores. When glycogen is present it converts to lactic acid, a preservative. If lactic acid is not present, the fish goes off quickly. Bacteria work on the flesh and produce trimethylamine, a strong-smelling nitrogen compound that gives spoiled fish its smell.
- [ ] S4 Enzymes: enzymes in the flesh cause deterioration even at low temperatures, so spoilage continues, slowly, in the fridge.

Effects of storage (one block)
- [ ] F1 Chilling slows bacterial growth and enzyme action but does not stop them, so fresh fish keeps for a short time only. Keep it chilled below 5 °C, on ice or on the lowest shelf of the fridge.
- [ ] F2 Store raw fish covered, on the lowest shelf so drips cannot contaminate other food, and apart from cooked and ready-to-eat food to prevent cross-contamination.
- [ ] F3 Covering the fish stops it drying out and stops its smell passing to other food.
- [ ] F4 Use fresh fish on the day of purchase or as soon as possible.
- [ ] F5 Oily fish deteriorates faster than white fish because its fat goes rancid; this also limits how long it keeps frozen.

Freezing fresh fish (one block)
- [ ] Z1 Principle: at freezer temperature the water in the flesh is held as ice, so micro-organisms cannot grow and enzyme action is stopped. Freezing slows or stops spoilage; it does not kill all bacteria, which can grow again after thawing.
- [ ] Z2 Rules (2006O scheme): freeze fish that is very fresh and of best quality; turn on fast-freeze 3 to 4 hours before; freeze only about one tenth of the freezer's capacity in 24 hours; freeze in usable quantities; wrap in moisture-proof packaging or vacuum pack; label with name and date; store at −18 °C or below.
- [ ] Z3 Thaw fish slowly in the fridge and do not refreeze it once thawed raw.
- [ ] Z4 Effects of freezing: colour and flavour largely unchanged and nutrients well kept; texture may change and some B vitamins and extractives are lost on thawing; poor wrapping causes freezer burn and air in the packaging lets oily fish go rancid.
- [ ] Z5 Advantage and disadvantage (2022O): simple, safe, little nutrient loss, allows bulk buying and out-of-season fish; but it needs packaging and freezer running costs.
- [ ] Z6 (assumed) The danger zone and bacterial growth are taught in the food safety chapter; use the terms without redefining them.
- [ ] Z7 Diagram: none required.

## 3. Traps

- A struggling fish has little glycogen, so little lactic acid forms and bacteria spread faster. Don't say lactic acid causes spoilage.
- Trimethylamine is made by bacteria and gives the strong smell.
- Freezing stops micro-organisms multiplying; it doesn't kill them or sterilise the fish.
- Open mussels: apply the tap test; don't claim every open mussel is dead.
- Belongs to a neighbouring subsection: effects of cooking, cooking methods and preparing shellfish; industrial freezing at about −30 °C, canning, salting and smoking; the role of the FSAI and HACCP (food safety chapter).
- Do not use: any number of days or months that fresh or frozen fish keeps, because guidance varies and changes by source; say "a short time" for fresh fish and "within the time on the pack" for frozen.
- Do not use: the Sea-Fisheries Protection Authority as a buying guideline or as "controlling hygiene in retail", because it covers production to distribution and not retail shops.
- Do not use: "the freezer stops enzymes completely", because enzymes are slowed to a near stop, not always destroyed; use the scheme wording "inactivated at freezer temperature" only when describing the principle.

## 4. What exams ask

- Guidelines for buying fresh fish (12–18 marks): eyes, gills, flesh, skin and scales, smell; shellfish closed and alive; reliable hygienic source, ice, date, in season. The 2004H scheme wants two points on fresh fish, two on fish products (smoked glossy, frozen solid) and two others.
- Outline the main causes of spoilage (15 marks, three at 5 marks) or explain two factors (6 marks): oxidative rancidity, bacterial action with glycogen, lactic acid and trimethylamine, and enzymes.
- General rules for freezing food: fast-freeze, one tenth capacity, best quality, usable quantities, packaging, labelling.
- An advantage and a disadvantage of freezing fish.
- Storing fresh fish at home: below 5 °C, covered, lowest shelf, away from cooked food, use quickly.
