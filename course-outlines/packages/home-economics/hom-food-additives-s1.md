# Package: hom-food-additives-s1 — Processed foods and three processed-food profiles

- **loId:** `hom-food-additives-s1` · **chapterId:** `hom-food-additives` · **syllabusRef:** 1.3.5
- **Syllabus scope:** the range of processed foods available; profiles of three types of processed food: food that undergoes extensive processing, food processed to extend shelf life, and added-value food. Settings: flour (extensive processing), milk (extending shelf life), prepared foods or meals (added value); a visit to a local factory or plant (creamery, mill, cheese plant). Also examined: added-value food profile (2023H, 2009H); functional foods (2025H, 2014H); genetically modified and organic food (2016H); merits of processed and convenience foods (2026H, 2018H, 2006H); ultra-processed foods (2025O); fortification (2016H). No Higher Level only items in this subsection; mark nothing HL.
- **Prepared by:** a higher model, 2026-11-23. Packaging and labelling, contaminants, additives, EU additive law and sensory analysis are separate subsections (see Traps). The syllabus is the 2001 one examined in June 2027.

## 1. Must cover

Why food is processed and the range available (one block)
- [ ] P1 A processed food is any food changed from its raw state by one or more processes (milling, heating, freezing, drying, canning, adding ingredients).
- [ ] P2 Reasons for processing (the Ordinary Level scheme lists these): extend shelf life and make food more edible; make food safe to eat; cut down on waste; provide a wide choice of good-quality food all year round; vary the diet with new food products.
- [ ] P3 The range of processed foods (Higher Level 2006 asks to "outline the range"): frozen, canned, dried, instant or ready-to-eat, cook-chill, plus preserved/pickled and fortified foods. Give one named example of each.
- [ ] P4 Minimal processing (washing, cutting, bagging leaves, freezing vegetables, nothing added) versus extensive processing (many stages and ingredients, product far from the raw material).

Profile 1: food undergoing extensive processing, flour (one block, process)
- [ ] F1 Wheat grain is cleaned and blended, then milled by break rolls and smooth rolls, sieved and air-classified (the stages in the 2011 scheme); the bran and germ are separated from the starchy endosperm; white flour is mainly endosperm.
- [ ] F2 Effect: lower fibre and fewer vitamins and minerals than wholemeal; the germ's oil is removed, so white flour keeps longer than wholemeal (which can go rancid).
- [ ] F3 Additives and fortification: improvers or added nutrients may be incorporated at the end of milling (the 2011 scheme says additives are incorporated). Nutrients lost in milling may be added back (see Traps for the Irish position).
- [ ] F4 Packaging and labelling: weighed into paper bags; label gives brand, product name, ingredients, best-before date, quantity, nutrition information and storage.
- [ ] F5 Uses: bread, cakes, pasta, breakfast cereals, each with further stages (mixing, baking, extrusion). Types of flour belong to the cereals chapter.
- [ ] F6 Setting: a local mill visit shows the stages.

Profile 2: food processed to extend shelf life, milk (one block, process)
- [ ] M1 Preservation slows or stops the micro-organisms and enzymes that spoil food.
- [ ] M2 Milk at the creamery: reception and testing, cooling, separation of cream from skimmed milk, standardising fat, pasteurisation, cooling, packing, chill storage.
- [ ] M3 Pasteurisation: heat to destroy pathogens and most spoilage organisms, then cool rapidly; the milk still needs refrigeration and keeps for days. Stable figure: about 72 °C for 15 seconds.
- [ ] M4 UHT (ultra-high temperature): heated to about 135 °C for a few seconds and packed aseptically; keeps for months at room temperature until opened. Sterilised milk is heated even more severely in the sealed bottle.
- [ ] M5 Homogenisation: forcing milk under pressure through tiny openings breaks fat globules so cream does not rise. It improves consistency; it is not a preservation step.
- [ ] M6 Other shelf-life processes (one line each): freezing at or below −18 °C, canning, drying, vacuum packing. Modified-atmosphere packing belongs to the packaging subsection.
- [ ] M7 Setting: a creamery or cheese-plant visit.

Profile 3: added-value food (one block, process)
- [ ] A1 Added value: a raw material is processed into a product of greater economic value to the manufacturer and better matched to consumer taste, convenience or health. Examples: cheese from milk, grated cheese, cook-chill meals, flavoured and probiotic yoghurt, margarine, sliced cooked meats, jams, breads.
- [ ] A2 Cheese (stages from the 2023 scheme): milk pasteurised; lactic acid bacteria added (lactose becomes lactic acid); rennet coagulates the milk into curds and whey; curds cut and whey drained; curds heated, cheddared, salted, moulded and pressed; cheese ripened for months, graded, cut and sealed.
- [ ] A3 Yoghurt: milk tested and standardised; homogenised; pasteurised; cooled to the culture temperature; lactic acid bacteria (starter, with probiotic strains if used) added and incubated; fermentation of lactose to lactic acid lowers the pH and coagulates the casein; chilled; flavour or fruit added; filled, sealed and kept chilled.
- [ ] A4 Cook-chill: food is cooked, chilled quickly (the 2011 scheme: to 3 °C within 30 minutes and fully chilled within 90 minutes), stored and transported at 1–3 °C, reheated thoroughly before eating.
- [ ] A5 Structure examiners expect in a profile: name of product, why it is added value, stages of production, packaging, labelling. Cheese (A2), yoghurt (A3) and cook-chill (A4) are the three worked profiles.

Functional foods and fortification (one block)
- [ ] G1 Functional food: gives a health benefit beyond its basic nutritional value because of an added or naturally present ingredient. Examples: probiotic yoghurt or drinks (digestive health); spreads with plant sterols (lower blood cholesterol); omega-3 enriched foods (heart health); folic-acid enriched foods (reduce the risk of neural tube defects in a baby).
- [ ] G2 Fortification: nutrients added so that the level is higher than naturally present. Scheme examples: breakfast cereals, milk, fruit juices, flour, margarine, bread, biscuits, dairy spreads. Reasons: replace nutrients lost in processing; meet a public-health need. Link: the nutritional-supplement additive class (classes subsection).

Merits and drawbacks of processed and convenience foods (one block)
- [ ] D1 Merits (four distinct points are marked): save time, labour and fuel; little skill needed; variety and wide choice all year round; consistent quality and portion size; safe food and long shelf life; less waste; many fortified; easy to store; emergency meal; frozen and canned foods keep many nutrients.
- [ ] D2 Drawbacks: can be high in salt, sugar and saturated fat and low in fibre; often dearer than home-prepared; more packaging and waste; additives (a minority react); reliance can unbalance the diet and reduce cooking skills.
- [ ] D3 Judgement: use selectively, compare labels, combine with fresh food.

Ultra-processed, GM and organic food (one block)
- [ ] U1 Ultra-processed food: industrial formulations from refined ingredients and additives, with little or no whole food (packaged snacks, sugary drinks, instant noodles, many ready meals); less healthy than fresh food (a 2025 Ordinary Level true/false). The classification is the NOVA system, group 4.
- [ ] U2 Genetically modified food: the DNA is altered to change a characteristic (size, speed of ripening, resistance to disease). EU rules require food that contains or is produced from GM ingredients (the scheme gives soya and maize) to be labelled.
- [ ] U3 Organic food: grown without synthetic chemical fertilisers or pesticides, with rules on fertiliser and on pest, weed and disease control; certified by inspection bodies approved by the Department of Agriculture, Food and the Marine; certified products carry the certifier's symbol; more labour-intensive with lower yields, so dearer.

## 3. Traps

- Do not use: "white flour is often fortified with calcium, iron and B vitamins" as a statement about Irish flour, because there is no Irish legal requirement to fortify flour; UK rules (calcium carbonate, iron, thiamin, niacin) apply to flour imported from the UK and the amounts are changing. Say instead that nutrients lost in milling (for example B vitamins and iron) may be added back, and that flour is a scheme-named fortified food.
- Do not use: "omega-3 enriched foods are linked with a lower risk of coronary heart disease" as a disease claim, because it is unconfirmed. Say omega-3 foods are promoted for heart health.
- Do not use: any percentage threshold for GM labelling (the 2016 scheme says 1%), because the EU limit has since been set at 0.9% and the figure is not needed; say only that GM food must be labelled.
- Do not use: a number of Irish organic certifying bodies (the 2016 scheme says three), because the number is not confirmed; name the Irish Organic Association and the Organic Trust at most.
- Do not use: iodised salt as an Irish fortification example, because it is in no scheme and Irish law does not require it. Use the scheme examples in G2.
- Homogenisation is not preservation: it stops cream rising. Keep it in the milk profile but don't say it kills bacteria.
- Fortification (raising the level of a nutrient) is not the same as "food supplements" (tablets); a functional food is not the same as a fortified food, though one product can be both.
- "Added value" means economic value, not "more nutritious".
- Packaging materials, MAP and labelling law belong to the packaging and labelling subsection; additive classes and E numbers belong to the classes and legal-control subsections; the sectors of the Irish food industry, Bord Bia and speciality foods belong to the food industry chapter (the 2024O sector question is tagged here but is not on this topic); food-poisoning bacteria and preservation methods in depth belong to the food safety chapter.
- Cheese and yoghurt chemistry (casein, rennet) belong to the dairy chapter; give the stages only.

## 4. What exams ask

- Explain "added value" and "functional" food in two lines each, with an example (6 marks, Higher Level Section A). Added value: raw material processed into a higher-value product, e.g. cheese from milk, cook-chill foods. Functional: health benefit beyond nutritive value, e.g. probiotic yoghurt, plant-sterol spreads.
- Profile an added-value food: name, stages of production, packaging, labelling (26 marks). A good answer picks cheese, yoghurt or a cook-chill meal, gives at least six ordered stages, then the pack (foil, plastic tub, waxed paper) and label contents (brand, producer, best-before date, nutrition, storage, allergens).
- Profile a food that undergoes extensive processing, flour: stages, packaging, labelling (20 marks).
- Give two reasons for processing (Ordinary Level, 4 marks); name fortified foods or explain the term fortified and give the consumer benefit (2008, 2016).
- Discuss merits, and often drawbacks, of processed foods in the diet: four developed points at about 4 marks each; Higher Level 2026 asks for both sides and a balanced judgement.
- Outline the range of processed foods (2006H), naming categories with examples.
- One-line notes on GM food, organic food or added-value food (2016H).
- True or false on ultra-processed foods (2025O).
