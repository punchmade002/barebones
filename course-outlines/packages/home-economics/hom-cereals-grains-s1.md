## 1. Must cover

Structure of the grain (one block, with a diagram)
- [ ] G1 A cereal grain (the seed of a grass) has three parts: bran, endosperm and germ. The wheat grain is the standard example and the one the papers use.
- [ ] G2 Bran: the outer covering, several layers, composed mainly of cellulose (insoluble fibre). About 13% of the wheat grain (2007 scheme). Removed in white-flour milling.
- [ ] G3 Endosperm: the inner and largest part, mainly starch with protein (which forms gluten in wheat). White flour is made from it. It is the food store for the young plant.
- [ ] G4 Germ: the embryo, a small part at the base of the grain, about 2% of the grain (2007 scheme). It would grow into a new plant. Rich in unsaturated fat, vitamin E, B group vitamins and some protein.
- [ ] G5 Diagram: longitudinal section of a wheat grain showing bran, endosperm and germ; label all three parts.

Nutritional significance of each part (one block; the 2015H question asks "each part")
- [ ] N1 Bran: cellulose gives dietary fibre (roughage), which adds bulk, absorbs water and stimulates peristalsis, preventing constipation and aiding digestion. The bran also supplies B group vitamins and minerals such as iron, magnesium and phosphorus.
- [ ] N2 Endosperm: starch, the main carbohydrate in cereals, supplies energy (heat and activity); it also supplies protein of low biological value.
- [ ] N3 Germ: mostly unsaturated fat, vitamin E (an antioxidant), B group vitamins (e.g. thiamine) and protein.
- [ ] N4 Refined (white) products lose most of the bran and germ, so they lose fibre, B group vitamins, vitamin E and minerals unless added back (fortification is in the cereals and products subsection).

Nutritive value of cereals (one block; 2022O, 2018O, 2012O, 2004H)
- [ ] V1 Protein: present but of low biological value (low in the essential amino acid lysine); used for growth and repair. Eating cereals with pulses, milk or eggs improves the protein's value (complementary proteins).
- [ ] V2 Carbohydrate: starch, for heat and energy; fibre in wholegrain cereals.
- [ ] V3 Fat: low overall, mostly unsaturated, mainly in the germ.
- [ ] V4 Vitamins: B group (thiamine, niacin, riboflavin) and vitamin E (germ).
- [ ] V5 Minerals: calcium, iron and phosphorus (the three the schemes name).
- [ ] V6 Water: low; cereals are dry foods that keep well.

Dietetic value of cereals (one block; 2022O, 2018O)
- [ ] D1 Starchy staple food: an inexpensive source of energy; the staple in many countries (2017O).
- [ ] D2 Fibre in wholegrain cereals prevents constipation and bowel disorders by stimulating peristalsis; fibre also gives a feeling of fullness, helping weight control.
- [ ] D3 Soluble fibre (oats, barley) helps lower blood cholesterol and slows glucose absorption.
- [ ] D4 The fat is unsaturated, so it does not contribute to cholesterol build-up (2018 scheme).
- [ ] D5 Versatile, quick and easy to prepare, inexpensive.
- [ ] D6 Limits: gluten cannot be tolerated by people with coeliac disease (wheat, barley, rye; oats unless labelled gluten-free); phytic acid in the bran binds iron and calcium and reduces their absorption (2018 scheme).

Wholegrain cereals (one block; 2026H "evaluate")
- [ ] W1 Wholegrain means all three parts are kept: bran, endosperm and germ (wholemeal flour and bread, brown rice, porridge oats, wholegrain breakfast cereals, wholewheat pasta).
- [ ] W2 They give more fibre, B group vitamins, vitamin E and minerals than refined products of the same cereal, and a slower release of energy; they are filling.
- [ ] W3 Contribution to a healthy diet: wholemeal cereals, breads, potatoes, pasta and rice form the second shelf of the Food Pyramid; wholegrain choices raise fibre intake. Evaluate with limits: coeliac disease, phytic acid, and sugar, salt or fat added to some wholegrain products (read the nutrition panel).
- [ ] W4 Why some people avoid or limit cereals (2012O): coeliac disease or gluten intolerance, low-carbohydrate or weight-loss diets, bloating, low glycaemic index diets.
- [ ] W5 Main sources of cereals in the Irish diet (2012O): bread, pasta, rice, breakfast cereals, porridge, biscuits, cakes.
- [ ] W6 Menu use (2022O): a high-fibre day for a school child uses wholegrain cereal, brown bread, brown rice or wholewheat pasta; give a one-line example only.

## 3. Traps

- Do not use: B group vitamins and vitamin E as endosperm nutrients (the 2004 scheme lists them there), because they are concentrated in the bran and germ; the endosperm gives starch and protein.
- Do not use: "bran is the richest source of all the B vitamins and of vitamin E", because the germ carries the vitamin E and much of the thiamine; say bran and germ are rich in B group vitamins.
- Do not use: percentages for the endosperm, or fibre amounts, because they vary and no scheme requires them.
- Cereal protein is low biological value, not high.
- Bran is cellulose (insoluble fibre), not starch. Soluble fibre belongs mainly to oats and barley.
- Wholegrain is not the same as "brown" or "multigrain": a brown loaf made from refined flour is not wholegrain.
- Neighbouring subsections: named cereals, products, fortification and storage are in the cereals and products subsection; milling, extraction rates, flour types and gluten structure are in the milling subsection; gelatinisation and heat effects are in the cooking subsection. Digestion, glycaemic index and coeliac disease as a diet belong to the digestion and special-diets chapters; give a line here, not an account.
- Do not describe grain varieties or growing; not examined.

## 4. What exams ask

- State the nutritional significance of each part of the wheat grain (bran: fibre and peristalsis, B vitamins, iron; endosperm: starch for energy, protein; germ: unsaturated fat, vitamin E, B vitamins). Marking is "relevant nutrient and its function".
- Identify grain parts from descriptions or fill gaps (endosperm largest, starch; bran cellulose; germ small, at the base).
- Give an account of the nutritive value (low biological value protein, starch, fibre, unsaturated fat, B vitamins, vitamin E, calcium, iron, phosphorus, little water) and the dietetic value (energy, fibre, versatile, cheap, unsaturated fat, gluten and phytic acid as limits) of cereals; typically five points at four marks, two nutritive, two dietetic and one other.
- Higher Level, evaluate the contribution of wholegrain cereals: all three parts kept, more fibre and nutrients than refined, named benefits, drawbacks, balance in the whole diet.
- Reasons some people limit cereals; main sources of cereals in the Irish diet.
- Plan a high-fibre menu (three meals including a packed lunch).
