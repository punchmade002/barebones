# Package: hom-food-additives-s5 — Legal control of additives in the EU

- **loId:** `hom-food-additives-s5` · **chapterId:** `hom-food-additives` · **syllabusRef:** 1.3.6
- **Syllabus scope:** outline of the legal control of the use of preservatives, colours, emulsifiers and antioxidants in the European Union under Regulation (EC) No 1333/2008 on food additives, to include E numbers. Also examined: 2022H, 2018H, 2006H (the 2018 and 2006 questions ask how EU law regulates additives, 8 to 10 marks; 2022H asks for additive functions and E-number use). No Higher Level marks in this outline entry; mark nothing HL.
- **Prepared by:** a higher model, 2026-11-23. The 2001 syllabus named an older EU control; the June 2027 instrument is Regulation (EC) No 1333/2008 (outline Appendix A). Marking schemes up to 2018 name the Scientific Committee for Food; that body no longer exists (see Traps). Teach the content the schemes expect in terms of today's regulation.

## 1. Must cover

The legal framework (one block)
- [ ] L1 Additive use in the EU is controlled by Regulation (EC) No 1333/2008 on food additives. It is a regulation, so it applies directly in every Member State, including Ireland, without a separate Irish Act.
- [ ] L2 Purpose: protect consumer health; allow a single market in food; make sure additives are used only where there is a technological need and consumers are not misled.
- [ ] L3 Preservatives, colours, emulsifiers and antioxidants are among the categories covered (the syllabus names these four); sweeteners and others are covered too.
- [ ] L4 Related EU rules (name only): flavourings are covered by their own regulation, Regulation (EC) No 1334/2008; vitamins and minerals added to food by Regulation (EC) No 1925/2006.

The approved list and safety testing (one block)
- [ ] U1 Only additives on the EU's approved list (the Union list) may be used, and only in the foods and at the maximum levels it permits.
- [ ] U2 Every additive is safety-tested before approval. The European Food Safety Authority (EFSA) assesses the safety and sets an acceptable daily intake; the European Commission authorises the additive. Scheme wording: "every product has to be tested".
- [ ] U3 Additives approved are re-evaluated as scientific knowledge changes.

E numbers (one block)
- [ ] E1 Each approved additive is given an E number; the E means Europe (EU approval). Flavourings are not given E numbers.
- [ ] E2 The E number or the additive's name must appear on the label, after its class name (e.g. preservative: E220 or preservative: sulphur dioxide).
- [ ] E3 Number ranges as a guide (approximate; there are exceptions): E100–E199 colours; E200–E299 preservatives; E300–E399 antioxidants and acidity regulators; E400–E499 thickeners, stabilisers and emulsifiers; E500–E599 acidity regulators and anti-caking agents; E600–E699 flavour enhancers; E900–E999 sweeteners and glazing agents. Examples tie to the classes subsection: E102 tartrazine, E202 potassium sorbate, E300 vitamin C, E320 BHA, E621 MSG, E951 aspartame, E471 mono- and diglycerides.
- [ ] E4 Benefit for the consumer: the label tells them what the additive is, what it does and that it is approved. A student can read E-number labelled foods to avoid additives they react to.

Conditions of use (one block; the points the schemes mark)
- [ ] C1 Must not be a hazard to health.
- [ ] C2 Must not mislead the consumer about the nature or quality of the food.
- [ ] C3 Must serve a reasonable technological need: not used to disguise faulty processing or poor-quality raw material, and must not reduce the nutritive value of the food.
- [ ] C4 Must be used at the lowest level needed to achieve the effect.
- [ ] C5 Restrictions by food: colourings are not permitted in fresh fruit, vegetables, meat, poultry and fish (the schemes' list); sweeteners are not permitted in food for infants and young children; baby foods are restricted in additives (the scheme: preservatives, BHA, BHT and colourings are not allowed in baby food).
- [ ] C6 Labelling of additives: class name followed by the name or E number (see the packaging and labelling subsection for the rest of the label).

Enforcement in Ireland (one block)
- [ ] I1 The FSAI (set up under the Food Safety Authority of Ireland Act 1998) is responsible for the safe use of additives in Ireland and coordinates enforcement; advises ministers, regulators, industry and consumers.
- [ ] I2 Checks (inspection and sampling) are carried out by the official agencies under FSAI service contracts: the Department of Agriculture, Food and the Marine, the HSE Environmental Health Service and Public Analyst Laboratories, local authorities.
- [ ] I3 The rules on official controls generally are Regulation (EU) 2017/625 (food-safety chapter); only name it here if needed.

## 3. Traps

- Do not use: the Scientific Committee for Food (SCF) as the body that tests additives (named in the 2018 scheme), because SCF has been replaced by EFSA, which does the EU safety assessment. JECFA (the FAO/WHO committee) is the international body that also evaluates additives, so naming it is not wrong; the answer is "tested before approval", with EFSA for the EU.
- Do not use: "FSAI processes applications for new foods and processing methods" as written in the old notes, because it is not confirmed as an FSAI function and isn't an additive rule; say the FSAI advises and coordinates.
- Do not use: E322 lecithin under "E400 to E499"; lecithin is E322, in the E300 range. Use E471 or E440 pectin as examples of the E400 range.
- Do not use: the earlier Irish additive instruments, the Food Hygiene Regulations 1950–1989 or the Labelling Regulations 1982 and 1991, because they are superseded.
- Do not use: lists of every additive's permitted level or the acceptable daily intake numbers, because they change.
- Not every E number is "bad" and not every additive has one: E300 is vitamin C, E100 turmeric.
- E number ranges are a guide, not a rule: E322 lecithin sits in the antioxidant range though it is an emulsifier; the next range overlaps. Say the number tells you it is approved, and the label's class word tells you what it does.
- The EU regulation sets the control; the Irish bodies enforce it. A question on "how is the use of additives regulated by EU legislation" is answered with the regulation's content (approved list, testing, E numbers, conditions), not with the FSAI's functions, though one enforcement sentence adds marks in the 2018 scheme.
- Neighbours: what each class does and its examples belong to the classes subsection; label contents in general belong to the packaging and labelling subsection; food-safety law and hygiene belong to the food-safety chapter; the Consumer Protection Act 2007 belongs to the consumer-rights chapter.

## 4. What exams ask

- Explain how EU legislation regulates the use of food additives (2018H, 10 marks, two points at 5 marks; 2006H, 8 marks, two accurate facts at 4 marks): list of approved additives that have been tested; each approved additive has an E number (except flavourings); E number or name on the label; must not reduce nutritive value; must not disguise poor processing or faults; must not be a health hazard; must not mislead the consumer; smallest effective quantity; colourings barred from fresh foods and baby foods; sweeteners barred from infant food; the FSAI enforces in Ireland. The strongest answer groups the points into approval, labelling and use conditions, and gives at least four concrete rules.
- Outline the function of an anti-caking agent and an emulsifier (2022H) and name the E-number system (links to the classes subsection).
- True/false on additives (2017O): additives prolong shelf life (true); artificial colourings are permitted in baby food (false); additive use is regulated by EU legislation (true).
- Use of E numbers on a label: they identify the additive, show it is approved and let consumers avoid those they react to.
