# Package: hom-vitamins-minerals-s5 — Factors affecting the absorption of minerals

## 1. Must cover

Absorption basics (assumed)
- [ ] B1 (assumed) Absorption is the movement of digested nutrients from the small intestine into the blood. A mineral must be soluble and in the right chemical form to cross the intestinal wall, so what is eaten with it, and what the body needs at the time, change how much of the mineral in a food is absorbed. This is "bioavailability": a food can contain plenty of a mineral yet give little of it.
- [ ] B2 (assumed) Most iron is absorbed in the duodenum (first part of the small intestine); it is carried in the blood by the protein transferrin and stored as ferritin in the liver; the body has little way of getting rid of excess iron, so absorption is regulated (the body absorbs more when stores are low, for example in pregnancy or heavy blood loss). Iron is lost through shed skin and gut cells, sweat and menstruation.

Haem and non-haem iron
- [ ] H1 Haem iron is the iron in haemoglobin and myoglobin, so it comes from animal foods: red meat, liver and offal, black pudding, poultry, fish. It is already in the ferrous form (Fe²⁺), is soluble, and is absorbed efficiently; other foods in the meal affect it little.
- [ ] H2 Non-haem iron comes from plant foods and eggs: wholegrain and fortified cereals, pulses, dark green leafy vegetables, dried fruit, nuts and seeds, egg yolk. It is mostly in the ferric form (Fe³⁺), which is not very soluble, and must be changed (reduced) to the ferrous form before it can be absorbed; it is absorbed poorly and is strongly affected by other foods in the meal.
- [ ] H3 The "meat factor": eating meat, fish or poultry in the same meal increases the absorption of non-haem iron from vegetables or cereals. Practical meal ideas: chilli with beans and beef; fish with potatoes and broccoli. Vegetarians and vegans, with only non-haem iron, need to be more careful (vitamin C at every meal).
- [ ] H4 A past paper asks students to describe both forms and give a source of each; give the form, the iron state (ferrous or ferric), the absorbability and one or two sources.

Factors that increase iron absorption
- [ ] IE1 Vitamin C (ascorbic acid): acts as a reducing agent: changes ferric iron to the soluble ferrous form, and keeps it soluble; so eat vitamin C foods with iron foods (orange juice with fortified cereal, peppers or tomatoes with a bean dish, broccoli with meat). The most-asked iron factor; the answer to "name a nutrient that helps iron absorption" is vitamin C.
- [ ] IE2 Hydrochloric acid in the stomach: the acid keeps iron soluble and helps change ferric to ferrous iron; people with low stomach acid, or who take antacids, absorb less.
- [ ] IE3 Haem iron or meat, fish and poultry in the same meal (meat factor, H3).
- [ ] IE4 Body need: absorption rises when iron stores are low (growth, pregnancy, blood loss).

Factors that decrease iron absorption
- [ ] II1 Tannins (polyphenols) in tea, and in coffee and red wine, bind iron into an insoluble compound; leave an hour or so between the drink and an iron-rich meal. Do not give exact times.
- [ ] II2 Phytic acid (phytates) in wholegrain cereals, bran and pulses binds iron (and zinc and calcium) in an insoluble form; soaking, sprouting, yeast fermentation (bread) and cooking pulses reduce phytate.
- [ ] II3 Oxalic acid (oxalates) in spinach, rhubarb and beetroot greens binds iron; this is why spinach is a poor iron source despite its reputation.
- [ ] II4 Excess dietary fibre carries minerals through the gut and binds them.
- [ ] II5 Calcium taken in large amounts at the same meal (dairy or supplements) competes with iron and reduces its absorption.
- [ ] II6 Excess alcohol and a lack of stomach acid; some medicines (antacids). 2023H scheme also lists excess protein, see Traps.

The role of vitamins in absorbing calcium and iron
- [ ] V1 Vitamin D is the key vitamin for calcium: it makes the lining of the small intestine produce a calcium-binding (carrier) protein, so more calcium crosses into the blood; it also helps the absorption of phosphorus and the laying down of calcium in bone. Without vitamin D, calcium absorption is poor even if the diet has plenty of calcium (rickets, osteomalacia). The answer to "which vitamin is needed for calcium absorption" is vitamin D.
- [ ] V2 Vitamin C: for iron as above (ferric to ferrous). Marking schemes also say it helps calcium absorption; present this as "helps" without a mechanism.
- [ ] V3 A 2009 paper asks to name a vitamin for each of calcium and iron: calcium D, iron C.

Factors that increase calcium absorption
- [ ] CE1 Vitamin D (V1).
- [ ] CE2 Lactose, the sugar in milk, increases absorption (so milk, cheese and yoghurt are good sources).
- [ ] CE3 An acid environment in the stomach and the top of the small intestine keeps calcium soluble; vitamin C and amino acids from protein help in this way.
- [ ] CE4 Adequate protein: some amino acids combine with calcium and keep it soluble.
- [ ] CE5 Phosphorus in a balanced ratio with calcium (the two are needed together for bone).
- [ ] CE6 Parathormone (parathyroid hormone) raises blood calcium by increasing absorption from the gut (through vitamin D activation) and release from bone; oestrogen supports calcium retention in bone, so after the menopause when oestrogen falls absorption and bone density fall.
- [ ] CE7 Weight-bearing exercise and growth periods increase demand; absorption is higher in childhood and pregnancy.

Factors that decrease calcium absorption (calcium inhibitors, 2025H and 2007H)
- [ ] CI1 Lack of vitamin D.
- [ ] CI2 [HL] Phytates and oxalates (separate block below).
- [ ] CI3 Excess dietary fibre binds calcium and speeds it through the gut.
- [ ] CI4 Excess fat in the gut forms insoluble calcium soaps with fatty acids.
- [ ] CI5 Excess salt and caffeine (tea, coffee) increase the loss of calcium in the urine; alcohol and smoking reduce absorption and bone density; fizzy drinks containing phosphoric acid are listed in the scheme (excess phosphorus upsets the calcium balance).
- [ ] CI6 Tannins in tea and coffee; lack of stomach acid; falling oestrogen after menopause; old age, when the gut absorbs less.

Phytates and oxalates and calcium (Higher Level only)
- [ ] PH1 Phytic acid (phytate) is found in the outer layers of cereal grains (bran, wholemeal), in pulses, nuts and seeds. It binds calcium to form calcium phytate, an insoluble salt that cannot be absorbed and passes out in the faeces. Yeast and soaking break phytate down (the enzyme phytase), so leavened wholemeal bread is less affected than unleavened.
- [ ] PH2 Oxalic acid (oxalate) is found in spinach, rhubarb, beetroot leaves, chard, cocoa and tea. It binds calcium to form calcium oxalate, which is insoluble and not absorbed. The calcium in spinach is therefore poorly absorbed despite the amount of calcium in the leaves; the calcium in milk or kale is absorbed better.
- [ ] PH3 The effect is greatest when high-phytate or high-oxalate foods are eaten at the same meal as the calcium-rich food, and when the diet is otherwise low in calcium. Suggested practical advice: eat calcium foods at a different time, use leavened bread, soak and cook pulses, and take vitamin D.

## 3. Traps

- Ferrous means Fe²⁺ (absorbable); ferric means Fe³⁺ (needs reducing). Vitamin C is the reducing agent; it does not change iron into haem iron.
- Phytates and oxalates affect calcium (Higher Level syllabus) but also iron and zinc; in this subsection give calcium as the main case and a one-line mention for iron.
- Calcium-rich spinach is the standard example of a food that has the mineral but poor absorption; do not list spinach as a good source of either calcium or iron.
- Vitamin D helps absorb calcium; vitamin C helps absorb iron. Mixing these is the commonest mark loss.
- Do not blur absorption (uptake from the gut) with loss (excretion in urine). Salt and caffeine mostly raise urinary loss; fibre, fat, phytate and oxalate lower absorption. Schemes treat both as "inhibiting" calcium, so name which mechanism you use.
- Do not use: "vitamins B and B12 help absorb calcium", because the 2025 scheme lists them loosely but the mechanism is not established; teach vitamins D and C only.
- Do not use: "protein reduces iron absorption" as a general rule, because meat protein raises non-haem iron absorption (meat factor); only some plant and dairy proteins reduce it. The 2023 scheme lists excess protein and excess alcohol, but give only the safe statement that very large amounts of protein or alcohol can upset iron and calcium balance.
- Do not use: any figure for percentage absorbed, hours between tea and a meal, or amounts of calcium or iron, because the numbers vary by source and are not in the schemes.
- Do not use: that cooking spinach "removes" oxalate fully or that boiling pulses removes all phytate, because only part is reduced.
- Neighbouring subsections: sources, functions and deficiency of calcium and iron, and anaemia and osteoporosis (mineral elements); functions of vitamins C and D (vitamin subsections); digestion and absorption (digestion chapter).

## 4. What exams ask

- Explain two factors that affect iron absorption (2026H, with part (d) of 2026 Ordinary): vitamin C reduces ferric to ferrous, tannins in tea and coffee bind iron; also acceptable: meat factor, phytates, oxalates, stomach acid, fibre. Each factor needs the "why".
- Identify and describe the factors that affect absorption of iron (2023H, 20 marks): haem with non-haem, vitamin C foods, hydrochloric acid, tannins, fibre, phytic and oxalic acids; practical meal advice scores.
- Describe haem and non-haem iron with a source of each (2024H): form, ferrous or ferric, absorbability, sources.
- Outline the factors that affect calcium absorption (2025H, 15 marks): aids (vitamin D, phosphorus, protein, acidity, lactose, hormones) and inhibitors (tannins, excess fibre, fat, salt, alcohol, phytic, oxalic and phosphoric acids, smoking); calcium inhibitors also in 2007H as a two-mark item.
- Name the vitamin for calcium (D) and iron (C) (2009H; also 2008 and 2004 Ordinary: name one nutrient that helps iron or calcium absorption).
- 2014H and 2021H absorption parts: the same families of factors set inside questions on bone health or iron-deficient diets; expect a practical link such as an iron-rich meal with a vitamin C drink, or advice for someone at risk of osteoporosis.
