## 1. Must cover

How and why guidelines are formulated (one block)
- [ ] F1 Dietary guidelines are official advice, based on scientific evidence, on the types and amounts of food that will keep a population healthy and lower the risk of diet-related disease. They are for the general healthy population, not for individuals with special medical needs.
- [ ] F2 How they are formulated: expert committees review nutrition and health research, national food consumption surveys and data on diet-related disease, and turn the findings into practical advice on foods rather than nutrients. They are reviewed as new evidence appears. In Ireland the Department of Health publishes the healthy eating guidelines and Food Pyramid; the HSE and the Food Safety Authority of Ireland (FSAI) use and support them.
- [ ] F3 Why: to ensure enough nutrients, prevent deficiency, and reduce the risk of obesity, type 2 diabetes, coronary heart disease, high blood pressure, stroke, some cancers and osteoporosis; to give consumers, schools and food planners one consistent message.
- [ ] F4 Current Irish healthy eating guidelines (give as eight or so principles): eat plenty of vegetables, salad and fruit (five to seven servings a day); base meals on wholemeal cereals, breads, potatoes, pasta and rice; include milk, yogurt and cheese (lower-fat choices); include lean meat, poultry, fish, eggs, beans and nuts, with fish regularly; use fats, spreads and oils sparingly, with unsaturated oils; limit foods and drinks high in fat, sugar and salt to small amounts (the top shelf; not needed for health); drink water as the main drink; limit alcohol; choose cooking methods that add little fat (grill, steam, bake, boil).
- [ ] F5 Why each matters (one clause each): fruit, vegetables and wholegrains give fibre, vitamins, minerals and antioxidants; saturated fat raises blood cholesterol and coronary heart disease risk; excess salt raises blood pressure; sugar adds energy without nutrients and causes tooth decay and obesity; fibre prevents constipation and bowel disorders.
- [ ] F6 Healthy eating guidelines older versions: schemes up to about 2013 describe older pyramids with different serving numbers; students should use the current pyramid (see Traps).

The Food Pyramid (one block)
- [ ] P1 The Food Pyramid turns the guidelines into the types and relative amounts of food needed each day. Foods with similar nutrients are grouped on shelves; the base shelves are eaten most, the top shelf least.
- [ ] P2 Shelves of the current Irish pyramid (Department of Health, "Healthy Food for Life"): vegetables, salad and fruit (the base); wholemeal cereals and breads, potatoes, pasta and rice; milk, yogurt and cheese; meat, poultry, fish, eggs, beans and nuts; fats, spreads and oils (a small shelf; use sparingly); and the top shelf of foods and drinks high in fat, sugar and salt, which are not needed for health and have no recommended servings. Water is advised as the main drink.
- [ ] P3 Servings: five to seven servings of vegetables, salad and fruit a day for adults and teenagers. The number of servings from the other shelves is adjusted for age, sex, activity level and life stage (for example children, teenagers, pregnant and breastfeeding women, older adults). Current figures: three servings of milk, yogurt and cheese a day (five for ages 9 to 18); three to five servings of wholemeal cereals, breads, potatoes, pasta and rice. Give a serving example (one apple or one medium piece of fruit; a glass of milk counts as one dairy serving).
- [ ] P4 How it is used to plan meals (2026O, 20 marks; 2013O, 16 marks): take most foods from the large lower shelves, some from the middle, very little from the top; vary foods within each shelf; include vegetables or fruit at every meal; choose wholemeal and lower-fat versions; plan meals across breakfast, lunch, dinner and snacks so the day's choices cover every shelf; match portions to the person; this supports energy balance and prevents deficiency and excess.
- [ ] P5 Diagram: draw and label the Food Pyramid with its shelves and the main foods on each, marking the base and top shelf. (2026O supplies a diagram of the pyramid.)
- [ ] P6 Limits: it guides what and roughly how much, not an exact diet; it does not replace advice for medical conditions.

RDAs (one block)
- [ ] R1 RDA = Recommended Dietary Allowance: the amount of a nutrient that meets the needs of almost all healthy people in a group. It is set per nutrient and differs by age, sex and life stage (growth, pregnancy, lactation).
- [ ] R2 Uses: planning menus, checking whether a day's diet supplies enough of each nutrient (compare the nutrient total with the RDA), setting food-labelling and supplement standards, guiding institutions such as schools and hospitals.
- [ ] R3 Limits: a guide for groups, not an exact requirement for one person; taking much more than the RDA gives no extra benefit and can be harmful for some nutrients.
- [ ] R4 Do not give RDA numbers for nutrients here (only where a marking scheme states them).

Food composition tables and computer programs (one block, method)
- [ ] T1 A food composition table lists the energy and nutrient content of foods, usually per 100 g of edible food, for raw or cooked states. Irish tables are produced from national food analysis.
- [ ] T2 Use with a menu: for each food, divide the grams eaten by 100 and multiply by the per-100 g values; add across all foods to get the day's totals for energy and each nutrient; compare with the RDAs and guidelines; identify what is low or high; adjust the menu.
- [ ] T3 Worked example with sample data: 150 g of a food with 3 g fibre per 100 g supplies 150 divided by 100 times 3, which is 4.5 g.
- [ ] T4 Computer nutrition programs hold a food composition database; the user enters foods and amounts and the program totals the nutrients and compares them with guidelines or RDAs. Advantages: fast, accurate, many foods and recipes; limitations: depends on correct portions and the right cooking state.

Serving recommendations (one block)
- [ ] S1 A serving (portion) is a standard measure of food used to plan and count intake against the pyramid (2007O).
- [ ] S2 Serving sizes and numbers rise for growing teenagers and active people and fall for older, less active adults; pregnant and breastfeeding women need extra servings of some groups.

Food supplements (one block)
- [ ] U1 Food supplements are concentrated sources of nutrients such as vitamins, minerals, polyunsaturated fatty acids and amino acids, in tablet, capsule, powder or liquid form (2013O scheme: "concentrates of important nutrients").
- [ ] U2 Why they are popular (2013O, 8 marks, "comment on the growing popularity"): busy lifestyles and poor diets, media and advertising, a wish to prevent illness, special needs (pregnancy, vegans, older adults, restricted diets, allergies, illness), sports performance, convenience.
- [ ] U3 Benefits: fill a gap (folic acid before and in early pregnancy to prevent neural tube defects; iron for iron-deficiency anaemia on medical advice; vitamin B12 for vegans; calcium and vitamin D where intake is low; vitamin D, 15 micrograms a day all year, advised for all adults aged 65 and over).
- [ ] U4 Cautions: a supplement does not replace a varied diet (fibre and other substances come only from food); excess of some vitamins and minerals can be harmful, especially fat-soluble vitamins; they can cost a lot; take medical advice.

Lifestyle guidelines for health (one block)
- [ ] L1 (2026O, three guidelines, 15 marks): regular physical activity, built into daily life (brisk walking, cycling, sport; weight-bearing and muscle-strengthening activity); it improves cardiovascular fitness, bone and muscle health, weight control and mental wellbeing and may raise protective HDL cholesterol.
- [ ] L2 Mental wellbeing: adequate sleep and rest, stress management, realistic workload, relaxation and social contact.
- [ ] L3 Avoid smoking and second-hand smoke; limit alcohol (empty kilocalories, harm to liver and judgement).
- [ ] L4 Drink plenty of water; keep a healthy body weight; maintain hygiene; have regular health checks.

## 3. Traps

- The Food Pyramid is not the same as the guidelines; it is the picture and servings that put them into practice.
- Fruit and vegetables form the base shelf in the current pyramid; the cereals and potatoes shelf sits above it. Do not describe wholegrain starches as the biggest shelf.
- Do not confuse RDA with the daily intake someone actually eats, or with a label's "reference intake".
- Food composition tables give values per 100 g, not per portion, and for raw or cooked food separately.
- Supplements are not the same as fortified foods.
- Vitamin and mineral roles are covered in their chapters; here name only the supplement uses.
- Menu planning for pregnancy, children, vegetarians, coeliacs and the elderly belongs to the meal-planning chapter; this subsection only teaches the pyramid and tools.
- Do not use: "bottom shelves: wholegrain breads, cereals, potatoes, pasta and rice, which form the largest share" and "the larger lower shelves... in the order cereals first", because the current Irish pyramid puts vegetables, salad and fruit on the base shelf.
- Do not use: serving numbers of 6 or more cereal servings, 4 or 5 fruit and vegetable servings from older pyramids (SLAN 2007 chart; 2013 schemes), because they belong to the superseded pyramid; use five to seven for vegetables, salad and fruit, three to five for cereals and three for milk (five for ages 9 to 18).
- Do not use: "Health Promotion Unit" as a current body (the syllabus setting names it), because its functions now sit with the HSE and Healthy Ireland.
- Do not use: any RDA or recommended number of grams or milligrams, any daily salt limit or fibre target, because the figures change by guideline version and are not stated by the schemes.

## 4. What exams ask

- Explain how the food pyramid is used to plan healthy balanced meals (2026O, 20 marks; 2013O, 16 marks; 2013H analysis of consumption by shelf in Q1). Content: shelves and what is on each, servings, most from the base, little from the top, portions adjusted to age, sex and activity, variety, link to energy balance and disease prevention.
- Healthy eating guidelines as a basis for menu planning in nearly every menu question (2007O, 2015O, 2019O, 2021O): write three meals that follow the guidelines and justify choices.
- Describe three lifestyle guidelines (2026O, 15 marks): activity, sleep and stress management, avoid tobacco and limit alcohol, each with a benefit.
- Comment on the growing popularity of food supplements (2013O, 8 marks, two points at 4 marks): definition and two reasons, one or two cautions.
- Use of food composition tables or nutrient analysis: calculate nutrients in a menu, interpret a table, or explain how tables are used when planning menus.
- RDA: define it, state what it varies with, and say how it is used to check a menu.
- Data on consumption by shelf (2013H): comment on compliance rates, reasons for differences, consequences. This needs the pyramid shelf names and an ability to describe trends.
- Exam-tip material: name the shelf and its foods; say "most from the base"; link each guideline to a health benefit; for supplements give a definition, a reason and a caution.
