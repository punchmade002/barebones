# Package: hom-food-preservation-s3 — Heat processing

- **loId:** `hom-food-preservation-s3` · **chapterId:** `hom-food-preservation` · **syllabusRef:** 1.3.9
- **Syllabus scope:** canning, bottling and other heat treatments; jam making and its principles. Also examined: principles of jam making (2024H); canning and dehydration of fruit and vegetables (2021H). No Higher Level only items marked. Ordinary and Higher Level.
- **Prepared by:** a higher model, 2026-11-23. This subsection teaches heat as a principle (what heat does to organisms and enzymes, why sealing matters), the heat treatments (pasteurisation, sterilisation and UHT, commercial canning, home bottling), the effect on food, and jam making with its principles. Chutney and pickles are heat plus chemicals and are taught in the chemical-preservation subsection. The practical write-up of jam (a method to carry out in full) is in the practical-application subsection; here the emphasis is why each step is done. Pectin chemistry (gel formation) belongs to the carbohydrates chapter and is used here at outline level.

## 1. Must cover

Principle of heat processing (one block)
- [ ] H1 Heat destroys micro-organisms (bacteria, yeasts, moulds) and inactivates (denatures) the enzymes that cause spoilage. Sealing the food in a clean airtight container while it is hot (or sealing before heating) then prevents re-contamination.
- [ ] H2 Vegetative cells are destroyed by moderate heat; spores of some bacteria (Clostridium botulinum is the named one) are very heat resistant and survive boiling at normal pressure, so low-acid foods need higher temperatures in a pressure cooker or retort. (Named bacteria belong to the food safety chapter: name only Clostridium botulinum.)
- [ ] H3 Heat changes the colour, texture, flavour and nutritive value of the food (it is cooked), and the heat-sensitive water-soluble vitamins (C and B group) are reduced.
- [ ] H4 Acid foods (fruit, tomatoes, pickles) are easier to preserve by heat because acid hinders organisms and their spores; low-acid foods (peas, beans, meat, fish) need a higher temperature.

Pasteurisation and sterilisation (one block; examined 2008O for milk)
- [ ] P1 Pasteurisation: a moderate heat treatment that destroys most disease-causing (pathogenic) bacteria and reduces spoilage organisms, but not all spores; the food keeps for a limited time and must be refrigerated. Milk: 72 °C for 15 seconds, then rapid cooling. Used for milk, cream, fruit juice, beer and some canned foods.
- [ ] P2 Sterilisation: a higher temperature for longer (milk sterilised in the bottle; canned foods) destroys all micro-organisms including spores, so the sealed product keeps at room temperature; flavour and colour change and vitamin losses are greater.
- [ ] P3 UHT (ultra-heat treatment): milk heated to about 135 °C for a few seconds, then packed aseptically into sterile cartons; keeps for months unopened at room temperature, refrigerate after opening; slight cooked flavour.
- [ ] P4 Three processing methods for milk to make it safe (2008O scheme): pasteurisation; sterilisation or UHT; evaporation or dehydration (drying).

Canning (one block; examined 2021H, 2005H, 2007O, 2004O)
- [ ] K1 Commercial canning: food is prepared (washed, peeled, trimmed, blanched where needed), packed into cans, covered with brine, syrup or oil, air is removed (exhausting), the can is sealed (double seam), then heated in a retort (a large pressure cooker) at a high temperature for a set time, then cooled quickly. The heat sterilises the contents and the sealed can prevents re-contamination. Cans are lined with a lacquer to prevent the metal reacting with the food.
- [ ] K2 Food in cans keeps at room temperature for a long time (shelf-stable) without refrigeration; can be eaten straight away.
- [ ] K3 Safety signs: a bulging can end or lid, hissing, leaking, rust, deep dents or an off smell mean the contents should be discarded; do not taste.
- [ ] K4 Effects on food (examined 2021H, 2005H): loss of heat-sensitive and water-soluble vitamins (C and thiamine B1), partly into the liquid; softer texture; change in colour and flavour; added sugar from syrup, added sodium from brine, added fat from oil; minerals and fibre largely kept; the liquid can be used in cooking to keep nutrients.
- [ ] K5 Foods suited to canning: fruit, tomatoes, vegetables such as peas and beans, fish (tuna, sardines, salmon), meat, soup, baked beans (named in the 2007O scheme: whole tomatoes and carrots).

Bottling and home heat methods (one block)
- [ ] B1 Bottling: fruit (and tomatoes) packed in clean sterilised glass jars with sugar syrup or water, sealed and heated in a water bath (or oven) so that the contents reach a temperature that destroys organisms and enzymes; seals are closed as the jar cools and a vacuum forms.
- [ ] B2 Jars must be sterilised, undamaged, with new or sound lids; leave headspace; lift hot jars carefully; label with date; store in a cool, dark, dry place; discard any jar whose lid is not tight or domed.
- [ ] B3 Low-acid vegetables and meat should not be bottled in a boiling-water bath at home, because boiling at normal pressure does not destroy Clostridium botulinum spores; they need pressure processing, so home bottling is restricted to fruit and acid foods. (The 2012H scheme names bottling as a heat method.)

Jam making and its principles (one block; examined 2024H, 2025O, 2008H, 2012H, 2011O)
- [ ] J1 Jam: fruit boiled with sugar to a set; its preservation depends on four principles: heat, sugar, acid and pectin, with a sealed jar.
- [ ] J2 Heat: boiling (about 100 °C or higher) destroys micro-organisms and inactivates enzymes in the fruit; fruit is first simmered gently to soften it and release pectin.
- [ ] J3 Sugar: a high concentration of sugar draws water out of any microbial cells by osmosis, so they cannot multiply (preservative action); sugar also helps the gel to form and gives sweetness.
- [ ] J4 Pectin: a carbohydrate in the cell walls of fruit (mostly in skin and core); it forms a gel with sugar and acid when the jam cools, so the jam sets. High pectin: cooking apples, blackcurrants, redcurrants, gooseberries, citrus fruit. Low pectin: strawberries, raspberries, cherries, pears (add pectin-rich fruit or a commercial pectin).
- [ ] J5 Acid: releases pectin from the fruit cell walls and helps the pectin to set; it improves colour and flavour; lemon juice is added for low-acid or low-pectin fruit.
- [ ] J6 Fruit selection: just ripe, firm, sound fruit; under-ripe fruit has more pectin; over-ripe fruit has less pectin and spoils the set.
- [ ] J7 Sealing: pour hot into clean sterilised jars, cover or seal at once so micro-organisms cannot re-enter; a vacuum forms as the jam cools; waxed disc plus cover is the traditional method.
- [ ] J8 Setting point tests: the cold saucer (wrinkle) test and a sugar thermometer (about 105 °C, 220 °F); skim the scum from the surface, using a clean dry spoon.
- [ ] J9 Why jam spoils (examined in method questions): too little sugar; setting point not reached; jars or lids not sterilised; poor seal; dirty or wet spoon; storage in a warm or damp place. Mould on the surface or fermentation (bubbles, alcoholic smell) is the result.
- [ ] J10 Effect of jam making on food: vitamin C lost in boiling; sugar concentration is high; flavour and colour are changed; the fruit is no longer fresh-tasting.
- [ ] J11 Diagram cue: a flow diagram of the jam-making stages is a useful revision aid (prepare, simmer, add sugar, boil to set, test, skim, pot, seal, label).

Link to dehydration of fruit and vegetables (examined 2021H)
- [ ] D1 The 2021H question pairs canning with dehydration as processing effects; this subsection covers the canning side (K4); the drying side is in the dehydration subsection. Cross-reference by title only.

## 3. Traps

- Jam is preserved by sugar and heat, not by "acid" alone; acid helps pectin release (and colour); the preservative action is sugar by osmosis plus sealing. A common wrong answer is "sugar kills bacteria by heat".
- Pectin is not a preservative. It makes jam set.
- Pasteurisation does not sterilise: it leaves some organisms and spores, so pasteurised foods are chilled. UHT and canning sterilise, so they keep at room temperature while sealed.
- Canning (commercial, in a can) and bottling (home, in glass) are different: commercial canning uses a retort at high temperature; home bottling of vegetables is not recommended without pressure processing.
- Do not mix up chutney and jam: jam sets with pectin, acid and sugar; chutney is boiled with vinegar, sugar and spices and does not need pectin. Chutney and pickles belong to the chemical-preservation subsection.
- Dried and frozen fruit and vegetables belong to their own subsections; do not describe freezing or drying here beyond the cross-reference.
- Sterilisation of jars is a hygiene step; do not call it "pasteurising the jars".
- Pectin gel chemistry (structure) belongs to the carbohydrates chapter (Higher Level); keep to "pectin forms a gel with sugar and acid".
- The step-by-step jam practical and safety guidelines belong to the practical-application subsection.
- Do not use: "In Ireland, commercial canning is covered by food hygiene law, which the FSAI enforces through its official agencies, such as the HSE Environmental Health Service", because the FSAI oversees rather than enforces (official agencies enforce under service contracts) and food law belongs to the food safety chapter; leave it out here.
- Do not use: "Canned vegetables ... frozen produce retains more vitamin C than canned produce of the same food because the processing temperatures are lower" as a general rule without a basis; keep to the scheme reason (canning needs very high temperatures for long periods; freezing needs only a short blanch).
- Do not use: the claim that "acidic foods such as fruit can be processed in boiling water because the acid stops the spores from germinating" stated as a rule for all acid foods; say acid foods are easier to preserve and fruit is routinely bottled in a water bath.

## 4. What exams ask

- Two underlying principles of jam making (2024H, 6 marks): heat destroys micro-organisms and enzymes; sugar draws water from cells by osmosis; pectin and acid make it set; sealing prevents re-entry. Two principles, each with its explanation.
- A method of home preservation for a surplus of fruit or vegetables (2008H, 15 marks; 2012H, 16 marks, "a method involving heat" with principles): jam or chutney making, bottling or drying; the answer names the method and explains heat, sugar (osmosis), fruit/pectin, acid.
- Account of jam making (2025O, 25 marks): wash and sterilise jars, prepare the fruit, simmer in a heavy pan to release pectin, add sugar and possibly lemon acid, boil to setting point, test, skim, pot hot, seal, label, store.
- Effects of canning and dehydration on fruit and vegetables (2021H, 6 marks): loss of water-soluble vitamins, change in colour, texture and flavour, micro-organisms and enzymes destroyed, added sugar, salt or fat from syrup, brine or oil.
- Data on canning (2005H, 24 marks): vitamin C and thiamine are lower in canned peas because of high temperatures and long processing; sodium is higher because salt is added.
- Matching and true/false (2011O, 2018O, 2021O, 2004O): pectin sets jam; sugar is a preservative in jam; heat treatment preserves by boiling fruit with sugar, vinegar and spices; name methods that use heat (jam, chutney, canning, bottling, pasteurisation, sterilisation).
- Processing milk (2008O): pasteurisation, sterilisation or UHT, evaporation or dehydration.
- Tip content: for "principles", give each principle with its effect (heat destroys; sugar draws out water by osmosis; pectin and acid set; seal prevents re-entry).
