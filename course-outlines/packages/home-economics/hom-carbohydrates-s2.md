## 1. Must cover

Elements (one block)
- [ ] E1 Carbohydrates contain three elements only: carbon, hydrogen and oxygen. They contain no nitrogen (proteins do). Asked directly at Ordinary Level.
- [ ] E2 In sugars the hydrogen and oxygen are in the ratio 2:1, as in water, which is why the group is called carbohydrate (hydrated carbon). Gloss in one phrase.

Classification (one block)
- [ ] C1 Carbohydrates are classified by the number of sugar (saccharide) units in the molecule: monosaccharides (one unit), disaccharides (two) and polysaccharides (many).
- [ ] C2 Monosaccharides (simple sugars): glucose, fructose, galactose. Formula C₆H₁₂O₆ for all three.
- [ ] C3 Disaccharides (double sugars): sucrose, lactose, maltose. Formula C₁₂H₂₂O₁₁ for all three.
- [ ] C4 Polysaccharides (complex carbohydrates, "complex non-sugars" in the schemes): starch, glycogen, cellulose, pectin. General formula (C₆H₁₀O₅)ₙ (the scheme convention; pectin is not actually made of glucose, so give no monomer for it). Starch, glycogen and cellulose are made of glucose units; sources of each are in the next subsection.
- [ ] C5 Sugars (mono- and disaccharides) are sweet and soluble; polysaccharides are not sweet. Starch and cellulose are the two "main" polysaccharides asked for in the simple three-way classification used by the schemes (sugar, starch and fibre/cellulose).
- [ ] C6 The two ways of grouping: "sugar, starch and fibre" (dietary language) against monosaccharide, disaccharide and polysaccharide (chemical language). Give both because both are asked.
- [ ] C7 A table for revision: class, number of units, formula, examples. This is the shape of the Higher Level question asked in several years.

Basic structure of a monosaccharide (one block, [HL])
- [ ] M1 [HL] A monosaccharide is a single sugar unit that cannot be split into a simpler sugar by hydrolysis.
- [ ] M2 [HL] Glucose has the formula C₆H₁₂O₆: six carbon atoms, twelve hydrogen atoms and six oxygen atoms.
- [ ] M3 [HL] In solution glucose forms a six-membered ring: five carbon atoms and one oxygen atom make up the ring, and the sixth carbon is a CH₂OH group above the ring. Hydroxyl (–OH) groups are attached to the other carbon atoms of the ring. Hydroxyl groups are the groups that join sugar units together.
- [ ] M4 [HL] Fructose and galactose have the same formula as glucose but their atoms are arranged differently (isomers). Fructose can form a five-membered ring, as in sucrose.
- [ ] M5 [HL] Diagram: a ring of glucose, labelling the ring oxygen, the carbon atoms numbered 1 to 6, the CH₂OH group and the hydroxyl groups. In the usual ring diagram for the form of glucose normally drawn (alpha-glucose), the –OH on carbon 1 points down, on carbon 2 down, on carbon 3 up and on carbon 4 down, with CH₂OH up on carbon 5. Show the 2005 scheme's simpler style if preferred: the ring of C, C, C, C and O with H and OH groups and a CH₂OH group. Do not ask the student to draw more than the labelled ring.

Formation of disaccharides and polysaccharides (one block, [HL])
- [ ] F1 [HL] A disaccharide forms when two monosaccharides join with the elimination of a molecule of water. This is a condensation reaction. Equation: C₆H₁₂O₆ + C₆H₁₂O₆ → C₁₂H₂₂O₁₁ + H₂O (given in the 2012 scheme).
- [ ] F2 [HL] Which monosaccharides give which disaccharide: glucose + glucose = maltose; glucose + fructose = sucrose; glucose + galactose = lactose.
- [ ] F3 [HL] The bond that joins the two units is a glycosidic link (the –O– bridge formed where the water is lost).
- [ ] F4 [HL] Polysaccharides form in the same way: many monosaccharide units (glucose) join in a long chain, with a molecule of water lost at each link. Chains may be straight or branched (2005 scheme).
- [ ] F5 [HL] Starch is a mixture of amylose (straight chains of glucose) and amylopectin (branched chains). Glycogen is a highly branched polymer of glucose, the form in which animals store carbohydrate, in the liver and muscles. Cellulose has straight, unbranched chains of glucose held side by side by hydrogen bonds, forming strong fibres in plant cell walls.
- [ ] F6 [HL] Reverse reaction: water added back splits the link (hydrolysis). One phrase, pointing to digestion and to the properties subsection.
- [ ] F7 [HL] Diagram (the Settings ask for labelled blocks): two glucose units drawn as blocks or rings, joined, with the water molecule removed and the resulting maltose; then a longer chain of blocks for a polysaccharide with a straight and a branched version.

Assumed knowledge (one block)
- [ ] A1 (assumed) Atoms and molecules: a compound is a substance made of two or more elements; a formula shows the atoms in a molecule. Gloss "polymer" (a long molecule built from many repeating units) when used.

## 3. Traps

- Do not use: any statement that pectin is built from glucose units, because pectin is made mainly of chains of galacturonic acid, not glucose; it is grouped with the polysaccharides and given the formula (C₆H₁₀O₅)ₙ by the marking schemes only as an exam convention. Say that pectin is a polysaccharide found in fruit and name no monomer. Starch, glycogen and cellulose are the three made of glucose.
- The three monosaccharides share one formula and the three disaccharides share one formula: they differ in the arrangement of atoms, not in the number of atoms. A common mistake is giving each sugar a different formula.
- The water lost in forming a disaccharide: C₆H₁₂O₆ + C₆H₁₂O₆ gives C₁₂H₂₂O₁₁, not C₁₂H₂₄O₁₂. Always show the water.
- Maltose is glucose + glucose (not glucose + fructose); sucrose is glucose + fructose (not glucose + galactose); lactose is glucose + galactose. Fructose is not in lactose or maltose.
- "Polysaccharide" is not "starch" only; cellulose, glycogen and pectin are polysaccharides too. Cellulose is a polysaccharide, so it is a carbohydrate even though people cannot digest it.
- The 2:1 hydrogen to oxygen ratio does not mean carbohydrates contain water.
- Do not list lactose, sucrose or maltose as monosaccharides, or glucose as a disaccharide.
- Hydrolysis and inversion belong to the properties subsection; condensation is the forward reaction taught here. Digestion of carbohydrate belongs to the properties subsection (enzymes) and the digestion and metabolism chapter.
- Do not mark the elements, the three classes or the formulas [HL]; only the structure of a monosaccharide and the formation of disaccharides and polysaccharides are [HL].
- Do not add the names of the specific glycosidic linkages (alpha 1,4 and so on) or the ring forms beyond what is listed; they are not needed.

## 4. What exams ask

- Elements: name the three elements in carbohydrates; a true or false statement that carbohydrates contain nitrogen (false). Ordinary Level, 6 marks.
- The table: classify carbohydrates into three classes, giving formula, example and food source for each. A very regular Higher Level Section A question and a Section B part. Full marks need the three class names (with "simple sugars", "double sugars"), C₆H₁₂O₆, C₁₂H₂₂O₁₁ and (C₆H₁₀O₅)ₙ, two examples of each class and a food source.
- Match examples to class at Ordinary Level (cellulose in vegetables, glucose in fruit, sucrose as table sugar).
- Higher Level accounts: the structure of a monosaccharide (formula C₆H₁₂O₆, ring, one sugar unit) and the formation of disaccharides (two monosaccharides join with the elimination of a water molecule, condensation, with the equation and the three glucose pairings). The older schemes also expect: polysaccharides are long straight or branched chains of monosaccharides.
- The structure of each of the three classes with one example of each (2005 style), with a labelled ring diagram.
- A few schemes mark the diagram of the glucose ring; the notes should say what to draw and label.
