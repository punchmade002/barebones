## 1. Must cover

Options when buying (one block)
- [ ] B1 Fresh, frozen, canned, dried and pre-prepared (convenience) produce are all available; also organic, Fairtrade, loose or pre-packed, in-season or imported/out-of-season, and exotic fruit. Each has a place in the diet.
- [ ] B2 Fresh: choose firm produce, no bruising, cuts, mould, wilting or discolouration; in-season produce is usually cheaper and has the best flavour; buy in usable quantities and shop often for the most perishable items.
- [ ] B3 Frozen: picked and frozen soon after harvest, so most nutrients are kept (see the Processing subsection for the effects); convenient, little waste, available all year; check the pack is solid, not damaged or frosted, and that it has been kept frozen.
- [ ] B4 Canned: cooked and sealed, long shelf life, ready to use; note the liquid: brine adds salt, syrup adds sugar; juice or water-packed options exist; check cans for dents, bulges and leaks.
- [ ] B5 Dried: most water removed, so light, long shelf life; dried fruit is concentrated in sugar and energy; pulses need soaking or cooking.
- [ ] B6 Pre-prepared (convenience) fruit and vegetables: washed, peeled, cut or ready to cook, such as bagged salad, stir-fry mix, fruit salad pots. Factors to check: use-by or best-before date, cost and value for money, packaging undamaged and unopened, no discolouration or sliminess, quantity suited to the household, storage and cooking instructions, nutritional information on the label. Advantages: saves time and skill, little waste. Disadvantages: dearer, shorter shelf life once prepared, some vitamin C lost through cutting.
- [ ] B7 Factors influencing the buyer (examined 2014O: cost, skills, time, storage space): buy in season and in bulk only if there is freezer or preserving capacity; loose is cheaper than packaged; buy direct from growers or markets; the household's skills and time decide how much is bought fresh and how much pre-prepared; storage space (fridge, freezer, cool dry store) limits the quantity.

Quality and grading (one block)
- [ ] Q1 Quality signs when selecting fresh: firmness, fresh colour, crispness, no bruising or blemish, no mould or sprouting, uniform size for even cooking, fresh-looking stalks and leaves, a pleasant smell, heavy for its size.
- [ ] Q2 EU grading: fresh fruit and vegetables sold in the EU must meet marketing standards; a product is graded Extra class (superior quality, free from defects in shape, size and colour), Class I (good quality, free from bruising, only slight defects) and Class II (reasonably good quality, slight defects of shape or colour allowed). Class II produce is still sound and good for cooking and processing.
- [ ] Q3 Packaging and labelling: fresh produce must show the country of origin and, where the standard requires it, the class and variety. Irish produce may carry the Bord Bia quality assurance mark, showing it has met an assured standard.

Effects of storage and storage guidelines (one block; examined as "describe three guidelines")
- [ ] S1 General effect: produce keeps living after harvest (respiring, losing moisture, ripening), so quality and vitamin C fall with time; buy little and often, use soon, rotate older stock to the front.
- [ ] S2 Fresh vegetables (salad and most): remove any plastic wrapping and damaged items, store in the refrigerator, in the salad or vegetable drawer, with air circulating; use quickly.
- [ ] S3 Potatoes: cool, dark, dry, ventilated place (a vegetable rack or paper sack); light turns them green and produces bitter solanine, which is harmful; discard green or sprouting parts or tubers.
- [ ] S4 Onions and root vegetables: cool, dry, dark, ventilated place; damp encourages rot; keep tops of roots trimmed.
- [ ] S5 Fruit: ripen at room temperature (bananas, tropical fruit, hard fruit) then refrigerate soft fruit and berries; apples and bananas give off ethylene gas, which speeds ripening of nearby produce, so keep them away from other fruit and vegetables. Wash berries only just before eating.
- [ ] S6 Frozen: keep at -18 degrees Celsius or lower; wrap in moisture-proof packaging; do not refreeze after thawing; use in date.
- [ ] S7 Canned and dried: store in a cool, dry place; once a can is opened, transfer contents to a covered container in the fridge and use within a day or two; discard dented, bulging or leaking cans. Dried fruit in a sealed container.
- [ ] S8 Keep fresh produce away from raw meat and fish to prevent cross-contamination; wash before use; clean the fridge regularly.

Spoilage by enzymes (one block)
- [ ] E1 Enzymes in the produce stay active after harvest and continue ripening until the produce becomes over-ripe, soft and finally decays.
- [ ] E2 When cells are cut, bruised or peeled, the enzyme oxidase is released and reacts with oxygen in the air: enzymic browning (apple, potato, banana). It also destroys some vitamin C and carotene.
- [ ] E3 Controls: cold slows enzymes; blanching (boiling water then cold) denatures them; acid such as lemon juice lowers pH and slows browning; air excluded by water, sugar syrup or wrapping; unblanched vegetables deteriorate even in the freezer.

Spoilage by moisture loss (one block)
- [ ] M1 Water is lost through respiration and evaporation, especially in warm, dry, uncovered storage; cells lose turgor, so leaves wilt, roots shrivel and skins wrinkle; produce loses weight and has poorer texture and appearance.
- [ ] M2 Controls: cool, humid storage (a salad drawer), suitable packaging, covering cut produce, not leaving produce in warm rooms.

Spoilage by micro-organisms (one block, Ordinary depth only)
- [ ] MO1 Bacteria, yeasts and moulds get onto produce from soil, water, dirty hands and equipment, pests, damaged skin and cross-contamination. Cuts and bruises give them access to nutrients and moisture.
- [ ] MO2 They multiply fast with warmth, moisture, food (the produce itself), time and a suitable pH. Bacteria cause soft, slimy rot and odours; moulds form furry coloured growth, and some produce mycotoxins that are harmful; yeasts ferment sugars in fruit, giving a sour or alcoholic smell and bubbles.
- [ ] MO3 Prevention: hygiene (clean hands, equipment and surfaces), removing damaged items, separating produce from raw meat, refrigeration, careful washing, prompt use, correct storage temperatures.
- [ ] MO4 Other causes of spoilage to mention in one line: physical damage (bruising, crushing in transport) and pests.

Rules for food businesses (one block, brief)
- [ ] L1 Businesses handling produce must follow the food hygiene rules in Regulation (EC) No 852/2004, given effect in Ireland by the European Communities (Hygiene of Foodstuffs) Regulations 2006; the Food Safety Authority of Ireland (FSAI), set up under the Food Safety Authority of Ireland Act 1998, enforces food law through service contracts and gives guidance. Marketing standards and grading are set at EU level.

## 3. Traps

- Grading: there are three classes, Extra, I and II.
- Do not use: "Class III" as a current EU grade, because the 2006 Higher scheme names it but the marketing standards no longer include it. If a student meets it in an old paper, they should use the three current classes.
- Do not use: "Regulation (EU) No 543/2011", because it was repealed and replaced by Delegated Regulation (EU) 2023/2429. If a regulation is named, name only the current one.
- Do not use: "Regulation (EU) No 1169/2011 requires the country of origin for fresh fruit and vegetables", because origin for fresh produce comes from the fruit and vegetable marketing standards; the Food Information to Consumers regulation is the one for processed food labels (Processing subsection).
- Do not use: "salad vegetables keep best in a sealed bag in the fridge" (the old note), because schemes 2021 and 2024 say to remove plastic wrapping and store in the vegetable drawer with air circulation; a sealed bag traps condensation and speeds rot.
- Do not use: "tropical fruit is refrigerated once ripe", because only bananas are credited in the schemes, and cold damages banana skins (they blacken); say bananas and tropical fruit ripen at room temperature and keep away from ethylene producers.
- Do not use: "fresh vegetables should be kept at about 5 degrees Celsius or below" as a rule for all produce, because some produce (potatoes, onions, bananas, tomatoes) is not kept in the fridge; give the temperature only for refrigerated salad and vegetables and the freezer.
- Do not use: "wash fruit and vegetables" as the only step against microbes, because soil-borne contamination persists; combine washing with hygiene and separation from raw meat.
- Do not use: "Bord Bia quality mark means the produce is Class I" or any claim that the mark and EU class are the same thing; the mark is a quality assurance scheme and class is the EU grade.
- Enzymic browning is the spoilage topic here; the chemistry of browning during cooking (and non-enzymic browning) belongs to the cooking-methods chapter. Home preservation (jam, chutney, bottling, home freezing and drying) belongs to the food-preservation chapter; here give only the control measures (blanching, cold, acid). The detail of micro-organisms (growth curve, conditions, food poisoning bacteria, HACCP) belongs to the food-safety chapter; this subsection gives one-line causes.
- Nutrient loss in cooking and the cooking-water guidelines are in the Preparation and cooking subsection; freezing, canning and drying methods and their effects are in the Processing subsection.

## 4. What exams ask

- "Discuss the options available to consumers when selecting and buying fruit and vegetables" (about 12 marks, Higher): fresh, frozen, canned, dried, pre-prepared; organic; fairtrade; in and out of season; loose or packaged; special offers; exotic.
- Four factors to be considered when buying pre-prepared fruit and vegetables (20 marks, Ordinary): date, cost and value, organic, usable quantity, damage or discolouration, package intact, nutrition information, storage, cooking time; each factor needs one extra point.
- Factors that influence a shopper buying fresh produce (cost, skills, time, storage space).
- Identify and explain two EU grading classes (4 marks, Higher).
- Describe three (or outline) guidelines for storing fruit and vegetables: remove wrapping and damaged items, refrigerate in the drawer with air, potatoes dark and dry, keep apples and bananas away from other produce, room temperature for some fruit.
- Discuss causes of spoilage in vegetables, with the three named headings: enzymes (ripening, over-ripening, browning, blanching), moisture loss (wilting, turgor, weight loss, humid storage), micro-organisms (sources, conditions, rot and mould, prevention). 18 marks, so each cause needs a mechanism, an effect and a control.
- Short items: enzymes cause spoilage of fruit and vegetables; in-season produce; a cut apple turning brown; controlling enzymic spoilage by blanching, cold or acid (explain two).
