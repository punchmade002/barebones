# Package: hom-consumer-rights-s1 — Consumer choices

- **loId:** `hom-consumer-rights-s1` · **chapterId:** `hom-consumer-rights` · **syllabusRef:** 2.2.1
- **Syllabus scope:** factors that affect consumer decision-making, with specific reference to varying household income, merchandising and advertising, packaging and labelling. Settings: merchandising techniques and shopping systems (advertising, packaging, mail order, "easy-pay" systems, television buying, shopper loyalty schemes, discount offers). Also examined: merchandising techniques (2025H, 2023H, 2016H, 2009H, 2005H); online shopping (2026O, 2021O, 2018O). No [HL] items in this subsection.
- **Prepared by:** a higher model, 2026-11-23, from the outline, the current notes and the past-paper marking schemes. Ordinary and Higher Level. The classification of outlets, retail psychology, shopping patterns and consumer research are Higher Level and belong to the purchasing process subsection; do not teach them here. The rights, the Consumer Rights Act 2022 and the Consumer Protection Act 2007 belong to the consumer protection legislation subsection.

## 1. Must cover

Factors that affect choice (one block)
- [ ] F1 Household income: income limits what can be bought; a tight budget leads to comparing unit prices, buying own-brand and in bulk, and delaying non-essential purchases.
- [ ] F2 Disposable income (the money left after tax and essential bills) decides how much is free for discretionary spending. Rising income: households trade up to brands and higher quality; falling income: they trade down to cheaper alternatives.
- [ ] F3 Needs and wants: essentials (food, heating, shelter) are bought first and luxuries are cut first. A needs-based decision is guided by necessity and income; a wants-based decision is more open to advertising and impulse buying.
- [ ] F4 Other personal factors named in the marking schemes: personal preference; values and lifestyle (health, convenience, ethics, culture and religion); household size and life-cycle stage; special diets (coeliac, diabetic, allergy); time available (pushes households to convenience foods and online shopping).
- [ ] F5 Influence of other people: word of mouth, sales staff, peers and current trends.
- [ ] F6 Product-related factors a buyer weighs for a large purchase (the washing-machine style question): energy rating, reliability of brand, ease of use and design, size or capacity for the family, purchase and running cost, quality marks, after-sales service and guarantee, and whether to pay cash or use credit.
- [ ] F7 Environmental impact of a product or its packaging is a modern factor (one bullet only; the detail belongs to the environmental issues chapter).
- [ ] F8 Impulse buying (an unplanned purchase triggered by a display, an offer or an item at the checkout) is the result of several of the factors above.

Brands (one block)
- [ ] B1 (assumed) A brand is a name, logo or design that identifies a manufacturer's product. Branded goods are advertised nationally and usually cost more; own-brand goods carry the retailer's name and are often cheaper because there is less advertising cost; brand loyalty is habitual repeat buying of one brand.

Advertising (one block)
- [ ] A1 Purposes: makes people aware of new products; builds brand loyalty; distinguishes a product by highlighting what makes it different (product differentiation); informs about features, price and offers.
- [ ] A2 Media used: television, radio, print, outdoor, internet and social media, sponsorship of sport and events. Celebrity endorsement transfers a famous person's image to the product.
- [ ] A3 Effect on buyers: can make people feel they need products they cannot afford; children and teenagers are especially influenced and prompt family purchases; can encourage impulse buying and the use of credit.
- [ ] A4 Advantage to the consumer: awareness of choice and information. Disadvantages: the cost is added to the price, claims can exaggerate, pressure to buy.

In-store merchandising (one block)
- [ ] M1 Merchandising: the techniques a retailer uses in the shop to present goods and encourage spending.
- [ ] M2 Store layout and aisle flow: goods in clearly identifiable departments, space to browse, a route that makes shoppers pass many products; essentials such as bread and milk placed at the back.
- [ ] M3 Product placement: complementary goods together (biscuits beside tea); impulse items at the checkout. Shelf position: premium goods at eye level; special offers on end-of-aisle displays.
- [ ] M4 In-store stimuli: background music, aromas such as baking, lighting, warmth and colour; samples and demonstrations.
- [ ] M5 Pricing and promotion: charm pricing (just below a round figure), multi-buy offers, reduction labels and clearance, loss leaders (a popular item sold at or below cost to bring shoppers in), coupons and money-off vouchers, discount offers.
- [ ] M6 The answer pattern: name the technique, say what the retailer does, say how it changes what the shopper buys.

Shopper loyalty schemes (one block)
- [ ] L1 How they work: points or tokens are earned on spending and exchanged for money off, vouchers or goods. Irish examples in the schemes: Tesco Clubcard, Dunnes Stores ValueClub card.
- [ ] L2 Why they encourage spending: a way of saving for an occasion such as Christmas; shoppers spend more to reach a reward; they return to the same shop; the retailer gains data on buying habits and can target offers.
- [ ] L3 Disadvantages: discourages shopping around, can lead to unplanned buying, ties the shopper to one shop.

Shopping systems (one block)
- [ ] S1 Easy-pay: paying for goods or bills in instalments over a set period. Hire purchase: the goods are used while instalments are paid; ownership passes only when the last instalment is paid. It is governed by the Consumer Credit Act 1995, which requires a written agreement (showing the cash price, the cost of credit and the APR) and gives a cooling-off period.
- [ ] S2 Credit advantages (use goods now, afford larger items, meet emergencies) and disadvantages (total cost is higher through interest and charges, encourages overspending, debt reduces future choice).
- [ ] S3 Mail order and television buying: goods are chosen from a catalogue, leaflet or screen and ordered by post, phone or online, then delivered. Advantages: convenient, wide range, no travel. Disadvantages: cannot see, touch or try on goods, delivery wait, postage cost. Distance purchases carry a statutory right to cancel.
- [ ] S4 Online shopping: advantages (any hour, price comparison, range, reviews, delivery to the door); disadvantages (cannot examine goods, delivery delay and charges, card fraud and phishing, impulse spending through stored card details, data collected to target advertising). Safe practice: pay only on secure sites (padlock symbol, https address) and keep records of the order.
- [ ] S5 Discount offers and discount stores lower the cost, but the consumer should still compare total value.

Packaging and labelling (one block)
- [ ] P1 (assumed) Functions of packaging: protects and preserves, informs, attracts attention on the shelf (colour, shape, design), gives convenience (portion size, easy open), suggests quality.
- [ ] P2 Mandatory food label content: name of the food; list of ingredients in descending order of weight; allergens emphasised (14 major allergens); net quantity; a date (use-by or best before); storage conditions and instructions for use; name and address of the food business; country of origin where required; nutrition declaration.
- [ ] P3 Use-by (highly perishable foods; unsafe after it) against best before (quality; the food may still be safe after it).
- [ ] P4 Nutrition declaration: energy and the amounts of fat, saturates, carbohydrate, sugars, protein and salt, per 100 g (or 100 ml), and optionally per portion.
- [ ] P5 Claims such as "low fat" or "high fibre" may be used only where the food meets defined criteria. Quality marks (the Bord Bia Quality Mark) and symbols (the gluten-free symbol, allergen emphasis) help consumers choose.
- [ ] P6 Benefits of labelling to the consumer (three-benefit exam answer): informed choice, safety (allergens, dates), value comparison (net quantity, nutrition), correct storage and use.
- [ ] P7 Legal basis: Regulation (EU) No 1169/2011, given effect by the European Union (Provision of Food Information to Consumers) Regulations 2014.
- [ ] P8 Labels on non-food goods: care labels, safety and hazard symbols (for example on household cleaning agents) prevent accidents and explain use.

## 3. Traps

- Merchandising is what the retailer does inside the shop; advertising is communication, mostly outside it. Keep the two blocks apart even though both influence choice.
- Retail psychology, outlet classes, changing shopping patterns and consumer research belong to the purchasing process subsection (Higher Level); the notes here describe techniques, not the research behind them.
- The statutory rights, remedies and the Acts that give them belong to the consumer protection legislation subsection; mention only the credit and cancellation points in S1 and S3.
- The Consumer Credit Act 1995 is named here only for hire purchase and credit agreements; do not add its other provisions.
- Food label detail (allergens, dates) is repeated in the food safety chapter; keep it short and consumer-focused here.
- Do not use: "twelve months" as the length of an easy-pay scheme, because no source fixes a period; say "a set period".
- Checked: the right to cancel distance and off-premises contracts within a cooling-off period is in Part 5 of the Consumer Rights Act 2022. Give no day count (periods differ by contract type).
- Do not use: the 28-day period for mail-order cancellation (2007 marking scheme), because it is superseded.
- Do not use: "SuperValu card" as the current SuperValu scheme, because it is now branded Real Rewards (the older name appears only in a marking scheme).
- Do not use: Superquinn as a current loyalty scheme (2011 scheme), because the brand no longer trades.
- Do not use: "fresh produce at the entrance makes shoppers take a trolley", because it is a retail-trade claim that no marking scheme gives; use only techniques the schemes name.
- Do not use: GDA as the label term (2008 scheme), because current labels use reference intakes; give the nutrients, not the acronym.
- Do not use: "per 100 g or per portion" as if both were required, because only the per-100 g (or 100 ml) figure is mandatory.

## 4. What exams ask

- Name and describe two to four merchandising techniques (layout, placement, shelf position, in-store stimuli, offers, loyalty) and how each changes spending. Asked at 6 marks and as a 20-mark analysis. Each technique needs a description of what the retailer does and the effect on what the shopper buys.
- Factors that influence choice, usually "name or discuss three or four": income, advertising, packaging and labelling, word of mouth, sales staff, peers and trends, environmental concern, after-sales service. Each needs a named factor plus its effect on the decision.
- A loyalty scheme: name one, say how it works, give an advantage and a disadvantage; explain why schemes encourage buying (saving for Christmas, points, return visits, data).
- Advertising effect on buying behaviour: awareness, loyalty, differentiation, pressure on children and teenagers, impulse buying, buying beyond means.
- Advantages and disadvantages of one shopping system (mail order, television or online), five or six points in all.
- Online shopping: convenience and price comparison against not seeing goods, fraud and delivery problems.
- Benefits of food labelling and what a label must show; the meaning of a symbol (for example gluten-free); items of nutritional information.
- Credit: advantages of credit buying, and what the Consumer Credit Act 1995 requires (written agreement, APR in advertising, cooling-off period).
- Planning a large purchase (a washing machine): energy rating, brand reliability, guarantee, capacity, cost, cash or credit.
