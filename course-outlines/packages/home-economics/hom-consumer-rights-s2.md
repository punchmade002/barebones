# Package: hom-consumer-rights-s2 — The purchasing process

- **loId:** `hom-consumer-rights-s2` · **chapterId:** `hom-consumer-rights` · **syllabusRef:** 2.2.1
- **Syllabus scope:** [HL] the purchasing process: classification of retail outlets, retail psychology, shopping patterns, consumer research. Also examined: desk and field research (2025H, 2021H, 2012H); changes in shopping patterns (2026H, 2024H, 2016H); classes of retail outlet (2021H). The whole subsection is Higher Level only.
- **Prepared by:** a higher model, 2026-11-23. Everything here is [HL]. Merchandising techniques, advertising, loyalty cards and labelling are taught in the consumer choices subsection; this subsection explains the shopper's behaviour, the outlet types, the trends and the research methods. Consumer research is examined at 6 marks (describe desk and field research, state its purpose) and as part of a 19-mark question; outlet classes at 6 marks and as an advantage/disadvantage part of a longer question.

## 1. Must cover

Classes of retail outlet [HL]
- [ ] R1 [HL] Independent shop: single, locally owned, personal service, specialised range (butcher, jeweller, boutique).
- [ ] R2 [HL] Chain or multiple store: several outlets under one owner, standard range, buying in bulk (Penneys, Dunnes Stores).
- [ ] R3 [HL] Supermarket: self-service, wide range of food and household goods (Tesco, SuperValu, Centra).
- [ ] R4 [HL] Department store: large store divided into departments (Brown Thomas, Arnotts).
- [ ] R5 [HL] Discount store: limited range, low prices kept low by minimal service and fast stock turnover (Dealz, Aldi).
- [ ] R6 [HL] Mail-order and online retailer: sells from a catalogue or website and delivers to the home (ASOS). Markets and farmers' markets: producers selling direct, mainly fresh produce.
- [ ] R7 [HL] One advantage and one disadvantage per outlet type, for example independent: personal service, but higher prices and a smaller range; supermarket: wide range and one-stop shopping, but impersonal; discount: low price, but limited choice; department store: range and quality, but higher prices; online: convenience and price comparison, but goods cannot be examined and delivery takes time.
- [ ] R8 [HL] Choosing an outlet for an item such as an appliance: price, range, advice and demonstration, after-sales service, delivery, guarantee and returns policy.

Retail psychology [HL]
- [ ] P1 [HL] Impulse buying and how displays, offers and checkout items trigger it.
- [ ] P2 [HL] Shoppers rely on quick cues (brand, colour, packaging, price) rather than reading everything; a known brand lowers the risk felt when buying an unfamiliar product.
- [ ] P3 [HL] Perceived value: a "was/now" price or a multi-buy makes a price seem a bargain.
- [ ] P4 [HL] Mood and environment: music, aroma, lighting and layout keep shoppers relaxed and browsing longer, so they see and buy more.
- [ ] P5 [HL] Urgency and social cues: "limited offer" labels, "best seller" displays and peer approval.
- [ ] P6 [HL] Needs against wants: a shopping list and a budget reduce impulse spending (consumer's response).

Shopping patterns [HL]
- [ ] T1 [HL] Frequency: from one weekly bulk shop to more frequent, smaller top-up shops.
- [ ] T2 [HL] Place: large out-of-town centres and retail parks (one-stop shopping) as well as local shops.
- [ ] T3 [HL] Hours: late-night, Sunday and 24-hour opening.
- [ ] T4 [HL] Channels: online, television and social media shopping with home delivery.
- [ ] T5 [HL] Payment methods: cash, debit card, contactless and mobile payment, credit card; each evaluated for convenience, security, cost and record keeping.
- [ ] T6 [HL] Technology in store: self-checkout, scan-as-you-go, loyalty apps.
- [ ] T7 [HL] Resale: charity shops, vintage stores and online resale platforms.
- [ ] T8 [HL] Causes of change: time pressure, working patterns, household structure, technology, budget, health and special diets, ethical and environmental concern.

Consumer research [HL]
- [ ] C1 [HL] Purpose: identify consumer wants and expectations; test the market before launching or developing a product; highlight trends, market size and potential markets.
- [ ] C2 [HL] Desk research (secondary): uses information that already exists, such as state agency reports, Central Statistics Office data and internet sources. Quick and cheap, covers a lot of information; may be general or out of date.
- [ ] C3 [HL] Field research (primary): new information collected directly from consumers by questionnaires, surveys, interviews, observation, vox pops, consumer panels and focus groups; application forms and loyalty card data are also named. Specific and up to date; takes longer and costs more.
- [ ] C4 [HL] Definitions: vox pop (short informal street interview), consumer panel (fixed group reporting over time), focus group (guided discussion with a small group).
- [ ] C5 [HL] Who benefits: the retailer (who the buyer is, whether the product meets requirements, size and location of the market); the manufacturer (what will sell before production is committed); the consumer (products improved in quality and matched to needs).

## 3. Traps

- Desk research is not just "the library": it is any existing (secondary) data, including internet and CSO figures. Field research is primary data collected for the question in hand.
- Merchandising techniques as such (layout, shelf position, loyalty schemes) are in the consumer choices subsection; here they appear only as causes of behaviour.
- Rights, legislation and complaints belong to other subsections.
- A classification question wants the class name plus one example per class; do not give two outlets of the same class. Aldi fits both a chain and a discount store; say which class you mean.
- Do not use: "desk research is quantitative and field research is qualitative", because a field survey also produces numbers and desk reports can be descriptive; separate them by who collects the data (existing against new).
- Do not use: Argos as a discount store, because a 2021 scheme names it but its Irish trading status is unchecked; use Dealz or Aldi.
- Do not use: Oxendales or iClothing as mail-order examples, because they are named in a 2021 scheme but are not checked as trading; use ASOS.
- Do not use: "competition between retailers informed by research keeps prices down" as a consumer benefit, because no scheme gives it; give the benefit as products improved in quality and matched to need.

## 4. What exams ask

- Classify three retail outlets and give an example of each (6 marks): class name plus a named store.
- Name two outlets for buying an appliance and give one advantage and one disadvantage of each (12-mark part).
- Describe desk research and field research (6 marks); differentiate the two; state the purpose of consumer research; name three research methods and give a benefit to the retailer and one to the consumer (19-mark part).
- Analyse changes in shopping patterns over a decade (20 marks): at least five developed points, for example payment methods, apps and loyalty, self-checkout, frequency, large centres, opening hours, online and social shopping, resale.
- Name and evaluate two in-store payment methods, giving an advantage and a disadvantage of each.

## Answer patterns

- Outlet question: class, example, then one advantage and one disadvantage, each as a separate point (the 2004 scheme gave 2 marks for the name, 2 for the advantage and 2 for the disadvantage).
- Shopping patterns: five developed points, each saying what changed and why shoppers or retailers changed (for example "self-checkout and scan-as-you-go let shoppers avoid queues and help retailers cut staff costs").
- Research: always say who collects the data (existing sources or the researcher), give two examples of each, and one benefit and one drawback.
- Payment method evaluation: name the method, give a feature that helps the consumer, and one weakness (security, interest, reliance on funds or technology).
- Retail psychology answers should link a shopper's reaction to a retailer's action: "a was/now price makes the reduction look larger, so shoppers buy now".
