# Package: hom-food-preservation-s6 — Fermentation

- **loId:** `hom-food-preservation-s6` · **chapterId:** `hom-food-preservation` · **syllabusRef:** 1.3.9
- **Syllabus scope:** [HL] Fermentation. Also examined: fermentation and its by-products (2017H, 2009H); uses of micro-organisms in food production appear on the food-safety side (2005H, 2008H, 2010O). The whole subsection is Higher Level only.
- **Prepared by:** a higher model, 2026-11-23. The syllabus names fermentation without detail, so the scope is set by the two past questions (define or explain fermentation, with the alcoholic equation, and name two by-products) plus the natural contexts in which fermentation appears in the course: bread, beer and wine, yoghurt and cheese, vinegar, and novel foods such as mycoprotein, miso and tempeh. The detailed biology of yeasts and moulds (structure, reproduction) is in the food safety chapter; the "uses of micro-organisms in food production" outline is a separate subsection there and the dairy products and bread are in their own chapters. Here the fermentation process and its by-products are explained once, with the foods as examples.

## 1. Must cover (Higher Level only)

What fermentation is
- [ ] F1 [HL] Fermentation is the breakdown of organic substances (mainly sugars) by micro-organisms (yeasts, bacteria, some moulds) into simpler substances, usually without oxygen, releasing a small amount of energy. The 2017H and 2009H schemes: breakdown by micro-organisms (bacteria and yeast) producing carbon dioxide, alcohol, acids and energy.
- [ ] F2 [HL] Alcoholic fermentation by yeast (Saccharomyces cerevisiae): glucose is converted to ethanol (alcohol) and carbon dioxide with a release of energy: C₆H₁₂O₆ → 2C₂H₅OH + 2CO₂ (+ energy). The 2017H scheme shows yeast + glucose + moisture + warmth giving 2CO₂ + 2C₂H₅OH + energy.
- [ ] F3 [HL] Lactic acid fermentation by lactic acid bacteria (Lactobacillus, Streptococcus): sugars (lactose in milk; sugars in cabbage) are converted to lactic acid.
- [ ] F4 [HL] Acetic acid fermentation by acetic acid bacteria (Acetobacter): ethanol is oxidised to acetic acid (vinegar); this stage needs oxygen.
- [ ] F5 [HL] Conditions: a fermentable sugar (substrate), moisture, warmth (the optimum temperature of the organism; too hot kills yeast), time, and often a starter culture; the absence of oxygen for alcoholic and lactic fermentations.

By-products
- [ ] B1 [HL] Carbon dioxide: makes bread dough rise, gives sparkling quality to beer and sparkling wine.
- [ ] B2 [HL] Alcohol (ethanol): in beer, wine and spirits; evaporates during baking, so little remains in bread.
- [ ] B3 [HL] Acids: lactic acid (yoghurt, sauerkraut, cheese), acetic acid (vinegar); acids give sour flavour, lower pH and so preserve.
- [ ] B4 [HL] Energy (released as heat); flavour compounds that give the characteristic flavour of cheese, bread and fermented drinks. The 2009H scheme lists carbon dioxide, energy, acids/vinegar and alcohol as by-products.

Foods made by fermentation (examples; each in one or two lines)
- [ ] X1 [HL] Bread: yeast ferments the sugars in the dough, the carbon dioxide is trapped in the gluten network and raises the dough; baking kills the yeast and drives off the alcohol.
- [ ] X2 [HL] Beer: yeast ferments sugars (maltose) from malted barley, with hops added for flavour; wine: yeast ferments the sugar in grape juice; the alcohol reaches a level at which the yeast stops. (Drink-making in outline only.)
- [ ] X3 [HL] Yoghurt: starter cultures of Lactobacillus bulgaricus (now named Lactobacillus delbrueckii subsp. bulgaricus) and Streptococcus thermophilus convert lactose to lactic acid, which lowers the pH, coagulates the milk protein (casein) and gives the thick, sour product. Soya yoghurt is fermented soya milk.
- [ ] X4 [HL] Cheese: starter bacteria produce lactic acid; rennet (an enzyme) coagulates the milk into curds and whey; ripening with bacteria and moulds develops flavour and texture; Penicillium roqueforti gives blue veins in blue cheese and Penicillium camemberti the white rind of Camembert (the 2005H scheme: moulds in blue-veined cheeses, starter culture in cheese making).
- [ ] X5 [HL] Vinegar: yeast ferments sugars to alcohol, then acetic acid bacteria with air convert the alcohol to acetic acid.
- [ ] X6 [HL] Sauerkraut (and kimchi): shredded cabbage with salt; lactic acid bacteria already on the cabbage produce lactic acid; the salt draws out juice and favours the lactic bacteria.
- [ ] X7 [HL] Soya-based fermented foods named in the 2018H scheme: tempeh (cooked fermented soya beans), miso (paste of soya beans with rice, salt and water, fermented), soya yoghurt.
- [ ] X8 [HL] Mycoprotein (Quorn): the fungus Fusarium venenatum grown in large fermenters on a sugar-based medium, harvested and processed into meat-substitute products (2018H scheme: made from mycoprotein (fungi)).

Fermentation as preservation and control
- [ ] P1 [HL] Fermentation preserves because the acid, alcohol and carbon dioxide produced, together with a controlled population of harmless organisms, make the food unsuitable for spoilage organisms; milk to yoghurt and cheese; cabbage to sauerkraut; grape juice to wine; wine to vinegar. Fermented foods are not sterile, and some need chilling.
- [ ] P2 [HL] Control: a pure starter culture of known strains in a clean, sterilised setting at the optimum temperature and for the right time; unwanted organisms are kept out; fermentation is stopped (by chilling, pasteurising or removing the yeast) at the right point.
- [ ] P3 [HL] Fermentation versus spoilage: the same organisms can spoil food (a yeast fermenting jam or fruit juice, a mould spoiling cheese); in food production fermentation is controlled and wanted.

## 3. Traps

- Fermentation does not need oxygen (alcoholic and lactic); the acetic acid stage does. Do not say all fermentation is anaerobic.
- Yeast is a fungus, not a bacterium; yoghurt is made by bacteria; blue cheese by a mould. Keep the organism with the product.
- The alcohol and carbon dioxide are produced from sugar; yeast does not make alcohol from starch directly (bread dough gets sugar from flour, and barley is malted first).
- Fermentation is not the same as respiration in the body or in biology notes; the food-chapter equation is only the one for yeast.
- Cheese and yoghurt manufacture in detail (pasteurising, culture addition, pressing, ripening) belong to the dairy chapter; give the fermentation step only. Bread making belongs to the cereals chapter.
- Moulds, yeasts and bacteria: structure, reproduction and conditions for growth belong to the food safety chapter (Higher Level); the uses of micro-organisms in production is a one-line outline subsection there.
- Sauerkraut also uses salt (chemical preservation); do not teach brining here.
- Alcohol as a chemical preservative (fruit in brandy) belongs to chemical preservation; do not confuse it with fermentation.
- Do not use: named strains beyond those in the Checked table (for example specific cheese-starter species), because none is confirmed here; say "starter bacteria".
- Do not use: "wine fermentation stops when the alcohol level becomes toxic to the yeast" with any percentage, because no figure is checked; the statement without a figure is acceptable.
- Do not use: Lactobacillus bulgaricus as the only current name; mention it as the name used in exams, alongside the newer name Lactobacillus delbrueckii subsp. bulgaricus.

## 4. What exams ask

- Define or explain fermentation and name two by-products (2009H and 2017H, 6 marks each): breakdown of organic substances by bacteria or yeast; products carbon dioxide, alcohol, acids or vinegar, energy; the 2017H answer also gives the equation (yeast + glucose + moisture + warmth → carbon dioxide + alcohol + energy).
- Uses of micro-organisms in food production (2005H, 2008H, 2010O, 2014H, 2015H, 2022H): starter cultures for cheese and yoghurt, moulds in blue cheese, yeast in bread, beer and wine, mycoprotein; bacteria in yoghurt.
- Novel and vegetarian foods (2018H): tempeh, miso, soya yoghurt and Quorn as fermented products.
- Possible role in preservation: lactic acid and acetic acid lower the pH and inhibit spoilage organisms.
- Tip content: always give the organism, the food and the by-product, and say which by-product does what (gas raises dough; acid thickens milk and preserves; alcohol is the drink).
