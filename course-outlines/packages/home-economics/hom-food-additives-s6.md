## 1. Must cover

Critical evaluation of dishes and meals (one block)
- [ ] E1 Evaluation judges a dish or meal against stated criteria, using evidence. Criteria: nutritional value (energy, fibre, fat, sugar, salt; link to the Department of Health Food Pyramid and healthy-eating guidelines), cost per portion, sensory qualities (appearance, aroma, flavour, texture), time and skill, suitability for the household or special diets, safety and hygiene, packaging and waste.
- [ ] E2 Method: state the criterion; give evidence from the recipe, the label or a tasting; make a judgement and give the reason; suggest an improvement. Reasons for modifying a recipe (2013H): more nutritious, to suit special diets such as coeliac, to change quantity, to save cost.
- [ ] E3 Setting: evaluate a family meal against the Food Pyramid and suggest changes in portions or ingredients.

Home-made and commercial products compared (one block)
- [ ] H1 Cost: home-made from basic ingredients is often cheaper per portion; commercial products carry labour, packaging and distribution costs.
- [ ] H2 Nutrition: the home cook controls salt, sugar, fat and fibre; the commercial label's nutrition declaration allows comparison.
- [ ] H3 Additives: home-made food usually has none; many commercial products have preservatives, colourings, emulsifiers.
- [ ] H4 Time and skill: commercial products save time and need little skill; home-made takes preparation, cooking and cleaning time.
- [ ] H5 Sensory quality and shelf life: home-made may taste fresher, commercial gives consistent taste and texture; commercial is processed to last, home-made needs refrigeration and is eaten soon.
- [ ] H6 Packaging and waste: a pre-packed commercial product creates more packaging than loose ingredients.
- [ ] H7 The method to use: compare the same product on a set of criteria in a table, then reach a judgement.

The senses and food choice (one block)
- [ ] S1 Sight: colour, size, shape, gloss and presentation are used to judge freshness and acceptability; expectations matter (mould is acceptable on Stilton cheese but not on bread; lemon is associated with yellow, mint with green).
- [ ] S2 Smell: aroma and odour show freshness or rancidity; a good smell makes food appetising.
- [ ] S3 Taste: taste buds detect sweet, salt, sour and bitter; umami (savoury, glutamate) is the fifth taste.
- [ ] S4 Touch and mouthfeel: nerves in the mouth detect texture (crisp, creamy, gritty), temperature (ice cream) and chemical heat (chilli).
- [ ] S5 Hearing: sizzling sausages, crunchy vegetables, fizzy drinks influence choice and eating.
- [ ] S6 Cooking changes the senses: colour changes, flavours intensify, aromas develop, textures change. Reference frame: choosing, cooking and eating.

Uses of sensory analysis in the food industry [HL] (one block)
- [ ] U1 Definition: the scientific evaluation of food using the senses, under controlled conditions.
- [ ] U2 Product development: find which version consumers prefer, gauge response to a new product; develop an own-label food to taste like the brand leader.
- [ ] U3 Modifying an existing product: e.g. healthier recipe, lower salt, different ingredient or supplier; difference tests confirm consumers cannot perceive a change.
- [ ] U4 Matching an existing product: copying a well-known brand.
- [ ] U5 Evaluation and quality control: check that the product meets its specification (e.g. shortness of a biscuit); compare own-brand with the leading brand; support marketing claims.

The three categories of sensory test [HL] (one block)
- [ ] T1 Preference tests: show whether people like or dislike a product, or prefer one to another. Tests: paired preference; hedonic rating scale (rate liking on a numbered scale from dislike extremely to like extremely); food action rating scale (how likely to buy or eat); preference ranking.
- [ ] T2 Difference tests: show whether a difference exists, and whether people would notice it. Tests: simple difference paired comparison (two coded samples, same or different?); directional paired comparison (which of two has more of a stated characteristic, e.g. sweetness); triangle test; duo-trio test.
- [ ] T3 Descriptive tests: describe the perceived sensory characteristics of a product (e.g. effect of processing, packaging or storage on quality). Tests: descriptive ranking; descriptive rating.
- [ ] T4 Duo-trio: a reference sample plus two coded samples; the tester identifies which coded sample matches the reference (so which differs); chance is one in two.

One difference test in full: the triangle test [HL] (one block, process; "Investigation" block type)
- [ ] G1 Aim: to find whether testers can detect a difference between two products (e.g. an established product and a lower-salt version), by identifying the odd sample among three.
- [ ] G2 Variables: samples are two identical and one different; temperature, amount, shape, colour, container and conditions are kept the same.
- [ ] G3 Method (implementation): set up trays with three coded samples (random three-digit codes); balanced or randomised serving order (six possible orders); testers taste in the order given, with water between samples, no discussion; each identifies the odd sample; responses recorded; codes revealed; correct answers counted.
- [ ] G4 Interpretation: by chance one in three would be right, so correct answers are compared with chance (a significance table); if enough testers are correct, the difference is detectable; a preference test would then show whether they like the change.
- [ ] G5 Diagram cue: a tray with three identical coded containers; the odd one marked only on the organiser's record.

Conditions for accurate sensory results [HL] (one block)
- [ ] K1 Place: quiet, clean, odour-free room with good ventilation; booths or space between testers; bright, even (preferably natural) light; silence so information is not shared.
- [ ] K2 Timing: mid-morning or mid-afternoon (taste sensitivity best); no strongly flavoured food or drink, smoking or gum for at least 30 minutes before; testers not ill; organisers do not take part as testers.
- [ ] K3 Samples: same temperature, uniform shape, colour and amount, identical containers; coded so codes do not bias; number of samples limited; order planned and balanced; tray set-up planned.
- [ ] K4 Palate cleansing: water, and a plain cracker where suitable.
- [ ] K5 Hygiene and special dietary needs (e.g. coeliac) catered for; clear identical instructions; one characteristic judged at a time.

## 3. Traps

- Do not use: a required number of testers, a significance value or a quoted p-value for the triangle test, because the schemes give none and figures vary with the test design; say "enough testers" and "compared with chance".
- Do not use: "the duo-trio tester identifies the sample that differs from the reference" without saying that this equals picking the sample that matches the reference; the 2017 scheme words it as "different from the control", and the usual wording is "matches the reference". Use "identifies which coded sample is the same as the reference, so the other is different" so both are covered.
- Do not use: "home-made food is cheaper" as a flat claim, because it depends on the dish and quantities; keep "often cheaper per portion".
- Preference test (likes) is not a difference test (can they tell). A triangle test says nothing about which they like.
- Hedonic scale measures liking; food action rating scale measures willingness to eat or buy; descriptive tests describe intensity.
- Taste is only sweet, salt, sour, bitter and umami; flavour includes aroma and mouthfeel. Chilli "heat" is a chemical stimulation of nerves, not a taste.
- Sensory analysis (controlled, with coding and panels) is not the same as a family saying whether they liked dinner; the evaluation blocks use criteria, the sensory blocks use controlled tests.
- Neighbours: the nutrient and energy content of a meal belongs to the nutrition chapters, meal planning to the meal-planning chapter; additives and labels to earlier subsections of this chapter; the practical-coursework evaluation record belongs to the cooking-methods chapter.
- Mark HL only on the sensory-analysis blocks; the senses block and the evaluation blocks are for all candidates (the senses and food choice are examined without an HL mark).

## 4. What exams ask

- Name three categories of sensory test with one test from each (2005H, 2007H, 2013H) and state the purpose of each category: preference (liked or preferred), difference (does a difference exist or would people notice), descriptive (describe characteristics).
- State when sensory analysis is used in the food industry (2013H, 2009H; 6–13 marks): product development, modifying an existing product, matching a brand, evaluation and quality control, with an example each.
- Write a detailed account of one difference test: name, aim, implementation (2017H and 2026H, 18 marks; 3 marks name, 3 marks aim, 6 points at 2 marks for implementation). Triangle is the common choice; the 2026 answer adds coded, balanced serving order, rinsing, recording, comparison with chance and the conclusion.
- Outline conditions needed for accurate results (2007H 20 marks; 2015H, 2017H, 2024H; 2026H 12 marks): at least four or five conditions, each with its reason.
- Name one preference test used to find a preferred product (2023H, 6 marks) with two uses of sensory analysis: paired preference, hedonic rating or preference ranking.
- Discuss the influence of the senses when choosing, cooking and eating food (2017H, 16 marks; 2007H, 15 marks; 2013H aesthetic awareness): at least three or four developed points across sight, smell, taste, mouthfeel and sound, with an example for each.
- Compare home-made and commercial dishes or evaluate a dish (practical coursework link): criteria-based answers with a judgement.
