# Package: hom-food-industry-s2 — Small businesses and home enterprises

- **loId:** `hom-food-industry-s2` · **chapterId:** `hom-food-industry` · **syllabusRef:** 1.3.1
- **Syllabus scope:** "their role within the food industry." Also examined: artisan producers
  and small businesses (2024H, 2017H, 2011H, 2009H); speciality foods (2022H, 2012H); the growth
  of small Irish producers (2021O, 2012O). No [HL] items. Common to Ordinary and Higher Level.
- **Prepared by:** a higher model, 2026-11-23. This is a high-yield 12-20 mark topic that has
  been asked in almost the same form many times, so the role and the reasons for growing
  popularity must be listed completely and kept separate. Food safety rules for small firms
  (registration, HACCP, labelling) are included only in outline, because the detail is in the
  food safety chapter.

## 1. Must cover

What these businesses are (one block)
- [ ] D1 Small business: produces in small quantities, often family-run and rural, selling
  locally or to a niche market.
- [ ] D2 Home enterprise (cottage industry): a business run from the home or a small unit near
  it, often started in a domestic kitchen, e.g. jams, chutneys, baking, chocolate, honey.
- [ ] D3 Artisan producer: makes food in limited quantities using traditional, non-industrial
  skills and hand methods.
- [ ] D4 Speciality foods: foods produced in small quantities by small businesses or home
  enterprises, often with local ingredients, little or no additives, a traditional or natural
  image, sold at farmers' markets, local shops, restaurants and online. Examples accepted:
  cheese, chutney, jam, honey, bread, home baking, smoked fish, chocolate, yoghurt, ice cream.
- [ ] D5 Speciality foods also include foods made for special diets (coeliac/gluten-free, vegan,
  diabetic) and organic foods.
- [ ] D6 Examples of artisan cheese: farmhouse cheeses such as Cashel Blue and Gubbeen.

Role within the food industry (one block; 6 points, each with a reason)
- [ ] R1 Creates employment, often in rural areas, with a skilled workforce (staff often learn
  every stage of production).
- [ ] R2 Diversifies the industry and changing markets: makes products large manufacturers do
  not; new exclusive ranges.
- [ ] R3 Identifies and enters old and new niche markets, meeting consumer demand (local, natural,
  traditional, special-diet foods).
- [ ] R4 Uses locally produced ingredients, supporting local farmers and suppliers and reducing
  food miles.
- [ ] R5 Promotes quality ingredients and innovation, with very high standards of hygiene.
- [ ] R6 Strengthens Ireland's reputation for high-quality food and drink, promotes the region
  it is based in, and supports rural communities and farm diversification.

Why they are growing in popularity (one block)
- [ ] G1 Consumers want greater product variety and flavour than mass-produced food offers.
- [ ] G2 Little or no additives; foods seen as natural and less processed or healthier.
- [ ] G3 Hand-made with care, traditional skills; a quality, premium image.
- [ ] G4 Interest in provenance and traceability: knowing the farm or maker, and Irish origin.
- [ ] G5 Desire to support local producers, jobs and sustainability (fewer food miles, organic or
  sustainable methods), including support for local goods in recession.
- [ ] G6 More routes to market: farmers' markets, food festivals and food tourism, specialist
  shops, restaurants, supermarkets that stock local lines, online selling, media coverage and
  food awards.
- [ ] G7 Growing demand for special-diet foods (coeliac, vegan, diabetic) and for food made in
  line with changing food culture (travel, multicultural eating).

Supports and growth (one block)
- [ ] U1 Local Enterprise Offices (LEOs): a network of offices run through the local authorities
  (31 teams) that give start-up and development supports to small businesses: training,
  mentoring, advice and networking. Food supports include the Food Starter Programme (idea stage)
  and the Food Academy (training and mentoring for new food companies, run with Bord Bia).
- [ ] U2 Bord Bia and Enterprise Ireland: market development and export advice (see the previous
  subsection).
- [ ] U3 Typical growth path: start at a farmers' market or from the kitchen, move to local
  shops, then supermarkets or export, and from the home kitchen to a registered unit as demand
  grows.

Food safety and rules in outline (one block)
- [ ] F1 A new food business, including one run from home, must register with the competent
  authority before it starts trading. For most (e.g. bakers, caterers, jam and chutney makers) this
  is the HSE Environmental Health Service. The Department of Agriculture, Food and the Marine
  registers premises handling foods of animal origin such as meat, dairy and eggs (seafood premises are registered with the Sea-Fisheries Protection Authority). (Assumed: the
  authority inspects the premises; the kitchen must be suitable.)
- [ ] F2 EU food hygiene law (Regulation (EC) No 852/2004, given effect in Ireland by the
  European Communities (Hygiene of Foodstuffs) Regulations 2006) requires every food business to
  operate a food safety management system based on HACCP principles. For a small producer this
  can be simple and proportionate to the business.
- [ ] F3 Labelling: prepackaged food must carry the information required by Regulation (EU) No
  1169/2011, given effect by the European Union (Provision of Food Information to Consumers)
  Regulations 2014: name of the food, ingredients with allergens emphasised, the business's name
  and address, date marking and storage instructions. Small firms must follow the same rules.
- [ ] F4 Traceability: producers must know their suppliers and customers (one step back, one step
  forward; Regulation (EC) No 178/2002).
- [ ] F5 The Food Safety Authority of Ireland (FSAI) provides guidance for small producers
  (including a guide to food law for artisan and small food producers).

## 3. Traps

- The ROLE of small producers (what they contribute to the industry: employment, diversification,
  niche markets, local ingredients, quality, reputation) is not the same as the REASONS FOR THEIR
  GROWING POPULARITY (why consumers buy them: variety, no additives, hand-made, provenance). The
  two have different marking points. A student must read which is asked. Keep two blocks.
- "Artisan" and "speciality" overlap but are not identical: artisan describes how it is made;
  speciality describes the product and market. Define by features; do not claim either is legally
  protected.
- Speciality foods are not automatically healthier or organic; say "perceived as" for health.
- The full legislation and national agencies (labelling rules in detail, official controls,
  HACCP principles) belong to the food safety chapter; give only the outline in F1-F5 here.
- Textile and fashion small businesses are a separate elective topic; do not mix them in.
- Careers belong to the careers subsection.
- Do not use: "the FSAI issues licences to food businesses" (a marking scheme says so),
  because food businesses are registered or approved by the competent authority (HSE, DAFM or a
  local authority), not licensed by FSAI.
- Do not use: "small producers have no exemption" without qualification, because the HACCP
  system may be simple and proportionate to the size and nature of the business; the legal
  standards are the same, the paperwork is scaled.
- Do not use: fees, grant amounts, course lengths or numbers of participants for any LEO or Bord
  Bia programme, because they change by county and year.
- Do not use: Forbairt, or the Food Hygiene Regulations 1950-1989, or the Labelling Regulations
  1982 and 1991, because they are superseded.
- Do not use: the statistic or ranking for the growth in number of artisan producers, because
  no checked figure exists; keep the growth qualitative.

## 4. What exams ask

- "Discuss/evaluate the role of artisan producers or small businesses/home enterprises in the Irish
  food industry" (12-16 marks; the most repeated question): employment (rural, skilled); speciality
  foods in limited quantities with traditional skills; family-run, rural; high quality and hygiene;
  promote the area; boost Ireland's reputation; diversify and fill niche markets; local
  ingredients; innovation. Aim for 4-5 developed points.
- "Comment on or analyse the growing popularity of speciality foods/foods from small businesses"
  (15-20 marks): variety, additive-free, hand-made, natural image, quality, local produce,
  farmers' markets, supermarkets and online, support for local goods, reduced food miles.
- "What are speciality foods; give two examples" (6 marks): small quantities, small business or
  home enterprise, local ingredients, little or no additives, special diets; two examples.
- "Give two reasons why production in small businesses and home enterprises is increasing"
  (with a second part on exports).
- Farmhouse cheese as a case: quality and variety, provenance and sustainability, promotion and
  food culture (farmers' markets, restaurants, food tourism, awards).
- Likely tips: develop each point in a sentence (point plus reason) rather than listing; use
  one named product (e.g. farmhouse cheese) as an example; do not repeat the same point for role
  and for popularity.
