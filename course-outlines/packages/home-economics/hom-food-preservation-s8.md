# Package: hom-food-preservation-s8 — Comparative evaluation of preserved foods

- **loId:** `hom-food-preservation-s8` · **chapterId:** `hom-food-preservation` · **syllabusRef:** 1.3.9
- **Syllabus scope:** [HL] Comparative evaluation of preserved foods: comparing foods preserved by different methods. Also examined: a study of one food preserved two ways (2010H); data on vitamin C and sodium after freezing and canning (2005H). The whole subsection is Higher Level only.
- **Prepared by:** a higher model, 2026-11-23. This subsection teaches students to compare two preserved versions of one food on stated criteria and to reach a judgement. It draws on the content of the earlier subsections (principles, freezing, heat processing, dehydration, chemical preservation, irradiation) and does not re-teach it: each comparison point should refer back in one clause. The 2010H question is a "findings of a study you carried out" question, so the notes must show a model comparison (strawberries frozen and as jam) and a method for planning one. The 2005H question is data interpretation (a table of fresh, frozen and canned peas), so the notes must teach how to read nutrient data and give reasons.

## 1. Must cover (Higher Level only)

Why and how to compare
- [ ] C1 [HL] A comparative evaluation compares two methods of preserving the same food against set criteria and ends with a judgement about which suits a stated purpose.
- [ ] C2 [HL] Fair test: use one food, from the same batch, in the same condition (sound, ripe), prepared equally, so differences come from the method and not from the raw material (2010H scheme).
- [ ] C3 [HL] Criteria: nutritive value; colour, flavour and texture (sensory qualities); safety and risk of spoilage; shelf life and storage conditions; time, skill, cost and energy; equipment; packaging; convenience and use in meals; environmental impact.
- [ ] C4 [HL] Structure of a written evaluation (2010H): the name of the food; the methods and the steps of each; the underlying principle of each method; the findings against each criterion; the risk of spoilage of each; a conclusion that weighs advantages and disadvantages and states which method is better for what.
- [ ] C5 [HL] Sensory evaluation uses the senses (appearance, aroma, flavour, texture); a simple ranking or rating of the two samples by several tasters, in identical conditions. (Sensory analysis methods belong to the sensory analysis subsection; give the idea only.)
- [ ] C6 [HL] A summary table, one column per method and one row per criterion, is a clear layout and a good revision device.

Worked example: strawberries frozen and as jam (2010H model)
- [ ] S1 [HL] Freezing: washed quickly, hulled, dried, open frozen in one layer, packed in portions in moisture- and vapour-proof bags with air removed, sealed, labelled, dated, fast frozen at about −25 °C and stored at −18 °C. Principle: low temperature inactivates micro-organisms and slows enzymes; water is ice and unavailable. Finding: colour and flavour close to fresh; texture softer on thawing and juice released because ice crystals damaged the cells.
- [ ] S2 [HL] Jam making: fruit simmered, sugar added, boiled to setting point, potted hot, sealed. Principle: heat destroys organisms and enzymes; sugar draws water out of organisms by osmosis; pectin and acid set the jam; sealing prevents re-entry. Finding: sweeter, darker, cooked flavour, firm set; some vitamin C lost in boiling; high sugar content.
- [ ] S3 [HL] Risk of spoilage: frozen fruit is not sterile and spoils if the freezer temperature fluctuates or after thawing; freezer burn from poor wrapping. Jam spoils (mould, fermentation) if sugar is low, setting point is not reached, jars are not sterile or the seal is faulty.
- [ ] S4 [HL] Practical comparison points: freezing needs a freezer and electricity to keep it running; jam needs cooking fuel, jars and time but keeps in a cupboard unopened; frozen fruit is versatile (desserts, smoothies); jam suits spreading and baking.

Nutrient data: freezing and canning (2005H)
- [ ] N1 [HL] Reading a table of nutrients per 100 g for the fresh, frozen and canned food: state the figure for each, say whether it rose or fell, and give a reason (2005H: "comment ... give one possible reason").
- [ ] N2 [HL] Vitamin C: lower in frozen peas than fresh (water-soluble and unstable at high temperature; blanching uses boiling water) and lowest in canned (extremely high temperatures and long processing; loss into the liquid).
- [ ] N3 [HL] Sodium: much higher in canned peas because salt is added in canning (brine); frozen is close to fresh.
- [ ] N4 [HL] Thiamine (vitamin B1): lower in canned peas because the B vitamins are unstable at the high temperatures of canning.
- [ ] N5 [HL] Protein, carbohydrate, vitamin A and minerals show small changes; energy values may differ from water content, not from the method. The scheme's aim: preservation tries to keep colour, flavour, texture and nutritive value as close to fresh as possible.
- [ ] N6 [HL] Other nutrient effects to compare (2021H scheme): canning may lose water-soluble vitamins and may add sugar (syrup), salt (brine) or fat (oil); dehydration may lose B vitamins, A and E and gives a sugar-concentrated product.

Comparing other methods (brief evaluation lines, each referring to an earlier subsection)
- [ ] M1 [HL] Freezing versus canning (vegetables): frozen retains more vitamin C, colour and texture; canned is ready to eat, needs no freezer and keeps for a long period at room temperature, but loses vitamins and may add salt or sugar.
- [ ] M2 [HL] Freezing versus drying: freezing keeps nutrients, colour and texture but needs a freezer and continuous energy; drying keeps for a long time without refrigeration, is light and compact, but loses vitamins and changes texture and colour, and dried fruit is high in sugar per portion.
- [ ] M3 [HL] Irradiation versus heat treatment: irradiation changes colour, flavour and texture little and leaves no residue, but may cause vitamin loss and rancidity and is limited by law and public acceptance; canning uses heat that changes the food.
- [ ] M4 [HL] Chemical preservation (jam, chutney, pickles, salting) versus freezing: sugar, salt and vinegar change the food and add sugar or sodium; freezing does not change the food's content.
- [ ] M5 [HL] Environmental and packaging points: glass jars can be washed and reused or recycled; plastic freezer bags are mostly single-use; a freezer uses electricity continuously; reduced waste is an advantage of every method.
- [ ] M6 [HL] Judgement: choose by purpose (long storage without a freezer, nutrient retention, use as a spread or in meals, cost, time), state the choice and justify it.

## 3. Traps

- A comparison needs both methods against the same criteria. Writing two separate descriptions without comparing earns little.
- A fair test needs the same food and batch; "frozen peas versus canned carrots" is not a fair comparison.
- Do not claim that one method is always better. The judgement depends on the purpose; state it.
- Frozen food is not sterile; jam and canned food are sealed, but spoil if the seal fails. Do not say frozen food cannot spoil.
- Describing how each method is carried out in full belongs to the freezing and heat processing subsections (and the practical-application subsection for the home-method account); here give the steps in one or two lines and concentrate on the findings.
- Sensory analysis methods (testing conditions, tests such as ranking and triangle) belong to a separate sensory analysis subsection in the cooking-methods and food-preparation chapters; give the idea of rating colour, flavour, texture and appearance only.
- Nutrient content of fruit and vegetables in general belongs to the fruit and vegetables chapter and the vitamins chapter; use only the effects of preservation.
- Do not use: the thiamine figures from the 2005H table (32 and 13 "mg"), because the scheme's unit is doubtful (the values are in the table's units, not milligrams); say that thiamine is lower in canned peas without figures.
- Do not use: "plastic freezer bags are usually made from non-renewable oil and are hard to recycle" as a flat statement, because bag materials and recycling vary by product and local scheme; say most freezer bags are single-use plastic.

## 4. What exams ask

- A study of one food preserved by two methods (2010H, 26 marks): name of food; suitable methods; the underlying principle of each; risk of spoilage; findings. Expected answer: strawberries frozen and as jam, or another food such as peas frozen and canned, or apples frozen and bottled, with the findings above.
- Data analysis (2005H, 24 marks, 6 marks per point): effect of freezing on vitamin C of peas; effects of canning on vitamin C, sodium and thiamine; each with one possible reason (heat and water solubility; salt added; B vitamins unstable at high temperature).
- Short comparisons in other questions (2021H: canning and dehydration effects; 2016H: effect of freezing on the food; 2022H and 2013H: quick versus slow freezing).
- Evaluation phrasing: "evaluate", "assess", "compare" need advantages, disadvantages and a judgement.
- Tip content: state the fair-test conditions, give the finding for each method on each criterion, and end with a reasoned choice.
