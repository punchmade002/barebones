Meat-specific facts come from the marking schemes and standard food science.

## 1. Must cover

Causes of toughness (one block, fact-card list; examiners ask for two or three)
- [ ] T1 Age of the animal: older animals have longer, thicker fibres and more connective tissue (and the collagen is more cross-linked), so meat is tougher; young animals (veal, lamb, chicken) are tender.
- [ ] T2 Activity of the muscle: muscles that work hard (neck, shin, leg, shoulder, flank) have larger fibres and more connective tissue than the little-used muscles (fillet, sirloin, loin). Link to the cut.
- [ ] T3 Treatment before slaughter: an animal that is rested and not stressed gives better meat; stress or exhaustion before slaughter lowers meat quality.
- [ ] T4 Treatment after slaughter: carcasses must be hung for the right time at the right temperature; if hung too briefly the muscle stays stiff (rigor mortis), and chilling too fast before rigor has passed causes cold shortening, so the meat is tough.
- [ ] T5 Cooking: wrong cooking toughens meat. Fast, dry, high heat on a tough cut (or overcooking any cut) coagulates and shrinks the protein, squeezing out juices, giving dry, tough meat; tough cuts need slow moist cooking.
- [ ] T6 Fat content: marbled meat feels juicier and tender.

Methods of tenderising and their principles (one block, with principle for each)
- [ ] M1 Hanging (maturing): after slaughter rigor mortis stiffens the muscle; during hanging in a cold room the stiffness passes, glycogen has become lactic acid, and the meat's own proteolytic enzymes break down fibres and connective tissue. Principle: natural enzyme action. Beef is hung for about 1-3 weeks in a cold room.
- [ ] M2 Mincing: a mincer cuts fibres and connective tissue into small pieces (mechanical breakdown); used for tough cuts (burgers, bolognese).
- [ ] M3 Pounding with a steak hammer or mallet, or cutting across the grain: breaks up long fibres mechanically.
- [ ] M4 Piercing or jaccard (needles, skewers, forks): breaks fibres and connective tissue and lets marinade in.
- [ ] M5 Marinating: soaking in an acid (lemon juice, vinegar, wine, yoghurt) which softens the surface protein; adds flavour. Marinade works on the surface of the meat only.
- [ ] M6 Proteolytic enzymes: papain (papaya), bromelain (pineapple), ficin (fig) and actinidin (kiwi) break down protein (the bonds within muscle and collagen). Commercial powder tenderisers are sprinkled on or injected into meat before cooking; they act mostly on the surface and while cooking warms up.
- [ ] M7 Brining and curing: salt solution dissolves some muscle protein and holds water, giving a juicy, tender meat (e.g. bacon, ham).
- [ ] M8 Slow moist cooking (stewing, braising, casseroling, pot roasting): collagen is converted to gelatine, which softens the connective tissue; elastin is not softened, so trim or mince it.
- [ ] M9 Pressure cooking: high steam pressure and temperature convert collagen to gelatine quickly. Also tenderising by slicing thinly across the grain and by choosing the right cut.
- [ ] M10 State for each method: what is done, the principle (mechanical, chemical, enzymic, heat), and which cuts it suits.

Effects of cooking on meat (one block, fact-card list)
- [ ] E1 Protein coagulates (denatures and sets), so the meat firms and fibres shrink; overcooking toughens and dries.
- [ ] E2 Shrinkage: water, juices and melted fat are lost so meat loses weight and size; lower, slower cooking loses less.
- [ ] E3 Fat melts and bastes the meat; the fat that runs out can be poured away.
- [ ] E4 Collagen changes to gelatine in moist heat, which tenderises; elastin does not change.
- [ ] E5 Myoglobin denatures, so red meat changes to brown or grey; the juices turn from pink to clear in well-cooked meat.
- [ ] E6 Micro-organisms are destroyed, making meat safe; cook to 75 °C at the centre (poultry, pork, mince, sausages, burgers).
- [ ] E7 Flavour and appearance: extractives are released; the Maillard reaction (amino acids with sugars at a high surface temperature) browns the surface and adds flavour.
- [ ] E8 Nutrients: protein remains available and is easier to digest when cooked correctly; water-soluble vitamins (B group, especially thiamine) and minerals dissolve into cooking juices or drip, so use the juices in gravy or sauce; some B vitamins are destroyed by heat; fat-soluble vitamins are mostly retained.
- [ ] E9 Palatability and digestibility: tender, juicy and well flavoured; overcooked meat is dry, tough and harder to digest.

Suitable cooking methods for different cuts (one block, with diagram not required)
- [ ] C1 Tender cuts (fillet, sirloin, rump, loin chops, chicken breast, lamb cutlets, pork loin): quick dry heat: grilling, frying, roasting, stir-frying, barbecuing.
- [ ] C2 Tough cuts (shin, chuck, brisket, neck, flank, oxtail, lamb shank, stewing steak, chicken legs): slow moist heat: stewing, braising, casseroling, pot roasting, pressure cooking, or mincing.
- [ ] C3 Joints for roasting (leg of lamb, loin of pork, topside, whole poultry); gammon and ham boiled or baked; sausages and burgers grilled or baked (add fat low).
- [ ] C4 Mince: fried then simmered (bolognese, chilli) or shaped and grilled (burgers); must be cooked through.
- [ ] C5 Poultry: cooked thoroughly; no pink meat, juices run clear, core 75 °C; roast whole or portions, stew, casserole.
- [ ] C6 Offal: liver and kidney cooked quickly (frying, grilling) or gently braised; overcooking toughens liver.
- [ ] C7 Link each cut to a method and say why: the connective tissue content decides.

Evaluating one method of cooking meat (one block, template for 2023H, 24 marks)
- [ ] V1 Template: name the method; underlying principle (how heat reaches the food: conduction, convection, radiation); how the method affects the meat (texture, colour, flavour, nutrient loss, fat); suitability and advantages and disadvantages.
- [ ] V2 Stewing (marking scheme choice): meat is simmered gently in a small amount of liquid at about 80-90 °C; heat moves by conduction through the pot and convection in the liquid; collagen turns to gelatine so tough cuts become tender; flavour passes to the liquid which forms the sauce, so nutrients that leached out are kept; cheap cuts are used; slow (but can be left unattended, uses little fuel or a slow cooker); lower vitamin content if the liquid is discarded.
- [ ] V3 Other methods the scheme accepts: boiling, poaching, steaming, braising, pressure cooking, roasting, grilling or barbecuing and frying. Each needs name, principle, effect on meat. Grilling: radiation, quick, little added fat, fat drains, best for tender cuts. Frying: conduction through hot fat, fast, adds fat. Roasting: convection (and radiation) in a hot oven, with a rack so fat drains. Microwave: electromagnetic waves make water molecules vibrate (penetrates about 2-4 cm), fast, no browning.
- [ ] V4 One well-evaluated method with principle, effect, one advantage and one disadvantage is better than a list.

Low-income family menus with meat (one block)
- [ ] L1 Choose cheaper cuts or mince and cook by a suitable method (stew, casserole, bolognese, shepherd's pie, curry) or use cheaper meat (chicken portions, sausages, liver).
- [ ] L2 Stretch the meat with vegetables, pulses (lentils), pasta, rice or potatoes; make it filling and balanced; give reasons: cost, nutritive value (protein, haem iron for children, teenage girls and pregnant women), ease of cooking, family tastes, skills and equipment, fuel saving (one-pot, slow cooker, batch cook and freeze).
- [ ] L3 Marking-scheme examples: spaghetti bolognese with an apple crumble (2023O); vegetable soup followed by spaghetti bolognese (2010O). Any suitable two-course menu with reasons is accepted.
- [ ] L4 Add vitamin C foods (vegetables, fruit) as meat is deficient in vitamin C; use stock from bones and cooking juices.

## 3. Traps

- Do not use: "a stressed or exhausted animal uses up its glycogen, so less lactic acid forms and the meat stays firm", because the mechanism is oversimplified and the link to toughness unconfirmed. Say only that poor rest or stress before slaughter lowers the quality of the meat.
- Do not use: "proteolytic enzymes injected into the animal before slaughter" (as one marking scheme says), because that is not a current practice in Irish meat production. Say enzymes are sprinkled on or injected into the meat before cooking.
- Do not use: exact hanging times for other meats (such as "7 days"), because times vary with meat and are unchecked.
- Do not use: "acid marinades soften the whole joint", because marinades penetrate only the surface.
- Do not use: "juices sealed in by searing". Coagulating the surface does not seal in juice; the 2005O scheme says it does, but it is a food-science myth, so say only that surface protein coagulates and browns.
- Tough cuts need slow moist cooking; tender cuts need fast dry cooking; reversing them is the most common error.
- Collagen softens, elastin does not.
- Marinading does not make meat safe or tender all the way through.
- Coagulation (setting of protein) and denaturation (change of shape) are related; use "coagulates" for firming, "denatures" for the myoglobin colour change.
- Belongs to neighbouring subsections: nutritional value and the protein descriptions to the first subsection; storage, thawing and safe handling to the second; curing and shelf-life processes to the fourth. The details of each cooking method's working, appliances and heat transfer are in the cooking methods chapter; refer to them by name.

## 4. What exams ask

- Causes of toughness (almost every year; 2025H, 2024O, 2021O, 2017O, 2014H, 2004O): two to three of age, activity or cut, treatment before and after slaughter (rest, hanging), cooking, each with a reason (longer thicker fibres, more connective tissue).
- Tenderising methods (2025H four methods; 2024O two; 2023O two; 2011H one with principle): name each method and its principle; for a mark, say "what is done" and "why it works".
- Effects of cooking on meat (2017O, 2018H, 2014O, 2005O; also protein effects): coagulation, firming and shrinkage, fat melts, collagen to gelatine, myoglobin colour change, Maillard flavour, loss of water-soluble vitamins, safety.
- Evaluate one cooking method (2023H, 24 marks): name, underlying principle (conduction, convection, radiation), effects on meat (tenderness, colour, flavour, juiciness, nutrients), plus advantages, disadvantages and cuts it suits.
- Low-income family menu (2023O, 2010O): a two-course menu with meat as main ingredient, and reasons (cost, nutrition, skills, equipment, time, taste).
- Fill-in-the-blank style (2014O): mincing, collagen to gelatine, myoglobin denaturation.
- Tip candidates: "cause, then reason" for toughness; "method, then principle" for tenderisers; match method to cut and say why; always add one disadvantage in an evaluation; use gelatine and coagulation vocabulary.
