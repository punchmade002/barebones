## 1. Must cover

Elements in protein (common to both levels)
- [ ] E1 Protein contains carbon, hydrogen, oxygen and nitrogen in every case; some proteins also contain small amounts of sulphur, and some phosphorus and iron.
- [ ] E2 Protein is the only nutrient that contains nitrogen (carbohydrates and fats contain only carbon, hydrogen and oxygen). Nitrogen is the element essential for growth.
- [ ] E3 (assumed) The elements are arranged into basic units called amino acids; a protein is a large molecule made of many amino acids joined together (examiners ask this as a true/false statement).
- [ ] E4 Nitrogen sits in the amino group of every amino acid, so the nitrogen in a food comes from its protein. Sulphur is in only some amino acids (e.g. cysteine, methionine); one clause, no detail.

Basic structure of an amino acid (common to both levels)
- [ ] A1 Every amino acid has a central carbon atom bonded to four things: an amino group (–NH₂), a carboxyl (acid) group (–COOH), a hydrogen atom, and a variable side group written R.
- [ ] A2 The amino group is basic (alkaline) and the carboxyl group is acidic; the R group differs between amino acids and gives each its identity.
- [ ] A3 About 20 amino acids are used to build the body's proteins; different proteins are different sequences of the same 20.
- [ ] A4 Diagram: the central carbon with H, R, –COOH and –NH₂ attached, each group labelled. (Examined as "complete the diagram", so the notes must say exactly what to draw and label.)

Essential and non-essential amino acids [HL]
- [ ] N1 [HL] Essential (indispensable) amino acids cannot be made by the body, or not in adequate amounts, so they must be supplied by the diet.
- [ ] N2 [HL] Irish marking schemes say eight are essential for adults and ten for children (give the scheme figure in an exam; some sources count histidine as essential for adults too). Adult eight: valine, leucine, isoleucine, lysine, methionine, phenylalanine, threonine, tryptophan. The two extra for children: histidine and arginine.
- [ ] N3 [HL] Non-essential amino acids can be made by the body from other amino acids and nitrogen-containing compounds, so they do not have to come from food. Examples the schemes accept: alanine, asparagine, aspartic acid, cysteine, glutamic acid, glutamine, glycine, proline, serine, tyrosine.
- [ ] N4 [HL] "Non-essential" does not mean unimportant; it means a dietary supply is not normally required.
- [ ] N5 [HL] The link to quality: a protein's biological value depends on whether it supplies all the essential amino acids in the right proportions (cross-reference by title: "Biological value").

Peptides and the peptide bond [HL]
- [ ] P1 [HL] A peptide bond forms when the carboxyl group (–COOH) of one amino acid reacts with the amino group (–NH₂) of another, and a molecule of water is released (condensation).
- [ ] P2 [HL] The link formed is –CO–NH–. Two amino acids joined give a dipeptide; a short chain is a peptide; a long chain is a polypeptide; a protein is one or more polypeptide chains.
- [ ] P3 [HL] Diagram: two amino acids side by side, then joined, with the –CO–NH– link and the released water labelled.

Hydrolysis [HL]
- [ ] H1 [HL] Hydrolysis breaks a peptide bond by adding a molecule of water; it is the reverse of condensation.
- [ ] H2 [HL] Repeated hydrolysis splits a polypeptide into smaller peptides and finally into free amino acids. In the body the enzymes pepsin and trypsin do this in digestion; acid and heat can also hydrolyse protein. One clause only on digestion.

## 3. Traps

- Amino group is –NH₂ (nitrogen); carboxyl group is –COOH (acid). Students swap them. R is a variable side group, not an element.
- Peptide bond formation releases water (condensation); hydrolysis uses water. Do not mix them up.
- The peptide bond is between the carboxyl of one amino acid and the amino group of the next; not between R groups.
- Essential means "must come from food", not "more important".
- Primary, secondary and tertiary structure, hydrogen bonds and disulphide links belong to the structure subsection; say only that amino acids are joined in a chain.
- Simple, conjugated, fibrous and globular proteins belong to the classification subsection.
- Deamination (removal of the amino group from surplus amino acids) belongs to the energy value subsection.
- Digestive enzymes, sites of action and absorption of amino acids belong to the digestion and metabolism chapter.
- Do not use: a flat statement that histidine is non-essential for adults, or a nine-amino-acid adult list, because current nutrition science counts histidine as essential for adults and the notes only follow the scheme figure (eight adult, ten child); if a question gives nine, accept it.
- Do not use: a claim that every amino acid contains sulphur, because only some do (e.g. cysteine, methionine).

## 4. What exams ask

- Name the four elements in protein and say which element is unique to protein (4–6 marks, both levels).
- Ordinary true/false: "proteins are large molecules made of amino acids" (true); "protein is the only nutrient that contains nitrogen" (true).
- Higher: complete the diagram of the basic amino acid structure (central carbon with H, R, COOH, NH₂); label the amino and carboxyl groups.
- Higher short questions: explain essential amino acid and give an example; differentiate essential from non-essential with an example of each (2026 set it at 8 marks). Strong answers: "cannot be made by the body in adequate amounts, must be supplied by food"; a correct named example; for non-essential, "made by the body from other compounds".
- Higher: describe how a peptide bond forms; the answer must name both groups and the water lost.
- Long "give an account of protein" questions open with composition: elements, amino acids as building units, then (Higher) essential and non-essential amino acids.
- Exam tips to build: label the amino (–NH₂) and carboxyl (–COOH) groups when drawing; name at least two correct essential amino acids when asked (lysine and valine are safe); say water is released when a peptide bond forms.
