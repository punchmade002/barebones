## 1. Must cover

What a social institution is (one block)
- [ ] I1 A social institution is an established, organised set of roles, rules and values through which a society meets a basic need: the family, education, religion, the economy (work) and government or the State. Institutions last across generations and are not the same as a single building or organisation (a school is an organisation within the institution of education).
- [ ] I2 Institutions shape behaviour: they carry norms and values (link to the first subsection), pass on culture, and give structure to social life. Each is an agent of socialisation (link to the groups and socialisation subsection).
- [ ] I3 Institutions are linked: what happens in one affects the others (the economy affects family life; education prepares people for work). One example is enough.
- [ ] I4 Functions: manifest (intended and recognised, for example a school educates) and latent (unintended, for example a school keeps children supervised while parents work). Optional extras; label as beyond the syllabus wording.

The family (one block)
- [ ] F1 The family is the first and most basic institution: it cares for children and dependants, socialises children, provides emotional security and shares economic resources. Details of functions are in the chapter on the family in society; give a two-line summary only.
- [ ] F2 Other institutions adopt or supplement family functions: schools take on much education, the State provides welfare and health services, employers provide income, religious bodies provide moral guidance. This supplementation is the link to later family questions (2006 scheme example: parenting courses for the emotional function, schooling for the educational function, social welfare for the economic function, medical services for the physical function).

Education (one block)
- [ ] E1 Education transmits knowledge, skills and culture from one generation to the next, formally (the curriculum, from primary to third level) and informally (the hidden curriculum: rules, punctuality, respect for authority, sharing and teamwork).
- [ ] E2 It socialises children, reinforcing behaviour and values learned at home (2024H scheme).
- [ ] E3 It prepares people for work: literacy and numeracy, qualifications, specialist skills, career guidance, work experience in Transition Year, the Leaving Certificate Applied and the Leaving Certificate Vocational Programme, and in college (2024H scheme).
- [ ] E4 It sorts people into jobs through qualifications and is the main route to social mobility; link to the role and status subsection (one clause).
- [ ] E5 Equality of opportunity: socio-economic status affects chances; costs of books, uniforms and equipment can exclude low-income families; helped by grants, book rental schemes and DEIS (Delivering Equality of Opportunity in Schools) supports; higher-status families often have more educational opportunities and go on to third level more often. No percentages.
- [ ] E6 In Ireland the Leaving Certificate is the state examination at the end of second level; many primary schools have a religious patron. (Optional context; no figures.)

Religion (one block)
- [ ] RE1 Religion gives a set of beliefs and values about the meaning of life, and moral guidance that supports norms (for example, rules against stealing or killing).
- [ ] RE2 It marks the main stages of life with ceremonies (birth, first communion or confirmation, marriage, death) and gives a sense of community and support.
- [ ] RE3 Churches and other religious bodies have supported education, health care and charity; the influence of religion on Irish society has changed (examples in the marriage and family questions: declining influence of the Church; more civil ceremonies; more secular society).
- [ ] RE4 Ireland has many faiths and people of no religion; the notes must be neutral and describe beliefs without judgement.

The economy and work (one block)
- [ ] EC1 The economy organises the production, distribution and consumption of goods and services; work provides income, and with it a standard of living and social status.
- [ ] EC2 Work is where people take on adult roles and learn workplace norms; it connects to family life (hours of work, dual-earner households, childcare needs).
- [ ] EC3 Economic changes (unemployment, recession, growth of service industries) affect family income and roles; see examples in the schemes: unemployment lowers living standards and strains relationships; service-industry growth creates jobs.
- [ ] EC4 Link to Resource Management and Consumer Studies: families are consumers (assumed).

Government and the State (one block)
- [ ] GO1 The State makes laws, keeps order, collects taxes and provides services: social welfare, health, education, housing, protection of the vulnerable.
- [ ] GO2 In Ireland the Oireachtas, the national parliament, consists of the President, Dáil Éireann and Seanad Éireann; the Government (the executive) carries out the laws and the courts (the judiciary) apply them. The separation of powers prevents any one body holding all the power. Local authorities provide local services (housing, planning, libraries).
- [ ] GO3 Citizens take part by voting in elections and referendums. One line only.
- [ ] GO4 The State supports the family in each function: welfare payments (economic), health services (physical), schooling (educational), child protection (the State's agency for children is Tusla, the Child and Family Agency). Do not give payment names or amounts.

Other institutions (one block)
- [ ] O1 Health services (the Health Service Executive provides public health care in Ireland) and the mass media are sometimes added as institutions. The media inform and entertain and shape attitudes; both are also agents of socialisation. Include as short bullets after the five main institutions; do not state that the list of institutions is fixed.
- [ ] O2 The legal system (courts and law) is covered through government; do not add a separate block.

## 3. Traps

- An institution is not a building or one organisation: the school down the road is part of the institution of education. Do not say "the institution of education is the school".
- The family is both a group (a primary group) and an institution; here treat it as an institution, and keep the functions list for the family chapter.
- Do not repeat the five family functions in full; refer to how other institutions supplement them (this is what examiners ask).
- Do not write notes on Irish education policy, school types or the number of schools or students; they change and are not needed.
- Do not use: a statement that religious bodies run a specific percentage or number of Irish schools or hospitals, because the figures change and are unconfirmed here; say only that many primary schools have a religious patron.
- Do not use: "the Seanad is elected by all citizens", because the Seanad is elected in part by graduates and panels and appointed in part; the notes may say the Dáil is elected by citizens in general elections.
- Do not use: the superseded names for State bodies in family and welfare examples (the Eastern Health Board, the regional health boards, the Department of Health and Children), because the bodies have been replaced.
- Do not use: "the Church controls Irish education" or similar value judgements; describe the historical role of religious patrons neutrally.
- Do not label an institution as "good" or "bad"; sociology describes functions.
- Belongs to a neighbouring subsection: definitions of culture, norms and values (first subsection); socialisation as a process and agents of socialisation (groups and socialisation); family structure, functions and the State's support for each function (the chapter on the family in society); equality of opportunity, special needs education and access initiatives (the social studies elective on education and employment); social welfare (the elective on poverty and unemployment); how institutions have changed (social change).

## 4. What exams ask

- Explain how one family function has been supplemented by another institution (2006 and later): name the function (emotional, educational, economic, physical) and the institution (state parenting courses, schools, social welfare, medical services) with one clear sentence. This subsection supplies the institutions; the family chapter supplies the functions.
- Discuss the role of education in society (2024H): as a method of socialisation (formal teaching, hidden curriculum, behaviour, punctuality, respect, sharing and teamwork) and as preparation for work (literacy, numeracy, qualifications, skills, guidance, work experience).
- Outline the contribution of education to the socialisation of young children: reinforcing the social skills learned at home, rules, punctuality, respect for authority, teamwork.
- Analyse how socio-economic status affects equality of opportunity in education: costs, parental support, expectations, early school leaving, and the supports that reduce the gap (grants, rental schemes, DEIS).
- Role of State and religion in family life in short questions: the State provides welfare, health and schooling; religion marks life stages and offers moral guidance; each with one example.
- Exam tip: always tie an institution to a function and an example; a list of names earns little.
