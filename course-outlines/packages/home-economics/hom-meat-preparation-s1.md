# Package: hom-meat-preparation-s1 — Nutritional significance and structure of meat

- **loId:** `hom-meat-preparation-s1` · **chapterId:** `hom-meat-preparation` · **syllabusRef:** 1.3.2
- **Syllabus scope:** nutritional significance and contribution to the diet of meat (red meat, poultry, offal); the structure of meat (muscle fibres, connective tissue, fat) and the proteins in meat. No [HL] marks in this subsection. (Myosin, actin and collagen are Higher Level items in the protein chapter's chemistry; here they are named as meat proteins only, at the depth past papers ask, and are not marked HL.)
- **Also examined:** nutritive and dietetic value (2025H, 2023O, 2010O, plus 2017O, 2014H); proteins in meat (2018H, 2006H).
- **Prepared by:** a higher model, 2026-11-23, from the outline, the current notes, the past-paper marking schemes and FSAI/safefood checks. "Nutritive value" means what meat supplies (nutrients); "dietetic value" means who benefits or should limit it and why. Examiners ask the two as separate headings.

## 1. Must cover

Categories of meat (one block)
- [ ] C1 Red meat: beef, lamb, pork (and veal). Poultry: chicken, turkey, duck. Offal: edible organs, e.g. liver, kidney, heart.
- [ ] C2 Poultry breast is paler than leg or red meat because it has less myoglobin; poultry fat is lower than most red meat, and most of it sits in and under the skin.
- [ ] C3 Offal: liver is the richest source of vitamin A, iron and several B vitamins; give one example each of why offal is valued (cheap, nutrient-dense) and cooked quickly (see "Preparation and cooking").

Nutritive value: what meat supplies (one block, fact-card list)
- [ ] N1 Protein: high biological value (HBV), meaning it supplies all the essential amino acids in the proportions the body needs. The marking schemes give meat as about 20-30% protein. Functions: growth and repair of cells, making enzymes, hormones and antibodies.
- [ ] N2 Fat: mainly saturated; marking-scheme range about 10-30%, varying with animal, cut and trimming (chicken is low). Functions: concentrated energy and insulation; fat-soluble vitamins in offal.
- [ ] N3 Carbohydrate: meat contains essentially none, so it is served with a starchy food (potatoes, pasta, rice, bread). Link to energy and balance of a meal.
- [ ] N4 Minerals: haem iron (red meat, offal, poultry dark meat), zinc, potassium, phosphorus, sulphur (in the sulphur-containing amino acids); sodium is high in processed meat only.
- [ ] N5 Haem iron is absorbed more readily than non-haem iron from plant foods; iron is used to make haemoglobin (carries oxygen in the blood) and myoglobin.
- [ ] N6 Vitamins: B group (release of energy from food; B12 occurs naturally only in animal foods); vitamins A and D mainly in liver. Meat is deficient in vitamin C (so serve with vegetables or fruit).
- [ ] N7 Water: the marking scheme gives about 50-60%; raw lean meat is about 70-75% water, so the figure varies with fat content and cooking. Extractives (flavour compounds that dissolve out in cooking) give meat its savoury taste and stimulate digestive juices.

Dietetic value: who benefits, who limits (one block)
- [ ] D1 Growing children and teenagers: protein for growth; haem iron for teenage girls (menstruation) and pregnant women to prevent iron-deficiency anaemia.
- [ ] D2 Adults and older people: protein for repair and maintenance; tender, lean or minced cuts cooked gently are easier to chew and digest.
- [ ] D3 People with raised cholesterol or coronary heart disease, and anyone controlling weight: choose lean cuts, trim visible fat, remove poultry skin, grill rather than fry, limit processed meats.
- [ ] D4 Low-income households: cheaper cuts and mince are good value protein and iron.
- [ ] D5 Healthy eating advice: the Department of Health healthy eating guidelines and the Food Pyramid place meat in the shelf with poultry, fish, eggs, beans and nuts, recommend lean and unprocessed choices, and advise limiting red and processed meat.
- [ ] D6 Vegetarians and vegans do not eat meat: they need other sources of HBV-equivalent protein, iron and B12 (one line, detail is in the protein and diets chapters).

Structure of meat (one block, with diagram)
- [ ] S1 Muscle is made of long thin muscle fibres (each is one muscle cell) lying parallel; groups of fibres form bundles; bundles are held together by connective tissue. The fibres run in the direction of the muscle's pull.
- [ ] S2 Each fibre contains myofibrils made of the proteins actin and myosin (the contractile proteins); they slide past each other to shorten the muscle. The fibre is surrounded by muscle fluid (water, extractives, soluble protein, vitamins, minerals).
- [ ] S3 Connective tissue: wraps fibres and bundles and attaches muscle to bone (tendons). It contains collagen (white, tough, converts to gelatine in slow moist heat) and elastin (yellow, stretchy, found in tendons and ligaments, not softened by cooking).
- [ ] S4 Fat: marbling (fat between and within muscle fibres, adds juiciness and flavour), fat under the skin or as a covering layer, and fat around organs. It melts on cooking.
- [ ] S5 Why structure matters: a tougher cut has longer, thicker fibres and more connective tissue (link to "Preparation and cooking"; give one sentence here only).
- [ ] S6 Diagram: a cross-section of muscle: a bundle of fibres inside a sheath of connective tissue, with a single fibre enlarged to show myofibrils (actin and myosin), plus a label for fat (marbling). Label connective tissue, fibre, bundle, fat.

Proteins in meat (one block)
- [ ] P1 Myosin and actin: contractile proteins of the myofibrils; most of the fibre protein; coagulate and firm up on heating.
- [ ] P2 Globulin: a soluble protein in the muscle fluid; coagulates and is partly lost in drip and cooking juices.
- [ ] P3 Myoglobin: the red oxygen-storing pigment of muscle; gives raw meat its colour; denatures on heating so the meat changes from red to brown or grey.
- [ ] P4 Collagen: insoluble connective-tissue protein; tough raw; converts to soluble gelatine in slow moist cooking.
- [ ] P5 Elastin: connective-tissue protein in tendons and ligaments; very tough; not softened by cooking (remove it or mince it).
- [ ] P6 (assumed) Protein: amino acids, HBV and LBV, coagulation and denaturation are taught in the protein chapter; use these terms without defining them at length.
- [ ] P7 Exam skill: be able to name two meat proteins and state one effect of cooking on each (2018H, 2006H style).

## 3. Traps

- Do not use: "meat contains no vitamin C" as an absolute, because liver has a little. Say "meat is deficient in (is not a source of) vitamin C".
- Do not use: any recommended weekly amount of red meat or number of Food Pyramid servings, because these are changing guideline figures.
- Do not use: exact fat or protein percentages per named cut, because none are checked. Only the scheme ranges above.
- "No carbohydrate" is the exam phrase; keep it as "essentially none".
- Nutritive value is about nutrients present; dietetic value is about suitability for groups. Do not mix them in one list.
- Collagen and elastin are both connective tissue but behave differently in cooking; the common mistake is saying both soften.
- Myoglobin is the muscle pigment; haemoglobin is the blood pigment. Meat colour is myoglobin.
- Haem iron absorption is better; do not say vitamin C is not needed. The vitamin C and phytate effects on iron are in the minerals chapter.
- Poultry skin and fat is where most poultry fat is; do not say poultry is fat free.
- Belongs to neighbouring subsections: causes of toughness, tenderising and cooking effects belong to the preparation and cooking subsection; buying, labelling, Bord Bia and storage belong to the selection and storage subsection; curing, canning and processed products belong to the processing subsection. Protein chemistry (amino acids, peptide bonds, foam and gel formation) belongs to the protein chapter.
- Do not describe offal in detail beyond liver, kidney and heart.

## 4. What exams ask

- "Discuss the nutritional significance of meat" (20 marks, 2025H, 2014H; 20 marks as nutritive and dietetic value, 2023O, 2017O, 2010O). Expected content: five or so points, each worth about four marks, each with a nutrient, a source form and its function: HBV protein for growth, repair and hormones; saturated fat for energy and insulation; no carbohydrate so pair with starch; B vitamins and A and D in liver; haem iron for blood; potassium, zinc and the other minerals; deficient in vitamin C; sometimes water. The ordinary-level version needs one nutritive and one dietetic reference plus two others.
- Dietetic value: which groups need meat (children, teenage girls, pregnant women, those recovering) and who limits it (heart disease, high cholesterol, weight control); lean cuts and trimming.
- Proteins in meat (2018H, 2006H): name two (myosin, actin, globulin, collagen, elastin) and give an effect of cooking (coagulation, shrinkage, collagen to gelatine, Maillard browning, colour change from myoglobin denaturing).
- Structure: describe muscle fibres, connective tissue and fat; labelled sketch of a muscle fibre bundle.
- Tip candidates: "name the nutrient, name the source, give the function" pattern; keep nutritive and dietetic lists separate; always mention that meat lacks carbohydrate and vitamin C and what to serve with it.
- Related Higher Level iron question (2010H) uses meat as the haem iron source: sources, functions and absorption factors are marked as iron content, and belong to the minerals chapter.
