# Package: hom-family-law-s6 — Making a will

- **loId:** `hom-family-law-s6` · **chapterId:** `hom-family-law` · **syllabusRef:** 3.1.7
- **Syllabus scope:** "Making a will" [3.1.7]: the importance of, and outline procedure for, making a will under the Succession Act 1965. Common to Ordinary and Higher Level: no [HL] mark.
- **Also examined:** 2026H, 2022H, 2017O, 2011O, 2010H. Also seen: 2023O (two reasons why an older person should make a will) and 2006O (outline the procedure). Typical wording: state why it is important to make a will and outline the procedure; explain why it is important for divorced parents; outline three features of a valid will.
- **Prepared by:** a higher model, 2026-11-23. The Succession Act 1965 is in force (as amended) and keeps its syllabus name. Sources checked: Citizens Information (making a will), irishstatutebook.ie (Succession Act 1965 sections 77, 78). Inheritance tax thresholds and rates change and are left out.

## 1. Must cover

What a will is and why it matters
- [ ] W1 A will is a legal document in which a person (the testator) sets out how their property and money (their estate) is to be shared after their death. The law is the Succession Act 1965.
- [ ] W2 Why make one: your wishes are carried out; the property passes to the people you choose; you appoint an executor; you can protect a spouse and dependants; you can state funeral or burial wishes; disputes among relatives are less likely; it gives peace of mind. These are the exam points.
- [ ] W3 Parents: a will can appoint a guardian for young children and make suitable provision for them; schemes credit this for parents. For divorced or remarried parents: the property passes as intended, the will can be changed after remarriage or new children (the scheme's reasons), and an executor is chosen.
- [ ] W4 Tax: careful planning (with a solicitor's advice) can reduce the inheritance tax burden on those who inherit. Say this only as "may reduce" and give no rates, thresholds or amounts (they change).
- [ ] W5 What happens without a will (intestacy): the estate is shared under fixed rules in the Succession Act, which may not match the person's wishes. If there is a spouse and no children the spouse gets the whole estate; if there are children, the spouse gets two thirds and the children share one third. Relatives inherit if there is no spouse or children; the State inherits only if there are no relatives (see Traps).

Rules for a valid will
- [ ] V1 The testator must be 18 or over (or be or have been married) and of sound mind: able to understand what a will is and what property they own.
- [ ] V2 The will must be in writing and signed by the testator (or by someone else in their presence and at their direction).
- [ ] V3 The signature must be made, or acknowledged, in the presence of two witnesses who are both present at the same time and who sign in the testator's presence.
- [ ] V4 Good practice (from the marking schemes): date the will; store it safely, for example with the solicitor or in a bank; tell the executor where it is.
- [ ] V5 A witness, or the witness's spouse, who is also a beneficiary loses the gift made to them under the will (assumed from Succession Act; one clause).

Procedure: how to make a will
- [ ] P1 Step 1: list all your assets (property, savings, investments, policies, personal possessions) with their value and location, and any debts or liabilities.
- [ ] P2 Step 2: decide who you want as beneficiaries and note their full names and details (nearest relatives first).
- [ ] P3 Step 3: choose an executor (a trusted person, willing to act): the person who collects the estate, pays the debts and carries out the will.
- [ ] P4 Step 4: decide how the estate is to be divided, including any specific gifts, provision for children and dependants, any guardian, and funeral or burial wishes. Check the legal restrictions in the Succession Act (see L1).
- [ ] P5 Step 5: have the will drawn up. You may write it yourself, but a solicitor is advised so that it is clear and valid.
- [ ] P6 Step 6: sign it before two witnesses (V2, V3) and store it safely; tell the executor where it is.
- [ ] P7 After the death: the executor applies for a grant of probate, which confirms the will and gives them legal authority to deal with the estate (assumed).

Changing a will and limits on a will
- [ ] L1 Surviving spouse's legal right: whatever the will says, a surviving spouse (or civil partner) has a legal right to one half of the estate if there are no children, or one third if there are children. A will cannot take it away. Children cannot be disinherited without thought: a child may ask the court for fair provision if the parent failed in their moral duty (assumed; one clause, no detail).
- [ ] L2 A will can be changed by a codicil (a short document added to the will, signed and witnessed in the same way) or replaced by a new will that revokes the old one. A will can also be revoked by destroying it with that intention.
- [ ] L3 Marriage (or civil partnership) revokes an earlier will unless it was made in contemplation of that marriage; this is why remarrying parents must make a new will.
- [ ] L4 Exam use: "state why and outline the procedure" (14 marks): the schemes expect about seven points, split between the reasons and the steps.
- [ ] L5 Diagram: none.

## 3. Traps

- Do not use: "the State gets a sizeable part of the estate if there is no will" (one 2017O scheme wording), because the State inherits only if the person has no relatives who can inherit.
- Do not use: inheritance tax rates, thresholds or exemption amounts, because they change; say only that planning may reduce the tax burden.
- Do not use: "the testator must be over 18" as the strict legal test, because the Act says 18 or over, or married; exam schemes say "over 18", so either is accepted, but the notes should use "18 or over (or married)".
- Do not use: "children are entitled to two thirds of the estate" (one past marking scheme's summary of the Acts), because it reverses intestacy: the spouse takes two thirds and the children one third; where a will exists, children have no automatic fixed share (only the court route in L1).
- Do not use: "the will must be dated" as a legal requirement. The Act does not require a date; the schemes list it as good practice.
- A witness should not be a beneficiary or the spouse of one (V5). The executor can be a beneficiary.
- A will does not take effect until death, and a will can be changed at any time while the testator is of sound mind.
- A codicil is not a new will. Divorce or judicial separation does not itself revoke a will; do not claim it does.
- Neighbouring subjects: guardianship of children and the rights of children belong to the notes on the family; the rights a spouse has in the family home during life belong to the Family Home Protection Act; maintenance belongs to the first Act.

## 4. What exams ask

- State why it is important to make a will and outline the procedure (14 marks, Higher Level, 2010, 2022, 2026): reasons (wishes carried out, property passes to chosen people, executor, guardians for children, fewer disputes, funeral wishes, peace of mind, tax planning) and the steps (list assets, beneficiaries, executor, allocate estate and provide for children, solicitor, sign before two witnesses, store safely).
- Why it is important for divorced parents to make a will (5 marks, Ordinary Level 2011).
- Outline three features of a valid will (Ordinary): 18 or over, sound mind, in writing, signed and dated before two witnesses, an executor chosen.
- One or two reasons why an older person or parent should make a will (Ordinary).
- Procedure only (12 marks, Ordinary): about four steps with detail.
- Expected depth: Higher answers need both halves in balance; Ordinary answers need two or three points per half in full sentences.
