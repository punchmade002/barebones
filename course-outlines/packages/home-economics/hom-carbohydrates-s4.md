## 1. Must cover

Sweetness and solubility of sugars (one block)
- [ ] W1 All sugars are sweet, to different degrees. Relative sweetness on a scale in which sucrose is 100: fructose is sweeter than sucrose (the 2017 scheme gives 170) and lactose is least sweet of the common sugars (15 in the same scheme). Sweetness is used to sweeten cakes, desserts, drinks and sweets.
- [ ] W2 Solubility: sugars are white crystalline solids that dissolve in water; solubility increases with temperature (more dissolves in hot water). A concentrated solution is a syrup. Uses: icing, syrups, bottled fruit, sweetening drinks.
- [ ] W3 Starch is insoluble in cold water (the 2012 O question): it does not dissolve but settles as a cloudy suspension.

Starch: gelatinisation and hygroscopic properties (one block)
- [ ] G1 Gelatinisation: when starch is heated with a liquid, the starch granules swell, burst and absorb the liquid, and the mixture thickens into a sol (colloidal solution). On cooling a concentrated mixture may set to a gel (the 2023 scheme says "gel/thickened mixture"). Uses: white sauce, custard, gravy, soups, pie fillings, risotto.
- [ ] G2 Preventing lumps: the starch is mixed with a little cold liquid first (cornflour paste) or coated in fat (roux: fat and flour cooked together before liquid is added) so the granules separate before they swell. A roux is a fat-and-flour base for white sauce; the fat separates the starch granules.
- [ ] G3 Hygroscopic: starch absorbs moisture from the air, so dry starchy foods such as flour, biscuits and crackers soften or clump when stored in damp air; store them in airtight containers. Sugar (especially brown sugar) is also hygroscopic.

Effects of dry and moist heat on carbohydrates in cooking (one block)
- [ ] H1 Dry heat (baking, roasting, toasting, grilling) on starch: the starch granules burst and absorb fat, and dextrinisation occurs (starch breaks down into brown dextrins), giving brown colour and crisp, flavoursome crust on bread and toast (the 2009 O scheme). On sugar: caramelisation and the Maillard reaction brown the surface.
- [ ] H2 Moist heat (boiling, steaming, stewing, poaching) on starch: gelatinisation; the granules swell, burst and thicken the liquid. Pasta, rice and potatoes become soft and digestible.
- [ ] H3 Moist heat softens the cellulose in the cell walls of vegetables, so vegetables become tender and easier to digest; the vegetable keeps its fibre.
- [ ] H4 Sugar dissolves in cooking liquid and forms a syrup, for example when stewing fruit; sugar also helps fruit keep its shape.

Effects of enzymes on carbohydrates during digestion (one block)
- [ ] E1 Digestion of starch begins in the mouth: salivary amylase starts to change starch to maltose (the schemes: "starch is changed to maltose in the mouth: true"; "amylase from the pancreas"). The enzyme stops working in the stomach acid; no carbohydrate digestion takes place in the stomach (the 2007 scheme marks "carbohydrate digestion takes place in the stomach" false).
- [ ] E2 Pancreatic amylase (in pancreatic juice, released into the small intestine) breaks the remaining starch into maltose.
- [ ] E3 Enzymes in the intestinal juice (made by the wall of the small intestine) finish the job: maltase splits maltose into two glucose units; sucrase splits sucrose into glucose and fructose; lactase splits lactose into glucose and galactose.
- [ ] E4 Only the monosaccharides (glucose, fructose, galactose) are absorbed through the wall of the small intestine into the blood. The detail of absorption is in the digestion and metabolism chapter.
- [ ] E5 Humans have no enzyme to digest cellulose, so it passes through as dietary fibre.

Culinary properties of sugar (one block)
- [ ] C1 The properties of sugar the schemes accept, each with a culinary use: sweetness (cakes, desserts, drinks); solubility (syrups, icing, bottled fruit); preservation (jam and jelly: sugar draws water from micro-organisms so they cannot grow); fermentation (sugar feeds yeast, which makes carbon dioxide that raises bread dough); aeration (whisked egg with sugar holds air: sponge cakes, meringue); caramelisation (crème caramel, toffee); Maillard reaction (browning of toast, roast potatoes and baked goods); gel formation (jam); crystallisation (confectionery); inversion (jam and boiled sweets).
- [ ] C2 Maillard reaction: sugar and amino acids react when heated (non-enzymic browning), giving brown colour and flavour (toast, roast potatoes, baked goods). It is not the same as caramelisation (sugar alone).
- [ ] C3 Aeration: creamed sugar and fat trap air in cake mixtures; sugar helps whisked egg white hold its foam and makes meringue stable.
- [ ] C4 Answer pattern: name the property, say what it is, give one culinary use of it (the question asks for three properties, each with one use).

Hydrolysis and inversion (one block, [HL])
- [ ] I1 [HL] Hydrolysis is the breakdown of a compound by reaction with water: a disaccharide reacts with water (with enzymes, or heat and acid) and splits into its monosaccharide units. It is the reverse of condensation and happens in digestion. Examples: sucrose gives glucose and fructose; lactose gives glucose and galactose; maltose gives two glucose units.
- [ ] I2 [HL] Inversion is the hydrolysis of sucrose by heating with an acid (lemon juice, cream of tartar) or by the enzyme invertase. The product, a mixture of glucose and fructose, is invert sugar (the 2012 scheme).
- [ ] I3 [HL] Invert sugar is sweeter than sucrose (it contains fructose), is more soluble and is less likely to crystallise. It is used in jam and boiled sweets and for smooth icing and fondant. Honey and golden syrup contain invert sugar.

Crystallisation, caramelisation and dextrinisation (one block, [HL])
- [ ] X1 [HL] Crystallisation: when more sugar is dissolved than the liquid can hold at that temperature (a saturated solution is cooled), crystals form as the mixture cools. Wanted in fudge, fondant and sweets; unwanted in jam (grainy); prevented by adding acid or glucose syrup (inversion) and by not stirring while the sugar dissolves.
- [ ] X2 [HL] Caramelisation: sugar heated strongly on its own melts, turns from colourless to golden and then brown, producing caramel with a pleasant flavour; overheating makes it dark, bitter and finally burnt (carbonisation). Used in crème caramel, caramel sauce, toffee and brûlée.
- [ ] X3 [HL] Dextrinisation: dry heat breaks starch down into shorter-chain polysaccharides called dextrins; they are brown (pyrodextrin in the 2005 scheme). It gives the brown colour of toast and bread crust, and browned flour used for a roux thickens less. It is a property of starch, not sugar.

Pectin extraction and gel formation (one block, [HL])
- [ ] P1 [HL] Pectin is extracted from fruit by simmering the chopped fruit in water until soft; acid (lemon juice) helps release the pectin from the cell walls. Fruit high in pectin: cooking apples and crab apples, citrus fruit, gooseberries, redcurrants and blackcurrants; low in pectin: strawberries. Under-ripe fruit has more.
- [ ] P2 [HL] A gel forms when pectin, sugar and acid are present in the right proportions, and the mixture is boiled and cooled: pectin molecules link in a network that traps the sugar solution (the liquid), so the jam sets. Too little pectin (strawberries) gives a runny jam; add lemon juice or a pectin-rich fruit.
- [ ] P3 [HL] Pectin test (optional): a little cooled pectin extract added to methylated spirits (alcohol) forms a jelly-like clot if much pectin is present.

Investigations (one block; HL where the property is HL)
- [ ] V1 [HL] Caramelisation: heat granulated sugar alone in a dry, heavy-based pan on a low heat and note the colour and smell at intervals (pale, golden, brown); safety: molten sugar causes severe burns. Control: an unheated sample. The current notes already have this block; keep it.
- [ ] V2 Gelatinisation: mix a spoonful of cornflour with cold water (it does not dissolve and does not thicken), then heat while stirring (it thickens). Compare. Variables: temperature.
- [ ] V3 [HL] Dextrinisation: toast white bread or brown flour in a dry pan, or compare a roux cooked for different times; browned starch is darker and thickens less. Safety: hot pan.
- [ ] V4 [HL] Inversion: boil a sucrose solution with a little lemon juice and cool; invert sugar does not crystallise as readily and is sweeter. A Benedict's test shows the invert sugar (reducing sugars give an orange-red colour; sucrose does not).
- [ ] V5 Label each investigation with aim, method, control and result.

## 3. Traps

- Do not use: "sugar denatures egg protein" as the way sugar aids aeration (it is in the 2017 and 2024 schemes), because whisking denatures the protein and sugar stabilises the foam; it does not denature the protein. Teach: whisking traps air in the egg white and the dissolved sugar keeps the foam stable (meringue, sponge cakes).
- Do not use: "ten gradual stages between 104 °C and 177 °C" for caramelisation (2017 scheme), because it is a loose version of the sugar-boiling stages used in sweet making and the temperatures are not a fixed property of the sugar. Describe the colour change and carbonisation only.
- Caramelisation is sugar alone, heated dry; the Maillard reaction needs sugar and amino acids; dextrinisation is starch, not sugar. These three are the most commonly confused browning terms; the notes should place them side by side.
- Gelatinisation is of starch in a liquid with heat (moist heat); dextrinisation is starch with dry heat; inversion is of sucrose with acid or enzyme (hydrolysis).
- A sol is the thickened liquid while hot; a gel is the set form on cooling or in jam. Do not call a thickened sauce a "gel" in the same sentence as sol.
- Hygroscopic ("absorbs water from the air") is not the same as soluble ("dissolves").
- Starch does not dissolve in cold water; it is not sweet. Sugars dissolve and are sweet.
- Digestion detail belonging to other subsections: the stomach, bile, bacteria in the large intestine, absorption and glycogen are in the digestion and metabolism chapter. Do not describe the villi here.
- Enzyme list: only amylase (mouth, pancreas), maltase, sucrase and lactase. Do not name enzymes for protein or fat.
- Do not give optimum enzyme temperatures or pH values; they are not asked.
- The functions of sugar and starch in recipes in full (a list of uses) belongs to the functions subsection; this subsection gives one use per property.
- Sweeteners are in the energy value and fibre targets subsection and the additives chapter, not here.

## 4. What exams ask

- Three properties of sugar, each with a culinary use (Higher Level 6 to 18 marks; 2021, 2024, 2017, 2005, 2010): the accepted answers are in C1. Name the property, say what happens, give one use.
- Caramelisation and crystallisation described (2026 and 2014, 6 marks): caramelisation, sugar melts and browns into caramel with a distinctive flavour (excess gives bitterness); crystallisation, a concentrated sugar solution cools and crystals form, wanted in fudge, unwanted in jam.
- Gelatinisation explained with a culinary example (2023, 2017, 2007): granules swell, burst, absorb liquid, thicken into a sol; sauces, custard, soups.
- Dextrinisation (2009, 2007): dry heat turns starch into brown dextrins; toast, bread crust.
- Hydrolysis and inversion (2012): disaccharide reacts with water to give monosaccharides (in digestion); inversion of sucrose with acid or an enzyme gives invert sugar, used in jam and boiled sweets.
- Starch properties at Ordinary Level: starch is hygroscopic, insoluble in cold water, and gelatinises when heated (2012: a fill-in-the-gap question); effects of heat on starch (2009).
- Digestion enzymes: name the enzyme that converts starch to maltose (salivary amylase; amylase from the pancreas); true or false on starch to maltose in the mouth, no digestion in the stomach, sucrase in the intestinal juice (2004, 2007).
- Properties of carbohydrates useful in food preparation (2005, 6 marks each): name the property, explain it and give a use.
