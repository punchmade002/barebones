## 1. Must cover

Principle of freezing (one block)
- [ ] F1 Freezing preserves by lowering the temperature so far that micro-organisms and enzymes are inactivated (they become dormant and enzyme action is greatly slowed) and by turning free water into ice, which is unavailable to micro-organisms. Freezing does not kill most micro-organisms; they can grow again after thawing, so frozen food is not sterile.
- [ ] F2 The food freezes below 0 °C (dissolved sugars and salts lower the freezing point); water expands as it freezes (about 9 per cent), which is why liquids need headspace.
- [ ] F3 Freezing retains colour, flavour, texture and nutritive value better than most other methods, because no high heat is used (except blanching).

Quick freezing and slow freezing (one block; examined 2022H, 2013H, 2016O, 2018O, 2026O)
- [ ] Q1 Quick (fast) freezing: food is frozen rapidly at about −25 °C (the schemes give −25 °C, and 2013H gives −25 to −40 °C) in the fast-freeze section of a freezer; small ice crystals form inside the cells, so there is little damage to cell walls; texture, colour, flavour and nutritive value are kept; little drip on thawing.
- [ ] Q2 Slow freezing: food freezes slowly at a higher temperature; large ice crystals form, mostly between cells, and pierce and rupture the cell walls; on thawing the food is soft, limp, loses juice (drip) containing soluble vitamins (B1 and C) and minerals, and loses colour and flavour.
- [ ] Q3 One advantage of quick freezing (examined 2022H): less cell damage, so better texture and nutrient retention.
- [ ] Q4 Diagram cue: two cell diagrams side by side, one with small crystals inside an intact cell wall, one with large crystals and a ruptured wall.

Blanching (one block; examined 2011O, 2015H, 2016O, 2011H)
- [ ] B1 Blanching = vegetables plunged into boiling water for a short, set time (depends on the vegetable), then plunged immediately into ice-cold water for the same time, then drained and dried.
- [ ] B2 Purpose: inactivates enzymes that would cause loss of colour, flavour, texture and nutrients (enzymic changes continue slowly even at freezer temperatures); also cleans the vegetable, destroys some surface micro-organisms, softens it slightly so it packs well, and keeps the bright colour.
- [ ] B3 The cooling stops cooking so vegetables do not go soft; drying removes surface water that would form ice crystals.
- [ ] B4 Effect on nutrients: some water-soluble vitamins (vitamin C, B1) are lost into the blanching water and by heat (examined 2011H); blanching small batches in plenty of fast-boiling water keeps the time short.
- [ ] B5 Fruit is not blanched; fruit is frozen with sugar or sugar syrup, or open frozen. Some foods are frozen cooked (soups, sauces, casseroles) after cooling.

Home freezing method (one block; examined 2024O, 2022O, 2022H, 2010O, 2016H, 2009O, 2016O)
- [ ] H1 Choose fresh, good-quality food that is in perfect condition and freeze it soon after harvest or purchase.
- [ ] H2 Prepare: wash, peel, trim or cut as needed; blanch vegetables; cool cooked foods quickly before freezing; portion into usable (family-sized) quantities so that only what is needed is thawed.
- [ ] H3 Open freezing (tray freezing): sticky or delicate items such as strawberries or raspberries, or items to be kept separate (sausages, burgers), are spread on a tray in a single layer, frozen hard, then bagged so that they stay separate.
- [ ] H4 Packaging: strong, moisture-proof and vapour-proof freezer bags, rigid plastic containers or foil; remove as much air as possible and seal tightly; leave headspace in containers holding liquids; label with contents and date.
- [ ] H5 Fast freeze: switch on the fast-freeze setting 3 to 4 hours before adding food; freeze no more than about one-tenth of the freezer's capacity in 24 hours (overloading raises the temperature and slows freezing); place new packs against the cold surfaces so they freeze quickly; keep them apart from already frozen food until frozen.
- [ ] H6 Store at −18 °C or colder; rotate stock (oldest first); storage time depends on the food (fatty foods and oily fish keep for a shorter time).
- [ ] H7 Suitable foods: vegetables such as peas, beans, carrots and broccoli; fruits such as apples and strawberries; meat and fish; bread, cakes and baked goods; soups, sauces and prepared meals. Unsuitable: lettuce, tomatoes whole (salad foods with high water content), bananas, melons, pears, whole eggs in shell, cream with low fat content, mayonnaise-based foods (they go limp, watery or separate).
- [ ] H8 Thawing: thaw in the fridge (or cook from frozen where the food allows, e.g. vegetables); thaw meat and poultry fully before cooking unless the pack says otherwise; use thawed food promptly; do not refreeze food that has thawed (raw food thawed in the fridge and then cooked can be refrozen only as a cooked dish).
- [ ] H9 Freezer burn: dry, discoloured, tough patches where air has reached the surface of the food (dehydration and oxidation), most often with meat and fish; prevented by airtight wrapping with the air removed. Rancidity can develop in fatty foods stored too long.
- [ ] H10 Freezer fault or power cut: keep the door closed; do not open; frozen food keeps for a time. (Assumed; one clause. Do not give hours.)

Commercial freezing methods (one block; examined 2006H, 2016H, 2023H, 2014H)
- [ ] C1 Commercial freezing is faster and colder than home freezing, so crystals are smaller and quality is higher; food is frozen soon after harvesting or catching, often on the farm or on the factory ship.
- [ ] C2 Air blast (blast) freezing: a very cold air stream (about −30 °C) blows over food, often on a conveyor belt or through a tunnel; suitable for most foods: fruit, vegetables, meat, bakery products.
- [ ] C3 Plate freezing: food is pressed between two hollow metal plates cooled by refrigerant; suitable for flat pieces of meat, fish fillets and burgers.
- [ ] C4 Flow (fluidised bed) freezing: cold air is blown up through a perforated belt so that small pieces float and freeze individually, free-flowing; suitable for peas, sweetcorn, berries.
- [ ] C5 Cryogenic freezing: food is exposed to liquid nitrogen (or carbon dioxide) at extremely low temperature; freezing is almost instant; suitable for delicate or high-value foods such as strawberries, prawns and fish.
- [ ] C6 Cold chain: frozen food is held at −18 °C or colder in cold stores, in refrigerated lorries and in supermarket freezer cabinets until it reaches the home; the 2006 scheme pairs each method with a food (plate or flat meat and fish; flow or peas; blast or fruit and vegetables; cryogenic or delicate foods).
- [ ] C7 Commercial food in the freezer: what to check on buying (pack intact, no ice crystals or frost inside, the load line on the cabinet, take home quickly in a cool bag). (Assumed; keep short.)

Effects of freezing on food and benefits in meal planning (one block; examined 2021H, 2016H, 2008H, 2005H)
- [ ] E1 Effect on nutrients: little loss in freezing itself; losses of vitamin C and B1 occur in blanching and in drip on thawing, and are greater when freezing is slow or vegetables are not cooked from frozen; frozen vegetables keep more vitamin C than canned vegetables (2005H data).
- [ ] E2 Effect on colour, flavour and texture: kept close to fresh when quick frozen; fruit and salad foods go soft after thawing because of cell damage.
- [ ] E3 Benefits in meal planning (examined with four points at three marks each, 2016H): prevents waste (surplus and leftovers); bulk cooking and freezing saves time and fuel; convenient and quick (ready meals and portions); emergency stock; extends shelf life; more economical (buy in season or in bulk); provides variety, with foods out of season; safe to eat; frozen foods are available all year; helps with planned menus and with households with different eating times.

## 3. Traps

- Freezing does not kill micro-organisms (the schemes say "inactivated"). Never write that freezing sterilises food or that frozen food is safe indefinitely.
- Quick freezing means small ice crystals; slow freezing means large crystals. The two are reversed in exam traps ("quick freezing results in large ice crystals" is false, 2018O).
- Blanching is for vegetables, not fruit; blanching is to inactivate enzymes, not to cook the vegetable; it also loses some vitamins C and B1.
- Quick freezing at −25 °C is the freezing step; storage is at −18 °C. They are different temperatures; do not mix them.
- Home freezers are not as cold or as fast as commercial methods; do not claim home quick freezing reaches commercial temperatures.
- Commercial refrigeration and cold-storage temperatures of chilled food are in the food safety chapter (food storage); this subsection covers frozen storage only.
- The step-by-step practical write-up for a home preservation account belongs to the practical-application subsection; here teach the reasons for each step.
- The role of micro-organisms and enzymes belongs to the food safety chapter and the principles subsection; give one-line links only.
- Packaging materials and labelling of frozen food belong to the food industry chapter; here name only moisture-proof and vapour-proof wraps.
- Do not use: "slow freezing takes place at about 0 °C to −24 °C", because the range is invented; say slow freezing happens at a higher temperature than −25 °C, as in an ordinary freezer compartment.
- Do not use: "air blast freezing ... taking about 2 to 3 hours" and the "−30 °C to −40 °C" ranges given for each commercial method, because no scheme gives these times or ranges; use "about −30 °C" for blast freezing only.
- Do not use: "freeze the packs against the cold walls or base" as stated for all freezers without qualification, because layout depends on the freezer type (chest, upright, drawer); say "place new packs against the cold surfaces or in the fast-freeze compartment, as the freezer instructs".
- Do not use: "thawed food should be used promptly and not refrozen" as an absolute, because raw food that has been thawed in the fridge can be cooked and then frozen as a cooked dish; say that thawed food is not refrozen as it is.

## 4. What exams ask

- Quick freezing and slow freezing (2022H, 2013H, 2016O, 2018O, 2009O, 2026O): temperature (−25 °C), size of ice crystals, effect on cell walls, effect on texture, colour, flavour and nutrients, and drip loss. Often asked as 3 marks for each.
- Blanching (2011O, 2015H, 2016O): why it is done (inactivates enzymes), how it is done (boiling water then ice-cold water, same time) and the vitamin loss (C and B1, 2011H).
- Guidelines for freezing fresh food (2024O, 6 marks, three guidelines): best-quality food, fast freeze on 3 to 4 hours ahead, no more than one-tenth of capacity, usable portions, cool first, blanch vegetables, airtight packaging, label and date, store at −18 °C.
- Foods that freeze or do not freeze (2016O, 2009O, 2010O): bananas, lettuce, melons and pears do not freeze well.
- Account of freezing vegetables (2016H, 20 marks; 2022H, 18 marks): description of the method (blanch, cool, pack, label, freeze at −25 °C, store at −18 °C), the principle (low temperature, water unavailable, enzymes inactivated) and the effect on the food (colour and flavour kept, some vitamin loss, texture changes if slow frozen). Commercial alternative accepted: air blast freezing on a conveyor belt.
- Commercial methods (2006H, 6 marks): name two with a suitable food each (plate and flow; blast; cryogenic).
- Benefits of frozen foods in meal planning (2016H, 12 marks, four points of three marks; 2021H): waste, time, fuel, emergency stock, shelf life, economy, convenience, variety.
- How loss of vitamin B1 and C occurs when freezing vegetables (2011H): blanching, slow freezing and drip on thawing, not cooking from frozen.
- Tip content: tie every freezing rule to its reason (small crystals, enzyme inactivation, moisture-proof pack, one-tenth capacity); examiners give marks for the reason.
