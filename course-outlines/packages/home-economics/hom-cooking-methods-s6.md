# Package: hom-cooking-methods-s6 — Recipe balance, presentation and dishes for different needs

## 1. Must cover

Recipe balance (one block)
- [ ] B1 A recipe is balanced when the dish or meal supplies the nutrients in sensible proportions for the people eating it, using the Department of Health Healthy Eating Guidelines and the Food Pyramid as the check: a main dish should include a protein food, a starchy food and vegetables or fruit, with little added fat, sugar and salt.
- [ ] B2 Food Pyramid shelves (current Irish pyramid): vegetables, salad and fruit at the base; wholemeal cereals and breads, potatoes, pasta and rice; milk, yogurt and cheese; meat, poultry, fish, eggs, beans and nuts; a small shelf of fats, spreads and oils, to be used sparingly; foods and drinks high in fat, sugar and salt at the top, not needed for health. (Pyramid detail is in the energy and nutrition and meal-planning chapters.)
- [ ] B3 Improving balance: add wholemeal or whole-grain cereals and pulses for fibre; extra vegetables for vitamins and minerals; oily fish; lean meat; low-fat dairy; use less fat, sugar and salt.
- [ ] B4 Balance also covers colour, texture, flavour and cost, so a meal is appealing and affordable.

Reasons for modifying (adapting) a recipe (one block)
- [ ] M1 To make it more nutritious in line with healthy-eating guidelines (e.g. more fibre; less fat, sugar or salt).
- [ ] M2 To cater for people on special diets (e.g. a coeliac).
- [ ] M3 To increase or decrease the number of portions (standard recipes serve about four adults).
- [ ] M4 To add variety to a basic recipe (e.g. nuts in a vegetable stir-fry).
- [ ] M5 To economise by replacing an expensive ingredient with a cheaper one (e.g. margarine for butter).
- [ ] M6 To suit the likes and dislikes of the people eating the dish.

Methods of modifying a recipe (one block)
- [ ] D1 Less fat: grill, bake, steam or poach instead of frying; trim visible fat; use a vegetable oil or low-fat spread; skim fat from stews.
- [ ] D2 Less salt: omit added salt and stock cubes; use herbs, spices, garlic, lemon juice; choose reduced-salt products.
- [ ] D3 Less sugar: reduce the amount; use fruit for natural sweetness; avoid sugary toppings.
- [ ] D4 More fibre: wholemeal flour, brown rice and pasta, oats, pulses, vegetables and fruit with skins on.
- [ ] D5 Scaling a recipe: multiply or divide every ingredient by the same factor, then adjust the tin size, dish size and cooking time (larger quantities take longer) and check seasoning.
- [ ] D6 Adapting flavour and texture: substitute herbs and spices; use a different fruit or vegetable in season; taste and adjust after changes.

Aesthetic awareness in choosing food (one block)
- [ ] A1 Sight: used to judge appearance, colour, size, shape and surface; colour and appearance show freshness and ripeness; colours are linked to flavours (yellow with lemon) and people expect certain appearances (mould is accepted on Stilton but not on bread).
- [ ] A2 Smell: receptor cells in the nasal cavity detect aroma and tell if food is fresh or rancid.
- [ ] A3 Texture: judged by mouth feel and sight; a variety of textures makes a meal interesting (a crisp salad with a soft dish).

Aesthetic awareness in preparing and presenting food (one block)
- [ ] P1 Preparation: cut evenly for neat appearance and even cooking; avoid overcooking, which dulls colour and softens texture (green vegetables kept bright by brief cooking); keep colour with correct cooking methods.
- [ ] P2 Presentation: add colour and contrast by garnishing or decorating (parmesan shavings on pasta, parsley on soup, icing sugar on cakes); the garnish must be edible and suit the dish; serve hot food hot on warm plates and cold food cold on cold plates; vary colour, shape and texture; arrange with height and neatness; clean plate rims; suitable portion size; table setting.
- [ ] P3 The importance of aesthetic awareness: food that looks and smells good is more appealing, eaten with more enjoyment, and helps appetite (especially for children, older people and the ill); dishes are judged on presentation.

Dishes and meals for individuals and family groups (one block)
- [ ] G1 Individuals: single portions, batch cooking and freezing in portions, simple one-pot dishes; avoiding waste; ensure enough nutrients when eating alone, e.g. older person or student.
- [ ] G2 Family groups: meals planned to suit the ages, tastes, activity levels and budget of the family; one-pot, batch and casserole dishes; advance cooking and reheating; involve the family in choices.
- [ ] G3 Life-stage needs in one line each: young children (small, soft pieces, little salt and sugar), teenagers (larger portions, extra iron and calcium), older people (smaller, nutrient-dense portions, softer texture if needed).

Dishes for modified diets (one block)
- [ ] H1 A modified diet changes ingredients and methods but the meal must still be balanced and attractive. For each diet, give the cooking changes (the full dietary rules are in the meal-planning chapter):
  - Coeliac: gluten-free flour and starches (rice, maize) replace wheat, barley and rye in bread, pastry, sauces and coatings; check labels.
  - Diabetic: control the amount of carbohydrate, reduce added sugar, choose wholegrain starches and high-fibre vegetables, use less fat.
  - Low fat or heart-healthy: lean meat, oily fish, low-fat dairy, grilling, steaming, baking.
  - Low salt: no added salt or stock cubes, avoid processed meats; flavour with herbs and spices.
  - Vegetarian and vegan: replace meat with pulses, tofu, eggs (vegetarian only), nuts and seeds; include iron and vitamin B12 sources; plant-based milks; check hidden animal ingredients.
  - Lactose intolerant: lactose-free milk or fortified plant alternatives, check labels.
  - High-fibre: wholemeal flour, pulses, vegetables and fruit.

## 3. Traps

- Dietary rules (what a coeliac, diabetic or vegan person may eat and why) belong to the meal-planning chapter; here cover how a dish is adapted and presented.
- Don't mark any diet [HL] in this subsection; the outline does not.
- Sensory analysis (taste panels, tests) belongs to the food additives and processing chapter; aesthetic awareness is a general reaction, not a test.
- Garnish is for appearance and must be edible; don't suggest inedible decoration.
- Special occasion and table-setting etiquette detail isn't on the syllabus; keep to a sentence.
- Oats can be contaminated with gluten; if mentioned, say oats must be labelled gluten-free for a coeliac. Don't write "oats are safe for coeliacs" without that.
- Don't call margarine automatically healthier than butter; the 2013 scheme gives it only as a cheaper substitute.
- Do not use: servings-per-day recommendations for the Food Pyramid shelves, because the guidance has been revised and no checked source is in this package.
- Do not use: "a main course should always include all pyramid groups" as a rule, because meals vary; say a balanced main meal usually includes a protein food, a starchy food and vegetables.

## 4. What exams ask

- State reasons why recipes may be modified (2013H, 12 marks): three reasons with an example each.
- Discuss the importance of aesthetic awareness in the choice and presentation of food (2013H): sight, smell and texture in choosing; colour, garnish and serving in presenting.
- Adapt a named recipe for someone on a given diet or to raise fibre or reduce fat, sugar or salt, with specific substitutions.
- Plan and present a meal for an individual, family or person with special needs (links to the practical coursework: key points for success and evaluation).
