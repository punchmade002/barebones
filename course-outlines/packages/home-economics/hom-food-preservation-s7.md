## 1. Must cover (Higher Level only)

What irradiation is
- [ ] I1 [HL] Irradiation (the 2008H scheme: a physical treatment) exposes food to a controlled, measured dose of ionising radiation for a set time, usually by passing it on a conveyor past a radiation source in a shielded chamber. (The 2004H scheme: "radioactive rays are passed through food".)
- [ ] I2 [HL] Sources permitted for food: gamma rays from cobalt-60 or caesium-137, X-rays and electron beams (accelerated electrons). The dose is measured in grays (Gy; one gray is one joule of energy absorbed per kilogram) and is controlled.
- [ ] I3 [HL] It is a cold process (the food is not noticeably heated), so it can be applied to food already sealed in its packaging, preventing re-contamination; sometimes called "cold pasteurisation".
- [ ] I4 [HL] Irradiated food does not become radioactive at the doses and sources used for food.

How it works
- [ ] W1 [HL] Ionising radiation damages the DNA and cell structures of micro-organisms, insects and parasites so that they cannot reproduce or they die; it also inactivates some enzymes (ripening enzymes) and stops sprouting by damaging growth tissue.
- [ ] W2 [HL] The dose decides the effect: a low dose delays ripening and sprouting and kills insects; a medium dose destroys most food poisoning bacteria and spoilage organisms and parasites (the "pasteurising" effect); a high dose sterilises. Bacterial spores and some viruses are highly resistant and need very high doses.

Effects on food (2004H asked for two effects)
- [ ] E1 [HL] Destroys food poisoning bacteria (for example Salmonella) and parasites; controls moulds on soft fruit; kills insects in grain, spices and dried foods; prolongs shelf life.
- [ ] E2 [HL] Slows ripening and prevents sprouting of vegetables such as potatoes and onions (the 2004H and 2008H schemes), where the process is permitted.
- [ ] E3 [HL] Little effect on colour, flavour and texture at the doses used (no heat); reduces the need for chemical preservatives (2008H, 2013H).
- [ ] E4 [HL] Disadvantages: vitamins can be destroyed (vitamin C and B group vitamins, 2004H, 2022H); free radicals and reactive molecules form in the food (2004H); fats can become rancid, so it is unsuitable for high-fat foods (2013H, 2022H); some microbes need very high doses (2013H, 2022H).

Evaluation: advantages and disadvantages (2013H 15 marks, 2022H 12 marks)
- [ ] A1 [HL] Advantages: destroys poisoning bacteria and parasites; longer shelf life; slows ripening and sprouting; little effect on colour, flavour and texture; no chemical residues and less need for chemical preservatives; can treat packed food; no cooking or heating of heat-sensitive foods such as herbs and spices.
- [ ] A2 [HL] Disadvantages: not suitable for high-fat foods because of rancidity; vitamin losses; high radiation needed to destroy some microbes (spores, viruses) so not a substitute for good hygiene; public concern and consumer reluctance; may be used to disguise poor manufacturing or poor hygiene (2013H); expensive plant and strict safety controls and licensing for the facility.
- [ ] A3 [HL] A balanced conclusion: useful for dried herbs and spices; not a replacement for good hygiene.

Regulation and labelling (Irish and EU position)
- [ ] R1 [HL] In the EU, the use of irradiation is controlled by a framework Directive 1999/2/EC (rules, labelling, authorisation, imports) and an implementing Directive 1999/3/EC that lists the foods that may be irradiated. At present the only category on the EU list is dried aromatic herbs, spices and vegetable seasonings, with a maximum dose of 10 kilograys (kGy), according to the FSAI.
- [ ] R2 [HL] Irradiated food, or food containing irradiated ingredients, must be labelled with the words "irradiated" or "treated with ionising radiation" (Regulation (EU) No 1169/2011, Annex VI). The Radura (a stylised plant in a circle) is the international symbol of irradiated food (2008H scheme); in the EU it may accompany the words but does not replace them.
- [ ] R3 [HL] The FSAI checks food on the Irish market for undeclared irradiation (through official agencies under service contract); the FSAI reports that no facility in Ireland has been approved for irradiating food. Any irradiated ingredient on sale here would therefore have been treated elsewhere. Food labelling and the irradiation checks appear as "irradiated food" in label questions (2005H, 2012H, 2013H, 2014O, 2018H schemes list "irradiated food" among label information).
- [ ] R4 [HL] Consumers can see on the label whether food (for example a herb or spice product, or an ingredient) was treated.

## 3. Traps

- Irradiated food is not radioactive and the process is not "heating". Do not say the food is "exposed to radioactive material".
- The marking schemes (2004H, 2008H, 2022H) say irradiation is used to prevent sprouting in potatoes and onions and to delay ripening in fruit. That is true of the process worldwide, but in the EU only dried aromatic herbs, spices and vegetable seasonings are on the permitted list. Do not write that irradiated potatoes, onions or fruit are sold in Ireland; present sprouting and ripening as effects of the process.
- The Radura is the symbol; the legal requirement is the words. Do not say the symbol is compulsory.
- Irradiation is not a substitute for good hygiene; it does not remove toxins already formed and does not destroy all spores or viruses.
- The "dose" is the energy absorbed (grays), not the number of rays.
- Do not confuse with microwave cooking or with cold pasteurisation by high pressure; irradiation uses ionising radiation (gamma, X-ray, electron beam).
- Food legislation in general (Regulation (EC) No 178/2002, Regulation (EU) No 1169/2011 in full) belongs to the food safety chapter; give only the irradiation labelling rule here.
- Food labelling as a topic (all required label information) belongs to the food industry chapter (labelling).
- Comparisons of irradiation with canning or freezing belong to the comparative-evaluation subsection; give one-line links only.
- Do not use: "Irradiated food sold in the EU must be labelled ... display the Radura symbol" as a requirement, because EU law requires the words, not the symbol.
- Do not use: "Two vegetables irradiated to prevent sprouting: potatoes and onions" as a statement of current practice in Ireland or the EU, because only herbs and spices are authorised; rewrite as "irradiation can prevent sprouting in potatoes and onions".
- Do not use: "In the EU, irradiation is permitted only for specified foods" without naming them, because the list is one category, dried aromatic herbs, spices and vegetable seasonings; name it.
- Do not use: "Irradiated food sold in Ireland follows the same EU rules" implying Irish production; there is no approved facility in Ireland.

## 4. What exams ask

- What irradiated food is (2004H, 2 marks): exposed to ionising radiation as a preservation method; and two effects (4 marks): destroys bacteria and parasites; prolongs shelf life; stops sprouting or slows ripening; vitamins can be lost; free radicals form.
- Informative note on food irradiation (2008H, 15 marks): a physical treatment with a defined dose of ionising radiation; destroys poisoning bacteria, controls mould on soft fruit, delays ripening, prevents sprouting; little effect on colour, flavour and texture; reduces the need for chemical preservatives; vitamins may be destroyed; irradiated food must be labelled and the Radura is the international symbol.
- Assess or evaluate irradiation (2013H, 15 marks; 2022H, 12 marks): three or more points on each side, as in A1 and A2.
- Label questions list "irradiated" among information a label must give (2005H, 2012H, 2013H, 2014O, 2018H).
- Tip content: give advantages and disadvantages with a reason each (cold process keeps colour and flavour; fats go rancid; some microbes need high doses; public concern).
