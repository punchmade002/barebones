# Package: hom-family-law-s2 — The Family Home Protection Act 1976

- **loId:** `hom-family-law-s2` · **chapterId:** `hom-family-law` · **syllabusRef:** 3.1.7
- **Syllabus scope:** "The Family Home Protection Act 1976" [3.1.7]. Common to Ordinary and Higher Level: no [HL] mark.
- **Also examined:** 2004H (state how the family is protected by the Act, 6 marks, 2 points at 3 marks each). It also appears as one named Act in broader "how does family law protect members when a marriage breaks down" questions (12 marks) and in "rights and responsibilities of a couple in marriage" answers.
- **Prepared by:** a higher model, 2026-11-23. The Act is still in force (as amended, notably by the Family Law Act 1995) and keeps its syllabus name (outline, Appendix A). Sources checked: irishstatutebook.ie (Family Home Protection Act 1976, sections 3 and 4) and Citizens Information. What happens to the home on judicial separation or divorce belongs to the notes on those Acts; here it is one line only.

## 1. Must cover

The family home and the core rule
- [ ] A1 The Act protects the family home: the dwelling in which a married couple ordinarily live together as their home. It protects the family, especially the spouse who does not own the home and any dependent children, from losing their home because one spouse deals with it alone.
- [ ] A2 The core rule: a spouse cannot sell, lease or mortgage the family home without the written consent of the other spouse. This is the answer to the 2004H question and the first point in every exam answer. Give all three verbs.
- [ ] A3 This applies whoever owns the home: whose name is on the deeds makes no difference. A spouse who does not own the home is still protected.
- [ ] A4 The consent must be given before the deal ("prior consent") and in writing. An oral agreement is not enough. A general written consent given in advance can count for later deals.
- [ ] A5 If the required consent is missing, the sale, lease or mortgage is void and has no legal effect, but under s.3(8)(b) it is treated as not void unless a court declares it void or the parties state in writing within six years that it is void. There are limited exceptions (for example a purchaser who paid full value and did not know it was the family home); one clause is enough.

When the court can step in
- [ ] C1 If a spouse refuses consent, the other spouse can apply to the court to dispense with (do without) the consent. The court does so only where it is unreasonable to withhold it, taking all circumstances into account, including the needs of any dependent children.
- [ ] C2 The court can also dispense with or give the consent where the spouse cannot be found after reasonable enquiries, lacks the mental capacity to consent, or has deserted.

Why it matters and links
- [ ] L1 Why the law exists: without it, a spouse who owned the home could sell or mortgage it without the other knowing and leave the family homeless. The Act gives the family, and the children, security of a home.
- [ ] L2 Link to housing finance: a lender taking a mortgage over a family home will want the other spouse's written consent before it lends. One sentence, as a practical effect (assumed link with the mortgage notes).
- [ ] L3 Scope: the Act protects married couples. It does not apply to unmarried cohabiting couples (assumed: students should know protection depends on marriage).
- [ ] L4 On judicial separation or divorce the court can make orders about who keeps the family home as part of its wider powers (see the notes on those Acts). One line only.
- [ ] L5 Exam use: two points at 3 marks each: (i) one spouse cannot sell, lease or mortgage the family home without the other's consent; (ii) the consent must be in writing (and applies whoever owns the home).
- [ ] L6 Diagram: none.

## 3. Traps

- Do not use: "the Act protects rented homes", because whether a tenancy of the family home is protected could not be settled (s.3(6) treats lessees as purchasers); leave it out unless confirmed. Do not say the Act does not apply to rented homes.
- Do not use: "a spouse's right of occupation can be registered as a burden on the title", because the Act gives no right of occupation; what a spouse can register (s.12) is a notice of the marriage against the property, which warns buyers and lenders that consent is needed.
- Do not use: the Act "protects essential services (such as electricity or heating) from being cut off", because marking-scheme summaries mention it but it could not be confirmed.
- Do not use: "the non-consenting spouse can ask the court to set the sale aside" as the route, because a dealing without prior written consent is void by the Act itself; the court's role is to dispense with consent (C1). The earlier notes' step 4 should say the dealing is void.
- Do not use: "right of occupation" as a technical term, because the Act is expressed as a consent rule. Say "protection against the home being sold, leased or mortgaged without agreement".
- The Act does not give the non-owning spouse ownership of the home. It gives a veto over dealings with it.
- Consent must be written and prior, not oral and not given afterwards.
- Neighbouring subjects: property orders on separation or divorce belong to the notes on those Acts; mortgage conditions belong to the housing finance notes; maintenance (income) belongs to the previous subsection.
- Marking schemes sometimes say only "cannot sell without consent"; the full list is sell, lease or mortgage.

## 4. What exams ask

- State how the Act protects the family (two points): no sale, lease or mortgage without the other spouse's consent; consent in writing. Add regardless of who owns the home, and that the family has security of a home.
- As one of several Acts in a marriage-breakdown question (12 marks): name the Act with one sentence: one spouse cannot sell or remortgage the family home alone.
- Rights and responsibilities of a couple: the home is protected by this Act.
- Expected depth: short and exact; examiners reward the three verbs (sell, lease, mortgage) and "written".
