This subsection takes society, culture, norms, mores and values. Role and status belong to the subsection on role and status; socialisation belongs to the one on groups and socialisation; the family, school, religion and State as institutions belong to the one on social institutions. ## 1. Must cover

Society (one block)
- [ ] S1 A society is a group of people who live in a defined territory and share a way of life, a set of institutions and a sense of belonging. Social life is organised, not random: people in a society depend on one another and follow common rules.
- [ ] S2 The term applies at different scales: Irish society, a small rural community, Western society. (assumed)
- [ ] S3 Sociology is the study of how people live together in groups and societies. One sentence only, as the lead to the chapter. (assumed)

Culture (one block)
- [ ] C1 Culture is the whole way of life of a society, passed from one generation to the next: language, customs, beliefs, values, norms, mores, music, art, dress, food, religion, technology and objects. This is the form examiners reward: "the way of life of a society, passed on from generation to generation", with an example such as Irish culture.
- [ ] C2 Culture is learned, not inherited biologically; it is passed on through socialisation (named only; the process is taught in the subsection on groups and socialisation).
- [ ] C3 Material culture (physical objects: clothing, tools, buildings) and non-material culture (ideas: language, beliefs, values, norms, customs, rituals). Optional extra; one line.
- [ ] C4 Cultures differ between societies and a culture changes over time. Examples of variation: language, dress, food, religious practice, wedding customs, attitudes to age and gender roles.
- [ ] C5 Subculture: a smaller group within a society with some distinct values, norms or styles of its own (a youth music scene, a sporting club, a religious minority) while still sharing the main culture. Optional extra.
- [ ] C6 Ethnocentrism (judging other cultures by the standards of your own) and cultural relativism (understanding a culture on its own terms). Optional extras, one line each; not examined in the sample years.

Norms and mores (one block)
- [ ] N1 A norm is an accepted, expected way of behaving in a given situation; it is a rule, mostly unwritten. Marking-scheme example: forming a queue. Others: greeting people, keeping quiet in a library, turning up on time, dressing suitably for an interview or a funeral.
- [ ] N2 Norms vary between cultures, between social settings and over time (what is acceptable at a match is not acceptable in church; what was normal fifty years ago may not be now).
- [ ] N3 Mores (pronounced "MOR-ays"): norms with strong moral weight, whose breach is seen as seriously wrong, such as the ban on theft, violence or disrespect to the dead. Breaking a more brings serious disapproval or punishment; breaking an everyday convention brings mild disapproval. Marking schemes list "norms, mores, customs" together as parts of culture, so mores must be defined in a clause.
- [ ] N4 Customs (folkways): everyday conventions and traditional ways of doing things (queueing, shaking hands, a birthday custom, a wedding custom). Breaking them is rude, not wrong. Helps separate norm from more.
- [ ] N5 Laws: norms written down and enforced by the State. Some mores become laws (theft, assault); many norms are not laws (queueing).
- [ ] N6 Sanctions: how a society enforces norms. Positive sanctions reward conformity (praise, a prize, promotion); negative sanctions punish breaking norms (a frown, ridicule, being left out, a fine, prison). Informal sanctions come from family, friends and neighbours; formal sanctions come from the courts, schools and employers.
- [ ] N7 Conformity (following norms) and deviance (breaking them). What counts as deviant depends on the society and the time.

Values (one block)
- [ ] V1 Values are the beliefs and attitudes a society holds about what is right and wrong, good and bad, important and worth striving for.
- [ ] V2 Values are general and long-lasting; norms are the specific rules that carry values into everyday behaviour. A value of respect for life lies behind the norm against violence; a value of honesty lies behind the norm of not cheating in exams.
- [ ] V3 Examples of values commonly given in Irish and Western societies: family, education, honesty, fairness and equality, respect for others, religious belief, freedom, community, care for the vulnerable. Offer them as examples of values, not as a claim about every person.
- [ ] V4 Values are learned from the family first, then from school, peers, religion and the media, and differ between families, groups and generations. Values change over time (attitudes to marriage, gender equality, diversity) and the changes feed through into new norms and laws; the causes of change are taught in the subsection on social change.
- [ ] V5 Values can conflict (personal freedom against duty to family; career against family time), and individuals and societies must balance them.

How the terms link (one block)
- [ ] L1 Culture is the umbrella; values, norms, mores, customs, language, beliefs and symbols are its parts. Values are the broad beliefs; norms and mores are the rules that put them into practice; sanctions enforce them; laws are the formal version. A chain of value, then norm, then sanction is fine as three bullets. No diagram cue.

Answering "explain the terms" questions (one block)
- [ ] Q1 Give each term its own line or short paragraph: a definition, then an example. A 3-mark term answer is one definition point, one example or extension point, and, where the terms are related, a link ("norms are the rules that follow from values").
- [ ] Q2 Use the wording the schemes credit: "way of life of a society" for culture, "accepted way of behaving" for norms, "beliefs about what is right and wrong" for values.

## 3. Traps

- Norm versus value: a value is a belief about what matters; a norm is the specific behaviour rule. "Respect for elders" is a value; "stand up when an older person enters the room" is a norm. Students often define both the same way.
- Norm versus more versus law: mores are norms with strong moral weight; laws are written and enforced by the State. Do not say "mores are laws". Some mores are also laws; some (serious social disapproval of lying) are not.
- Culture is not only "the arts": it is the whole way of life. A mark is lost for "culture is music and art".
- Culture is not the same as society: society is the people; culture is their way of life.
- "Irish culture" is not one fixed thing: Ireland has subcultures and growing diversity. Do not write that all Irish people share one language, religion or custom.
- Do not use: the claim that all members of Irish society share the Irish language as a common symbol, because most people speak English as their first language; use the Irish language only as an example of a cultural symbol.
- Do not use: a list of "Irish values" presented as measured fact or a ranking of what Irish people value, because it is an unsourced generalisation; present family, education, honesty and equality only as examples of values.
- Do not name theories (functionalism, symbolic interactionism) here; one reference to functionalism belongs to the subsection on social institutions.
- Belongs to a neighbouring subsection: role, status and social class (role and status); primary and secondary groups, kinship, marriage forms and the process of socialisation (groups and socialisation); the family, school, religion and State as institutions (social institutions); how and why values and norms change (social change); family structures and functions (the chapter on the family in society).

## 4. What exams ask

- "Explain the following terms" with a set of four or five (culture, norms, values and role in 2013H; socialisation, socio-economic group, social mobility, social change and kinship in 2019H). For this subsection the expected content is: culture as the way of life of a society passed down the generations (language, customs, values, norms); norms as accepted ways of behaving with an example such as queueing; values as beliefs about right and wrong. Each term earns about 3 marks, so the exam tip is one definition plus one example per term, with no essay.
- Terms inside a larger family question, such as how the family transmits values and norms, or how culture shapes marriage customs. The expected answer links the terms: the family passes on values, which show up as norms.
- Short-answer questions: "give one example of a norm", "state what is meant by a value". Prepare one example each of a norm, a more, a custom, a value and a sanction.
- Examiners reward Irish or everyday examples and give little for a bare definition with no example.
- Cultural variation between societies is used in questions on marriage customs and family structures in other societies.
