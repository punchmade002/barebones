# Package: hom-dairy-eggs-s6 — Eggs: selection, storage, properties and culinary uses

## 1. Must cover

Selecting eggs and reading the label (one block)
- [ ] L1 Choose eggs from a clean shop with clean, uncracked shells, within the best-before date; a cracked shell lets bacteria in. Eggs sold as Class A are not washed.
- [ ] L2 Class and size: shop eggs are Class A; sizes are small, medium, large (and extra large), by weight.
- [ ] L3 The pack (carton) shows: the producer or packing centre's name, address and registration number; class and size; the number of eggs; the week number (the week of packing); the best-before date; storage instructions; the country of origin; and the farming method (organic, free-range, barn, cages).
- [ ] L4 The egg stamp: each Class A egg is stamped with a producer code. The first digit shows the method of production (0 organic, 1 free-range, 2 barn, 3 caged), then IE for Ireland, then a code identifying the producer and county, so the egg is traceable to the farm. Many Irish eggs also carry the Bord Bia quality-assurance mark and a best-before date.
- [ ] L5 Best-before date: no more than 28 days after the egg was laid (FSAI guidance). Use eggs by the date.
- [ ] L6 Farming methods: organic (hens with outdoor access, organic feed with no artificial pesticides or fertilisers, no routine antibiotic or growth-hormone use); free-range (hens have daytime access to the open air); barn (hens roam indoors in a house); caged (enriched cages). Differences in animal welfare and price; nutrition is much the same, though free-range eggs may contain more of some vitamins (B12, niacin).
- [ ] L7 Role of labelling for the consumer (2021H, 2011O, 2016O): information on origin and traceability; farming method to match values and budget; freshness from date; quality assurance marks; storage and use; the label must be clear, legible, indelible and not misleading (Regulation (EU) No 1169/2011 as given effect by the European Union (Provision of Food Information to Consumers) Regulations 2014, with the EU marketing standards for eggs).

Quality assurance in egg production (one block; 2015H)
- [ ] Q1 Ireland has an EU-approved national salmonella control plan for laying hens, to keep the national flock healthy and the eggs safe.
- [ ] Q2 Incoming young hens (pullets) must be certified salmonella free; laying flocks are tested regularly (monthly); feed is heat treated; full traceability of every egg through management systems; the best-before date, house code and the Bord Bia quality-assurance logo appear on the eggs.
- [ ] Q3 All producers and suppliers are inspected and approved, and every system is independently inspected before the Bord Bia quality mark is awarded.
- [ ] Q4 Why it matters: Salmonella can be in a hen's gut and, rarely, in the egg itself; control at farm level lowers the risk of food poisoning.

Storing eggs (one block; 2016O)
- [ ] ST1 Store in the fridge at about 4 °C, in the egg compartment (the door is warmer and shaken), with the pointed end down so the yolk stays centred and the air cell stays at the top; keep away from strongly flavoured foods because the shell is porous; do not wash eggs, because washing removes the protective cuticle and speeds staling; use within the best-before date; keep away from cooked foods.
- [ ] ST2 Leftover egg white can be kept in a sealed container in the fridge for a few days (the 2016O scheme says 2 to 4 days); leftover yolks can be kept covered in a little water in the fridge for a few days (the scheme says up to 4).
- [ ] ST3 Hygiene: wash hands after handling raw eggs; do not use cracked or dirty eggs for foods that are not cooked; cook eggs until firm for vulnerable groups; raw egg dishes (mayonnaise, mousse) carry risk.
- [ ] ST4 Freshness test: a fresh egg sinks and lies flat in water because the air cell is small; an old egg tilts or floats; a fresh egg has a thick white and a high, round yolk.

Properties: coagulation (one block)
- [ ] CO1 Definition: egg protein sets (changes from liquid to solid) when heated. The white coagulates at about 60 to 65 °C and the yolk at about 65 to 70 °C (2015H scheme).
- [ ] CO2 Explanation: heat makes the protein chains untwist (denature) and straighten, and they bond to each other, trapping small pockets of water so the egg sets. Overheating makes the protein clump tighter and squeeze out its water, causing curdling and a tough, rubbery texture.
- [ ] CO3 Culinary uses: cooking as a dish (boiled, poached, scrambled, fried, baked eggs, omelette); thickening (custard, quiche, sauces); binding (burgers, fishcakes, meatballs, stuffings); coating (egg and breadcrumbs on fish, chicken goujons); glazing (scones, pastry, bread: egg wash gives gloss and colour); clarifying (egg white traps solid particles in consommé); enriching (rice pudding, mashed potato).
- [ ] CO4 Gentle, low heat and stirring give a creamy custard; a high heat curdles it. Adding egg to a hot liquid slowly prevents lumps.

Properties: aeration, foam formation (one block)
- [ ] AE1 Whisking or beating egg (white especially) traps air as many small bubbles. The protein partly unfolds (denatures) and forms a thin film around each bubble, giving a foam.
- [ ] AE2 The foam is temporary; it is set permanently by heat (the protein coagulates in the oven) or stabilised by sugar or by a setting agent such as gelatine; do not leave it standing, or it collapses.
- [ ] AE3 Culinary uses: whole eggs and sugar whisked for a whisked sponge and Swiss roll; whisked whites for meringues, soufflés, mousses, pavlova and lemon meringue topping; egg and fat creamed in cake mixtures.
- [ ] AE4 Tips: a clean, grease-free bowl (fat or yolk stops the foam), a whisk, whites at room temperature, add sugar gradually.

Properties: emulsification (one block)
- [ ] EM1 An emulsion is a permanent mixture of two liquids that do not normally mix (oil and water or vinegar), with one broken into tiny droplets spread through the other.
- [ ] EM2 Egg yolk contains lecithin, a natural emulsifier. The lecithin molecule has a hydrophilic (water-loving) head that attaches to the water and a hydrophobic (water-hating, oil-loving) tail that attaches to the oil; this holds the two together and stops them separating.
- [ ] EM3 Culinary uses: mayonnaise (oil and vinegar), hollandaise and similar sauces (butter and lemon juice), salad dressings, blending the fat and sugar in a cake mixture so the batter does not curdle.
- [ ] EM4 Making an emulsion: add the oil drop by drop to the yolk while whisking; too fast and it curdles.
- [ ] EM5 Organic eggs and lecithin (2019H): organic = from hens not treated with growth hormones or antibiotics and fed on feed grown without artificial pesticides and fertilisers; lecithin = a natural emulsifier in the yolk that joins liquids that normally separate.

Effects of heat on eggs (one block; 2025O, 2021O, 2016O)
- [ ] EH1 The protein coagulates (sets); the white changes from transparent to opaque white and becomes insoluble.
- [ ] EH2 Too much heat or time curdles the protein, makes the egg tough, rubbery and harder to digest.
- [ ] EH3 Heat kills bacteria, including Salmonella (thoroughly cooked eggs are safer).
- [ ] EH4 Some loss of B vitamins; most other nutrients little changed.
- [ ] EH5 A green-grey ring forms around the yolk of an overcooked hard-boiled egg, from a reaction between the sulphur in the white and the iron in the yolk; cooling the egg quickly in cold water reduces it.
- [ ] EH6 Different cooking methods and times give soft, medium and hard results; digestibility is best with light cooking.

Ways to include eggs in the diet (assumed link; 2021O, 2011O)
- [ ] U1 Boiled, poached, scrambled, fried, omelettes, frittata, quiche, French toast, pancakes, egg salad, sandwiches, mayonnaise and sauces, baking and desserts, garnishes (chopped egg). Name three uses of eggs with an example of each (dish, enriching, binding, coating, thickening, aerating, emulsifying, glazing, clarifying).

## 3. Traps

- Do not use: "keep eggs in their carton" or "remove eggs from the carton" as a rule, because the 2016O scheme says remove while consumer guidance generally says keep, and neither was confirmed; say only "store pointed end down in the fridge egg compartment, away from strong-smelling foods".
- Do not use: "use-by date" for eggs, although the 2021H scheme says so, because EU rules require a best-before date on Class A eggs (28 days from laying, FSAI); say "best-before".
- Do not use: Regulation (EC) No 589/2008 for the egg marketing rules (for example "Regulation 589/2008"), because it has been replaced by Commission Delegated Regulation (EU) 2023/2465 (FSAI cites it); say "EU marketing standards for eggs (Delegated Regulation (EU) 2023/2465)" and Regulation (EU) No 1169/2011 for general label rules.
- Do not use: a 21-day limit for shops to sell eggs after laying, because it comes from the old Regulation 589/2008 and could not be confirmed under Delegated Regulation (EU) 2023/2465; say only the 28-day best-before limit.
- Do not use: weights in grams for the egg size classes, or a count of days a leftover white or yolk keeps beyond the scheme's "2 to 4" and "up to 4", because they differ by source; write "a few days".
- Do not use: a producer code example with digits, because the county and producer numbers are not checked; describe the code's parts only.
- Coagulation is the setting of the protein; aeration is trapping air; emulsification is the lecithin holding oil and water. An egg used to glaze is coagulation, not aeration.
- Overheating coagulated egg protein causes curdling and toughness; do not say the protein "burns".
- Raw egg white does not whisk if fat or yolk is present.
- The green ring forms from iron and sulphur, not from overcooking the white alone.
- Free-range or organic eggs are not different in nutritive value except possibly some vitamins; do not claim they are safer without quality assurance.
- Egg structure (shell, air cell, albumen, yolk) and the nutrients belong to the previous subsection; salmonella as a food poisoning organism belongs to the food safety chapter.

## 4. What exams ask

- "Properties of eggs and culinary uses" (Higher 2015H, 2009H): name the property, explain it (coagulation temperatures, protein denaturing and bonding around water, curdling when overheated; aeration: trapped air, protein around bubbles, temporary foam; emulsification: lecithin with a water-loving head and oil-loving tail), and give a culinary use for each (custard, quiche, burgers, glazing; meringue, sponge, soufflé; mayonnaise, hollandaise).
- "Describe how eggs act as an emulsifier, with one application" (2021H, 18 marks): lecithin structure, immiscible liquids held in a permanent emulsion, mayonnaise or hollandaise made step by step.
- Label questions (2021H "evaluate the role of labelling when buying eggs"; 2011O, 2016O two items on a carton): origin, producer or packer, class, size, week number, date, farming method, storage, egg stamp for traceability, Bord Bia mark; label clear and not misleading.
- Quality assurance (2015H, 10 marks): five or six points on the salmonella control plan, certified hens, monthly testing, heat-treated feed, traceability, inspection, Bord Bia mark.
- Storing eggs (2016O, 10 marks): fridge at 4 °C, egg compartment, pointed end down, away from strong flavours, do not wash, use by date, leftover white and yolk storage.
- Effects of heat on eggs (2025O, 2021O, 2016O): coagulation, curdling and toughness, colour change, loss of B vitamin, bacteria killed, green ring, white becoming insoluble.
- Three uses of eggs with an example each (2011O, 2021O): dish (scrambled), enriching, binding, coating, thickening, aerating, emulsifying, glazing, clarifying.
- Organic and lecithin (2019H, 6 marks): organic eggs from hens not given growth hormones or antibiotics and fed pesticide-free feed; lecithin is a natural emulsifier in yolk.
