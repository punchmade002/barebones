# Package: hom-clothing-selection-s6 — The clothing and textile industries in Ireland

- **loId:** `hom-clothing-selection-s6` · **chapterId:** `hom-clothing-selection` · **syllabusRef:** 5.4
- **Syllabus scope:** an overview of the structure of the industries; the role of small businesses and cottage industries; career opportunities in clothing, textiles and related industries. Settings: a visit to a factory or craft worker. Also examined: Irish fashion designers (2024H, 2019H, 2016H, 2014H, also 2023O, 2017O, 2012O); challenges facing the industry (2014H, 2018O, 2007H); traditional crafts (2022H, 2013H). No [HL] items marked.
- **Prepared by:** a higher model, 2026-11-23, with web checks (see section 3). Spellings and details of designers are limited to what the schemes and sources support. Support bodies and organisations are given by their current names.

## 1. Must cover

Structure of the industries (one block)
- [ ] ST1 Parts of the industry: designers (design ranges), manufacturers (clothing and textile), distributors, and retailers (shops, department stores, online).
- [ ] ST2 What is made: menswear, womenswear, children's wear, knitwear, sportswear, accessories, lingerie, footwear (2023O scheme).
- [ ] ST3 Industry provides employment in manufacture, distribution and sales, and earns export income; the sector is market-driven and depends on skill, imagination and creativity.
- [ ] ST4 Irish designers sell under their own label, produce ranges for high street stores, and export.
- [ ] ST5 Support: Local Enterprise Offices (county/city enterprise support) help small firms and designers with advice, training and grants.

Small businesses and cottage industries (one block, 2014H)
- [ ] SB1 Cottage industry: work done in or near the home, often by hand, selling to craft shops or customers directly; small firms in rural areas.
- [ ] SB2 Roles: provide employment in rural areas and keep people in their communities; work can be fitted around family life; low start-up cost; some work subcontracted to local people at home (e.g. Aran knitting); keep traditional skills alive; produce distinctive products; can grow into larger businesses; hobbies can become businesses, especially when the economy is poor.
- [ ] SB3 Weaknesses: small scale, limited marketing and finance, income can be low.
- [ ] SB4 Growing popularity of textile craft work (2013H): socialising in knitting and stitching clubs, learning traditional skills, saving money, more free time, start-up businesses, desire for original items, and social influence of celebrities.

Traditional crafts and Irish design (one block, 2022H, 2013H)
- [ ] TC1 Hand-knitting and hand-weaving inspire coats and coatigans with Aran stitches.
- [ ] TC2 Irish linen (woven from flax) is used from light tops and skirts to heavy coats and jackets.
- [ ] TC3 Lace and crochet appear in wedding dresses and occasion wear.
- [ ] TC4 Tweed, Aran sweaters and handweaving are recognised Irish products that link design to Irish heritage; a source of identity for marketing abroad.
- [ ] TC5 John Rocha was examined for using Irish fabrics to create unique designs with Celtic influence (2017O scheme).

Irish fashion designers (one block, examined Ordinary and Higher most years)
- [ ] DS1 Be able to name one Irish designer or milliner and give two or three points about their work. Names in schemes: John Rocha, Simone Rocha, Philip Treacy, Lainey Keogh, Paul Costelloe, Louise Kennedy, Carolyn Donnelly, Orla Kiely, Sybil Connolly (also Peter O'Brien, Joanne Hynes, Richard Malone, Emma Manley, Natalie B. Coleman, milliner Aisling Ahern (as printed) in the 2016H scheme).
- [ ] DS2 Philip Treacy: milliner from Ahascragh, County Galway; studied fashion in Dublin (NCAD), then millinery at the Royal College of Art in London; international career creating hats for couture and for royalty and celebrity.
- [ ] DS3 Work of a designer: clothing and accessories for royalty, celebrities, world leaders and major events; John Rocha uses Irish fabrics and Celtic influence; Louise Kennedy designed for a previous president and is known for detail; Lainey Keogh is known for knitwear.
- [ ] DS4 Contribution of designers: create employment (manufacture, distribution, sales); export menswear, womenswear, children's wear, knitwear, accessories and more; promote Irish design at home and abroad (2023O).

Promoting Irish fashion (one block, 2017H, 2013H)
- [ ] PM1 Support from Local Enterprise Offices; shops and design centres that sell Irish design (Kilkenny Design Centre is named in schemes); designers producing ranges for high street stores; showing at fashion weeks and international fashion shows; fashion blogs and social media; press coverage and public figures wearing Irish designers.

Challenges facing the industry (one block, 2014H, 2018O, 2007H)
- [ ] CH1 Competition from imports made in low-wage countries; cheaper goods and discount retailers.
- [ ] CH2 Labour-intensive industry; high labour costs for skilled workers.
- [ ] CH3 Global recession and less disposable income; small home market so growth needs export.
- [ ] CH4 Adapting to new shopping patterns (online and mobile); keeping up with trends and technology.
- [ ] CH5 Shortage of skilled workers; limited funds for small firms.

Careers (one block, 2023H, 2014O, 2017H)
- [ ] CR1 Fashion designer, tailor, milliner, machinist, clothing manufacturer, fashion buyer, fashion journalist or writer, stylist, photographer, model, advertiser, blogger, retail sales assistant, factory worker.
- [ ] CR2 For one career give the name and two details: what the person does, skills or qualities needed, where they work, training route (assumed).

## 3. Traps

- Do not use: "Enterprise Boards" as a current body, because the County and City Enterprise Boards were dissolved in April 2014 and replaced by Local Enterprise Offices; schemes up to 2017 still say Enterprise Boards. Name the current body.
- Do not use: "the design centre at Powerscourt Townhouse, Dublin" as a present-day outlet, because a search found only older descriptions and no confirmation that it still operates in that form.
- Do not use: "Council for Irish Fashion Designers" as a current body, because its present status could not be confirmed.
- Do not use: "Avoca Handweavers" or other businesses as facts about outlets or numbers, because those details change and were not confirmed.
- Do not use: "Riverdance" costume claims or any named garment for a named event, because the scheme extract is cut off and no designer-event pair was confirmed.
- Do not use: "China" as the named low-cost competitor, because the schemes' wording is dated and general "countries with cheaper labour" is the point.
- Do not use: "Orla Kiely is an Irish designer based in Ireland": she was born in Dublin but works largely in London; if named, say Irish-born.
- Do not use: "Philip Treacy" training dates (graduating years) because sources vary; say only the places.
- Challenges: do not mix with causes of changes in fashion (influences subsection).
- Fabric profiles (linen, wool) belong to the fibres and fabrics material; here only their role in Irish craft and design.
- Textile finishes and design application belong to the fibres and fabrics material.
- Careers: name and give details of one; do not list without detail for a 6-mark answer.

## 4. What exams ask

- Name one Irish fashion designer and outline their work or contribution (2024H, 2019H, 2016H, 2014H, 2023O, 2017O, 2012O): name (2-3 marks) plus clothing and accessories for royalty, celebrities, world leaders, events; employment; export; promoting Irish design; typical points for John Rocha (Irish fabrics, Celtic designs) and Philip Treacy (millinery, international clients).
- Describe the contribution of Irish designers to the industry (2023O): employment in manufacture, distribution and sales; export of the range of products; promotion of Irish design.
- Discuss challenges (2014H, 2018O, 2007H): imports and cheap labour abroad, recession and reduced spending, high labour costs, adapting to online and mobile shopping, small home market, skill shortages.
- Outline how Irish fashion is promoted at home and abroad (2017H, 2013H): Local Enterprise Offices (the schemes' Enterprise Boards), Kilkenny Design Centre, high street ranges, fashion weeks, blogs.
- Role of small businesses and cottage industries (2014O): rural employment, work done at home, subcontracting, low start-up cost, traditional skills.
- How traditional crafts influence Irish design (2022H): Aran stitch coats, linen, lace and crochet.
- Reasons for the growing popularity of textile craft work (2013H): socialising, new skills, saving money, free time, start-up business, originality.
- Name and give details of one career (2023H, 2017H): designer, tailor, milliner, journalist, photographer, model, stylist, retail assistant with two details.
