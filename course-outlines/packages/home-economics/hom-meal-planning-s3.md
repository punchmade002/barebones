## 1. Must cover

Obesity and energy balance (one block)
- [ ] O1 Obesity: excess accumulation of body fat, when energy intake regularly exceeds energy used and the surplus is stored as fat (adipose tissue). BMI = weight in kg divided by height in metres squared; 30 or above in adults is classed obese (25 to under 30 overweight, assumed).
- [ ] O2 Dietary causes: energy-dense foods high in fat and sugar, large portions, sugary drinks, cream sauces, fried foods, snacking, late-night eating, too much processed food, low fibre. Non-dietary: inactivity, heredity, stress, emotional eating.
- [ ] O3 Health risks: coronary heart disease, high blood pressure, Type 2 diabetes, gallstones, joint strain and arthritis, breathing problems, low self-esteem.
- [ ] O4 Guidelines and lifestyle changes (2024O, 2010O): balanced low-energy meals; lower fat especially saturated (lean meat, remove visible fat, low-fat cooking methods, avoid cream sauces); less sugar and salt; more fibre, fruit and vegetables and water; fewer processed foods; regular exercise; avoid alcohol and late-night eating; smaller portions.

Coronary heart disease (one block)
- [ ] H1 CHD: the coronary arteries that supply the heart muscle become narrowed or blocked by fatty deposits (atherosclerosis), which can cause angina or a heart attack.
- [ ] H2 Causes (2008O): diet high in saturated fat, cholesterol and salt; smoking; lack of exercise; obesity; excess alcohol; stress; high blood pressure; heredity; age and gender.
- [ ] H3 Diet links: saturated fat and trans fats raise LDL cholesterol; excess salt raises blood pressure; low fibre, fruit and vegetables remove protection; excess refined sugar contributes to weight gain.
- [ ] H4 Cholesterol (2007H "informative account"): a soft, wax-like substance found in every body cell, made mainly by the liver with some from animal foods; forms part of cell membranes and bile, is needed for some hormones, and helps transport fats in the blood. LDL (associated with saturated fat) deposits cholesterol in artery walls and hardens and narrows them; HDL (associated with unsaturated fat) carries cholesterol away from the arteries to the liver. Diet and CHD meal guidelines go to "Modified diets" (one-line link only).

Diabetes (one block)
- [ ] B1 Insulin: a hormone made by the pancreas that lets glucose move from the blood into cells; diabetes is raised blood glucose.
- [ ] B2 Type 1 (insulin-dependent) (2019H): the pancreas produces little or no insulin; often starts in childhood, adolescence or young adulthood; may be hereditary; controlled with insulin, diet and exercise; cannot be reversed.
- [ ] B3 Type 2 (non-insulin-dependent): insulin is produced but cells respond poorly or too little is made; usually later in life; linked to overweight, inactivity and a diet high in refined carbohydrate and sugar; can often be controlled with diet, weight loss, exercise and tablets. Most diabetes (around 90 per cent, approximate) is Type 2.
- [ ] B4 Gestational diabetes in pregnancy (usually resolves after the birth) (assumed extra).
- [ ] B5 Symptoms: thirst, frequent urination, tiredness, hunger, weight loss. Long-term complications: eye, kidney, nerve and heart problems (assumed; one line).

Bowel disease (one block)
- [ ] W1 Constipation: hard, dry stools passed with difficulty, from too little fibre, too little fluid and inactivity.
- [ ] W2 Diverticular disease: small pouches (diverticula) form in the wall of the large intestine under the pressure of straining; diverticulitis when they become inflamed or infected.
- [ ] W3 Haemorrhoids (swollen veins around the anus from straining); a low-fibre diet raises the risk of bowel disorders, including bowel cancer (name only).
- [ ] W4 Prevention: high-fibre foods (wholegrain cereals, brown bread, brown rice, pulses, nuts and seeds, fruit and vegetables with skins), plenty of fluid, exercise, regular meals. A high-fibre diet reduces the risk of bowel disease (true, 2021O).

Osteoporosis (one block)
- [ ] S1 Bones become thin, porous and brittle and break easily; bone mass peaks in early adulthood and then declines; women are at higher risk after the menopause (falling oestrogen).
- [ ] S2 Dietary causes: too little calcium, vitamin D, vitamin C (collagen) or phosphorus over life; factors that reduce calcium absorption or increase loss: phytates, oxalates, excess fibre, tannins, excess caffeine and alcohol, excess saturated fat; also smoking, inactivity, age.
- [ ] S3 Prevention (2024H meal-planning guidelines): increase calcium, vitamin D, vitamin C and phosphorus; build bone in childhood and adolescence; weight-bearing exercise; limit the inhibitors above.

Dental caries and gum disease (one block)
- [ ] G1 Dental caries (tooth decay): bacteria in plaque ferment sugar to acid, which dissolves enamel; frequent sugary snacks, sticky sweets and sugary drinks keep the mouth acidic.
- [ ] G2 Periodontal (gum) disease: plaque builds up and inflames the gums and the tissues around the teeth (true statement, 2021O).
- [ ] G3 Protection: fluoride strengthens enamel; calcium, phosphorus and vitamin D for tooth formation; limit sugary food to mealtimes; brush; dental check-ups; cheese and crunchy fruit and vegetables.

Inter-relationships (one block; the syllabus's key idea)
- [ ] I1 Surplus: excess energy leads to obesity, which raises the risk of CHD, Type 2 diabetes and high blood pressure; excess sugar leads to obesity and caries; excess salt raises blood pressure and CHD risk.
- [ ] I2 Deficiency: too little fibre and fluid leads to constipation and bowel disease; too little calcium and vitamin D leads to osteoporosis; too little iron leads to anaemia (named in 2017O, 2005O, 2004O; one line).
- [ ] I3 A poor diet for a teenager can cause several problems at once (2017O, 2005O): overweight, CHD risk, tooth decay, constipation, anaemia, diabetes risk, osteoporosis later.

## 3. Traps

- Do not use: a claim that sugar causes hyperactivity or acne (mentioned in a 2017 scheme list), because the evidence does not support the hyperactivity claim; stick to obesity, caries and diabetes risk.
- Do not use: Type 1 and Type 2 as "juvenile" and "adult" only; say Type 1 often starts young and Type 2 usually later, as some young people now develop Type 2.
- Do not use: BMI cut-offs for children or any other changing national statistics (obesity rates, number of people with diabetes or osteoporosis).
- Type 2 is not caused by eating sugar alone; it is linked to excess weight, inactivity and refined carbohydrate.
- Diabetes is not caused by "too much insulin"; cells respond poorly (Type 2) or too little is made (Type 1).
- Osteoporosis is not the same as osteomalacia or rickets (vitamin D deficiency diseases, covered with vitamin D).
- Dental caries is not gum disease: caries damages tooth enamel; periodontal disease inflames gums.
- Do not give full meal-plan guidelines for CHD or diabetic diets here (Modified diets); do not list nutrient functions (nutrient chapters); the effect of fibre on digestion is in the carbohydrate chapter.

## 4. What exams ask

- Explain Type 1 and Type 2 diabetes (6 marks, Higher): cause, age of onset, treatment, reversibility.
- Outline three lifestyle changes or guidelines for an obese person (6 marks).
- Describe three causes of CHD (12 marks); write an informative account of cholesterol with LDL and HDL (12 marks).
- Diet guidelines for bowel disease prevention; true/false on gum disease and fibre.
- Meal planning guidelines for osteoporosis (two guidelines, Higher): increase calcium, vitamin D, vitamin C, phosphorus; avoid inhibitors of calcium absorption (phytates, oxalates, excess fibre, tannins, caffeine, alcohol).
- Identify and explain diet-related problems from a given poor menu (four at 4 marks each, Ordinary): name the condition (2 marks), explain how the menu causes it (2 marks).
- Name one diet-related condition and one cause (obesity from excess calories; CHD from saturated fat; osteoporosis from low calcium).
