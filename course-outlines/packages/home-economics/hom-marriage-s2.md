# Package: hom-marriage-s2 — Cultural variations and the marriage relationship

- **loId:** `hom-marriage-s2` · **chapterId:** `hom-marriage` · **syllabusRef:** 3.1.5
- **Syllabus scope:** "outline knowledge of cultural variations in marital arrangements, commitment, customs, legal obligation, and rights and responsibilities within marriage." Settings: case studies, photographs, media or literature extracts on cultural variations. No Higher Level only items.
- **Also examined:** the outline lists none for this subsection; exams ask often on it (2025O, 2022O, 2017H, 2016O, 2015H, 2014O, 2009H, 2008O, 2005O).
- **Prepared by:** a higher model, 2026-11-23. Common to both levels. This is the most examined subsection of the chapter: "rights and responsibilities of a couple in marriage" appears almost every year, and cultural variations (marriage customs) very often. Marriage's definition and validity are in the first subsection; ending a marriage is in the last.

## 1. Must cover

Cultural variations in marital arrangements
- [ ] C1 Minimum age for marriage differs between countries and cultures (in Ireland it is 18).
- [ ] C2 Choice of partner: in an arranged marriage parents or family choose the spouse on the basis of background and suitability, usually with the couple's consent. Contrast with a marriage freely chosen by the couple.
- [ ] C3 Some cultures restrict whom a person may marry by religion, social class or kinship.
- [ ] C4 Number of spouses: monogamy (one husband, one wife; the norm in Western countries, including Ireland) and polygamy (more than one spouse at once). Polygyny is one man with more than one wife; polyandry is one woman with more than one husband. Polygamy is not permitted in Ireland; marrying while already married is bigamy, a criminal offence.
- [ ] C5 Same-sex marriage: legal in some countries (including Ireland since 2015) and not in others.
- [ ] C6 Religious and civil marriage; interfaith marriages (partners of different religions must agree how the ceremony and family religious life are arranged); secular or humanist ceremonies.
- [ ] C7 (assumed) Marriage may be a civil ceremony before a registrar or a religious ceremony; both are legally valid when registered (first subsection).

Customs and ceremonies
- [ ] M1 Customs differ by culture and religion: the dress (a veil, a wedding ring), throwing rice or confetti, the venue (church, mosque, synagogue, registry office, other approved venue) and the form of the ceremony.
- [ ] M2 Jewish weddings: held under a canopy symbolising the couple's future home; a glass is broken underfoot at the end, recalling the destruction of the Temple in Jerusalem.
- [ ] M3 Quaker weddings: no priest or official conducts the ceremony; the couple marry in front of the gathering because Quakers believe God alone joins a couple.
- [ ] M4 Arranged marriages are traditional in some communities (the marking scheme names Sikh and Japanese cultures and the Travelling community).

Commitment and legal obligation
- [ ] K1 Marriage is a public commitment, made through vows or declarations before witnesses and sealed by registration. It carries legal obligations: spouses owe each other the rights and responsibilities below, which the law can enforce.
- [ ] K2 Commitment is maintained by communicating, working at the relationship, loyalty and sharing responsibility for home, money and children.

Rights of spouses
- [ ] RT1 The right to each other's company and to live together as a couple.
- [ ] RT2 The right to a sexual relationship with each other (consummation); an unconsummated marriage can be annulled in some circumstances.
- [ ] RT3 The right to faithfulness and loyalty in a monogamous relationship.
- [ ] RT4 The right to inherit from each other under the Succession Act 1965. A spouse who dies without a will (intestate): if there are no children the surviving spouse inherits the whole estate; if there are children the spouse takes two-thirds and the children share one-third.
- [ ] RT5 The legal right share: even where a will leaves less, a surviving spouse may claim one half of the estate if there are no children, one third if there are children.

Responsibilities of spouses
- [ ] RS1 Financial support: each spouse must support the other (a dependent spouse) and any dependent children, as provided by the Family Law (Maintenance of Spouses and Children) Act 1976. (The Act's detail is in the Family Law chapter.)
- [ ] RS2 Joint guardianship: married parents are both guardians of their children and share responsibility for their care and upbringing (Guardianship of Infants Act 1964, as amended by the Children and Family Relationships Act 2015).
- [ ] RS3 Providing for the children's physical, emotional, social, moral, educational and spiritual needs.
- [ ] RS4 Commitment to the relationship, loyalty and positive communication, and caring for a sick spouse (exam schemes include "caring for a sick partner").

## 3. Traps

- Do not use: the old notes' line that the Jewish glass-breaking is "a reminder that a marriage can also end", because it is not a standard explanation; give only that it recalls the destruction of the Temple in Jerusalem.
- Do not use: "arranged marriage is found in Japanese communities" without the qualifier, because it is a traditional, now less common practice; say "traditional in some communities, for example Sikh, Japanese and Travelling communities" as the marking scheme does.
- Do not use: any named country as a place where polygamy, polygyny or polyandry is legal, because none has been checked.
- Do not use: "Sikh" or "Quaker" customs beyond the facts in the table (no named ceremonies, rituals or vows), because they are unchecked.
- Do not use: any amount of maintenance, or the notes' phrase "set out in" the 1976 Act for the duty to support as if the Act created it from nothing; say the Act allows a spouse to seek maintenance through the court.
- Do not use: "right to a sexual relationship" as a legal right a spouse can enforce; say each spouse is entitled to sexual relations with the other and that consummation is the basis of the marriage.
- Marking schemes use "conjugal rights" and "cohabitation" for the first rights; use plain words.
- Not marriage customs: dowry and bride price, unless named in a checked source; none is checked here.
- Belongs to a neighbouring subsection: the legal requirements for a valid marriage and the definition (first subsection); pre-marriage courses (next); separation, nullity, divorce (last). Barring orders, the Family Home Protection Act 1976 and the detail of the maintenance order belong to the Family Law chapter; will-making belongs there too.
- Common mistake: confusing polygamy (the general term) with polygyny and polyandry (the two forms), and saying monogamy is "illegal elsewhere".

## 4. What exams ask

- "Explain three or four ways marriage customs differ between cultures" (about 5 to 6 marks each): the usual points are arranged marriage (choice of spouse), number of spouses (monogamy and polygamy), age of marriage, same-sex marriage, restrictions by religion, class or kinship, ceremony customs (veil, ring, rice throwing, venue), civil, religious or interfaith ceremony. Each needs a short explanation, not just a label.
- "Discuss the rights and responsibilities of a couple": usually 4 or 5 points at 4 to 5 marks each. Rights: each other's company and living together, faithfulness or loyalty, sexual relations (consummation), inheritance (Succession Act 1965). Responsibilities: financial support of spouse and children (1976 Act), joint guardianship, meeting children's needs (physical, emotional, social, moral, educational), commitment and communication, and caring for a sick spouse. Answers should balance rights and responsibilities and give two to four of each.
- "Describe two cultural variations in marital arrangements": two clear variations, each with a one-line description.
- "Why marriage is popular in Ireland today": choice of civil or same-sex marriage, security for the couple and children, social and legal recognition. Not detailed law.
- Each item above can become an exam tip: name the Act where one applies (Succession Act 1965; Maintenance Act 1976) because marking schemes reward the Act.
