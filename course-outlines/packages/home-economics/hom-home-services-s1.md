## 1. Must cover

Ideas the section rests on
- [ ] E1 (assumed) Energy is converted from a source into heat, light, power or movement. A fuel is a material burned to release heat. Energy for the home is measured in kilowatt-hours (kWh); 1 kWh = 3.6 MJ (3600 kJ). Food and fuel tables use kJ. Kilowatt-hour is explained fully in the electricity subsection; one line here.
- [ ] E2 Renewable source: replenished naturally within a human lifetime (or never used up), e.g. solar, wind, hydro, biomass (wood), geothermal, tidal and wave. Non-renewable source: finite reserves that form far more slowly than they are used: coal, peat (turf), natural gas, oil (and uranium).
- [ ] E3 Sustainability (the word the syllabus uses): meeting present energy needs without using up resources or damaging the environment for future generations. For every source say (a) will it run out, (b) what it does to the environment, (c) can it be supplied reliably.
- [ ] E4 (assumed) Fossil fuels: coal, oil and natural gas formed over millions of years from the remains of plants (coal) and tiny sea organisms (oil, gas) buried and compressed. Burning releases carbon dioxide that was locked away long ago. Peat is not millions of years old (see Traps).
- [ ] E5 Primary and secondary energy: fuels dug up or collected (gas, oil, coal, turf, wood, sunlight) are primary; electricity is secondary because it is made from another source and carried to the house.

Electricity
- [ ] EL1 Electricity is generated in power stations where a turbine turns a generator: the turbine is driven by steam (from burning gas, coal or other fuel), by flowing water (hydro) or by wind. Solar panels make electricity directly with no turbine.
- [ ] EL2 Ireland's electricity comes from a mix of natural gas, wind and other sources, imported in part through interconnectors. Name no percentages.
- [ ] EL3 Delivery in outline: high-voltage national grid (high voltage means low current, so less heat lost in cables), step-down transformers, local network, service cable, meter. Full detail belongs to the electricity subsection; one or two lines here only.
- [ ] EL4 Advantages: clean and quiet at the point of use, no storage, instantly available, suits lighting, appliances and cooking, can be used for heating (storage heaters, panel heaters, heat pumps), and can be produced at home by solar panels. Disadvantages: how green it is depends on how it was generated; electric heating is expensive to run unless on night rate; no supply in a power cut.
- [ ] EL5 Sustainability: electricity from wind, hydro and solar is renewable; electricity from burning gas, coal or oil is not. A home on the grid gets a mix.

Natural gas
- [ ] G1 Source: a fossil fuel, mainly methane (CH₄), found under the seabed or land and brought ashore or up by pipe. Ireland's own supply is the Corrib field off the Mayo coast; the rest is imported by subsea pipeline from Britain.
- [ ] G2 Delivery: through underground pipe networks to a meter and regulator at the house (mains gas); where there is no pipeline, bottled or tank liquefied petroleum gas (LPG) is used.
- [ ] G3 Advantages: burns almost completely and cleanly (compared with coal and oil), controllable instantly, no storage tank needed on mains gas, relatively cheap to run, can be used for cooking, heating and hot water. Disadvantages: non-renewable, produces carbon dioxide, dangerous if it leaks or burns incompletely (carbon monoxide), needs a flue and a registered installer, not available everywhere.
- [ ] G4 Natural gas has no smell, so an odorant is added to warn of a leak.
- [ ] G5 Sustainability: finite reserves; demand rising worldwide; releases less CO₂ per unit of heat than coal or oil but is still a fossil fuel; Ireland's own gas field is running down and imports are rising (no figures).

Oil (kerosene)
- [ ] O1 Source: crude oil, a fossil fuel pumped from under the ground or sea and refined; home heating oil is kerosene. Ireland imports its oil, so supply and price follow world markets.
- [ ] O2 Delivery and use: delivered by tanker to a storage tank outside the house (needs space, a bund or safe base, and regular refills); fed to a boiler with a burner; needs a flue and an annual service.
- [ ] O3 Advantages: high heat output, suits houses beyond the gas network, central heating and hot water from one boiler, can be bought when the price is low. Disadvantages: non-renewable, price varies, tank can leak or be stolen, burning gives CO₂, sulphur dioxide and soot if the burner is badly adjusted, boiler needs servicing.
- [ ] O4 Sustainability: non-renewable, finite reserves, more CO₂ per unit of heat than natural gas.

Solid fuels
- [ ] S1 Coal (mined, a fossil fuel), turf or peat (cut from bogs), wood (logs, pellets, chips) and manufactured smokeless fuel (processed coal or other material that burns with little smoke).
- [ ] S2 Use: open fire, stove or solid-fuel boiler (a back boiler or stove can also heat water for radiators); needs dry storage, a chimney or flue, regular cleaning and ash removal.
- [ ] S3 Advantages: local supply (turf, wood), attractive fire, can heat water as well, stove burns efficiently. Disadvantages: labour of carrying and cleaning, dust and ash, soot and chimney fires, carbon monoxide risk if the flue is blocked, an open fire loses most of its heat up the chimney, smoke pollutes air.
- [ ] S4 Sustainability: coal is non-renewable; turf is non-renewable in practice because bogs take thousands of years to form; wood is renewable if trees are replanted and the forest managed (growing trees take in carbon dioxide); smokeless fuel is still a fossil product.

Solar energy
- [ ] SO1 Source: radiation from the sun. Free to collect and renewable, but intermittent (night, winter, cloud).
- [ ] SO2 Three ways a house uses it. (i) Solar thermal collectors (panels on the roof) absorb heat into a fluid that heats water stored in a cylinder. (ii) Photovoltaic (PV) panels convert sunlight to direct-current electricity; an inverter changes it to alternating current; surplus can go to the grid or into a battery. (iii) Passive solar design: large south-facing windows let in winter sunshine, which walls and floors absorb and release (heating principle is in the heating subsection).
- [ ] SO3 Advantages: no fuel cost, no emissions in use, lowers bills, reduces carbon footprint, may raise the BER, SEAI grants support it. Disadvantages: high installation cost, depends on the weather and the season, needs a suitable unshaded roof (best south-facing), a back-up supply is still needed, panels need space and some find them unsightly.
- [ ] SO4 Sustainability: inexhaustible and renewable; making and disposing of panels uses energy and materials.

Other renewables and the comparison (one block)
- [ ] R1 Wind (turbines feed the grid; small turbines can serve one house), hydro (flowing water turns a turbine; Ardnacrusha on the Shannon is the Irish example), biomass (wood, pellets and other plant material burned), geothermal and heat pumps (ground-source heat pump raises the temperature of heat drawn from the ground; it uses electricity to run), tidal and wave. State, for each, one fact, not a profile.
- [ ] R2 Comparison table content: renewable sources have low emissions in use, free fuel, but high start-up cost and depend on the weather; non-renewable sources are reliable and well established but finite and polluting.
- [ ] R3 Role of SEAI (Sustainable Energy Authority of Ireland): advice, the Building Energy Rating (BER) system and grants for renewable heating and solar.
- [ ] R4 How to answer the "one source in detail" question: name the source; its origin; how it reaches or heats the home; two advantages; two disadvantages; whether it will last. Give the structure as a short example of a model answer using solar energy or natural gas.

## 3. Traps

- Peat (turf) is classed with the non-renewables in the marking schemes, but it is not a fossil fuel formed "over millions of years"; it builds up over thousands of years in bogs. Say "non-renewable because it forms far more slowly than it is cut".
- Electricity is not a fuel or a source in itself; say how it was generated before calling it clean or dirty.
- Heat pumps use electricity to move heat; do not call them "free" energy or a fuel. They are covered further in the heating subsection.
- Wood is renewable only when forests are replanted and managed; do not say burning wood releases no carbon dioxide.
- Energy sources used by the home versus the emissions they produce: effects of the gases (acid rain, global warming) belong to the emissions subsection; here give only a line for each fuel.
- Safe use of gas, carbon monoxide and electricity belongs to the electricity subsection; heating systems and boilers to the heating subsection; solar heating principles to the heating subsection.
- Nuclear energy is named in some marking schemes as a non-renewable example, but Ireland has no nuclear power station and no home uses it directly; do not add a profile.
- Do not use: "Ireland's main gas supply is Corrib" or any percentage split between Corrib and imports, because the share is a changing figure (Corrib is declining and imports now supply most of the gas).
- Do not use: peat-fired power stations as a current source of Irish electricity, because they have closed; coal burning for electricity is also being phased out, so give no dates.
- Do not use: "wood is carbon neutral" as a flat statement, because it depends on regrowth and the burning still gives CO₂ and particles; say renewable if replanted.
- Do not use: "solar energy supplies 50-60 percent of household hot water" or "saves up to 68 percent of heating bills" (past-paper marking scheme figures), because they are changing, unverified figures.
- Do not use: "gas is a clean fuel" without qualification, because it is still a fossil fuel that releases carbon dioxide; say "cleaner than coal or oil".

## 4. What exams ask

- Name two renewable and two non-renewable sources, and one benefit of renewable energy (Ordinary Level, 6 marks). Expected: sources from the lists above; benefit such as will not run out, low emissions, cleaner.
- Explain "renewable resource" and "non-renewable resource" with an example of each: replenished naturally versus finite.
- Discuss one non-renewable source (gas or oil, coal, peat): where it comes from, advantages, disadvantages, and sustainability (finite reserves, rising demand, emissions). 12-18 marks at Higher Level.
- Discuss one renewable source (almost always solar, sometimes wind, hydro or biomass): name; how it is collected and used in the home; advantages; disadvantages; sustainability (inexhaustible). Solar appears most often: panels types, cost, weather, orientation, grants, BER.
- Recommend one energy supply other than electricity and give reasons (Ordinary Level): reasons such as heats the home and water, efficient, clean, local supply, cheap to run.
- Recommend a renewable type for a new house: source, sustainability, efficiency or effectiveness.
- Reducing consumption of energy in the home and the benefits of renewable choices turn up in the neighbouring emissions and efficiency subsection; do not duplicate.
