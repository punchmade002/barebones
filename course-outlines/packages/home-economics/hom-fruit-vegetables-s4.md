# Package: hom-fruit-vegetables-s4 — Processing

- **loId:** `hom-fruit-vegetables-s4` · **chapterId:** `hom-fruit-vegetables` · **syllabusRef:** 1.3.2
- **Syllabus scope:** commercial processing of fruit and vegetables (freezing, canning, drying) and its effects; processed and convenience products. No [HL] marks on this subsection (fermentation and irradiation are Higher Level in the preservation chapter, not here).
- **Also examined (outline):** canning and dehydration (2021H); effects of freezing and canning on vitamin C and sodium (2005H); additives in processed fruit and vegetables (2004O). Related in the papers: commercial freezing methods (2006H, 2016H) and the advantages and disadvantages of convenience foods (2006O, 2026H).
- **Prepared by:** a higher model, 2026-11-23. This subsection is about what processing does to the food and its nutrients. How each preservation method works in principle (water activity, micro-organism control) and the home versions are in the food-preservation chapter; keep to commercial processing and effects here, with a brief reason each method preserves. Higher Level questions ask for the effect of the method on nutrients, colour, texture and flavour, plus sugar, salt and fat added.

## 1. Must cover

Why produce is processed (one block)
- [ ] P1 Processing turns perishable fresh produce into products with a longer shelf life, greater convenience, availability all year, less waste, or all of these. Fruit and vegetables are often processed close to the farm soon after harvest.
- [ ] P2 The three methods named in the syllabus are freezing, canning and drying (dehydration). Steps common to all: sorting, washing, peeling, cutting, blanching or cooking, packaging, labelling.
- [ ] P3 (assumed) Spoilage is caused by enzymes and micro-organisms (see Selection, storage and spoilage); every method works by inactivating, killing or preventing one or both.

Commercial freezing (one block)
- [ ] FZ1 Process: fruit or vegetables are washed, prepared and (vegetables) blanched, then frozen quickly and packed in moisture-proof packaging; stored and distributed at -18 degrees Celsius or lower.
- [ ] FZ2 Commercial methods: air blast (cold air at very low temperature blown through a tunnel over food on a conveyor belt); plate or contact freezing (food pressed between two cold metal plates, for flat items); flow or fluidised-bed freezing (cold air blown up through a perforated belt so each piece, such as peas, freezes separately and does not stick together); cryogenic freezing (immersion or spray in liquid nitrogen, suited to delicate foods such as strawberries). Marking schemes give a food for each (peas for flow freezing).
- [ ] FZ3 Principle: low temperature stops micro-organisms multiplying and slows enzymes; water becomes ice, unavailable to micro-organisms. Blanching first destroys the enzymes that would otherwise keep working.
- [ ] FZ4 Quick freezing at about -25 degrees Celsius forms small ice crystals, which damage cells little, so the food keeps its texture; slow freezing forms large ice crystals that rupture cell walls, so the food goes limp and loses juice (drip) when thawed.
- [ ] FZ5 Effects: colour, flavour and most nutrients are well kept; some vitamin C and B vitamins are lost into the blanching water and in the drip on thawing; frozen produce can have more vitamin C than "fresh" produce that has been stored for days. Foods with a high water content and delicate texture (lettuce, bananas) do not freeze well; they go soft and mushy on thawing. Frozen foods are convenient, with little waste and no salt added; they should be cooked from frozen.

Commercial canning (one block)
- [ ] CN1 Process: produce is washed, graded, peeled and cut, blanched or cooked briefly, packed into cans or jars with brine, syrup, juice or water, sealed (with air removed), and heated in a retort (a pressure cooker on an industrial scale, heated to a temperature above boiling) to sterilise, then cooled.
- [ ] CN2 Principle: heat destroys micro-organisms and enzymes; the airtight seal stops re-entry. Canned food keeps for a long time without refrigeration until opened.
- [ ] CN3 Effects: loss of water-soluble vitamins (vitamin C, thiamine, folate) from heat and leaching into the liquid (the liquid contains them, so use it); colour, texture and flavour change (softer and duller); salt added in brine raises the sodium content; syrup raises the sugar content; fibre, minerals, carotene and protein are well kept. Fruit canned in natural juice contains less added sugar than fruit in syrup; vegetables in water or with no added salt are lower in sodium.
- [ ] CN4 Advantages: long shelf life, ready to use, cheap, available all year, safe from spoilage if the can is undamaged. Disadvantages: added salt or sugar, loss of vitamin C and thiamine, altered texture.

Commercial drying and dehydration (one block)
- [ ] DR1 Process: washed, cut and (usually) blanched or treated with a preservative; most of the water is removed by controlled warm air (hot-air drying), or by sun drying, spray drying or freeze-drying; packed in moisture-proof packaging.
- [ ] DR2 Principle: without water, micro-organisms cannot grow and enzymes cannot work. Dried food is light, compact, cheap to store and transport, and keeps for months without refrigeration.
- [ ] DR3 Effects: loss of B vitamins, vitamin A and E; vitamin C is lost unless a sulphite preservative is used; colour, texture and flavour change; the food shrinks and is chewy; with most water gone, dried fruit is concentrated in sugar, energy and fibre; dried pulses and dried vegetables are rehydrated before use (soaked or cooked).
- [ ] DR4 Examples: raisins, sultanas, prunes, apricots, dried peas, beans and lentils, instant mashed potato, dried herbs.

Additives in processed fruit and vegetables (one block; examined 2004O)
- [ ] A1 Additives are substances added to food to improve colour, flavour, texture or keeping quality or to replace nutrients lost in processing. Approved additives are identified by an E number; the EU rules are in Regulation (EC) No 1333/2008 on food additives, and the label must list them by function and name or number.
- [ ] A2 Colourings (E100 to E180) restore or enhance colour lost in processing, for example in canned peas and processed vegetables.
- [ ] A3 Preservatives (e.g. sulphur dioxide E220 in some dried fruit; sugar and salt) prevent growth of micro-organisms.
- [ ] A4 Antioxidants (e.g. ascorbic acid, vitamin C, E300) stop cut or frozen fruit browning.
- [ ] A5 Others: flavourings, sweeteners, acids, firming and bulking agents, and nutrient fortification; explanation of "why additives are used": improve appearance, taste and texture, extend shelf life, replace lost nutrients, and make processing and storage easier.

Processed and convenience fruit and vegetable products (one block)
- [ ] PC1 Examples: frozen peas and mixed vegetables, canned beans, tomatoes and sweetcorn, dried fruit, bagged salad and stir-fry mixes, fruit juices, smoothies, jam, vegetable soups, ready meals and sauces with vegetables, potato products (chips, wedges, crisps, instant mash).
- [ ] PC2 Advantages: save time, skill and effort, available all year, long shelf life, little waste, can be fortified, some (frozen) retain nutrients well.
- [ ] PC3 Disadvantages: often higher in salt, sugar and fat than fresh produce, lower in fibre and vitamins, more expensive, packaging waste, lower sensory quality, can unbalance the diet if eaten too often.
- [ ] PC4 Fruit juice and smoothies: juice has the sugar of fruit with little fibre; unsweetened juice counts as at most one serving a day; smoothies and juice are not a full replacement for whole fruit. Check labels for added sugar.
- [ ] PC5 Label information on processed products: ingredients list (in descending order of weight), additives with their functions, net quantity, date mark (best before or use by), storage instructions and cooking instructions where needed, nutritional information (energy, fat, saturates, carbohydrate, sugars, protein, salt). The law is Regulation (EU) No 1169/2011 on food information to consumers, in force in Ireland through the European Union (Provision of Food Information to Consumers) Regulations 2014.

## 3. Traps

- Quick (-25 degrees Celsius) gives small crystals; slow gives large crystals. Students confuse them. The storage temperature is -18 degrees Celsius; -25 degrees Celsius is the freezing temperature.
- Blanching before freezing destroys enzymes; it does not "kill all micro-organisms" and it does not sterilise.
- Canning is heat sterilisation, so most spoilage organisms and enzymes are killed; freezing and drying do not kill them, they only stop growth and activity.
- Frozen vegetables are not less nutritious than fresh; fresh produce stored for several days can have lost as much vitamin C.
- Do not use: "juice made from concentrate has a high sugar content", because reconstituted juice has the same natural sugar as the original; the sugar concern is free sugars in juice and added sugar in drinks. Use "juice has the sugars of the fruit with little fibre".
- Do not use: "canned fruit packed in heavy syrup is unsuitable for a diabetic diet" as a flat rule, because diabetic diets are individual; say syrup raises the sugar content and people controlling blood glucose should choose fruit in natural juice or water.
- Do not use: "melons and pears do not freeze well" as a scheme fact, because the schemes name lettuce and bananas; use those two.
- Do not use: "retort temperature of about 121 degrees Celsius" or "quick freezing at -25 degrees Celsius or lower", because 121 is not in the schemes and "or lower" is not needed; use -25 for quick freezing and do not give a canning temperature.
- Do not use: "vegetable crisps and wedges" as examples of convenience fruit and vegetables unless the point is that they are high in fat and salt, because they are often counted among snacks rather than as a serving.
- Do not use: "Processed products must carry ... date and storage instructions" as the whole list of mandatory label items; 1169/2011 requires more (allergens, name of food, nutrition declaration). Give the label items listed in PC5 and say allergens are in bold or emphasised in the ingredients list.
- Neighbouring subsections: causes of spoilage and storage of fresh and frozen produce at home are in Selection, storage and spoilage; blanching as a home cooking step is in Preparation and cooking. Home preservation (jam, chutney, bottling, home freezing and drying) and the principles of preservation (including fermentation and irradiation), HACCP and food-safety legislation belong to the food-preservation and food-safety chapters. Additives in general (classes, E numbers, safety debate) belong to the food-additives chapter; give only the fruit and vegetable uses here. Ultra-processed food and the profile of processed foods belong to the food-industry and additives chapters.

## 4. What exams ask

- "Explain the effect of canning and of dehydration in the processing of vegetables and fruit" (6 marks, Higher 2021): vitamin losses, colour, texture and flavour, micro-organism and enzyme control, added sugar, salt or fat in the liquid, concentrated sugar in dried fruit.
- Interpretation of a table of vitamin and mineral content of fresh, frozen and canned peas (Higher 2005): vitamin C falls on freezing (blanching, thawing drip) and more on canning (high temperature and long processing), sodium rises on canning (brine), thiamine falls on canning. Each comment needs the data (from the table), then a reason. Do not memorise figures.
- Freezing: true/false on quick freezing and small ice crystals, bananas and lettuce, blanching before freezing; explain how vitamin B1 and C are lost when freezing vegetables (blanching, slow freezing drip, thawing before cooking); commercial freezing methods with a suitable food for each; detail of freezing vegetables (method, principle, effect).
- Name two additives used in processed fruit or vegetables and why additives are used (Ordinary 2004): colourings in canned vegetables, preservatives, antioxidants; to improve colour, flavour, texture and keeping qualities, and to replace nutrients lost in processing.
- Advantages and disadvantages of convenience and processed foods (tinned, packet).
- Matching and short items: dehydration removes moisture; pre-prepared fruit and vegetables; drying as a method of removing moisture.
