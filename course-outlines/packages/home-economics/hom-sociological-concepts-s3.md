- **Syllabus scope:** "Primary and secondary social groups, kinship, socialisation" [3.1.1]. Also examined: monogamy (2023H, with the universality of the family and kinship). No Higher Level only items marked in the outline. Across the chapter: explaining sets of terms (2019H: socialisation and kinship in a set of five; 2023H: universality of the family, kinship, monogamy).
- **Prepared by:** a higher model, 2026-11-23. The terms are taught at "define and exemplify" depth. Socialisation is the process by which people learn the culture of their society; the norms and values themselves are in the first subsection of the chapter (assumed here). Kinship is defined here; family types (nuclear, extended) are taught in the chapter on the family in society, and are touched only to illustrate kin. Monogamy is the only marriage form examined; related terms (polygamy, polygyny, polyandry, bigamy) appear in marriage-customs questions, and a brief mention is expected.

## 1. Must cover

Social groups (one block)
- [ ] G1 A social group is two or more people who interact regularly and share something (a purpose, an identity, a place). Not every crowd is a group.
- [ ] G2 Primary group: small, close, personal, face-to-face, long-lasting, with strong emotional ties; examples: the family and close friends (and, in some cases, a close neighbourhood or peer group). A primary group meets emotional needs, gives a sense of belonging and shapes personality and values; it is where primary socialisation mainly happens.
- [ ] G3 Secondary group: larger, more formal and impersonal, organised around a purpose, with relationships that are often short-term or limited to a role; examples: school class, workplace, trade union, sports club, political party, a church congregation. Members often join by choice and leave when the purpose is finished.
- [ ] G4 Contrast in a short table or paired bullets: size, closeness, duration, purpose, example. (assumed: a person belongs to both types at once and some groups sit between the two, for example a small close-knit club.)
- [ ] G5 In-group and out-group; reference group (a group whose standards a person uses to judge themself). Optional extras, one line each.
- [ ] G6 Peer group: people of similar age and status; important in adolescence and a secondary or primary agent depending on closeness. Link to socialisation only; the family-and-adolescent aspects are in the family chapter.

Kinship (one block)
- [ ] K1 Kinship means family ties: relationships based on blood (common ancestry or lineage), marriage or adoption. Marking-scheme wording: family ties, blood relationships, common ancestry or lineage.
- [ ] K2 Kin by blood (parents, children, siblings, grandparents, aunts, uncles, cousins) and kin by marriage (spouse, in-laws, step-relatives). The technical terms consanguineal and affinal are optional.
- [ ] K3 Kinship is found in every society (link to the universality of the family, taught in the chapter on the family in society, one clause only).
- [ ] K4 Kin provide support: emotional support, childcare, financial help, care in old age, inheritance, a sense of identity. Kin networks have become looser as families have become smaller and more mobile, but contact is kept through phones and social media; the change is taught in the subsection on social change.
- [ ] K5 Family of orientation (the one you are born into) and family of procreation (the one you form). Optional extra.
- [ ] K6 Descent is traced through the mother's side, the father's side or both; most modern Western societies trace both sides. Optional extra, one line.

Socialisation (one block, process)
- [ ] SO1 Socialisation is the lifelong process by which individuals learn the norms, values, behaviour and skills of their society and so become members of it. Marking-scheme sense: lifelong learning of society's norms, values, behaviour and skills, primarily in the family and secondarily through school, work and media.
- [ ] SO2 Primary socialisation: early childhood, mainly in the family; the child learns language, basic behaviour, toilet training, manners, right from wrong and a first sense of identity, through observation, imitation and consistent discipline.
- [ ] SO3 Secondary socialisation: later, in wider settings (school, peers, workplace, religion, media), where the person learns the roles and norms of those settings: punctuality, rules, teamwork, respect for authority.
- [ ] SO4 Agents of socialisation: family, school, peer group, mass media (television, internet and social media), religion, workplace. One line each on what is learned. The school teaches formally through the curriculum and informally through the "hidden curriculum" of rules and routines (the education marking scheme wording).
- [ ] SO5 How people learn: observation, imitation, rewards and punishments (sanctions, link to the first subsection), role models, instruction.
- [ ] SO6 Why socialisation matters: it passes culture from one generation to the next, produces conformity and social order, and lets individuals take part in society. Without it a child could not use language or form relationships (state without citing a named case).
- [ ] SO7 Anticipatory socialisation (learning the role you will have later) and resocialisation (learning new norms after a big change, such as prison or the army) are optional extras, one line each.

Monogamy (also examined) (one block)
- [ ] MO1 Monogamy: marriage or a partnership with one partner at a time (one husband, one wife). It is the norm in Western societies.
- [ ] MO2 In Irish law a person cannot lawfully marry while still married to someone else; doing so is bigamy, an offence. Polygamy (more than one spouse at once) is not legal in Ireland.
- [ ] MO3 Forms of polygamy: polygyny (one man with more than one wife) and polyandry (one woman with more than one husband). Both are found in some other societies.
- [ ] MO4 Monogamy carries expectations for spouses: faithfulness and loyalty, living together, financial support of each other and any children (the marriage rights and responsibilities in the marriage-law chapter; one clause here).
- [ ] MO5 Serial monogamy: a series of one-partner relationships, one after the other (for example, remarriage after divorce). Optional extra.
- [ ] MO6 Cultural variation in marriage (arranged marriages, number of spouses, age at marriage, ceremony type) is an example of cultural variation; it is examined in the marriage chapter, so one line here only.

## 3. Traps

- Primary versus secondary group is about the type of relationship (close, personal, lasting versus formal, impersonal, purpose-based), not about the order in which a person joins them or the size alone. Primary socialisation versus primary group are separate terms: primary socialisation happens in early childhood, mainly in the family, a primary group.
- Do not equate "primary" with "more important" in every situation; say only that primary groups shape personality early and strongly.
- Socialisation is lifelong, not only childhood. A student who writes only "learning in childhood" loses the second mark.
- Socialisation is not the same as education in the classroom; education is one agent.
- Kinship is not limited to people who live together: it includes relatives who live apart. An extended family is a household or network pattern; kinship is the relationship. Do not define kinship as "the extended family".
- Monogamy is not the same as marriage and not only marriage: it can describe a partnership. Do not describe monogamy as the only form of marriage worldwide; it is the norm in Western societies and the only one lawful in Ireland.
- Same-sex marriage is lawful in Ireland and is monogamous; if mentioned, say "marriage of two people" only. Do not claim that all Irish marriages are between a husband and a wife.
- Belongs to a neighbouring subsection: culture, norms and values (first subsection); role, status and class (role and status); the family and the school as institutions, and the functions of the family (social institutions); family structures (nuclear, extended, blended) and the universality of the family (the chapter on the family in society); marriage law, the legal rights of spouses and divorce (the chapter on family law); the influence of media and technology on change (social change).

## 4. What exams ask

- Explain-the-terms questions: socialisation (2019H, in a set of five) as lifelong learning of norms, values and skills, mainly through the family and then school, work and media; kinship (2019H and 2023H) as family ties by blood, marriage or common ancestry; monogamy (2023H) as one partner at a time. Each is worth about 3 marks: a definition plus an example.
- Socialisation as a family function in functions-of-the-family questions (2015 and others): the children learn through observation, imitation and consistent discipline, developing a set of values and learning to conform and to fit into society. The notes in this subsection supply the definition; the family function belongs to the family chapter.
- Education as a method of socialisation (2024H) and the contribution of school to socialisation of young children: reinforcing behaviour learned at home, punctuality, respect for authority, rules, sharing and teamwork, and the informal hidden curriculum.
- Marriage customs and cultural variation (marriage chapter): number of spouses (monogamy, polygamy), arranged marriages, and wedding customs; each term should be definable in a line.
- The exam tip: give an example from Irish life for each term, and in a set of terms answer each separately.
