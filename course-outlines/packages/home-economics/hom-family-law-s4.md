# Package: hom-family-law-s4 — The Judicial Separation and Family Law Reform Act 1989

- **loId:** `hom-family-law-s4` · **chapterId:** `hom-family-law` · **syllabusRef:** 3.1.7
- **Syllabus scope:** "The Judicial Separation and Family Law Reform Act 1989" [3.1.7] **[HL]**. Higher Level only: every block heading in the notes ends " (Higher Level only)". (The 2001 syllabus calls it the Judicial Separation Act 1989; the outline uses the full current title, "as amended".)
- **Also examined:** none listed for this item. The Act appears in marriage-breakdown questions about legal options (separation, judicial separation, divorce) and in a 12-mark Section B question listing Acts that protect family members; those answers are the exam use here.
- **Prepared by:** a higher model, 2026-11-23. Sources checked: Citizens Information (judicial separation; legal options following marital breakdown), irishstatutebook.ie (1989 Act, section 2). The Act has been amended, notably by the Family Law Act 1995 and the Family Law Act 2019. Divorce and the deed of separation belong to the notes on marriage breakdown and to the divorce law; here they are comparisons only.

## 1. Must cover

What judicial separation is
- [ ] J1 Judicial separation is a decree (formal order) made by a court that relieves the spouses of the duty to live together. They live apart under the court's orders. It does not end the marriage.
- [ ] J2 Because the marriage continues, neither spouse is free to remarry. Only a divorce ends a marriage and allows remarriage. This is a favourite exam point.
- [ ] J3 The application is made to the Circuit Court or the High Court. It is heard by a judge. (Not the District Court.)
- [ ] J4 It is needed in particular where the couple cannot agree the terms of separating: if they can agree, they use a separation agreement (see J11).

Grounds
- [ ] G1 The applicant must prove at least one ground (reason) in the Act. The six grounds are: adultery; unreasonable behaviour; desertion for a continuous period of at least one year immediately before the application; the spouses have lived apart for at least one year and both consent to the decree; the spouses have lived apart for at least three years (even if the other spouse does not consent); the marriage has broken down so that a normal marital relationship has not existed for at least one year before the application.
- [ ] G2 The marking schemes name adultery, unreasonable behaviour, desertion and no normal marital relationship for at least one year. Students need at least those four; the notes give all six.
- [ ] G3 The last ground is the one most commonly used because it does not require either spouse to be at fault (assumed from Citizens Information).

Orders the court can make
- [ ] R1 Maintenance: orders for one spouse to support the other and the children (links to the maintenance Act).
- [ ] R2 Property orders: dividing or transferring property between the spouses, including who lives in the family home (property adjustment), and orders about the use of the family home.
- [ ] R3 Custody of and access to children: where children live and how the other parent sees them (the notes may say "custody and access" as the schemes do).
- [ ] R4 Extinguishing succession rights: the court may order that a spouse loses their right to inherit from the other spouse (past marking schemes list "extinguishment of succession rights"). Without such an order the right to inherit continues.
- [ ] R5 These orders can be made together with the decree so that money and children are settled at once. Maintenance orders can later be varied if circumstances change.
- [ ] R6 A pension adjustment order is possible (mention only if the notes need a further example; no detail).

Comparisons
- [ ] C1 Judicial separation vs divorce: divorce ends the marriage and lets both remarry; judicial separation does not. A divorce requires the spouses to have lived apart for at least two of the previous three years (Family Law (Divorce) Act 1996 as amended by the Family Law Act 2019).
- [ ] C2 Judicial separation vs separation agreement (deed of separation): a separation agreement is drawn up by solicitors and agreed by both spouses (often with a mediator), it is cheaper and avoids court, and it is binding as a legal agreement. Judicial separation is a court decree and is used when terms cannot be agreed.
- [ ] C3 The result in both: the spouses are not required to live together, but they remain married.
- [ ] C4 Exam use: in a "legal options when a marriage breaks down" question, give grounds, the court, the orders, and "does not allow remarriage".
- [ ] C5 Diagram: none.

## 3. Traps

- Do not use: the old divorce rule of living apart four of the previous five years, because it was superseded by the Family Law Act 2019 (two of the previous three years). The earlier notes use the correct current rule; keep it.
- Do not use: "legal separation" as another name for judicial separation, because a legal (or deed of) separation is an agreement by the spouses, whereas judicial separation is a court decree. The earlier notes' wording is right but easily blurred; keep the two apart.
- Do not use: "the three grounds are adultery, unreasonable behaviour and desertion" as the full list, because the Act has six grounds; leaving out the living-apart and "no normal marital relationship" grounds is an error of omission that costs marks.
- Do not say judicial separation lets a spouse remarry or ends succession rights automatically. Only divorce permits remarriage, and inheritance rights end only if the court so orders (R4).
- Do not detail "divorce" procedure, the "nullity" or church annulment: those belong to the notes on marriage breakdown.
- Do not use: any claim about how long or how much a judicial separation costs, because costs change and are not in the scope.
- Neighbouring subjects: barring and safety orders (domestic violence) are a different remedy for protection from abuse, not a form of separation; maintenance (the first subsection) and the family home (the second) are things a separation decree can order, not separate legal routes.

## 4. What exams ask

- Explain judicial separation or "outline what the Act provides" (Higher Level): decree from the court that spouses may live apart; marriage not ended; grounds (adultery, unreasonable behaviour, desertion, no normal marital relationship); orders on maintenance, property, children and succession rights.
- Compare legal separation (agreement), judicial separation (decree) and divorce (ends marriage): the usual set of three; each with who decides, whether the marriage ends, and the effect on remarriage.
- State why a person cannot remarry after judicial separation: the marriage still exists.
- State the court: Circuit or High Court.
- Expected depth: for a 12-mark part, three or four points with each ground named; for a comparison, one clear difference per option.
