# Package: hom-education-s3 — The provision of education in Ireland

- **loId:** `hom-education-s3` · **chapterId:** `hom-education` · **syllabusRef:** 6.2
- **Syllabus scope:** "The provision of education in Ireland [6.2]: pre-school, primary, second-level, third-level, adult and second-chance education, and **[HL]** special needs education. Also examined: pre-school initiatives (2026H, 2013O); work experience (2021O, 2011O); supports for special educational needs (2024H, 2009H, 2005H)."
- **Prepared by:** a higher model, 2026-11-23. Only the special needs education block is Higher Level only; everything else is common. Mark just that block as HL. The "Also examined" items for second-level work experience, pre-school initiatives and special-needs supports are in scope for the matching blocks. Reasons adults return to education, and reasons for rising third-level numbers, are examined (2004H, 2011H, 2014H, 2019O) and are in scope here. Old notes have wrong facts here (see Traps).

## 1. Must cover

Pre-school education and initiatives (one block)
- [ ] PS1 Pre-school (early childhood) education is for children before primary school; it is not compulsory. Types: playschools/playgroups, Montessori pre-schools, crèches and day nurseries with an educational programme, Naíonraí (Irish-medium pre-schools), childminders (see the child care subsection in the chapter on work and leisure for care options).
- [ ] PS2 Benefits: language and social skills (sharing, taking turns, following group rules), preparation for the routines of primary school, early detection of problems, and a better start for children from disadvantaged backgrounds.
- [ ] PS3 The Early Childhood Care and Education (ECCE) scheme: State-funded free pre-school programme for children in the year(s) before they begin primary school, delivered by participating (approved) pre-school providers; free pre-school place, currently 3 hours a day, 5 days a week for 38 weeks, from about age 2 years 8 months until primary school. Used in exams as "one government scheme that supports the provision of pre-school education" (2013 OL).
- [ ] PS4 Early Start: a one-year pre-school intervention funded by the Department of Education, run in selected primary schools in disadvantaged areas for three- to four-year-olds, to give a positive start to education and prevent later school failure; staffed by primary teachers with child care assistants (2008 HL).
- [ ] PS5 Supports for children with disabilities in pre-school: the Access and Inclusion Model (AIM) helps children with a disability take part in the free pre-school programme.
- [ ] PS6 Choosing a pre-school (2013 OL four factors, 16 marks): location, qualifications and experience of staff, adult to child ratio, hygiene and safety, facilities (eating, sleeping, changing, washing, play), ages of other children, programme and play activities, cost, opening hours, Tusla registration and inspection (pre-schools must be registered with Tusla, the Child and Family Agency, which inspects them).
- [ ] PS7 Síolta (national quality framework) and Aistear (curriculum framework for early childhood) guide quality and learning in early years settings. (assumed: use as names only, one clause.)

Primary education (one block)
- [ ] PR1 Children may enrol from age four and by law must be in school by age six; primary school is an eight-year cycle from junior infants to sixth class; most pupils finish at about 12 (some up to 13 or 14).
- [ ] PR2 Types: State-funded primary schools (the State pays teachers' salaries and most building and running costs, with a local contribution; run by a Board of Management under a patron), special schools, and private (fee-paying) primary schools. State-funded schools may be denominational (religious: most are Catholic, others Church of Ireland, Muslim, Jewish and so on), inter-denominational or multi-denominational, or non-denominational, for example Educate Together; Gaelscoileanna teach through Irish; some are single-sex or co-educational.
- [ ] PR3 Curriculum: a broad curriculum covering languages (Irish and English), mathematics, science and social studies, arts, physical education, SPHE (social, personal and health education) and religious or ethical education/ethos. The curriculum framework has been revised recently; the notes describe the broad areas without listing a particular framework.
- [ ] PR4 Educational supports/resources: learning support and special education teachers, SNAs, language support, books/book rental or schemes, ICT, school transport, school meals programme in DEIS schools (marking scheme 2012). Provision for pupils with special needs: special classes, special schools, resource/special education teachers (detail in the special needs block).
- [ ] PR5 Alternatives: some parents educate children at home; home-educated children are registered with Tusla (Education (Welfare) Act 2000 provides this and school attendance rules).
- [ ] PR6 Compulsory school age: between 6 and 16 (or until three years of second-level education have been completed), set by the Education (Welfare) Act 2000; enforced by the Tusla Educational Welfare Service. (Replaced the National Educational Welfare Board; see Traps.)

Second-level education (one block)
- [ ] SL1 Second-level schools: secondary (voluntary; many fee-paying or with patron such as a religious order or trust), vocational schools and community colleges (run by Education and Training Boards), community schools and comprehensive schools; some single-sex, mixed, Irish-medium; most receive State funding.
- [ ] SL2 Junior Cycle: a three-year programme (first to third year) with a broad curriculum (compulsory and optional subjects, Irish, English, maths, SPHE, wellbeing). Assessment: classroom-based assessments and an assessment task, plus state-certified examinations in most subjects at the end of third year, set and marked by the State Examinations Commission (SEC). Awards are recorded in the Junior Cycle Profile of Achievement (JCPA), which replaced the Junior Certificate. Subjects at common level (the old Higher/Ordinary levels are used in Irish, English and maths).
- [ ] SL3 Transition Year (TY): an optional year (fourth year in most schools, compulsory in a minority of schools) with no state examination; personal development, work experience, community involvement, new subjects and independent learning.
- [ ] SL4 The Leaving Certificate (established): two-year programme (fifth and sixth year), final state examinations set by the SEC, at higher and ordinary level in most subjects; foundation level exists only in Irish and mathematics; grades convert to CAO points for third-level entry. Broad subject choice (typically six or seven subjects), vocational and academic.
- [ ] SL5 Leaving Certificate Vocational Programme (LCVP): a Leaving Certificate with a vocational focus; students take two link modules (Preparation for the World of Work and Enterprise Education); work experience is part of Preparation for the World of Work; assessed by portfolio and written exam; counts for CAO points.
- [ ] SL6 Leaving Certificate Applied (LCA, the 2015 OL initiative): a two-year, self-contained, modular programme (four half-year sessions) with continuous assessment, student tasks and practical, oral and written examinations; credits are awarded and certified as Pass, Merit or Distinction; designed for students who prefer practical or work-related learning; it is not accepted directly for CAO points but can lead to Post Leaving Certificate (PLC) courses and work.
- [ ] SL7 Junior Certificate School Programme (JCSP): a support programme within Junior Cycle for students at risk of early school leaving, with learning statements and a profile; named here in one clause only and described in the access subsection.
- [ ] SL8 Facilities and resources in schools (a past HL question on second-level change): PE halls, libraries, computers and ICT, guidance counselling, home-school liaison, learning support, SNAs, school transport, work experience and enterprise activities; opportunities for school leavers: work, third level, PLC, apprenticeships, further education and training, Youthreach, VTOS, night classes, literacy courses.

Work experience (one block, 2021O, 2011O)
- [ ] WE1 Work experience is a placement in a workplace: part of Transition Year, of LCVP (inside the Preparation for the World of Work link module) and of LCA.
- [ ] WE2 Benefits (three needed for 12 marks): clarifies career preferences and what a job involves; students learn workplace cooperation and how work differs from school; develops communication, teamwork, planning, organisation, punctuality and problem solving; builds confidence and responsibility; gives a reference and a work record for a CV; introduces health and safety and workplace expectations.

Third-level education (one block)
- [ ] TL1 Third-level institutions: universities, technological universities (for example TU Dublin, created under the Technological Universities Act 2018) and institutes of technology, plus colleges of education and private colleges; full-time and part-time degrees and diplomas at Levels 6 to 10 on the National Framework of Qualifications (qualifications recognised by QQI).
- [ ] TL2 Entry: the Central Applications Office (CAO) allocates places using Leaving Certificate points (grade-based; a student with PLC awards may apply through the Higher Education Links Scheme); alternative routes include mature student entry, and HEAR and DARE (reduced points; named here, detailed in the access subsection).
- [ ] TL3 Funding: the Free Fees Initiative pays tuition for eligible undergraduates, with students paying a student contribution; the SUSI (Student Universal Support Ireland) grant helps with costs and is means-tested; scholarships and distance learning (for example the Open University) widen access.
- [ ] TL4 Post Leaving Certificate (PLC) courses: full-time further education courses (usually one or two years) at QQI Levels 5 and 6, run by Education and Training Boards in colleges of further education and some second-level schools, combining training and work experience; lead to jobs or to higher education.
- [ ] TL5 Reasons for the rise in third-level participation (2019 OL, four reasons): families can afford it better; qualifications are valued as a route to well-paid work; wide course choice and local colleges; student motivation; supports and access schemes (DARE, HEAR, SUSI); more Leaving Certificate completers; technological change raising the qualifications needed.

Adult and second-chance education (one block)
- [ ] AD1 Adult and second-chance education lets adults, including early school leavers, return to learn or train: part-time, evening, weekend, distance and modular courses, and full-time courses. It supports lifelong learning, as government and EU policy encourage.
- [ ] AD2 Providers and bodies: Education and Training Boards (ETBs), which replaced the Vocational Education Committees in 2013 and run adult, community and further education; SOLAS, the Further Education and Training Authority (set up 2013), which funds and coordinates further education and training; colleges and universities; Aontas, the Irish national association of adult education; NALA (National Adult Literacy Agency) and adult literacy services in ETBs.
- [ ] AD3 Named opportunities (detail in the access subsection): Youthreach, VTOS, Back to Education Allowance, Back to Education Initiative, Springboard+, community education, apprenticeships.
- [ ] AD4 Why adults return (2004H, 2011H, 2014H, 2019O): improve qualifications and job prospects or promotion; change career; unemployment and retraining; keep skills up to date with technology; personal fulfilment; to pursue a hobby or interest; socialising; help children with homework; literacy and numeracy second chance; flexible courses and financial supports available. Considerations when deciding: course availability and suitability, cost, time and family commitments, childcare, travel, supports, how well the qualification is recognised.

Special needs education (Higher Level only) (one block)
- [ ] SN1 [HL] Special educational needs (SEN) include learning difficulties, physical and sensory disabilities, autism spectrum disorder, emotional and behavioural difficulties. The aim is inclusion in mainstream school where possible.
- [ ] SN2 [HL] Planning and allocation: the National Council for Special Education (NCSE), set up under the Education for Persons with Special Educational Needs Act 2004, plans and coordinates supports; Special Educational Needs Organisers (SENOs) allocate supports to schools; the NEPS (National Educational Psychological Service) psychologists assess needs and advise schools; individual education plans (IEPs) set goals and supports for a pupil, drawn up by school and parents.
- [ ] SN3 [HL] Supports in school: special education teachers (the title for the learning support and resource teachers, under the model in place since 2017), special needs assistants (SNAs) for care needs, special classes in mainstream schools (for example autism classes with a low pupil-teacher ratio), special schools, language support, adapted furniture and equipment, assistive technology (laptops, text-to-speech), special transport.
- [ ] SN4 [HL] Visiting teachers advise pupils who are deaf or hard of hearing, or blind or vision-impaired, and their teachers. Speech and language therapy and occupational therapy are provided by the HSE.
- [ ] SN5 [HL] Reasonable accommodations at the certificate examinations (RACE): reader, scribe, extra time, a separate room, assistive technology.
- [ ] SN6 [HL] Exam use (2024H "evaluate a range of supports in second-level schools"): give at least four supports with what each does and for whom, and an evaluation sentence on whether it meets the need.

## 3. Traps

- Do not use: "three link modules in the LCVP, including work experience", because the LCVP has two link modules (Preparation for the World of Work and Enterprise Education); work experience sits inside the first.
- Do not use: "Leaving Certificate subjects are offered at higher, ordinary and foundation levels" without qualification, because foundation level exists only for Irish and mathematics.
- Do not use: "Junior Certificate" as the current Junior Cycle award, because it was replaced by the Junior Cycle Profile of Achievement; past papers (2018) still say Junior Certificate, so mention it once as the old name only.
- Do not use: "Early Start is staffed by trained teachers, special needs assistants and parents", because it is staffed by primary teachers working with a child care assistant; parents are not part of the staffing.
- Do not use: "Steiner schools" as a named primary school type, because it is unconfirmed; use the types in PR2.
- Do not use: the National Educational Welfare Board (NEWB), FÁS, FETAC, HETAC, VECs or the National Adult Learning Council as current bodies, because they were replaced (by Tusla's Educational Welfare Service, SOLAS and the ETBs, and QQI); marking schemes before 2014 still name them.
- Do not use: "learning support and resource teachers" as the only current title; say special education teachers (formerly learning support and resource teachers), because the allocation model changed in 2017. Marking schemes still use the old titles, so mention both.
- Do not use: any number of SNAs, special classes or schools, because they change.
- A primary school is "state-funded", not "state-run": the patron (religious body, Educate Together, ETB) runs it. Do not call Educate Together or denominational schools "private".
- Confusable: Transition Year (no exam, optional) vs LCVP vs LCA; PLC (further education, Levels 5 and 6) vs third level (higher education, Levels 6 to 10); Youthreach (early school leavers aged 15 to 20) vs VTOS (unemployed adults aged 21 and over).
- Pre-school care options (crèche, childminder, nanny) and how to evaluate them for the family belong to the child care subsection of the work and leisure chapter; give only what pre-school education offers here.
- Legal protection for young workers (the Protection of Young Persons (Employment) Act 1996) belongs to work and leisure; do not describe it as part of work experience.
- Equality, disadvantage and early school leaving as problems belong to the equality subsection; access schemes (DEIS, HEAR, DARE, Youthreach, VTOS, BTEA, Springboard+) get one clause each here and detail in the access initiatives subsection.

## 4. What exams ask

- Describe primary education (2025 OL, 20 marks; also earlier years): age range (enrol at 4, begin by 6, eight years), choice and types (state-funded religious and non-denominational, Educate Together, Gaelscoil, special, private), curriculum, educational supports and special needs provision.
- Name and describe two programmes provided at second level (2018 HL, 10 marks each): name (2), curriculum (4), assessment (4). Expect Junior Cycle (three years, broad curriculum, state-certified exams), Leaving Certificate, LCVP, LCA, Transition Year, PLC; for each: length, subjects/modules and how it is assessed.
- Discuss second-level changes: range of programmes, facilities, and opportunities for school leavers.
- Benefits of work experience at second level (2021 OL, three benefits at 4 marks each; 2011 OL): career clarity, cooperation, communication, teamwork, planning, punctuality, problem solving, confidence, CV.
- Pre-school: one government scheme (ECCE, Early Start; 2013 OL, 2026 HL) with what it provides and who it is for; factors when choosing a pre-school (2013 OL).
- Adult and second-chance education: explain the meaning (2019 OL, 6 marks), describe provision (2014 HL), reasons for demand and for returning (2004 HL, 2011 HL, 2014 HL).
- Reasons for the rise in third-level participation (2019 OL, four reasons).
- Special needs supports at second level (2024 HL "evaluate a range"; 2009 HL, 2005 HL): learning support/special education teachers, SNAs, psychologists, IEPs, visiting teachers, assistive technology, exam accommodations, special classes, language support, transport; evaluate by who each helps and any limits (waiting times and resource allocation).
