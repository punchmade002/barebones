# Package: hom-carbohydrates-s6 — Energy value and dietary fibre targets

- **loId:** `hom-carbohydrates-s6` · **chapterId:** `hom-carbohydrates` · **syllabusRef:** 1.1.3
- **Syllabus scope:** contribution to the total energy value of the average diet; dietary targets for non-starch polysaccharide intake and how they can be achieved. Settings: a comparative study of the energy value of foods with and without added sugar (e.g. breakfast cereals); comparing the energy in a food (e.g. a bar of chocolate) with the time an activity takes to use it up. Also examined: the EFSA fibre recommendation and strategies to meet it (2016H); reducing sugar intake (2024H, 2019O, 2017H); effects of high sugar intake (2018O, 2017H); artificial sweeteners (2026H, 2011H, also in the additives chapter). Also in the extract: three ways to increase fibre (2024O, 2023O, 2014O, 2011O). No [HL] items in this subsection.
- **Prepared by:** a higher model, 2026-11-23, from the outline, the current notes and the past-paper extract. Ordinary and Higher Level. The energy values of the other nutrients are given here for comparison; the full energy balance, BMI and nutritional needs are in the energy balance and nutritional needs chapter. Food additive rules for sweeteners are in the additives chapter; here give the two classes, examples and uses. Fibre sources are in the sources subsection; the bowel functions of fibre are in the functions subsection. Type 2 diabetes management and special diets are in the meal planning chapter.

## 1. Must cover

Energy value of carbohydrate (one block)
- [ ] V1 Carbohydrate provides 16 kJ (3.75 kcal) per gram. Sugars and starch give the same energy per gram. Compared with protein 17 kJ (4 kcal), fat 37 kJ (9 kcal) and alcohol 29 kJ (7 kcal) per gram.
- [ ] V2 Fibre (non-starch polysaccharide) is not digested by human enzymes and gives little usable energy.
- [ ] V3 Calculation: grams of carbohydrate × 16 gives kJ (× 3.75 gives kcal). Worked example: 30 g sugar gives 30 × 16 = 480 kJ, or 30 × 3.75 = 112.5 kcal. One kilocalorie is about 4.2 kilojoules (1 kcal = 4.184 kJ).
- [ ] V4 Settings, comparative study: compare two breakfast cereals, one with added sugar and one without, using the label's carbohydrate, sugar and energy per 100 g; discuss which supplies more energy and more fibre. Settings, activity comparison: work out the energy in a bar of chocolate and compare it with the time an activity such as walking or running takes to use it up (use the energy values from a source given with the question; do not invent figures in the notes).
- [ ] V5 Label point: the nutrition declaration on a label states carbohydrate and, of which, sugars; fibre is an optional item. The energy figure on a label is calculated by the manufacturer and may not match a student's calculation exactly.

Contribution of carbohydrate to the average diet (one block)
- [ ] D1 Carbohydrate, mostly as starch, is the main source of energy in most diets and should provide the largest share of the energy in the diet; fat and protein supply the rest. Give no percentage.
- [ ] D2 Healthy eating guidelines (the Department of Health Food Pyramid): the second shelf (up from the base) is wholemeal cereals, breads, potatoes, pasta and rice, with the base shelf of vegetables, salad and fruit; the top shelf holds foods high in fat, sugar and salt, to be eaten in small amounts and not every day. Choose wholemeal and wholegrain starchy foods.
- [ ] D3 Foods with a lot of added sugar supply energy with few other nutrients (energy-dense and nutrient-poor, "empty calories"); starchy foods and fruit and vegetables supply energy along with fibre, vitamins and minerals.
- [ ] D4 Replacing sugary foods and refined starchy foods with wholegrain starchy foods, fruit and vegetables increases the fibre and nutrient intake without raising energy.

Dietary fibre: targets and how to meet them (one block)
- [ ] T1 Dietary fibre in the diet is measured as non-starch polysaccharides (the syllabus's term); the target is a daily amount of dietary fibre for adults. The European Food Safety Authority (EFSA) recommends 25 g of fibre a day for adults (EFSA's adequate intake, set in 2010 for normal bowel function). The 2016 paper's stem gives a range of 25–30 g a day and states that many Irish adults do not reach it.
- [ ] T2 Who sets it: EFSA (the European Food Safety Authority); Irish healthy eating guidance from the Department of Health and the HSE (Healthy Ireland Food Pyramid) also advises wholegrain cereals, fruit and vegetables daily.
- [ ] T3 How to reach it (strategies, as in the 2016 scheme and the Ordinary Level schemes): breakfast with high-fibre cereal (look for at least 3 g of fibre per 100 g), porridge or bran, or wholegrain bread or toast; wholemeal or wholegrain bread, brown rice, wholemeal pasta, brown flour in baking; add seeds (sunflower, sesame, pumpkin, linseed), two tablespoons a day, to cereal, yoghurt, bread, salads; pulses (beans, lentils, chickpeas) in soups, stews, salads (the scheme says half a tin of beans gives about 7.5 g fibre, and chickpeas, kidney beans and butter beans about 10 g); vegetables at lunch and dinner (about a third of each meal) with frozen vegetables as good as fresh; potatoes with skins on (most of the fibre is in the skin); fruit, 2–3 pieces a day (a piece gives about 2 g); snacks of nuts, dried fruit, fresh fruit.
- [ ] T4 Practical pointers: leave skins on fruit and vegetables; choose cereals by the label's fibre per 100 g; drink plenty of water with a high-fibre diet (fibre absorbs water). Increase fibre gradually.

Reducing sugar intake (one block)
- [ ] R1 Purchasing: read labels and ingredient lists (added sugars appear as sucrose, glucose syrup, fructose, honey, cane sugar, brown sugar, corn syrup and fruit juice concentrate); choose lower-sugar and no-added-sugar versions of drinks, cereals, yoghurt and jam; choose fruit canned in juice rather than syrup; avoid sugar-coated cereals; front-of-pack colour (traffic-light) labelling, where used, shows at a glance whether a food is high, medium or low in sugars (2017 scheme; it is voluntary).
- [ ] R2 Preparing: reduce the sugar in recipes; flavour with spices, vanilla or ripe fruit; use pureed fruit, banana, dates or apple; do not add sugar to tea, coffee or cereal; replace fizzy drinks with water, or dilute juice with sparkling water.
- [ ] R3 Snacks and treats: fresh fruit, vegetable sticks, nuts and natural yoghurt in place of sweets, biscuits and cakes; limit sweet sauces and sugary drinks.
- [ ] R4 Eat whole fruit rather than fruit juice.
- [ ] R5 Answer pattern in "devise strategies" questions: give purchasing and preparing separately, one or two points each.

Effects of high sugar intake (one block)
- [ ] H1 Tooth decay: bacteria in plaque use sugar to make acid, which dissolves the enamel.
- [ ] H2 Weight gain and obesity: sugary foods are energy-dense and nutrient-poor, so regular intake adds to energy intake.
- [ ] H3 Type 2 diabetes: a risk factor, through excess weight and energy.
- [ ] H4 Heart disease and raised blood pressure (the 2017 scheme links high sugar, including high-fructose intake, with higher blood pressure and with refined carbohydrate causing excess insulin that can raise LDL cholesterol).
- [ ] H5 Nutritional deficiency: sugary foods displace fruit, vegetables and other nutrient-rich foods.
- [ ] H6 Sugary foods are also low in fibre.

Artificial sweeteners (one block)
- [ ] W1 Classification (2011H, 12 marks): intense (high-intensity) sweeteners, many times sweeter than sugar, so tiny amounts are needed and they add almost no energy: aspartame and saccharin. Bulk sweeteners, which have a bulk similar to sugar and supply some energy (less than sugar): sorbitol, mannitol, xylitol.
- [ ] W2 Uses in food manufacture (2011H, 8 marks): sweeten and improve palatability; make low-calorie, "sugar-free", slimming and reduced-sugar foods, soft drinks and confectionery; humectants (retain moisture and stop products drying out; sorbitol); used where sugar's bulk is needed (confectionery, fudge) or to avoid tooth decay (xylitol and sorbitol do not cause tooth decay).
- [ ] W3 Limits: sweeteners do not replace all of sugar's functions: not the bulk, browning, preserving or bulk-texture roles. Sweeteners are food additives with E numbers controlled under Regulation (EC) No 1333/2008 (assumed; one line, owned by the additives chapter). Labels must say "contains sweetener". Aspartame carries a warning for people with phenylketonuria.
- [ ] W4 Excess bulk sweeteners can have a laxative effect (labels carry a warning).

## 3. Traps

- Do not use: "bread, cereals, potatoes, rice and pasta at the base of the Food Pyramid" (the old note), because the current Food Pyramid (Department of Health and HSE, 2016) has vegetables, salad and fruit on the bottom shelf and the wholemeal cereals, breads, potatoes, pasta and rice on the second; the older pyramid with cereals at the base appears in an older paper and is superseded.
- Do not use: "EFSA recommends a daily intake of dietary fibre, measured as non-starch polysaccharides" (the old note), because EFSA's 25 g adequate intake is a figure for dietary fibre measured by a broader method than non-starch polysaccharide alone; the older NSP target (18 g) belonged to earlier UK and Irish guidance and is superseded. Say EFSA recommends a daily intake of dietary fibre and give 25 g; do not give an NSP gram target.
- Do not use: "fruit juice or smoothies contain less fibre" as the reason to choose whole fruit (the old note), because the schemes disagree (the 2017 Ordinary scheme lists smoothies as a fibre source, the 2019 scheme advises whole fruit instead). Say juice contains little fibre and the sugar in juices and smoothies is released from the fruit's cells so it counts as free sugar; whole fruit keeps its fibre and fills you up.
- Do not use: "diabetic foods" (the old note, in the uses of sweeteners), because foods can no longer be marketed with a special "diabetic" status and it is unconfirmed as a current category. Say "low-calorie and sugar-free foods and drinks".
- Do not use: "hyperactivity" or "headaches" as effects of a high-sugar diet (accepted in the 2018 Ordinary scheme), because evidence does not support a sugar-hyperactivity link; keep to the effects in H1 to H5.
- Do not use: Splenda as a bulk sweetener (2011 scheme), because its sweetener, sucralose, is an intense sweetener; use aspartame and saccharin as the examples of intense sweeteners, and name no brands.
- Fibre gives little usable energy, not "no energy"; do not say fibre has 0 kJ; do not use the fibre energy value on labels in an exam calculation.
- The energy value of carbohydrate is 16 kJ (3.75 kcal) per gram, the value used in the course and marking schemes; use it for calculations. Food labels (EU rules) use 17 kJ (4 kcal) per gram for carbohydrate, so a label figure may differ slightly; do not mix the two within one calculation. Protein is 17 kJ (4 kcal).
- Do not give the percentage of daily energy that should come from carbohydrate, sugar-intake limits in grams or teaspoons or the number of servings per shelf of the pyramid; they are changing figures and no scheme gives them.
- Digestion and the use of glucose in the body are in the digestion and metabolism chapter; energy balance, BMI and the daily energy needs are in the energy balance chapter; type 2 diabetes diets are in the meal planning chapter; additive legislation is in the additives chapter.
- Do not describe carbohydrate as "bad": the targets are to eat more wholegrain starchy foods and fibre and less added sugar.
- Do not use the 2017 scheme's blood-sugar range or hypoglycaemia detail; they belong to the diabetes diet subsection.

## 4. What exams ask

- Strategies to increase fibre (Ordinary Level 6 marks; 2024, 2023, 2014, 2011; Higher 2016, 12 marks): three ways, each with a reason or an example, such as bran in cereal, more fruit and vegetables with skins, brown bread, pasta and rice, seeds and nuts, brown flour. The Higher Level version ties them to the EFSA recommendation: breakfast, seeds, beans and pulses, vegetables, potato skins, fruit, brown rice and pasta, snacks.
- Strategies to reduce sugar (2024H 20 marks; 2019O 9 marks; 2017H 20 marks): purchasing and preparing, as in R1 to R5. Give at least one purchasing and one preparing strategy.
- Effects of high sugar intake (2018O: identify three problems; 2017H: assess three effects, one at 4 marks and two at 3): obesity (energy-dense, nutrient-poor), tooth decay, type 2 diabetes, heart disease, high blood pressure and nutritional deficiency; each needs a reason.
- Artificial sweeteners (2011H): classify into intense and bulk with an example of each; outline uses in food manufacture. 2026H also asks about them.
- Energy value: the energy given by carbohydrate per gram and a calculation (30 g sugar); comparison with fat and protein; the Settings tasks (comparing cereals, chocolate and activity).
- Contribution to the diet: foods rich in fibre and starch instead of sugar; which shelf of the pyramid holds starchy foods; 2005H asks to adjust the food sources of carbohydrate in the diet (reduce confectionery, increase fruit and nuts and wholegrain) with a reason.
