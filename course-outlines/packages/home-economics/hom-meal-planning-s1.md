# Package: hom-meal-planning-s1 — Food choices

- **loId:** `hom-meal-planning-s1` · **chapterId:** `hom-meal-planning` · **syllabusRef:** 1.1.1
- **Syllabus scope:** [1.1.1] factors affecting food choices: culture, eating patterns, sensory aspects, nutritional awareness, health status, availability, finance, marketing and advertising. No [HL] items; Ordinary and Higher Level alike.
- **Settings (suggestions only):** discussing differences in meal patterns between societies; a theme day or week on the foods of a culture or period.
- **Also examined (outline):** Q1 data questions on food choices (2025H, 2024H, 2023H, 2019H); food choices of teenagers, students and pre-school children (2022O, 2017O, 2005O). Past papers also ask for changes in Irish eating patterns (2009O, 2012O, 2014O, 2019O), culture and young people (2015O), finance (2018O), health status and purchasing (2022H), health and responsible living in an infographic (2021H), and the reasons teenagers become vegetarian (2016O).
- **Prepared by:** a higher model, 2026-11-23. Neighbouring subsections are named by subject, never by id.
- **Scope note:** this subsection is about why people choose the foods they do. What a given group needs nutritionally is in "Dietary requirements through the life cycle"; special diets are in "Modified diets"; planning a menu is in "Meal management and planning".

## 1. Must cover

The eight syllabus factors (one block, each factor named and explained, with an example)
- [ ] F1 Culture: the shared customs and traditions of a group decide which foods are normal, acceptable or celebratory; certain foods are linked with particular countries or festivals. Travel, television and immigration spread foods from one culture to another (2015O).
- [ ] F2 Religion (examined under culture and under teenage vegetarianism): some Jews and Muslims do not eat pork; some Hindus are vegetarian. Give these as "some", never as every follower.
- [ ] F3 Eating patterns: how many meals, when they are eaten, snacking, eating out, whether the family eats together. Irregular family routines change what is bought and cooked.
- [ ] F4 Sensory aspects: taste, smell, appearance, texture (and temperature) decide whether a food is accepted; likes and dislikes, presentation, and early exposure to fatty, salty and sugary food shape preferences.
- [ ] F5 Nutritional awareness: knowledge of healthy eating guidelines and of food labels leads people to choose lower-fat, lower-sugar, higher-fibre foods; low awareness leads to poorer choices.
- [ ] F6 Health status: allergies and intolerances, coeliac disease, diabetes, coronary heart disease, high blood pressure, weight management limit or direct choices (lactose-free, gluten-free, low-cholesterol, allergy-safe foods) (2022H).
- [ ] F7 Availability: seasonal produce, what local shops stock, distance to a supermarket, access to a car, foods available in a school canteen or vending machine.
- [ ] F8 Finance: income sets the food budget. People with more money buy expensive foods more often, eat out and meet foreign foods on holidays; people with less money choose cheaper foods, own brands, special offers and inexpensive protein such as pulses (2018O, 2026O).
- [ ] F9 Marketing and advertising: packaging, brand image, special offers and shelf placement, celebrity and influencer endorsement, social media; advertising to children and teenagers.
- [ ] F10 Convenience and lifestyle (examined, not a listed syllabus factor): lack of time or cooking skill increases use of ready meals and takeaways (2008O eating food away from home; 2022H). Mention as part of eating patterns and availability.
- [ ] F11 Health and wellbeing and responsible living (2021H infographic): choosing foods for health (less fat, salt and sugar, more fibre) and for ethical and environmental reasons (organic, Fairtrade, local and seasonal, less food waste, animal welfare, sustainability). Treat as examples under nutritional awareness and culture/values.

Changes in Irish eating patterns (one block; examined at Ordinary Level in most years)
- [ ] E1 More convenience, ready-meal and processed foods, with more salt, sugar, saturated fat and additives and less fibre.
- [ ] E2 More eating out, takeaways, fast food and eating on the go; "eating food away from home" covers restaurants, cafés, hotels, takeaways, delicatessens and pub food (2008O).
- [ ] E3 Irregular meals, family members eating at different times, the main meal now in the evening and less often eaten together.
- [ ] E4 A wider food range and foreign influences from travel, television, the internet, immigration and food bloggers: pasta, rice, new breads, ethnic cuisines.
- [ ] E5 New cooking methods: microwaving, stir-frying, barbecuing.
- [ ] E6 Greater health and nutritional awareness: more fruit, vegetables, wholegrain, organic, functional and fortified foods; intermittent fasting and plant-based eating as recent trends (2021H "contemporary trends"). Also less time for cooking at home.

Particular age groups (one block)
- [ ] A1 Pre-school children: parents make the choices; consider nutrition and the food pyramid, cost, degree of processing, allergies and health, portion size and ease of handling, packaging and labelling, family habits (2022O). Young children often refuse new foods; repeated offering, involving them in preparation and attractive presentation help.
- [ ] A2 Teenagers: peers, advertising and social media, pocket money or part-time earnings, independence, wish to eat out, body image, taste preferences for fatty, salty and sugary foods, convenience and busy lives; reasons some teenagers become vegetarian: culture, religion, moral or ethical views (animal welfare), health, environment, economic reasons, family or peers (2016O). Link to the factors that make healthy eating difficult for teenagers (2022H chart).
- [ ] A3 Students in post-primary school (2017O): personal preference, cost, nutritional awareness, advertising, peers, health status and allergies, activity level, the range in the school canteen and vending machines, whether food can be reheated.
- [ ] A4 Students living away or on a budget (assumed, same factors): limited time, money and cooking skill.

Answering data questions (one block, exam technique)
- [ ] D1 Describe the overall trend first, then support it with figures read from the data; compare groups (for example parents and children, or boys and girls) saying which is higher and by how much.
- [ ] D2 Explain differences using the factors in F1–F11, and relate the results to the current healthy eating guidelines (for example, whether five or more daily portions of fruit and vegetables are being reached). Say what this means for health or meal planning.

## 3. Traps

- Do not use: survey percentages and figures from past-paper data questions (for example the SLAN 2007 portion-consumption figures), because they are dated and the papers print the data themselves.
- Do not use: a claim that all followers of a religion avoid a food. Say "some Hindus" and "Jews and Muslims do not eat pork", as the schemes do.
- Culture and religion are different factors in answers: give two separate marks if asked for two factors.
- Do not mix up "availability" (what can be bought) with "finance" (what can be afforded) or with "eating patterns" (when and how often).
- "Nutritional awareness" is about knowledge; "health status" is about a condition that forces a choice.
- Changes in Irish eating patterns are about the whole population over time; teenage or student choices are about a group at one time. Questions on "changes since the beginning of the twentieth century" want trends (more convenience food, less home cooking), not a list of factors.
- Belongs to a neighbouring subsection: the nutrient needs of babies, children and teenagers ("Dietary requirements through the life cycle"); the details of vegetarian and vegan diets ("Modified diets"); how to cost a menu and avoid waste ("Meal management and planning"); food labelling law (in the chapter on food legislation).
- Do not name or promote specific brands, shops or influencers.

## 4. What exams ask

- Outline or discuss two or three factors affecting food choices, often for a named group (Irish consumers, teenagers, students, pre-school children). Expected: name the factor, then explain it with an example in a sentence (finance: more money buys expensive foods and eating out; less money means cheaper foods).
- Explain how culture or religion influences the food choices of young people: foods linked with cultures, religious restrictions, travel, television, immigration and multicultural influences.
- Identify or discuss changes in Irish eating patterns or habits (three at 6 marks Ordinary; four at 16 marks): the E1–E6 list with a short explanation of each effect.
- Suggest reasons why some teenagers become vegetarian (20 marks, four reasons at 5 marks each).
- Data-based Q1 parts at Higher Level: comment and elaborate on a chart (factors making healthy eating hard for teenagers; how health and responsible living affect choices; contemporary eating trends). Expected: trend, comparison, reasons from the factor list, link to guidelines.
- Discuss how health status influences purchases (Higher): name conditions and the foods bought as a result.
- Meaning of "eating food away from home" and why more people do it: more money and choice, less time, lack of cooking skill.
