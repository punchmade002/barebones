## 1. Must cover

Why bacteria cause food poisoning
- [ ] F1 [HL] Food poisoning is illness caused by eating food contaminated with pathogenic (disease-causing) bacteria or their toxins. Most bacteria in food are harmless or useful; only pathogens cause poisoning.
- [ ] F2 [HL] Environmental factors that affect bacterial growth (six): food (protein-rich, moist foods are high risk), moisture, warmth, oxygen requirement, pH (most favour near neutral) and time. Under good conditions a bacterium divides by binary fission about every 20 minutes, so numbers rise very quickly.
- [ ] F3 [HL] The danger zone: 5 to 63 °C is the range in which food poisoning bacteria multiply fastest (stable course figure). Below 5 °C growth slows; above 63 °C most bacteria stop multiplying and thorough cooking destroys them (cooking temperatures belong to safe food preparation).

Grouping micro-organisms (examined)
- [ ] G1 [HL] By temperature (scheme, 2018): psychrophiles grow best at about -5 to 20 °C; mesophiles at about 20 to 45 °C; thermophiles at about 45 to 75 °C. Most food poisoning bacteria are mesophiles, which is why the danger zone is around body temperature (Salmonella optimum about 37 °C).
- [ ] G2 [HL] By oxygen (scheme, 2013): aerobic micro-organisms need oxygen to survive (moulds); anaerobic thrive without oxygen (Clostridium botulinum); facultative adapt to aerobic or anaerobic conditions (Salmonella, Staphylococcus aureus, yeast).

Infectious and toxic food poisoning (examined every few years)
- [ ] I1 [HL] Incubation period: the time between eating the contaminated food and the first symptoms.
- [ ] I2 [HL] Infectious food poisoning: caused by eating food containing large numbers of living pathogenic bacteria; they multiply in the gut; the endotoxins are inside the bacterial cell and released when it dies; endotoxins and bacteria are easily destroyed by proper cooking or reheating; symptoms appear after about 12 hours or more. Examples: Salmonella, E. coli, Campylobacter, Listeria.
- [ ] I3 [HL] Toxic food poisoning: caused by eating food already containing an exotoxin made by bacteria as they grow in the food; exotoxins are hard to destroy (the scheme says boiling for 30 minutes may be needed); symptoms start quickly, often within about 2 hours. Examples: Staphylococcus aureus, Clostridium botulinum, Clostridium perfringens.
- [ ] I4 [HL] Comparison in one line each: infectious = bacteria themselves eaten, slower onset, destroyed by cooking; toxic = toxin eaten, fast onset, toxin survives cooking.

Three strains, each with: structure, habitat and sources, high-risk foods, growth, toxin type, incubation, symptoms
- [ ] S1 [HL] Salmonella (infectious): rod-shaped, Gram-negative, non-spore-forming; reproduces by binary fission (asexual). Habitat and sources: intestines of humans, animals and birds; faeces; polluted water; unwashed hands. High-risk foods: raw or undercooked poultry, meat, fish and eggs; unpasteurised milk and dairy. Growth: food, moisture, time, near-neutral pH; mesophile (20 to 45 °C, optimum about 37 °C); facultative. Incubation: usually about 12 to 36 hours (the wider published range is about 12 to 72 hours). Symptoms: diarrhoea, nausea, vomiting, abdominal pain, fever.
- [ ] S2 [HL] Staphylococcus aureus (toxic): spherical (coccus), Gram-positive, grape-like clusters, non-spore-forming. Habitat and sources: skin, nose, throat and hair of humans; infected cuts and boils; spread to food by handling. High-risk foods: cooked meats, ham, poultry, egg products, cream-filled cakes, custard, salads with mayonnaise. Growth: mesophile; tolerates salt and sugar better than most bacteria; facultative. Toxin: enterotoxin, heat-stable, survives normal cooking. Incubation: short, within a few hours (usually about 2 to 4; the wider published range is about 1 to 6 hours). Symptoms: sudden nausea, vomiting, abdominal cramps, diarrhoea.
- [ ] S3 [HL] Clostridium botulinum (toxic): rod-shaped, Gram-positive, spore-forming, obligate anaerobe. Habitat and sources: soil and water, which reach vegetables and other foods as spores. High-risk foods: home-canned low-acid vegetables, badly processed canned or vacuum-packed foods, cured meats; honey must not be given to babies under one year. Growth: sealed, low-acid, oxygen-free conditions. Toxin: a very potent neurotoxin that blocks nerve signals to muscles. The toxin itself is destroyed by boiling for about 10 minutes; the spores survive ordinary cooking. Incubation: typically 12 to 36 hours. Symptoms: double vision, drooping eyelids, difficulty swallowing and speaking, muscle weakness, paralysis, breathing difficulty; can be fatal.
- [ ] S4 [HL] Other strains the schemes accept (one line each, so a student can choose a different strain): Listeria (infectious; pâté, coleslaw, soft cheese, smoked fish; grows at fridge temperature; serious for pregnant women, newborns, elderly); E. coli (infectious; undercooked minced meat, polluted water, unpasteurised milk); Campylobacter (infectious; raw poultry, unpasteurised milk); Bacillus cereus (cooked rice, reheated rice); Clostridium perfringens (toxic; stews, gravies, large cooked joints cooled slowly).

Contributing factors (Settings: media reports)
- [ ] K1 [HL] Typical contributing factors in outbreaks: undercooking; cooked food left in the danger zone; cross-contamination from raw to cooked or ready-to-eat food; food handlers with poor hygiene or illness; contaminated water or unpasteurised dairy; slow cooling of large batches; inadequate reheating; preparing food too far in advance.
- [ ] K2 [HL] Spotting factors in a short case report: pick out the temperature, time and handling failure and link each to the growth conditions above.

## 3. Traps

- Infectious versus toxic is the most-examined point. Infectious: eat the bacteria; endotoxin in the cell, released at death; killed by cooking; slow. Toxic: eat the toxin; exotoxin made while growing in the food; hard to destroy; fast. Do not swap the toxin words.
- Incubation period is not the length of the illness. Do not define it as how long symptoms last.
- Staphylococcus aureus and Clostridium botulinum are toxic; Salmonella is infectious. Do not call Salmonella toxic.
- Spores are not the toxin: botulinum spores survive ordinary cooking, which is why home canning of low-acid food is risky.
- Temperature groups are about where a micro-organism grows best, not about death temperature.
- Belongs to a neighbouring subsection: the structure and diagram of a bacterial cell, and mould and yeast detail (moulds and yeasts); control by preservation and the principles of controlling spoilage (micro-organisms and the control of spoilage); storage, reheating, hygiene rules (safe food preparation); the role of the FSAI and HSE in outbreaks (national agencies).
- Do not use: "Campylobacter with shellfish" (printed in one scheme), because Campylobacter is mainly from raw poultry and unpasteurised milk.
- Do not use: "Staphylococcus aureus is not killed by cooking", because the bacterium is killed; it is the preformed toxin that survives.
- Do not use: any pH numbers for bacteria, because no checked figure exists (say "near neutral").

## 4. What exams ask

- Name and give a detailed account of one food poisoning bacterium (2004H 20 marks, 2013H, 2019H, 2025H 18 marks): name; description (shape, Gram, spores); habitat and sources; conditions for growth (mesophile, moisture, time, pH, oxygen); reproduction by binary fission; high-risk foods; symptoms. About 2 to 4 marks per point.
- Short: name two or three common food poisoning bacteria with a high-risk food for each (2023H, 2011H); give two parts of a structured table (2017H).
- Differentiate infectious and toxic food poisoning (2004H, 2009H, 2014H, 2021H, 2024H, 2026H): two points each side, with examples; 6 to 10 marks. Define incubation period (2009H).
- State the temperature ranges for psychrophiles, mesophiles and thermophiles (2018H) and differentiate aerobic, anaerobic and facultative (2013H): six marks, two per term.
- Contributing factors in a described case: identify failures in storage, cooking, cross-contamination and hygiene.
