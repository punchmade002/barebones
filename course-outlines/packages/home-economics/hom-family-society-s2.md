# Package: hom-family-society-s2 — The historical development of the Irish family

- **loId:** `hom-family-society-s2` · **chapterId:** `hom-family-society` · **syllabusRef:** 3.1.3
- **Syllabus scope [HL]:** "the historical development of the Irish family from the beginning of the twentieth century to the present day." The whole subsection is Higher Level only [HL]. Also examined: 2025H, 2023H, 2014H (and 2004H).
- **Prepared by:** a higher model, 2026-11-23. The examiners use the same frame every time: a pre-industrial period (about 1900 to 1960), a post-industrial period (about 1960 to 1990) and the modern family (about 1990 to now). 2023H and 2025H set the question as "from the middle of the twentieth century to the present day", so the notes must let a student answer from either start date. The full list of social, economic and technological causes belongs to "Changes affecting family structures"; this subsection is the narrative of what the family looked like and when it changed, with only the key causes needed to explain each period.

## 1. Must cover

Overall frame (one block)
- [ ] F1 [HL] The Irish family is described in three phases: the traditional rural family (to about 1960), the family in a period of industrialisation and rising living standards (about 1960 to 1990), and the modern family (1990s onward). Students should be able to give four or five contrasts between past and present: structure and size, authority and roles, work, marriage and relationships, living standards and State supports.
- [ ] F2 [HL] Summary of past versus present (the 2004H scheme's pairs): extended family to nuclear, blended and lone-parent; agriculture to industry and services; high child mortality to improved life expectancy; patriarchal to egalitarian; large to smaller families (family planning); roles of men and women; education; standard of living.

The family to about 1960 (one block)
- [ ] E1 [HL] Structure: the extended family was the most common, with grandparents, unmarried aunts and uncles and children in one household or nearby, especially in rural areas.
- [ ] E2 [HL] Work: agriculture provided most employment; the farm was the family's economic unit and all members worked on it; farming became less labour-intensive from the 1940s.
- [ ] E3 [HL] Size: large families; child mortality was high.
- [ ] E4 [HL] Authority and roles: patriarchal control (the father decided and held the money and land); strictly segregated gender roles (men outside work, women housework and children); authoritarian discipline.
- [ ] E5 [HL] Marriage: marriages were often arranged or matched, often tied to land and inheritance; many people never married, and marriage came late; emigration took many adult children away.
- [ ] E6 [HL] Religion: the Catholic Church had a strong influence on family life, marriage and morality.
- [ ] E7 [HL] Women's work: the marriage bar required women in the civil service and public sector to leave their jobs on marrying, until it was lifted in 1973 (one bullet).
- [ ] E8 [HL] Rural electrification (from the 1940s, ESB scheme) reduced mothers' workload; the general standard of living improved in the 1950s.

The family from about 1960 to about 1990 (one block)
- [ ] M1 [HL] Free second-level education, announced 1966 and introduced in 1967: more children stayed in school longer.
- [ ] M2 [HL] Industrialisation and the growth of services drew people from farms to towns and cities. The rural extended family declined and the nuclear family became more common; families became more mobile.
- [ ] M3 [HL] Couples married younger and increasingly for love rather than for land or money.
- [ ] M4 [HL] Both parents could work outside the home; more women entered paid employment. Wages and the standard of living rose; children were nurtured and educated for longer.
- [ ] M5 [HL] Family size fell as family planning became available: the Health (Family Planning) Act 1979 allowed contraceptives on prescription for bona fide family planning (in practice, married couples); amendments in 1985, 1992 and 1993 widened access.
- [ ] M6 [HL] Emigration continued in the 1980s; many families were separated when adult children left to find work.
- [ ] M7 [HL] Legal change: the Judicial Separation and Family Law Reform Act 1989 allowed spouses to separate under a court order.

The modern family, 1990s to now (one block)
- [ ] N1 [HL] Divorce: the referendum in November 1995 narrowly approved removing the constitutional ban; the Family Law (Divorce) Act 1996 set the conditions (in operation from 1997): the spouses must have lived apart for a set period (two of the previous three years since the Family Law Act 2019), no reasonable prospect of reconciliation, and proper provision for spouses and dependent children. More lone-parent and blended families followed.
- [ ] N2 [HL] Same-sex couples: civil partnership under the Civil Partnership and Certain Rights and Obligations of Cohabitants Act 2010 (in force 1 January 2011); same-sex marriage approved by referendum on 22 May 2015 and made law by the Marriage Act 2015. Same-sex parent families are a recognised structure.
- [ ] N3 [HL] Smaller families; dual-income families are common; democratic or egalitarian relationships between parents, with shared decisions and household tasks; some fathers are the main carers at home.
- [ ] N4 [HL] Higher standard of living; State supports for families (welfare, childcare schemes, free pre-school, medical cards).
- [ ] N5 [HL] Demand for childcare rose as both parents work; families use crèches, childminders and grandparents.
- [ ] N6 [HL] Role overload and role conflict: parents managing their own children and elderly parents together.
- [ ] N7 [HL] The recession after 2008: unemployment (even among highly qualified parents), mortgage arrears, emigration, stay-at-home fathers.
- [ ] N8 [HL] Communication technology: relatives abroad keep in touch by mobile phone, email, video calls and social media.
- [ ] N9 [HL] Declining religious influence; Ireland more multi-denominational and multicultural. Later and fewer children; cohabitation and births outside marriage are common.
- [ ] N10 [HL] Gender equality: women's education and employment; the Employment Equality Acts 1998 to 2015 prohibit discrimination in employment.

## 3. Traps

- Do not mix up the three periods; the 1960s are the turning point, with free second-level education and the growth of industry.
- Contraception: the 1979 Act did not make contraceptives freely available. Prescription only, bona fide family planning; later Acts liberalised. Say "available on prescription from 1979".
- Do not write "divorce legal in 1995". The referendum was in 1995; the Act is 1996; divorce was available from 1997.
- Civil partnership: Act of 2010, in operation 2011. Same-sex marriage: referendum 2015, Marriage Act 2015.
- The extended family did not disappear; it declined as the typical form. Grandparents remain important in childcare.
- "Patriarchal" is power held by the father, not "men's work". "Egalitarian" is shared power and roles.
- Neighbouring subsections: the list of social, economic and technological changes, with their effect on structure, belongs to "Changes affecting family structures"; characteristics of each family type belong to "Modern family structures"; roles within the family and role conflict belong to the chapter on the family as a caring unit; the legal content of divorce and judicial separation belongs to the family law chapter. Give each one line here.
- Do not use: "Contraception was legalised for married couples in 1979", because the 1979 Act allowed contraceptives only on prescription for bona fide family planning (in practice married couples); sales without prescription came later (1985 onward).
- Do not use: "Civil partnership, introduced in 2010", because the Act is dated 2010 but came into operation on 1 January 2011.
- Do not use: the Anti-Discrimination (Pay) Act 1974 or the Employment Equality Act 1977 (named in an old marking scheme), because they are superseded by the Employment Equality Acts 1998 to 2015.
- Do not use: any census figure for family size, number of births or age at marriage, because these change and no scheme sets them.
- Do not use: "divorce requires four years apart", because the Family Law Act 2019 reduced it to two of the previous three years.

## 4. What exams ask

- "Outline the historical development of the Irish family from the beginning of the twentieth century" (2004H, 2014H: 16 marks, four points at four marks): four developed points, ideally across the periods: extended family, large families and high child mortality; the 1960s move to nuclear families, free second-level education and both parents working; fewer children and a higher standard of living; modern diversity (lone-parent, blended, same-sex), divorce, dual incomes, childcare demand, role overload and egalitarian roles.
- "Discuss / give an account of changes from the middle of the twentieth century to the present" (2023H, 2025H, 20 marks): the schemes list structure and size, location, patriarchal to egalitarian roles, living standards, marriage patterns, income, State supports, communication, childcare, education, gender equality, religion, child mortality and technology. Aim for four or five developed contrasts, each past versus present.
- Tip: one fact per point is not enough; add the reason or consequence (for example, why the extended family declined).
- Candidates should choose their four strongest points and develop each, not list ten words.
