# Package: hom-clothing-selection-s2 — Influences on clothing

- **loId:** `hom-clothing-selection-s2` · **chapterId:** `hom-clothing-selection` · **syllabusRef:** 5.1
- **Syllabus scope:** **[HL]** social, economic and industrial influences on the design and construction of clothing; factors that influence clothing requirements: function, cost versus money available, availability, new fibres, the media, trendsetters, fashion, social and cultural influences. Settings: family photographs and magazine pictures from different decades showing the "look" of the time; comparing past fashion trends with recent ones. Also examined: 2026H, 2024H, 2021H, 2019H, 2018H, 2009H (and 2012H, 2022O, 2021O, 2018O, 2017H, 2016O, 2015H, 2013O, 2011O, 2008O, 2005O).
- **Higher Level marking:** only the "social, economic and industrial influences on the design and construction of clothing" is [HL]. The factors that influence clothing requirements (function, cost, availability, new fibres, media, trendsetters, fashion, culture) are for both levels. Put the [HL] items in their own block.

## 1. Must cover

Factors that influence a person's clothing requirements (both levels)
- [ ] F1 Function: clothing serves protection (weather, injury, heat, cold), warmth, modesty, safety (high visibility, flame-retardant), hygiene, identity (uniforms, professions) and self-expression. Fabric and design follow function: e.g. a waterproof outdoor jacket needs a close-weave water-repellent fabric and covered or sealed seams and openings.
- [ ] F2 Cost versus money available: budget determines value, quality, brand and where a person shops (chain stores, charity shops, online, second-hand); cost per wear and durability matter; low budgets lead to choices such as sales and second-hand.
- [ ] F3 Availability: only what is in the shops or online in the size, style and colour wanted can be bought; local stock, season, delivery, and online shopping widen the choice.
- [ ] F4 New fibres and fabrics: developments such as stretch (elastane/Lycra), microfibres and easy-care blends create new garments (sportswear, hosiery) and give consumers new properties.
- [ ] F5 The media: magazines, newspapers, television, film, advertising and the internet/social media show what is fashionable; young people copy the clothes worn by actors, presenters and celebrities.
- [ ] F6 Trendsetters: public figures, pop stars, singers, sports stars, bloggers and influencers whose clothes are copied. Define the term. Named examples the marking scheme gives: James Dean (jeans, white T-shirt, black leather jacket), Jackie Kennedy (A-line suit and pillbox hat), Princess Diana.
- [ ] F7 Fashion: the style accepted at a given time, changing with seasons and decades, driven by designers, fashion houses, magazines, celebrities and the economy; comparing family photographs with recent fashion shows how the "look" changes.
- [ ] F8 Social and cultural influences: culture sets standards of modesty and traditional dress and inspires design (e.g. the sari, the pashmina shawl, traditional embroidery motifs); religion and customs; peers and acceptance; occasion (school, work, wedding, graduation); lifestyle and personality; climate and season; age; world and sporting events; music and dance (e.g. punk, hip-hop, grunge).
- [ ] F9 Teenagers' choices (examined repeatedly): fashion trends, peers, social media and influencers, lifestyle, budget, comfort, occasion, personality, colour and style, ethics (sustainability).
- [ ] F10 Work wear and uniform: comfort, function, washability, ease of movement, visibility, waterproofing, style, cost and easy care (2024O).

Social, economic and industrial influences on design and construction (Higher Level only)
- [ ] H1 Social: social conformity (the wish to belong); changing status of women and changing male roles (e.g. women's trouser suits, men's dress becoming more varied); media and celebrities; culture, events and occasions, status and profession; awards ceremonies and world events such as the Olympics; leisure and a healthier lifestyle (leisure wear).
- [ ] H2 Economic: consumer spending and disposable income; in prosperity, spending on fashion and power dressing; in a downturn, simpler, more sombre styles and lower-cost materials; production costs (raw materials, labour) set the selling price; investment in research and technology makes new fibres and blends possible and helps new designers emerge.
- [ ] H3 Industrial/technological: computer-aided design (CAD); computerised cutting and sewing and mass production that make garments quicker and cheaper; new fabrics (e.g. scuba, Lycra), new fibres, blends and finishes; fashion shows and instant image sharing (phones, internet) so trends spread worldwide quickly; high-tech sportswear fabrics.
- [ ] H4 How to answer: pair each influence with its effect on the garment (design, fabric, price, construction), not just name it.

Comparing past and present (settings)
- [ ] P1 Use photographs and magazines to show a decade's look; the 1980s look is examined as a party theme (large jewellery, slouch knitted dresses, bright colours, puffed sleeves). Compare how a past trend (e.g. denim, punk, retro) returns in a different form.

## 3. Traps

- Current trends (athleisure, vintage, accessories, social media) are taught in "Current fashion trends"; here, mention social media only as one influence in a line.
- Elements and principles of design, flattering the figure and design evaluation belong to "Elements and principles of design in clothing".
- Fibre production, properties and fabric finishes belong to the textiles chapter's fibres and fabrics material; mention a new fibre only as an influence.
- Design and construction influences are Higher Level only; the functional/cost/availability/media/trendsetter points are common to both levels. Do not label the whole subsection [HL].
- Function is not the same as aesthetic appeal: keep "what it must do" separate from "how it looks".
- Trendsetters are people; fashion is the accepted style; a trend is a short-lived direction. Do not define them interchangeably.
- Do not use: Indian sari and pashmina as the only cultural examples, because over-reliance on them reads as stereotype; give them alongside the general rule that culture sets modesty and dress codes.
- Do not use: "fast transport brings styles to markets within days" and "within hours" as exact claims, because the speed is a changing figure; say "very quickly".
- Do not use: "Garda uniform" or other uniform names as the sole identification example without the principle (a uniform signals role); the principle is what is examined.

## 4. What exams ask

- Higher: discuss or analyse how social influences affect clothing design (2026H, 2021H): celebrities, musicians, athletes, bloggers and influencers expose audiences to silhouettes, colours and brands; fashion houses and designers; environmental concerns; dance and music; travel and culture. Expect two to three developed points.
- Higher 2024H and 2012H/2009H: discuss social and economic (and industrial) factors affecting design and construction; give one or two points each with effect on the garment.
- Higher 2018H: analyse technological influences: CAD, new fabrics and finishes, international fashion shows with images sent quickly to magazines.
- Higher 2019H: how the economy affects design and fashion: reduced spending leads to simpler sombre styles and low-cost materials; investment supports CAD, new fibres and new designers.
- Describe, with one example, how function influences choice (2026H, 2021H): protection, comfort, movement, fabric and design features; example a waterproof jacket with a water-repellent close-woven fabric and covered fastenings.
- Ordinary and Higher: how factors such as available money, climate and cultural influences affect clothing choice (2011O, 2021O); three factors influencing teenagers' choices (2022O, 2019H); how the media influences teenagers (2016O); define trendsetters with an example (2008O); three factors causing fashion changes (2018O, 2008O).
- Music and clothing (2015H): name a genre/subculture and how it shaped dress (punk, goth, hip-hop).
- Coursework link: design specification must justify choices against function, cost and the wearer; the exam answers here supply that vocabulary.
