# Package: hom-digestion-metabolism-s1 — Digestion and absorption of protein

## 1. Must cover

Hydrolysis and enzymes (assumed)
- [ ] H1 (assumed) Digestion is the breakdown of large food molecules into small soluble ones that can pass through the wall of the small intestine into the blood. It is chemical digestion, carried out by enzymes.
- [ ] H2 (assumed) Enzymes are protein catalysts: they speed up a reaction without being used up, and each acts on one substrate because its active site fits that substrate's shape. Digestive enzymes work at body temperature (about 37 °C), each with an optimum pH.
- [ ] H3 Hydrolysis is the splitting of a bond by adding water (H joins one part, OH the other). Protein is a chain of amino acids joined by peptide bonds; proteases (proteinases) hydrolyse these bonds.
- [ ] H4 Diagram: two amino acids joined by a peptide bond, with the water added during hydrolysis shown.
- [ ] H5 Order of products as protein is digested: protein, then peptones (shorter chains), then peptides (shorter again), then amino acids. Only amino acids (and the smallest peptides) are small enough to absorb.

The digestion sequence (the table)
- [ ] T1 Table to learn in full: organ, secretion, enzyme, substrate, product.
  - Stomach: gastric juice; pepsin; protein; peptones.
  - Stomach (infants): gastric juice; rennin; caseinogen; casein.
  - Pancreas (acts in the duodenum): pancreatic juice; trypsin (a proteinase); peptones; peptides.
  - Small intestine: intestinal juice; peptidases (also called erepsin); peptides; amino acids.
- [ ] T2 Mouth: no protein digestion, because saliva has no protein-digesting enzyme. Chewing breaks food into pieces and so increases surface area, but this is mechanical only.
- [ ] T3 Stomach: gastric glands in the stomach wall secrete gastric juice containing hydrochloric acid and the inactive enzyme pepsinogen. Acid unfolds (denatures) the protein, converts pepsinogen into active pepsin, and gives the acid pH (about 2) pepsin needs. Acid also kills most bacteria in food.
- [ ] T4 Pepsin hydrolyses protein to peptones. The stomach churns food into chyme, a semi-liquid that passes slowly into the duodenum.
- [ ] T5 Rennin in the stomach of infants changes the soluble milk protein caseinogen into insoluble casein (curds), so that milk stays in the stomach long enough for pepsin to act. (Same idea as cheese-making, taught elsewhere in the course.)
- [ ] T6 Duodenum: the pancreas secretes pancreatic juice containing trypsinogen. Enterokinase, an enzyme from the lining of the small intestine, converts it into active trypsin. Trypsin hydrolyses peptones to peptides. Pancreatic juice is alkaline (it also contains alkaline salts), so it neutralises the acid chyme and gives trypsin its working pH.
- [ ] T7 Small intestine: glands in the intestinal wall secrete intestinal juice containing peptidases (erepsin), which hydrolyse peptides to amino acids. Protein digestion is complete.
- [ ] T8 Why enzymes are made inactive (pepsinogen, trypsinogen): so they do not digest the cells that make them. One sentence only.
- [ ] T9 Diagram: the digestive system, labelling mouth, oesophagus, stomach, liver, pancreas, small intestine and large intestine, with where protein digestion begins and ends marked.

Absorption and route
- [ ] A1 Absorption is the passage of digested nutrients through the wall of the small intestine into the blood or lymph. The small intestine is long, and its lining is folded into villi (finger-like projections) whose cells have microvilli, so surface area is very large. Each villus has a thin one-cell-thick wall, a capillary network and a central lacteal.
- [ ] A2 Amino acids are absorbed mainly by active transport (uses energy and carrier proteins, coupled with sodium ions) through the cells of the villus into the capillaries. Very small peptides (two or three amino acids) may be absorbed too and are broken down inside the cells.
- [ ] A3 Route: mouth (no digestion), stomach (pepsin; rennin in infants), duodenum (trypsin, then peptidases), villi (amino acids into capillaries), capillaries join to form the hepatic portal vein, which carries the amino acids to the liver, which processes them before they go into the general circulation.
- [ ] A4 Diagram: cross-section of a villus labelling the capillary network, lacteal, epithelial wall and microvilli.

Utilisation of amino acids [HL]
- [ ] U1 [HL] Body cells take amino acids from the blood to build body protein for growth and repair, and to make enzymes, hormones and antibodies.
- [ ] U2 [HL] The liver makes plasma proteins (for example albumin) from amino acids and passes the rest on in the blood. The body has no store of amino acids, so amino acids not needed are broken down in the liver.
- [ ] U3 [HL] Deamination: in the liver the amino group (–NH₂) is removed from the amino acid and converted to ammonia, then to urea. Urea is carried in the blood to the kidneys and excreted in urine.
- [ ] U4 [HL] The remaining carbon part (with its carboxyl group, –COOH) is oxidised to release heat and energy, or converted to glucose or fat. This is why protein is an energy source when eaten in excess.

## 3. Traps

- Pepsin acts on **protein** and produces **peptones**; trypsin acts on **peptones** and produces **peptides**. Students swap them. The 2010H scheme also accepts protein as trypsin's substrate, but teach the clean sequence so the table is not ambiguous.
- The pancreas is a gland, not part of the alimentary canal: food never passes through it. Its juice reaches the duodenum by the pancreatic duct.
- Rennin works on **caseinogen (soluble) to casein (insoluble)**, in infants. Do not say it digests lactose or that it is the same as pepsin. The same enzyme is used in cheese-making; do not teach cheese-making here.
- Bile is not a protein-digesting secretion and has no enzymes; it belongs to lipid digestion. The mouth has no protein digestion.
- Protein-digesting enzymes are themselves proteins. They are made inactive (pepsinogen, trypsinogen) and activated only where needed, which is why they do not digest the cells that make them (T8).
- Amino acids go to the liver by the hepatic portal vein; they do not enter the lacteal. The lacteal is the route for fat (see the lipids subsection).
- Deamination is the breakdown of excess amino acids. Do not confuse it with digestion or with the making of body protein. Do not call ammonia the final excretory product; it is turned to urea first because ammonia is toxic.
- Essential amino acids, protein quality, complementary proteins and protein deficiency belong to the protein chapter, not here.
- Do not use: the old-note statement that small peptides "not fully hydrolysed" are absorbed through the villi without qualification, because it can lead students to answer "peptides" in a product box where the scheme wants amino acids; use A2's wording (very small peptides only, digested inside the cell).
- Do not give amounts of protein (grams per day), because requirements change and no scheme here states them.

## 4. What exams ask

- A six-mark table in Section A, repeatedly (2025H, 2010H): a given organ or secretion is shown, and the student fills in enzyme, substrate and product. Pancreas is the favourite row. The answer is the T1 row, using the exact words. Expect stomach and small intestine rows too, so each row of T1 needs a card.
- A fill-in sentence at Ordinary Level (2008O): rennin changes caseinogen to casein in the stomach, and trypsin from the pancreas changes peptones to peptides in the duodenum. Teach the two sentences in that form.
- Absorption and route (2006O): amino acids pass through the walls of the villi into the capillary network that joins up to form the hepatic portal vein, which carries nutrients to the liver. The student must name the villi and the vein.
- A longer answer for Higher Level (Section B or C): trace protein from mouth to liver, naming organ, secretion, enzyme, substrate and product at each stage, then how the products are absorbed. Expected content: T1 to T7 and A1 to A3, with the acid's roles in the stomach.
- Utilisation, Higher Level: marks for the uses of amino acids (growth and repair, enzymes, hormones, antibodies), the liver making plasma proteins, and deamination to urea (see the examined wording in the protein chapter: excess amino acids broken down in the liver, amino group to ammonia then urea then excreted in urine, remaining group oxidised for heat and energy). Each of these should be a short fact card.
- Tips to make: always name the organ and the secretion as well as the enzyme; learn substrate and product as a pair; peptones before peptides before amino acids; the pancreatic enzyme works in the duodenum, not in the pancreas; name the hepatic portal vein, never just "a vein".
