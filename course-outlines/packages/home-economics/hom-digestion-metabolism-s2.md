# Package: hom-digestion-metabolism-s2 — Digestion and absorption of carbohydrates

- **loId:** `hom-digestion-metabolism-s2` · **chapterId:** `hom-digestion-metabolism` · **syllabusRef:** 1.1.3
- **Syllabus scope:** hydrolysis, digestion sequence and absorption mechanism; **[HL]** outline of the utilisation of glucose. Also examined: the digestion table for carbohydrates (2022H, 2007H); the same table in 2018H.
- **Prepared by:** a higher model, 2026-11-23. This subsection takes carbohydrate only. The classification and nutritional role of carbohydrates (sugars, starch, fibre, sources, functions) belong to the carbohydrates chapter; the names mono-, di- and polysaccharide are assumed. Protein and lipid digestion are separate subsections. Ordinary and Higher Level share everything except the utilisation of glucose, which is Higher Level only.
- **Exam style:** a six-mark table in Section A: a secretion or organ is given and the student supplies enzyme, substrate and product. Ordinary Level asks true/false or fill-in (for example that carbohydrate is converted to glucose during digestion).

## 1. Must cover

Hydrolysis (assumed and core)
- [ ] H1 (assumed) Digestion turns large food molecules into small soluble ones by hydrolysis, using enzymes (biological catalysts, each specific to one substrate, working at about 37 °C).
- [ ] H2 Carbohydrate hydrolysis splits glycosidic bonds by adding water. Starch is a polysaccharide of many glucose units. The disaccharides are maltose (glucose + glucose), sucrose (glucose + fructose) and lactose (glucose + galactose). The end products are the monosaccharides glucose, fructose and galactose, the only forms small enough to be absorbed.
- [ ] H3 Enzyme names end in -ase and name the substrate: amylase (starch), maltase (maltose), sucrase (sucrose), lactase (lactose).
- [ ] H4 Diagram: two glucose units joined by a glycosidic bond, with the water added in hydrolysis shown, giving two monosaccharides.

The digestion sequence (the table)
- [ ] T1 Table to learn in full: organ, secretion, enzyme, substrate, product.
  - Mouth: saliva (from the salivary glands); salivary amylase; starch; dextrins and maltose.
  - Pancreas (acts in the duodenum): pancreatic juice; pancreatic amylase; starch; maltose.
  - Small intestine: intestinal juice; maltase; maltose; glucose.
  - Small intestine: intestinal juice; sucrase; sucrose; glucose and fructose.
  - Small intestine: intestinal juice; lactase; lactose; glucose and galactose.
- [ ] T2 Mouth: chewing breaks food up (mechanical), and saliva (with salivary amylase) begins starch digestion. Digestion in the mouth is brief because food is swallowed quickly, so most starch reaches the small intestine undigested. Dextrins are shorter chains of glucose units.
- [ ] T3 Stomach: the acid (hydrochloric acid) denatures salivary amylase, so starch digestion stops. The stomach makes no carbohydrate-digesting enzyme. Starch, dextrins and maltose leave in the chyme.
- [ ] T4 Duodenum: pancreatic juice enters by the pancreatic duct. Pancreatic amylase hydrolyses the remaining starch and dextrins to maltose. Pancreatic juice is alkaline, which neutralises the chyme.
- [ ] T5 Small intestine: intestinal juice from glands in the wall contains maltase, sucrase and lactase, which finish the job. Each disaccharidase acts on its own substrate.
- [ ] T6 Dietary fibre (non-starch polysaccharide) is not digested by human enzymes, so it passes into the large intestine. It is the carbohydrate that is not hydrolysed.
- [ ] T7 Diagram: the digestive system, labelling mouth, salivary glands, stomach, pancreas, small intestine and where each enzyme acts.

Absorption and route
- [ ] A1 The small intestine is adapted for absorption: very long, lining folded into villi with microvilli on their cells, so surface area is large; each villus has a thin wall and a capillary network (and a central lacteal, used for fat).
- [ ] A2 Glucose and galactose are absorbed by active transport (uses energy and carrier proteins, coupled with sodium ions). Fructose is absorbed by facilitated diffusion (down its concentration gradient through carrier proteins, no energy).
- [ ] A3 The monosaccharides enter the capillaries of the villi, which join to form the hepatic portal vein. The vein carries all of them to the liver. Fructose and galactose are converted to glucose in the liver (assumed detail, one clause).
- [ ] A4 Route: mouth (salivary amylase), stomach (digestion pauses), duodenum (pancreatic amylase), small intestine (disaccharidases), villi (absorption), hepatic portal vein, liver, then the general circulation.
- [ ] A5 Diagram: cross-section of a villus with the capillary network, lacteal and direction of movement.

Utilisation of glucose [HL]
- [ ] U1 [HL] Glucose in the blood is carried to the cells, where it is oxidised in respiration to release energy (some as heat, some stored as ATP). It is the main fuel for the brain and red blood cells.
- [ ] U2 [HL] Excess glucose is converted to glycogen (a branched polysaccharide of glucose) and stored in the liver and muscles. Once these are full, the extra is converted to fat and stored in adipose tissue. (2025H-era schemes in the carbohydrates chapter: excess carbohydrate converted to glycogen, stored in liver and muscles, or to fat.)
- [ ] U3 [HL] Blood glucose is kept steady by two hormones from the pancreas: insulin (from the beta cells) lowers blood glucose by promoting uptake and storage as glycogen; glucagon (from the alpha cells) raises it by causing the liver to break glycogen back into glucose.
- [ ] U4 [HL] Glucose releases heat and energy, and carbohydrate spares protein, so protein is used for growth and repair instead of energy.

## 3. Traps

- Amylase acts on **starch**, giving **maltose** (and dextrins in the mouth). Maltase acts on **maltose**, giving glucose. Students blur these; the table must keep the pairs separate.
- Starch digestion is in two places, the mouth and the duodenum, with a pause in the stomach. Do not say the stomach digests carbohydrate.
- Do not say all disaccharides give glucose only. Sucrase gives glucose and fructose; lactase gives glucose and galactose. Do not name the end products as "sugar".
- The pancreas is a gland; its juice does the digesting in the duodenum. Insulin and glucagon come from the same organ but are hormones released into the blood, not digestive secretions; do not confuse the two jobs of the pancreas.
- Salivary amylase (mouth) versus pancreatic amylase (duodenum) are different enzymes for the same substrate; name which one when asked.
- Lactose intolerance (lactase deficiency), coeliac disease and diabetes belong to the health and disease chapters; do not treat them here. Fibre's role in the diet belongs to the carbohydrates chapter.
- Glycogen is in the liver and muscles, not in the pancreas or blood. Glycogen is the animal store; starch is the plant store.
- Do not use: "Liver: receives the glucose and galactose first" from the old notes, because fructose also travels in the hepatic portal vein to the liver; say the vein carries all the monosaccharides, and the liver converts fructose and galactose to glucose.
- Do not give blood glucose concentrations, because the notes need no figure and ranges vary by source.

## 4. What exams ask

- The Section A table, in 2022H (pancreatic juice: amylase, starch, maltose), 2018H (pancreas and small intestine rows: amylase on starch giving maltose; maltase on maltose giving glucose; sucrase on sucrose giving glucose and fructose; lactase on lactose giving glucose and galactose) and 2007H (saliva and intestinal juice rows: salivary amylase on starch giving dextrins and maltose; lactase on lactose giving glucose and galactose). Expect any row, so teach all five.
- Ordinary Level 2023: a true/false statement that carbohydrate is converted into glucose during digestion (true), alongside a false statement that bile is made in the stomach. Ordinary Level 2006: salivary amylase starts the digestion of starch in the mouth.
- Higher Level long answers: describe the digestion of starch through the alimentary canal and its absorption. Expected content: T2 to T6, A2 and A3.
- Higher Level utilisation: marks for oxidation to give heat and energy, storage as glycogen in liver and muscles, conversion of excess to fat, and the roles of insulin and glucagon if the question is about regulation. In the carbohydrates chapter, schemes give: excess carbohydrate to glycogen stored in the liver and muscles.
- Tips to make: name the secretion and the enzyme with the organ; the last enzyme row is the disaccharidases; salivary versus pancreatic amylase; end products are monosaccharides; fructose differs in how it is absorbed; "glucose" alone is not enough for lactose, give galactose too.
