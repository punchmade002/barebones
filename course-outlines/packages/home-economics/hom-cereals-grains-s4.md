## 1. Must cover

Effects of heat on cereals (one block; 2022O, 12 marks)
- [ ] H1 Moist heat (boiling, steaming): starch granules absorb liquid, swell and burst (gelatinisation); the cereal softens and thickens its liquid.
- [ ] H2 Dry heat (baking, toasting, roasting): starch breaks down into dextrins (dextrinisation), browning and crisping the surface; sugars caramelise, adding colour and flavour. In frying, the starch absorbs fat (2022 scheme).
- [ ] H3 Cellulose in the bran and cell walls softens, so cooked cereal is easier to digest.
- [ ] H4 Protein coagulates, setting the structure of batters, doughs and baked goods.
- [ ] H5 Some B group vitamins are lost, more if they leach into cooking water that is discarded.
- [ ] H6 Colour, flavour and texture change.

Gelatinisation (one block; 2004H, 3 marks)
- [ ] GE1 Definition: when starch is heated with liquid, the granules absorb water, swell and burst, and the mixture thickens.
- [ ] GE2 Stages: granules absorb water and swell; amylose leaks out; the liquid thickens as the swollen granules and amylose form a gel-like network; if heated further, granules burst.
- [ ] GE3 Starch granules must be separated before heating (stirring, blending flour with a little cold liquid, rubbing fat in a roux) to avoid lumps; the sauce must cook long enough or it tastes floury.
- [ ] GE4 Roux: a cooked mixture of equal weights of fat and flour (the 2024 scheme counts "different amounts of fat and flour" as false), used as the base of a sauce; the fat coats the flour granules and stops lumps.

Cooking rice and other cereals (one block; 2025O)
- [ ] R1 Two effects of cooking on rice the scheme accepts: grains swell, burst and absorb liquid; the fibre softens; gelatinisation occurs; some vitamin B is lost; colour changes and it becomes digestible.
- [ ] R2 Methods: boiling in excess water (drained), or absorption (measured water, all absorbed, covered, off the heat to finish). Rinsing removes surface starch so grains separate.
- [ ] R3 Brown rice takes longer because the bran layer slows water entering the grain.
- [ ] R4 Other cereals: pasta boiled in plenty of boiling water until al dente; oats (porridge) cooked in milk or water, stirred as the starch gelatinises.
- [ ] R5 Reducing vitamin loss: use only the water needed, cover, do not overcook, use cooking liquid in soups where practical.

Raising agents (one block; 2023O, 2011O, 6 marks)
- [ ] A1 Principle: a raising agent produces or traps a gas (air, steam or carbon dioxide) that expands in the heat of baking; the protein and starch set around the bubbles to hold the structure.
- [ ] A2 Air (physical): beating, whisking eggs, creaming fat and sugar, sieving flour, folding, rubbing in; used in sponge cakes, meringues, shortcrust pastry (slightly).
- [ ] A3 Steam: water turns to steam in the hot oven, expanding the mixture, as in choux, puff and rough puff pastry, Yorkshire pudding, pancakes.
- [ ] A4 Yeast (biological): ferments sugar in warm, moist dough, producing carbon dioxide and alcohol (and releasing energy); yeast works best at about 35-40 degrees Celsius and is killed above about 55-60 degrees Celsius; gluten traps the gas so the dough rises (proving); baking kills the yeast.
- [ ] A5 Bread soda (bicarbonate of soda): releases carbon dioxide when it reacts with an acid, such as buttermilk, in the presence of moisture and heat; used in soda bread, scones.
- [ ] A6 Baking powder: bicarbonate of soda plus an acid salt and a starch filler; acid and alkali react when moist and heated to release carbon dioxide; used in cakes, muffins, scones.
- [ ] A7 Self-raising flour: plain flour with baking powder already added.
- [ ] A8 Named products (schemes): bread - yeast, bread soda, air or baking powder; sponge cake - air or self-raising flour/baking powder; muffins - baking powder or bread soda; lemon drizzle cake - baking powder or air. Each raising agent in an answer must be different.
- [ ] A9 Conditions for success: accurate measuring, correct oven temperature, no opening the oven early, correct handling.

## 3. Traps

- Do not use: "gelatinisation occurs at 100 degrees Celsius" or any exact temperature, because starch types vary (it begins at roughly 60 to 80 degrees Celsius and no scheme asks for a figure).
- Do not use: "baking powder is bicarbonate of soda and cream of tartar only", because it contains an acid salt and a starch filler (cream of tartar is one acid salt but not the only one).
- Do not use: "flour thickens by absorbing liquid only", because gelatinisation also needs heat; flour left cold in liquid just forms a paste.
- Gluten is protein, not starch; gluten's rise comes from trapping gas, not producing it.
- Dextrinisation (dry-heat browning of starch) is different from caramelisation (browning of sugar) and from enzymic or Maillard browning; keep to dextrinisation and caramelisation here.
- Neighbouring subsections: starch chemistry, hydrolysis and pectin gels in the carbohydrate chapter; sauce classification and roux, blended and all-in-one methods, and the heat-transfer methods in the cooking-methods chapter; pastry method in the pastry subsection; gluten and flour types in the milling subsection.
- Fat absorbed in frying is a heat effect; do not teach deep-fat frying safety here.

## 4. What exams ask

- Outline two or three effects of heat on cereals (starch swelling and bursting in moist heat; dextrinisation; absorbing fat; cellulose softening; protein coagulating; B vitamin loss; caramelising).
- Explain gelatinisation (3 marks).
- Outline two effects of cooking on rice (swelling and absorbing liquid, fibre softening, gelatinisation, vitamin loss, colour change).
- Name a suitable raising agent for named baked products (bread, sponge cake, muffins, lemon drizzle cake); explain the principle behind one (e.g. yeast ferments sugar producing carbon dioxide and alcohol; bread soda reacts with an acid).
- True or false statements on bran fibre, starch granules and roux.
