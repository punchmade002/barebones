# Package: hom-energy-nutrition-s2 — Energy balance

- **loId:** `hom-energy-nutrition-s2` · **chapterId:** `hom-energy-nutrition` · **syllabusRef:** 1.2.1
- **Syllabus scope:** the importance of balancing energy intake and output. Settings: case studies of lifestyle and diet with recommendations to adjust intake and expenditure; menus for the individuals or groups in the case studies. Also examined: causes and health risks of obesity (2015O, 2013H, 2007O); BMI from data (2017H); energy balance defined (2005O, 2019O, 2024O); empty kilocalories (2024O).
  No item in this subsection is Higher Level only.
- **Prepared by:** a higher model, 2026-11-23. Energy needs themselves (BMR, growth, activity, the factors) are in "Energy requirements and the role of energy". The kJ per gram values are in "Energy value of the nutrients". Healthy eating guidelines and the Food Pyramid are in "Dietary guidelines"; this subsection only uses them to write menus. Diabetes and heart disease as conditions in their own right belong to the special diets material; here they are named as risks of obesity.

## 1. Must cover

Energy balance (one block)
- [ ] B1 Energy input is the energy in the food and drink taken in; energy output (expenditure) is the energy the body uses for BMR, physical activity, growth and digestion.
- [ ] B2 Energy balance: input equals output, so body weight stays steady (2024O fill-in, 2005O, 2019O).
- [ ] B3 Positive energy balance: intake is greater than output; the surplus is converted to fat and stored in adipose tissue, so weight is gained (2019O scheme: "excess energy is stored as adipose fat").
- [ ] B4 Negative energy balance: output is greater than intake; the body draws on its stores (glycogen first, then fat) and weight is lost.
- [ ] B5 Importance of balance: maintains a healthy body weight, prevents overweight and obesity and their diseases, and supplies energy for work, study, growth and activity. (2019O: "outline the importance of balancing energy intake and energy output", 6 marks.)
- [ ] B6 Underweight or prolonged negative balance: a short statement that too little energy leads to weight loss, tiredness and poor resistance to infection. Do not teach eating disorders here.

Empty kilocalories (one block)
- [ ] K1 Empty kilocalories (empty calories) are calories from foods that supply energy but little or no nutritional value (2024O): sweets, sugary drinks, crisps, alcohol, cakes and biscuits, fried snack foods.
- [ ] K2 They raise energy intake without protein, vitamins, minerals or fibre, so they can cause weight gain while the diet is still poor in nutrients. Over-indulgence in sugary food can cause obesity (2024O).
- [ ] K3 Swap: replace them with fruit, vegetables, wholegrain foods, lean protein or low-fat dairy, giving more nutrients for the same or less energy.

Adjusting intake and output; case studies (one block, method)
- [ ] C1 Reduce intake: smaller portions, fewer sugary drinks, sweets, fried and fatty foods, more fruit and vegetables and fibre (which fills), grill or bake instead of frying, avoid snacking on high-energy foods (2007O, 2009O suggestions).
- [ ] C2 Increase output: daily walking or cycling, regular sport or gym, stairs rather than lifts, active leisure, less time sitting at the television or computer (2009O: join a gym or sport, cut television time).
- [ ] C3 Slow, sustained change beats crash dieting (a short statement; a sustained gradual loss).
- [ ] C4 Case-study method: (1) identify the person's energy output (job, activity, age, growth or pregnancy); (2) assess the energy input from the menu; (3) state whether the balance is positive or negative and its effect on weight; (4) recommend a change to diet; (5) recommend a change to lifestyle with a named activity; (6) write a menu for the day that follows the healthy eating guidelines.
- [ ] C5 Menus for case studies: three meals for a day for a person who is obese (2015O b), a teenager who plays sport (2019O b), a person with a high energy requirement (2013O b), a family with young children (2007O c). Guide for the weight-reducing menu: low-fat milk, grilled or poached foods, wholegrain cereals, vegetables and salad, fruit, water; one example menu laid out as breakfast, lunch, dinner, snacks. The guidelines for the menu come from "Dietary guidelines".
- [ ] C6 Diagram: none needed. A simple balance diagram (input on one side, output on the other, with positive or negative tilt) is optional.

Obesity: causes (one block)
- [ ] O1 Definition: obesity is a condition of excess body fat that harms health, usually the result of a long-term positive energy balance. Overweight is milder.
- [ ] O2 Causes (2015O scheme, two to four with explanation): imbalance of energy intake and expenditure; a sedentary lifestyle; lack of exercise; psychological factors such as depression, boredom and stress (comfort eating); hormonal imbalance such as an underactive thyroid gland; unhealthy eating patterns (snacking, skipped meals, takeaways); poor food choices (processed foods, sugary drinks, fatty foods); large portion sizes.
- [ ] O3 Childhood obesity (2007O): causes include sweets and fast food, sugary drinks, too much television and computer time, less outdoor play, and advertising aimed at children, parents using food as a reward. Do not give numbers from the paper.
- [ ] O4 Genetic and family factors may be mentioned in one clause as influences, but the schemes stress lifestyle causes.

Obesity: health risks (one block)
- [ ] R1 Health risks (2015O, 2013H, 2007O; give two to four with how): type 2 diabetes (excess fat makes the body less responsive to insulin); coronary heart disease and stroke; high blood pressure; raised blood cholesterol; gallstones; arthritis and joint pain, back pain (weight stresses joints); some cancers; breathing difficulties and sleep apnoea; depression and low self-esteem; problems in pregnancy and childbirth; reduced life expectancy. Varicose veins and infertility are also correct risks; give them after the four the schemes name (type 2 diabetes, high blood pressure, high cholesterol, heart disease).
- [ ] R2 Link to lifestyle: type 2 diabetes can be caused by sedentary lifestyle and unhealthy diet (2014O true/false: True). Bowel disease is not caused by eating high-fibre foods (2014O: False).
- [ ] R3 Corrective measures: return to energy balance by the changes in C1 to C3, support from a doctor, dietitian or a weight management programme.

BMI (one block)
- [ ] M1 BMI = weight in kilograms divided by height in metres squared (kg/m²).
- [ ] M2 Adult categories: below 18.5 underweight; 18.5 to 24.9 healthy weight; 25 to 29.9 overweight; 30 and above obese.
- [ ] M3 Worked calculation with sample data (for instance 70 kg and 1.75 m: 1.75 squared is 3.0625; 70 divided by 3.0625 is about 22.9, healthy). Show every step.
- [ ] M4 Limits: BMI does not separate muscle from fat, so a muscular athlete can read as overweight; waist measurement is a useful addition because fat around the abdomen carries greater risk (no cut-offs). Children use age and sex charts, not the adult categories.
- [ ] M5 Reading BMI from data (2017H): read the table or chart, find the share of each group in each category, compare groups, and link to diet and activity (see the data-reading method in "The Irish diet"; give a short version here).

## 3. Traps

- "Calories" and "kilocalories" are the same; do not mix kJ and kcal in one calculation without converting (1 kcal is about 4.2 kJ).
- Positive does not mean good. Positive energy balance is the surplus that causes weight gain.
- Obesity is not simply "eating too much fat"; it is any long-term excess of energy.
- BMI is a screening measure, not a diagnosis; do not state that BMI over 25 always means unhealthy.
- Calculation mistake: height must be in metres and squared; weight divided by the squared height, not multiplied.
- Do not call an underactive thyroid the usual cause of obesity; it is a hormonal cause that applies to some people.
- Energy requirements of different people belong to the previous subsection; the Food Pyramid and RDAs belong to "Dietary guidelines"; diabetes as a condition and the diabetic diet belong to the special diets material.
- Do not use: "obese when weight is 20% or more above the recommended weight" (2014O), because it is the old weight-for-height definition; use BMI of 30 and above.
- Do not use: the 2007O data on childhood obesity (the EU yearly increase and the estimated numbers who will develop type 2 diabetes), because they are changing statistics from 2007.
- Do not use: any calorie target for weight loss (such as "500 kcal a day less") or a safe rate of weight loss in kilograms, because they are changing guidance figures not in the schemes.
- Do not use: waist-circumference cut-offs, because the figures differ by sex and source and are not in the syllabus.

## 4. What exams ask

- Define energy balance, or fill in the term (2024O, 2005O): input equals output keeps weight steady; surplus is stored as fat.
- Outline the importance of balancing intake and output (2019O, 6 marks): excess stored as adipose fat and weight is gained; a deficit gives weight loss; balance keeps the correct weight and good health.
- Define empty kilocalories (2024O): calories with little nutritional value, for example sugary foods; they lead to obesity.
- Discuss the causes and the health risks of obesity (2015O, 20 marks; 2007O: three causes of obesity in children, two risks, 12 and 8 marks; 2013H). Expected answer content: at least two causes each with a sentence of explanation and two or three risks each with a how, followed by a corrective measure when asked.
- Plan or set out a menu of three meals for a person who is obese, a sport-playing teenager or a family (2015O, 2019O, 2007O), having regard to healthy eating guidelines: breakfast, lunch, dinner each with two or three items, low-fat choices, fibre, fruit and vegetables, water, and a reason linked to energy.
- Evaluate a given day's menu for energy value and healthy eating and suggest lifestyle and diet changes (2009O): identify the high-energy items, state the energy balance, recommend changes to diet and to exercise.
- BMI from data (2017H): calculate or read from a table, say which category each group falls in, compare groups and explain a link to diet and lifestyle.
- Exam-tip material: write "excess energy is stored as adipose (fat) tissue"; give a cause and its mechanism; tie each recommended change to the cause you identified.
