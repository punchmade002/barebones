## 1. Must cover

What minerals are, and the two groups
- [ ] M1 Minerals are inorganic elements (not made by living things), needed in the diet in small amounts; they build body structures, regulate body processes and form parts of enzymes and hormones. They are not destroyed by cooking, but they can be lost into cooking water and in the trimming of food.
- [ ] M2 Major (macro) minerals: needed in larger amounts: calcium, phosphorus, potassium, sodium, chloride, magnesium and sulphur. (Identification of these is the syllabus's "major mineral elements".)
- [ ] M3 [HL] Trace (micro) minerals: needed in only tiny amounts (milligrams or micrograms a day): iron, zinc, iodine, fluoride, copper, selenium, manganese, chromium, cobalt. Name at least four and the amount idea (tiny, yet essential). Iron is a trace element by amount but is studied at Ordinary Level in detail.
- [ ] M4 RDA: the daily amount that meets the needs of almost everyone in a group; it differs with age, sex, pregnancy and breastfeeding. Examples of groups with raised need: teenagers and menstruating women for iron; children, teenagers, pregnant and breastfeeding women and older adults for calcium. State the idea; no amounts.
- [ ] M5 Phosphorus (assumed): works with calcium to harden bones and teeth, forms part of ATP and cell membranes; sources: milk, cheese, meat, fish, eggs, wholegrains, nuts; deficiency is rare; past papers accept calcium or phosphorus lack for osteomalacia.

Calcium
- [ ] CA1 Sources: milk, cheese and yoghurt (the richest); canned sardines and salmon eaten with their bones; green leafy vegetables such as kale, broccoli and cabbage (but not spinach, see Traps); calcium-set tofu; fortified plant milks; fortified breakfast cereals.
- [ ] CA2 Functions: forms the hard part of bones and teeth together with phosphorus; blood clotting; contraction of muscles (including the heart); transmission of nerve impulses; activates some enzymes; helps regulate blood pressure (scheme 2022 H).
- [ ] CA3 Deficiency: in children rickets (soft, bent bones; poor teeth); in adults osteomalacia and, over years, osteoporosis (bones thin, porous, brittle, fracture-prone, loss of height, stooped back); tooth decay; slow or poor blood clotting; muscle cramps (tetany) and poor nerve function. Rickets and osteomalacia also depend on vitamin D and phosphorus.
- [ ] CA4 Who needs most: periods of bone building (childhood, teenage years, pregnancy, breastfeeding) and later life when bone is lost (women after menopause). Bone density peaks in the late teens and twenties, so teenage calcium intake matters. (Ties to osteoporosis risk: low calcium and vitamin D, little weight-bearing exercise, smoking, alcohol, low oestrogen after menopause, family history.)

Iron
- [ ] I1 Two forms: haem iron (from animal foods; absorbed easily) and non-haem iron (from plant foods and eggs; absorbed less easily). Sources: red meat, liver, kidney, black pudding, poultry, oily fish (haem); wholegrain and fortified breakfast cereals, wholemeal bread, pulses, dark green leafy vegetables, dried apricots and other dried fruit, nuts, seeds, eggs (non-haem).
- [ ] I2 Functions: part of haemoglobin, the red pigment in red blood cells that carries oxygen from lungs to all cells; part of myoglobin, which holds oxygen in muscle; part of enzymes in the release of energy in cells; supports immunity and concentration.
- [ ] I3 Deficiency: iron-deficiency anaemia: too few red blood cells or too little haemoglobin: tiredness, pale skin, breathlessness, dizziness, irritability, poor concentration, feeling cold, reduced resistance to infection. Causes: a diet low in iron or poor in vitamin C; blood loss (menstruation, ulcers, operations); high needs in growth spurts, pregnancy and infancy; poor absorption. Corrective measures: eat more iron-rich foods with vitamin C, avoid tea with meals, iron supplement on medical advice.
- [ ] I4 Groups at risk: teenage girls, menstruating and pregnant women, infants and toddlers, vegetarians and vegans (non-haem iron only), people with heavy blood loss. A past paper asks why iron deficiency is common in children (fast growth, picky eating, low-iron foods).
- [ ] I5 Anaemia is a condition (not a mineral) and has other causes (B12 and folate lack, see the water-soluble vitamins subsection); "anaemia" means too little haemoglobin in the blood.

[HL] Zinc
- [ ] Z1 Sources: oysters and shellfish, red meat, poultry, liver, dairy products, eggs, wholegrain cereals, pulses, nuts and seeds.
- [ ] Z2 Functions: part of many enzymes; growth and cell division; wound healing; immune function; sense of taste and smell; healthy skin and hair; reproductive health.
- [ ] Z3 Deficiency: slow growth in children, slow wound healing, dry flaky skin and rashes (2019H), loss of taste and appetite, lowered immunity, hair loss.

[HL] Iodine
- [ ] IO1 Sources: sea fish and shellfish, seaweed, milk and dairy products, eggs; iodised salt.
- [ ] IO2 Function: a part of thyroxine, the hormone from the thyroid gland that controls the rate of metabolism (and growth, brain development).
- [ ] IO3 Deficiency: goitre (swelling of the thyroid gland in the neck as the gland works harder), tiredness and sluggishness (lethargy), weight gain, and in children and in babies of deficient mothers delayed physical and mental development (2021H: iodine and goitre).

[HL] Potassium
- [ ] PO1 Sources: bananas, potatoes (especially with skins), tomatoes, dried fruit, orange juice, pulses, green vegetables, milk, meat, fish, nuts.
- [ ] PO2 Functions: with sodium keeps the fluid balance in and around cells (potassium is the main positive ion inside cells); nerve impulses; muscle contraction, including the heartbeat; helps lower blood pressure (2008H asks one main function).
- [ ] PO3 Deficiency (hypokalaemia): muscle weakness and cramps, irregular heartbeat, tiredness; usually caused by severe vomiting, diarrhoea or some medicines, not by normal diets.

[HL] Sodium
- [ ] SO1 Sources: table salt, cured and smoked meats and fish (bacon, ham), cheese, bread, crisps and snack foods, processed and ready meals, sauces, stock cubes, canned foods; unprocessed fresh foods contain little.
- [ ] SO2 Functions: with potassium keeps the fluid balance outside cells (sodium is the main positive ion outside cells); nerve impulses; muscle contraction; helps absorb glucose and amino acids in the small intestine; helps keep blood pressure (acceptable amounts); enzyme function (2025H, 2012H schemes).
- [ ] SO3 Excess: raises blood pressure (hypertension) and so the risk of stroke, heart disease and kidney problems; most people in Ireland eat more salt than advised, mostly from processed foods; reduce by cooking from fresh, not adding salt at the table, reading labels (salt is on the nutrition declaration), choosing low-salt products.
- [ ] SO4 Deficiency: rare; causes muscle cramps, headache, nausea and confusion; can follow heavy sweating, vomiting or diarrhoea. The FSAI began a voluntary Salt Reduction Programme in 2003 to cut salt in processed food; industry reformulation work continues under the FSAI-Healthy Ireland Food Reformulation Task Force.

## 3. Traps

- Osteomalacia is soft bones from lack of calcium, phosphorus or vitamin D (adults); rickets is the child form; osteoporosis is thin, porous bone with normal mineral content (a different condition, linked to low calcium over years, hormones, age).
- Spinach and rhubarb contain calcium but it is bound by oxalate and poorly absorbed; do not offer them as calcium sources. Detail is in the absorption subsection.
- Goitre is iodine deficiency. Do not confuse goitre with cretinism or with an overactive thyroid.
- Anaemia is not a synonym for iron deficiency; "anaemia" has several causes, iron deficiency being the commonest. Anaemia is "too little haemoglobin / too few red blood cells".
- Sodium is not the same as salt: salt is sodium chloride; labels give salt, which is about two and a half times the sodium weight. Do not give the conversion or a daily limit (changing guidance).
- Potassium and sodium work as a pair; do not say sodium is "bad": the body needs it, the problem is excess.
- Do not use: any RDA or intake figure (for example 14 mg iron for a teenager, 1200 mg calcium in pregnancy, 6 g salt a day), because RDAs change and the two figures in old papers appear in question stems rather than as taught facts; teach who has raised needs instead.
- Do not use: "the FSAI leads Ireland's national salt reduction programme", because the programme began in 2003 and later moved to industry-led reformulation work; the FSAI now monitors and, with Healthy Ireland, runs the Food Reformulation Task Force.
- Do not use: iodised salt as a typical Irish source of iodine, because most Irish iodine comes from milk, dairy and fish; say only that iodised salt exists as a source.
- Do not use: that "minerals are destroyed by heat", because minerals are elements and are not destroyed by cooking; they are lost into cooking water.
- Neighbouring subsections: vitamin D and the rest of the vitamins; haem and non-haem iron absorption, vitamin C, phytates, oxalates, tannins (absorption subsection); water as a nutrient and electrolyte balance (water chapter); heart disease and high blood pressure (diet and health chapter).

## 4. What exams ask

- State two or three functions of calcium and name two or four sources (2004, 2006, 2007, 2017, 2022): bones and teeth, clotting, muscle contraction, nerve function; cheese, milk, yoghurt, sardines, kale; three effects of calcium deficiency (2004O, 2011H): rickets, osteoporosis, osteomalacia, tooth decay, poor clotting.
- Gap-fill and true or false: deficiency of calcium causes osteoporosis; cheese is a calcium source; iron is essential to haemoglobin; anaemia caused by lack of iron (2018 Ordinary, 2021 Ordinary).
- Iron: functions (2007, 2008, 2014, 2023), sources (2007), the iron-deficiency effect (2008), an account under dietary sources, functions and deficiency (2015, 2026 Ordinary); anaemia: meaning (2012 Ordinary), a table of cause, effect and corrective measure (2017 Higher), why children are at risk (2015 Ordinary). Sources should be split into haem and non-haem.
- Match minerals to conditions (2019 Higher): osteomalacia is calcium or phosphorus; dry flaky skin is zinc; goitre is iodine. Nutrient and disease matching (2021 Higher): iodine and goitre.
- Sodium: one main function and two sources (2012 Higher), two functions and two sources (2025 Higher); potassium: one main function (2008 Higher).
- Teenage nutrition or label questions ask which minerals matter and why (iron, calcium) and use per-portion figures from a chart.
