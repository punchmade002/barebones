# Package: hom-home-services-s6 — Insulation

- **loId:** `hom-home-services-s6` · **chapterId:** `hom-home-services` · **syllabusRef:** 4.5.4 (Elective 1)
- **Syllabus scope (outline):** "Insulation [4.5.4]: underlying principles and methods of insulation." Settings: evaluating the insulation in the student's own home and recommending improvements. Also examined: 2025O, 2024H, 2022H, 2021O, 2009H, 2005H. No [HL] marks.
- **Prepared by:** a higher model, 2026-11-23. Insulation is the most predictable question in the chapter: the answer is "principle (poor conductors that trap air), then a named method for attic, walls and windows", and the advantages of a well-insulated home. Heat transfer science is taught in the heating subsection and assumed here; finding heat loss is in the efficiency subsection.

## 1. Must cover

Principle
- [ ] P1 (assumed) Heat is lost from a warm house to the cold outside by conduction through solid parts, convection (warm air rising and leaking out) and radiation, and by air leakage through gaps. Insulation reduces this so less fuel is needed.
- [ ] P2 Underlying principle: insulating materials are poor conductors of heat. Trapped still air is a very poor conductor, so materials that hold a lot of air in small pockets (fibreglass, mineral wool, sheep's wool, cellulose, polystyrene and foam) slow conduction; the pockets are too small for convection currents.
- [ ] P3 Double glazing: two panes with a sealed gap of air or argon; the trapped gas is a poor conductor and cannot circulate, so conduction and convection across the gap are reduced. Low-emissivity (low-E) coating reflects heat radiation back into the room.
- [ ] P4 Reflective foil reflects radiant heat back; draught-proofing stops heat leaving in moving air.
- [ ] P5 Insulation also keeps the house cool in hot weather.

Attic (roof)
- [ ] A1 Why first: warm air rises, so the roof is a main route of heat loss; also the cheapest and most effective place to start.
- [ ] A2 Methods: fibre blanket or quilt (mineral or glass fibre, wool) laid in strips between and over the ceiling joists; loose fill (pellets of polystyrene or vermiculite poured between joists); sprayed or foam insulation applied between rafters (it expands and sets); blown fibre (cellulose or mineral fibre blown into the roof space); rigid boards between rafters in a converted attic.
- [ ] A3 Lag the cold water tank and attic pipes (the attic is colder once the floor is insulated) and leave the hatch insulated and draught-proofed. Keep the eaves ventilated.
- [ ] A4 Diagram: cross-section of a roof with joists, ceiling and an insulation layer between and over the joists.

Walls
- [ ] W1 Cavity wall (two leaves with a gap): the cavity can be filled with polystyrene beads, foam or mineral wool blown in through small holes; even an unfilled cavity holds some still air.
- [ ] W2 Internal insulation (dry lining): insulated plasterboard fixed to the inside of the wall; it reduces room size and takes time to fit but needs no scaffolding.
- [ ] W3 External insulation: boards of polystyrene or mineral wool fixed outside and finished with reinforced waterproof render; keeps the wall itself warm and avoids thermal bridges; needs planning checks.
- [ ] W4 Solid walls have no cavity and can be insulated only inside or outside.
- [ ] W5 Diagram: a cavity wall with outer leaf, insulation in the cavity, inner leaf and internal dry lining.

Floors, windows, doors and pipes
- [ ] F1 Floors: seal gaps between boards, add insulation between joists on suspended floors, carpet with underlay, rigid insulation under a new concrete floor.
- [ ] F2 Windows: double glazing (air or argon gap); triple glazing; low-E glass; secondary glazing; heavy lined curtains and blinds close to the glass; shutters.
- [ ] F3 Doors and gaps: draught excluders, brush strips on doors, letterbox flap, keyhole covers, sealing gaps around windows and floorboards; a porch.
- [ ] F4 Hot water cylinder and pipes: insulating jacket or factory-fitted foam, pipe lagging; saves heat and prevents freezing.
- [ ] F5 Condensation link: warm inner surfaces stay above the temperature at which moisture condenses, so insulation reduces condensation, but an airtight, well-insulated house needs controlled ventilation (see ventilation).

Advantages of a well-insulated home (exam list)
- [ ] V1 Lower heating bills as heat loss is reduced and the heating runs for shorter periods.
- [ ] V2 Comfort: steadier temperature, no cold surfaces, fewer draughts.
- [ ] V3 Less noise (insulation absorbs sound from outside and between rooms).
- [ ] V4 Less fuel burned: lower carbon dioxide emissions and less use of fossil fuel; a more sustainable house.
- [ ] V5 Less condensation and damp, so healthier air and less mould.
- [ ] V6 Better BER and higher property value; SEAI grants may help with cost.

Evaluating your own home (practical context)
- [ ] E1 Inspect the attic: depth, gaps, uncovered joists, lagged tank and pipes; check windows and doors for draughts and the type of glazing; note cold rooms, condensation and damp; find out the wall type (cavity or solid); check the cylinder jacket.
- [ ] E2 Recommend improvements in order of cost and effect: draught-proofing and attic insulation first, then cylinder jacket and lined curtains, then wall insulation and glazing; justify each by the heat it saves and the cost; mention SEAI grants and the BER.

## 3. Traps

- Insulation does not "keep cold out" or "generate" heat; it slows heat escaping. The material is a poor conductor; the air trapped in it does the work.
- Say "still air in small pockets"; moving air carries heat by convection, so a big open gap is not insulation. This is also why cavity beads and fibres are used.
- Radiation: reflective surfaces and low-E coatings address radiation; air-trapping materials address conduction and convection. Do not claim one principle covers everything.
- Name a method for each area asked (attic, walls, windows): "insulate the attic" alone earns few marks. Give the material and where it goes.
- Heat transfer theory belongs to the heating subsection; finding where the heat is lost and the full list of efficiency strategies belong to the efficiency subsection; the condensation processes belong to ventilation.
- An insulated attic floor is colder than before, so lag the tank and pipes (see the water subsection).
- Do not use: "BER scale A1 to G" as the current scale, because the BER was simplified on 24 May 2026 to A0 and A to G; say "A (best) to G (worst)" and that SEAI runs it.
- Do not use: any depth in millimetres, U-values or percentages of heat lost through the roof or walls, because these are changing regulations figures or unsourced.
- Do not use: "argon conducts heat less well than air" as a precise claim beyond "a poor conductor, used instead of air in some units", because the extent was not confirmed in the sources; keep it as in the marking schemes.

## 4. What exams ask

- Four methods of insulating a home (2025O, 20 marks): attic (blanket, loose fill, foam, blown fibre), walls (cavity, dry lining, external), windows (double or triple glazing, low-E, lined curtains), floors (fill gaps, carpet and underlay), doors and pipes (draught excluders, lagging).
- Recommend a method for the attic, walls and windows (2021O, 2017O, 2009H): name, description and the principle each time.
- Explain the underlying principle of insulation with an example (2022H, 2024H): poor conductors, trapped air, fibreglass, polystyrene, wool, cellulose; the example given (cavity wall, double glazing, lagging jacket).
- Principle of double glazing for windows (2005H): sealed trapped air or gas between two panes reduces conduction and convection.
- Advantages of a well-insulated home (2025O, 2021O, 2017O): three or four advantages, each with a reason.
- Three areas of the house to insulate with a method and principle (2014H); an area of a bungalow to insulate (2007O); insulation in a kitchen evaluation (2008O).
- Evaluate insulation in your own home and recommend improvements (practical context).
