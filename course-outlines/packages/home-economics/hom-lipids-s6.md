# Package: hom-lipids-s6 — Fats and oils as a food commodity

- **loId:** `hom-lipids-s6` · **chapterId:** `hom-lipids` · **syllabusRef:** 1.3.2
- **Syllabus scope:** nutritional significance, contribution to the diet, selection, effects of storage, preparation, cooking and processing of fats and oils (butter, margarine and spreads, oils); storage of foods containing lipids; use of fats and oils in cooking.
  Also examined: production of margarine with packaging and labelling (2024H); low-fat and functional dairy spreads (2013H); evaluating spreads from data (2008H).
- **Prepared by:** a higher model, 2026-11-23. Common to Higher and Ordinary Level; no [HL] items are listed for this subsection, though hydrogenation and plasticity are Higher Level properties met in the process (see "Properties"; cross-refer by title only). Chemistry of fatty acids, the properties themselves, functions and energy value are in the other subsections of this chapter; the labelling regulation applies to all food and is treated fully in the food legislation chapter, so here give only what appears on a fat product label.

## 1. Must cover

Nutritional significance and contribution to the diet (one block; 2024H 20 marks)
- [ ] N1 Fats and oils are very concentrated in fat: butter and margarine are about four-fifths fat or more, oils almost pure fat (marking scheme: 82–99.9%). The balance is mostly water in butter, margarine and spreads (water varies inversely with fat).
- [ ] N2 They supply energy (37 kJ or 9 kcal per gram) and fat-soluble vitamins (butter has vitamins A and D naturally; margarine and spreads are fortified with A and D; vegetable oils supply vitamin E), and fatty acids; essential fatty acids come from plant oils.
- [ ] N3 They supply almost no protein, carbohydrate, fibre or minerals; butter and margarine contain traces of minerals such as calcium and sodium (salt added).
- [ ] N4 Type of fat by source: animal fats (butter, lard, dripping) are saturated and raise LDL; olive oil is monounsaturated; plant and nut oils are polyunsaturated.
- [ ] N5 Contribution to the diet: spreads and oils are eaten daily but in small amounts; much of the fat in the diet is hidden in processed foods, meat products, dairy and takeaways, so fat intake is greater than the visible fats suggest. Link to the Food Pyramid: fats, spreads and oils are eaten sparingly, choosing mono- or polyunsaturated types.

The commodities: how they are made (one block each, kept short)
- [ ] B1 Butter: made by churning cream (the fat droplets clump, the buttermilk is drained off, and the butter is worked, salted or unsalted). In the EU butter is at least 80% milk fat (and under 90%), with at most 16% water. It is an animal, saturated fat, a water-in-oil emulsion.
- [ ] B2 Oils: pressed or extracted from seeds, nuts and fruits (olive, rapeseed, sunflower), then refined (cleaned, bleached, deodorised) to remove impurities, giving a bland oil with a higher smoke point. Extra virgin olive oil is made from the olives by mechanical pressing without chemical refining and keeps its flavour; it is used for dressing and gentle cooking.
- [ ] B3 Margarine and spreads: made from vegetable (and sometimes fish) oils; for stages see the margarine block below. Spreads are lower in fat than margarine and butter and contain more water.
- [ ] B4 Types for consumers: butter, margarine, low-fat spreads, functional dairy spreads (cholesterol-lowering), blended spreads (butter with vegetable oil), oils (olive, rapeseed, sunflower, vegetable), lard, suet, dripping, cooking fat.

Selection (one block)
- [ ] S1 Read the label: type of fat; ingredients (largest first); energy per 100 g; total fat; saturates; salt; additives; allergens (milk, soya).
- [ ] S2 Choose by use: oils for frying (high smoke point); butter or hard margarine for baking, creaming and pastry; spreads for spreading (low-fat spreads are unsuitable for cooking and baking because of their high water content).
- [ ] S3 Choose by health: more mono- and polyunsaturated, lower saturates, avoid partially hydrogenated fats, low-fat or functional spreads for those watching weight or cholesterol; olive or rapeseed oil as healthier choices.
- [ ] S4 Other factors: cost, taste, dietary needs (dairy-free, vegan, allergy), brand, packaging, shelf life, organic, whether Irish (Bord Bia quality mark).

Effects of storage and storage of foods containing lipids (one block)
- [ ] T1 Rancidity is the main storage problem (oxidative, hydrolytic; see "Properties"): store fats and oils in airtight, dark (or opaque) containers, in a cool place; oils away from light and heat; buy in sensible quantities and use before the date.
- [ ] T2 Butter and spreads: keep in the refrigerator, covered or in the original wrapper or tub, because fat absorbs strong smells (onion, fish); freeze butter for longer storage.
- [ ] T3 Nuts and seeds go rancid, so store in airtight containers in a cool place; oily fish and fatty meat are stored in the fridge and used promptly or frozen.
- [ ] T4 Frying oil: strain after use, cover, do not top up old oil; discard when dark, smoking at a low temperature or foaming.
- [ ] T5 Foods with fat (pastries, cakes, biscuits, crisps, nuts, whole-grain flours) are kept in airtight tins or packs.

Preparation, cooking and processing (one block)
- [ ] C1 Methods that add little or no fat: grilling, steaming, poaching, microwaving, baking on a rack; trim visible fat and skin; use a non-stick pan; cut fat in recipes.
- [ ] C2 Frying adds fat and energy: shallow frying in a little fat; deep-fat frying in a deep pan at a controlled temperature (do not overheat, drain on absorbent paper); fat is absorbed by food, so frying raises energy.
- [ ] C3 Uses in cooking: creaming fat and sugar traps air for cakes; rubbing fat into flour for shortcrust pastry (shortening: fat coats flour proteins and limits gluten); layering fat in flaky and puff pastry; frying and roasting (heat transfer, browning); greasing tins; emulsions (mayonnaise, hollandaise, dressings); adding flavour, richness and moistness; roux and sauces.
- [ ] C4 Effects of heat: fat melts; overheating breaks the fat down (smoke point, acrolein); fat reaching flash point can ignite, so never leave a deep-fat fryer unattended; repeated heating lowers the smoke point and produces off flavours.
- [ ] C5 Processing of fats and oils: refining, hydrogenation (oil to fat), blending, emulsifying and adding vitamins and colour. Hydrogenation can form trans fatty acids; the EU limit is 2 g per 100 g of fat.

Production of margarine, with packaging and labelling (one block; 2024H 15 marks, 10 scheme points)
- [ ] M1 Extract the oil from the seeds (for example sunflower, rapeseed, soya, palm) and refine it.
- [ ] M2 Hydrogenation: hydrogen gas is passed through the heated oil in the presence of a nickel catalyst, which adds hydrogen at the double bonds and changes liquid oil to a semi-solid fat; the catalyst is removed.
- [ ] M3 Blend the hardened fat with other oils to give the required texture (plasticity) and spreadability.
- [ ] M4 Prepare the water phase: skimmed milk or water with salt, and add colour (carotene), flavouring, and vitamins A and D; lecithin is added as the emulsifier.
- [ ] M5 Emulsify: mix the fat and water phases to make a water-in-oil emulsion (churning).
- [ ] M6 Cool and work: chill rapidly while working in a votator (a scraped-surface heat exchanger) so a plastic, spreadable fat forms.
- [ ] M7 Weigh and package in foil-wrapped blocks or plastic tubs (an airtight, light-proof pack that protects the fat from oxidation and absorbed smells).
- [ ] M8 Labelling: name and type (margarine or spread); list of ingredients in descending order of weight, with allergens (for example milk, soya) highlighted; nutrition declaration per 100 g (energy, fat, saturates, carbohydrate, sugars, protein, salt); net quantity; date mark (best before) and storage instructions; name and address of the manufacturer; country of origin and batch (lot) code. In Ireland the rules are Regulation (EU) No 1169/2011 as given effect by the European Union (Provision of Food Information to Consumers) Regulations 2014; the FSAI gives guidance.

Low-fat and functional dairy spreads (one block; 2013H)
- [ ] D1 Low-fat spreads: about half the fat of butter (the 2013H scheme gives 38–40%); contain more water, made from vegetable oil, water and milk proteins; lower in energy and saturates, high in unsaturated fat; suitable for a calorie-controlled diet; spread on bread but not suitable for frying or baking because of the high water content.
- [ ] D2 Functional dairy spreads: contain added plant stanol or sterol esters, which block the absorption of cholesterol in the small intestine, so more is excreted; clinically shown to lower blood (LDL) cholesterol when eaten regularly as part of a healthy diet; contain no hydrogenated fat and virtually no trans fatty acids; made from vegetable oil, buttermilk, plant stanol or sterol ester, water, salt. Examples in the scheme: Benecol (plant stanol ester) and Flora pro.activ (plant sterol).

Evaluating spreads from data (one block; 2008H, 24 marks)
- [ ] V1 Method: compare per 100 g (or per serving) the energy, total fat, saturated fat, monounsaturated and polyunsaturated fat, salt (and omega-3 where given).
- [ ] V2 Judge against dietary advice: lower energy and total fat suit weight reduction; lower saturates and higher unsaturated fat suit heart health; lower salt suits blood pressure.
- [ ] V3 Reach a verdict for each product (for example, suit weight reduction or CHD risk reduction) with a reason from the figures; mention suitability for cooking.
- [ ] V4 Use the products as a comparison of butter versus a spread versus a light spread; state the fat content differences and how they follow from water content.

## 3. Traps

- Butter is made from cream by churning and is an animal fat. Margarine is made from vegetable oils (and in the past sometimes fish oils). Do not say margarine is made from butter. Spreads are not all margarine.
- Hydrogenation hardens oil by adding hydrogen to double bonds; it does not make it saturated by adding water. It is done to make fat plastic and spreadable, and to improve shelf life.
- Low-fat spread vs functional spread: low-fat means less fat (more water); functional means added plant stanols or sterols to lower cholesterol; not the same product. A functional spread is not automatically low in fat.
- Plant stanols do not "remove" cholesterol already in the blood vessels; they reduce absorption in the gut.
- The label lists ingredients in descending order of weight; allergens are highlighted in the list. Spreads, butter and margarine normally carry a best-before date, not a use-by.
- Storage: it is oxidative rancidity (air, light, heat) that makes oils go off in the cupboard; it is the odour absorption (smells) that is why butter is covered in the fridge.
- Do not use: "a use-by date" on the margarine label, because margarine and butter normally carry a best-before date (use-by is for highly perishable foods); write "date mark (best before)".
- Do not use: the brand examples Dairygold Light and Avonmore Extra Light from the 2013H scheme, because products are reformulated or discontinued and are unconfirmed; use Benecol and Flora pro.activ only if a brand is needed.
- Do not use: "95 kcal per 25 g versus 185 kcal for butter" and any fat percentage for margarine, because product-specific figures change with the brand and recipe and the margarine fat percentage is unconfirmed; use "about half the fat of butter".
- Do not use: "Hard margarines and shortenings made by hydrogenation suit pastry" combined with "hard margarines are rich in trans fat", because modern products are reformulated; use "hard margarine or block fat suits pastry because it is plastic".
- Do not use: "functional spreads have virtually no trans fatty acids and need to be eaten regularly" as a promise of a specific amount of cholesterol lowering, because no percentage or daily amount is checked; say the effect depends on regular use.
- Do not use: a deep-frying temperature figure or oil price, because it varies by recipe and fat and is not checked here.

## 4. What exams ask

- Nutritional significance of fats and oils (2024H, 20 marks): fat content (82–99.9%), types of fat by source and effect on LDL and HDL, energy, insulation, organ protection, fat-soluble vitamins, small amounts of protein, carbohydrate and minerals.
- Margarine production (2024H, 15 marks, 10 points): oil extraction and refining, hydrogenation with nickel catalyst, blending, water phase (skimmed milk or water, salt, colour, flavour, vitamins A and D), lecithin, churning in a votator, weighing; package in foil or plastic tubs; label with ingredients, allergens, date, storage instructions, weight and nutrition information. Write the stages in order.
- Low-fat and functional dairy spreads (2013H, 6 marks): differentiate; one example each; content, who they suit, and why low-fat spreads cannot be used for baking.
- Evaluating spreads from data (2008H, 24 marks): compare energy, total and saturated fat, polyunsaturated fat and salt of three products against current dietary advice and reach a verdict for each.
- Choosing fats and reducing fat intake (2016O, 2021O): three ways to cut fat (lean meat, trim fat, low-fat products, grill or steam instead of frying, avoid takeaways); four factors when selecting foods (choose low-fat foods, avoid hydrogenated fats, compare labels and traffic lights for saturates, choose fat-free or low-fat dairy).
- Storage and cooking use of fat: why oils are stored in the dark, why butter is covered, why frying oil is strained, how fat is used in pastry (shortening) and cakes (creaming).
- Data and label questions link to food labelling (2018H, 2013H): ingredients in descending order of weight, nutrition information, date marks, allergens.
- Practical coursework: coursework journals often use fat in pastries, cakes and frying; cooking-method notes elsewhere hold the details.
