# Package: hom-cooking-methods-s4 — Soups and sauces

- **loId:** `hom-cooking-methods-s4` · **chapterId:** `hom-cooking-methods` · **syllabusRef:** 1.3.4
- **Syllabus scope:** classification, preparation, cooking and presentation of soups and sauces. Also examined: roux sauces (2023H); classes of sauces and soups (2022O, 2015H, 2014O, 2006H); convenience soups (2014O). No [HL] marks in this entry; mark nothing.
- **Prepared by:** a higher model, 2026-11-23. Common to both levels. Two kinds of questions: short definitions and classification (6 marks), and the 2014 long question on homemade and convenience soups (classes, range of convenience soups, advantages and disadvantages, role of labelling).

## 1. Must cover

Classes of soup (one block)
- [ ] S1 Thin soups: clear soups and consommé (clarified stock), broths (e.g. chicken or vegetable broth).
- [ ] S2 Thick soups: purée soups (vegetables sieved or blended, e.g. carrot, leek and potato) and thickened soups (thickened with roux, cornflour, potato or a liaison of egg yolk and cream).
- [ ] S3 Cold soups: served chilled, e.g. gazpacho.
- [ ] S4 Stock is the base of most soups: made by simmering bones, meat or vegetables with herbs in water; it gives flavour. Skim off scum, don't boil hard, strain.

Preparing and cooking soups (one block)
- [ ] P1 Prepare vegetables: wash, peel and cut into even pieces so they cook at the same rate. Sweat them (cook gently in a little fat in a covered pan, without browning) to release flavour.
- [ ] P2 Add the stock, bring to the boil and simmer until tender (fierce boiling breaks up vegetables and loses flavour); thicken if needed.
- [ ] P3 Purée soups: blend or sieve until smooth, with care when using a blender (do not overfill, hold the lid down); then reheat and season.
- [ ] P4 Adjust seasoning and consistency at the end; add cream, milk or a liaison off the boil so it doesn't curdle.
- [ ] P5 Safe practice: cool and chill leftover soup quickly and reheat until piping hot (one line).

Presenting soups (one block)
- [ ] R1 Serve hot soup hot and in warm bowls, cold soup chilled; garnish for colour and flavour: croutons, chopped parsley or chives, a swirl of cream, grated cheese; thin strips of vegetable on clear soups; accompaniments such as brown bread. A garnish must be edible, suit the soup and be small.

Roux-based sauces (one block)
- [ ] X1 A roux is a cooked mixture of equal amounts of fat and flour (by weight); the amount of liquid added decides the thickness: more liquid for a pouring sauce, less for a coating sauce, least for a binding sauce (panada).
- [ ] X2 Method: melt the fat, stir in the flour and cook gently, add the liquid gradually stirring or whisking to prevent lumps, bring to the boil and simmer to cook the starch; season.
- [ ] X3 Why it thickens: starch granules absorb liquid and swell (gelatinisation) as the sauce heats.
- [ ] X4 A white roux is cooked briefly without colour (white, parsley, cheese, mushroom, béchamel sauces); a brown roux is cooked longer to a brown colour and thickens less (brown and stewing sauces).
- [ ] X5 Béchamel is a white sauce made with milk infused with flavourings (onion, bay leaf, cloves); panada is a very thick roux-based paste used to bind croquettes and similar mixtures.
- [ ] X6 Examples the scheme accepts as roux based: white, parsley, béchamel, cheese, mushroom, curry, stewing, panada, brown sauce.

Other classes of sauce (one block)
- [ ] Y1 Classification used in the schemes: roux based; egg based (custard, mayonnaise, hollandaise); sweet (chocolate, toffee, caramel, butterscotch); fruit (apple, orange, coulis); cold or dressings (mint sauce, French dressing, mayonnaise); miscellaneous (bread sauce).
- [ ] Y2 How the others are made in one line each: custard sets because egg protein coagulates in hot milk; mayonnaise and hollandaise are emulsions of oil (or butter) held in egg yolk; bread sauce is thickened with breadcrumbs.
- [ ] Y3 Uses: white sauce with fish, cheese sauce with cauliflower, apple sauce with pork, mint sauce with lamb, custard with apple tart, French dressing on salad.

Convenience soups (one block)
- [ ] V1 The range: dehydrated or packet soups (dried mix, add boiling water or milk), cook-chill soups (chilled, heated before eating), canned soups, frozen soups, and single-portion soups (the scheme says "soupfulls", pots or cups).
- [ ] V2 Advantages: little preparation time; simple skills needed; useful in an emergency; wide variety; easy to store; long shelf life for dried and canned soups.
- [ ] V3 Disadvantages: can be expensive per portion; often high in salt; may contain additives and have less fibre and flavour than homemade; less choice over ingredients.
- [ ] V4 Improving a convenience soup by adding fresh vegetables, herbs, milk or a topping.

Labelling on convenience soups (one block)
- [ ] L1 Why labelling helps the consumer: compare products and choose for health or allergy needs. Required information in outline: name of the food, ingredients list in descending order of weight, net quantity (metric), nutrition information (energy, fat, saturates, carbohydrate, sugars, protein, salt per 100 g), date mark (use-by or best before), storage and preparation instructions, name and address of the business, allergens emphasised in the ingredients list.
- [ ] L2 Legal basis: Regulation (EU) No 1169/2011 on food information to consumers, given effect by the European Union (Provision of Food Information to Consumers) Regulations 2014. Details of labelling law belong to the food law chapter; one sentence here.
- [ ] L3 The scheme list (2014O): name, ingredients, quantity in metric units, nutritional content, cooking instructions, storage, best-before date, manufacturer details.

## 3. Traps

- A roux has equal fat and flour; the amount of liquid changes the sauce, not the amount of roux ingredients.
- Béchamel is made with infused milk; "white sauce" is the same method without the infusion; don't call béchamel a cream sauce.
- Mayonnaise appears under egg-based in one scheme and under cold sauces in another; give it under egg based and mention it is also served cold.
- Clear soups and broths are thin, not thick (2013O true/false: "clear soups and broths are types of thick soups" is false); a liaison is a thickener (egg yolk and cream) and is true.
- Convenience soups: don't list a cook-chill soup as having a longer shelf life than canned.
- Labelling law, additives and date marks belong to the food law and additives chapters; one sentence and the list here.
- Egg coagulation, emulsions and mayonnaise science belong to the dairy and eggs chapter and the lipids chapter; one clause here.
- Do not use: "genetically modified or irradiated ingredients must be declared" as part of Regulation 1169/2011 labelling, because irradiation is covered by 1169/2011 but genetically modified food is labelled under a separate regulation (EC) No 1829/2003; leave GM out.
- Do not use: "seasoning is added at the end because salt concentrates as soup reduces" as the only reason, because cooks also season in stages; say adjust seasoning at the end and taste first.

## 4. What exams ask

- Name two or three classes of homemade soup with an example of each (2014O, 2006H).
- Classify sauces with an example per class (2015H); give one roux, egg-based and sweet sauce (2022O); describe a roux-based sauce with an example (2023H): equal fat and flour with liquid added, e.g. white, cheese or parsley sauce.
- Outline the range of convenience soups (dehydrated, cook-chill, canned, frozen, single-portion).
- Advantages and disadvantages of convenience soups, three of each.
- Role of food labelling for a consumer buying convenience soup, three points each explained (ingredients, nutrition, date marks, allergens, storage and cooking instructions).
- True/false on soups (liaison, croutons as garnish).
