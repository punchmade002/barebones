## 1. Must cover

Denaturation and coagulation (common)
- [ ] D1 Denaturation is the unfolding of the protein chain, breaking the weak bonds of the secondary and tertiary structure (not the peptide bonds), so the protein changes shape; the change is irreversible.
- [ ] D2 Causes: heat; mechanical action (whisking, beating); acid (pH); enzymes; salt. Each with a food example (see below).
- [ ] D3 Coagulation: the denatured chains join together and set into a solid or semi-solid mass; it is caused by heat (and by acid and rennin in milk).
- [ ] D4 Effects: change in texture, appearance, colour and flavour; protein becomes more digestible; protein loses solubility.
- [ ] D5 Examples of coagulation: egg white from transparent to opaque and firm at about 60 °C (yolk about 68 °C); omelette, quiche, boiled egg, meringue, soufflé, roast meat; fish flesh changes from translucent to opaque.

Foam formation [HL]
- [ ] F1 [HL] Whisking stretches (partly denatures) the albumin in egg white; the chains unfold and arrange around air bubbles, forming a foam.
- [ ] F2 [HL] Heat in cooking coagulates the albumin around the bubbles and sets the foam permanently. Examples: meringue, pavlova, soufflé, sponge cake.
- [ ] F3 [HL] Over-whisking breaks the film so the foam collapses and goes grainy.

Gel formation [HL]
- [ ] G1 [HL] A gel forms when protein (gelatine) dissolved in hot liquid cools and the chains link into a network that traps the liquid.
- [ ] G2 [HL] Gelatine is the example: jelly, aspic, mousses, cheesecake. More gelatine gives a firmer gel.

Effects of heat, mechanical action, pH and enzymes
- [ ] H1 Dry heat (roasting, grilling, baking, frying): protein denatures, coagulates, shrinks and firms; meat browns; overcooking makes meat tough and dry.
- [ ] H2 Maillard reaction: non-enzymic browning when amino acids of protein react with sugars under dry heat; gives brown colour and savoury flavour (bread crust, roast meat, grilled cheese, biscuits).
- [ ] H3 Moist heat (stewing, boiling, poaching, braising): collagen in connective tissue changes to gelatine, tenderising cheap cuts with long slow cooking; high heat for long toughens muscle protein.
- [ ] M1 Mechanical action: whisking egg white (foam), beating, mincing, and beating meat with a mallet breaks up fibres; kneading bread dough develops gluten.
- [ ] P1 pH: acid denatures protein (strong alkali does too, but the examiners' examples are all acid). Lemon juice or vinegar curdle milk; sour milk; marinades with vinegar, wine or lemon tenderise meat; acid helps set milk protein in cheese-making; the acid in the stomach denatures food protein before digestion.
- [ ] P2 Salt: increases firmness of curds in cheese-making (a chemical cause listed in the schemes).
- [ ] E1 Enzymes: rennin coagulates casein (cheese-making); proteolytic enzymes such as papain (papaya) break protein down and tenderise meat; in digestion, protease enzymes hydrolyse protein to amino acids (link only).
- [ ] E2 Heat destroys enzymes, so cooking stops enzyme action.

Investigation
- [ ] I1 Investigation: the effect of heat and of whisking on egg white: aim, control, method, safety (hygiene with raw egg), result (heated white opaque and firm; whisked white foam; unheated white clear and runny); conclusion.

## 3. Traps

- Denaturation is not coagulation: denaturation is the unfolding; coagulation is the setting that follows. Both are irreversible.
- Peptide bonds are not broken in denaturation; hydrolysis is a different process (digestion).
- Foam formation is the mechanical action; coagulation by heat fixes it (so meringue stays set). Do not say heat creates the foam.
- Moist heat tenderises (collagen to gelatine) while excessive dry heat toughens; students mix these up.
- Maillard reaction is non-enzymic and needs both protein and sugar; it is not caramelisation (sugar alone, carbohydrates chapter).
- Gel formation (gelatine, protein) is different from gelatinisation of starch (carbohydrates chapter).
- Do not describe the structure bonds in detail (structure subsection); do not teach digestion steps (digestion chapter); biological value belongs to its own subsection.
- Do not use: kneading as an example of denaturation, because gluten develops by hydration and alignment of gluten proteins, not by unfolding; say kneading develops gluten and keep whisking, beating and mincing as mechanical-action examples.
- Do not use: a single "set" temperature for egg white or yolk stated without "about", because eggs set over a range.

## 4. What exams ask

- State two causes of denaturation with an example each (2021H, 2007H): heat (hard-boiled egg, omelette, shrinking meat, collagen to gelatine), mechanical action (whisking egg whites for meringue, sponge, soufflé), acid (cheese-making, vinegar tenderising meat, souring milk), enzymes (rennin on casein, tenderising meat), salt (cheese-making).
- Explain denaturation with reference to heat, mechanical action and enzymes (2026H, 18 marks): irreversible unfolding without breaking peptide bonds; heat with egg white opaque at about 60 °C and collagen to gelatine; whisking foam; rennin and papain.
- Ordinary: explain denaturation and coagulation with an example of each (2013O); explain coagulation and name two dishes (2019O: egg white at 60 °C, quiche, meringue); complete statements: coagulate, aeration, Maillard reaction, opaque (2023O, 2015O).
- Higher: explain foam formation with a culinary example (2022H); describe gel formation (gelatine).
- Three effects of heat on protein foods (2018O): coagulation, colour change, denaturation, more digestible, collagen to gelatine, softer meat, improved flavour, destroys micro-organisms.
- Exam tips to build: "Say irreversible and unfolding for denaturation"; "Name the dish with each property"; "Moist heat tenderises through collagen to gelatine"; "Maillard reaction is the non-enzymic browning."
