# Package: hom-food-industry-s1 — Structure of the Irish food industry

- **loId:** `hom-food-industry-s1` · **chapterId:** `hom-food-industry` · **syllabusRef:** 1.3.1
- **Syllabus scope:** "brief outline of its structure, to include identification of the various
  sectors and the major food imports and exports." Settings: collect information on a local
  factory, business or home enterprise and assess how it fits into the industry structure, what
  it contributes to the area and the careers it offers. Also examined: sectors of the food
  industry (2024O, 2019H, 2012H); food exports and imports (2012O, 2010H, 2008O); state agencies
  in the food industry, including Bord Bia (2014H, 2010O, 2007H, 2006O). No [HL] items. Common
  to Ordinary and Higher Level.
- **Prepared by:** a higher model, 2026-11-23. "Brief outline" means named sectors with example
  products, a few major exports and imports in kind, and the main state agencies with one job
  each. No statistics: export values, tonnages, percentages and rankings change every year and
  are left out.

## 1. Must cover

Structure: primary, secondary, tertiary (one block)
- [ ] S1 The food industry takes raw produce from land and sea, processes it, and gets it to the
  consumer. It is organised in three levels (sectors in the economic sense).
- [ ] S2 Primary: resource-based production: farming, horticulture, fishing and fish farming
  (mariculture). Products: milk, cattle, sheep, pigs, poultry, eggs, grain, fruit, vegetables,
  fish and shellfish.
- [ ] S3 Secondary: manufacturing and processing of primary produce into finished food and drink
  (e.g. milk into cheese, butter and milk powder; wheat into flour and bread; carcasses into cuts
  and meat products; barley into beer and whiskey).
- [ ] S4 Tertiary: services that support, distribute, sell and regulate food: transport,
  wholesale and retail, catering and food outlets, tourism and hospitality, research, education,
  advice, marketing and regulation.
- [ ] S5 The three levels are linked: each depends on the one before it (a processor needs
  farmers' produce; a retailer needs the processor's goods).
- [ ] S6 The industry is important to the economy: it provides employment, much of it in rural
  areas, and earns income from export sales (state this qualitatively).

Sectors of the industry (one block)
- [ ] T1 Dairy and ingredients: milk, cheese, butter, yoghurt, cream, milk powder, and dairy
  ingredients supplied to other food makers.
- [ ] T2 Meat and livestock: beef, lamb, pigmeat (pork, bacon) and poultry.
- [ ] T3 Beverages: beer and stout, whiskey and other spirits, fruit juice, water.
- [ ] T4 Prepared consumer foods: ready meals, snacks, confectionery, sauces, bakery goods.
- [ ] T5 Seafood, including mariculture (marine farming) and fish processing.
- [ ] T6 Edible horticulture (fruit, vegetables, mushrooms, potatoes) and cereals/bakery
  (milling, bread, biscuits).
- [ ] T7 Exam wording to expect: "dairy and ingredients, meat and livestock, beverages, prepared
  consumer foods, seafood/mariculture, edible horticulture". A student naming any three, each
  with one product, scores.

Major exports and imports (one block)
- [ ] X1 Exports (Ireland produces far more of these than it eats): dairy products (butter,
  cheese, milk powder, infant formula ingredients), beef, lamb, pigmeat, poultry, prepared foods,
  seafood, whiskey and other beverages, edible horticulture (mushrooms, potatoes). Main markets
  are the UK and other EU countries, with further markets elsewhere (e.g. North America, Asia);
  no shares or values.
- [ ] X2 Why Ireland exports so much: a small population and a climate and soil suited to grass,
  so livestock and dairy output is far larger than home demand.
- [ ] X3 Imports (foods Ireland cannot grow, or cannot grow year-round, or chooses to buy in):
  tropical and out-of-season fruit (bananas, oranges, pineapples), coffee, tea and cocoa, spices,
  sugar, wine, rice and dried pasta (assumed: grain-based items), some vegetables, herbs and
  processed foods, and soya and other animal feed ingredients.
- [ ] X4 Ireland also imports some foods it exports (e.g. cheeses, meat products) for variety
  and price; trade runs both ways. Overall, the value of food and drink exported is greater than
  the value imported, so Ireland is a net exporter of food.
- [ ] X5 Reasons for imports: climate (tropical crops), seasonal gaps, consumer demand for
  variety and ethnic foods, ingredients for manufacturers, feed for livestock.

State agencies (one block; one function each, Bord Bia in more depth)
- [ ] A1 Bord Bia (Irish Food Board): promotes Irish food, drink and horticulture at home and
  abroad; supports Irish food and drink companies (market research, marketing, trade fairs,
  introductions to buyers); develops national and international markets; runs quality assurance
  schemes (e.g. for beef and lamb, dairy, pigmeat, poultry, eggs), shown by a quality mark on
  pack; runs Origin Green, its sustainability programme (companies set measurable targets,
  progress independently verified).
- [ ] A2 Teagasc: the agriculture and food development authority: research, advice and training
  for farmers and food producers.
- [ ] A3 Bord Iascaigh Mhara (BIM): the seafood development agency: supports fishers, fish
  farmers and processors with training, technical advice and funding, and promotes Irish seafood.
- [ ] A4 Enterprise Ireland: supports Irish-owned companies, including food and drink firms, to
  grow, innovate and export. (The Local Enterprise Offices, for very small businesses, are in the
  next subsection.)
- [ ] A5 Department of Agriculture, Food and the Marine: the government department with
  responsibility for the sector (policy, schemes, and inspection of meat and dairy
  premises; seafood premises are controlled by the Sea-Fisheries Protection Authority).
- [ ] A6 Food Safety Authority of Ireland (FSAI): coordinates the enforcement of food safety
  law and advises the Minister; protects consumers; works with industry on HACCP. Detail is in
  the food safety chapter; give one line here.

Settings: researching a local food business (one block)
- [ ] L1 A method for a local factory, business or home enterprise: its sector and level
  (primary, secondary, tertiary); raw materials and their sources; products; markets (local,
  national, export); what it contributes (jobs, local suppliers, income); careers it offers.
- [ ] L2 Reasons the industry matters locally: employment, support for local farms and suppliers,
  keeping income in the area.

## 3. Traps

- Primary, secondary and tertiary here mean the production chain (farm, factory, service). They
  are not the same as the "sectors" of dairy, meat and so on (a sector can contain all three
  levels). Teach both lists and keep them apart.
- Tertiary is services, not "retail only": the marking schemes include tourism, health, media and
  education too.
- Net exporter is about the total value of food traded, not that every food is exported. Ireland
  still imports many foods.
- Bord Bia promotes and certifies; it is not the food safety body. Enforcement belongs to the
  FSAI and its official agencies (see the food safety chapter's notes on national agencies).
- Teagasc is for farming and food research and advice; BIM is for seafood only.
- Careers in the industry belong to the careers subsection; small producers belong to the small
  businesses subsection. S6 and L1 name them in one phrase only.
- Do not use: Forbairt as an agency, because it was merged into Enterprise Ireland in the 1990s;
  a marking scheme still lists it.
- Do not use: IDA Ireland as a food industry agency, because it attracts overseas investment
  across all sectors and is not a food body; a marking scheme accepts it generally.
- Do not use: the Rural Environment Protection Scheme (REPS) as a current Teagasc example,
  because it has been replaced by later agri-environment schemes.
- Do not use: Department of Agriculture, Food and Rural Development or Department of Health and
  Children as old names, because they are superseded; use the
  current names.
- Do not use: any figure for export value, number of jobs, share of exports to the UK, share of
  farmland or number of farms, because they change every year.
- Do not use: "pork, poultry and processed meat sold mainly to the UK and other EU states", and
  the claim that "most Irish food output is exported" for every product, because pigmeat also
  has large markets outside the EU and shares change. Say instead that the UK and EU are the
  main markets for Irish food overall and that much dairy and beef output is exported.
- Do not use: "Irish whiskey, beer and stout are sold in large volumes abroad" as a volume
  claim, because it is unquantified; say they are exported and leave volumes out.

## 4. What exams ask

- Name and give one product for three sectors of the industry (6-15 marks; most common).
- Comment on the contribution of the primary, secondary and tertiary sectors (a 12-mark
  question): a definition of each with one or two examples.
- Name two major Irish food exports (and sometimes two imports), 2-6 marks. Any two from dairy,
  beef, lamb, pigmeat, poultry, seafood, prepared foods, drinks, horticulture.
- Name a government body or agency in the food industry and state its function (6 marks).
  Teagasc, BIM, Bord Bia, FSAI are all accepted, each with its function.
- State two functions of Bord Bia (6 marks): promote Irish food at home and abroad; support
  companies; develop markets; run quality assurance schemes.
- Short-answer packages: "name two bodies; list two careers" (careers in the third subsection).
- Likely tips: always give a product with each sector; for exports, say what the product is
  (not just "dairy"); for agencies, pair the name with one function in the same sentence.
