# Package: hom-family-law-s3 — Barring orders under the Domestic Violence Act 2018

- **loId:** `hom-family-law-s3` · **chapterId:** `hom-family-law` · **syllabusRef:** 3.1.7
- **Syllabus scope:** "Barring orders under the Domestic Violence Act 2018" [3.1.7] **[HL]**. Higher Level only. (The 2001 syllabus names barring orders under the Family Law Act 1976 section 22; the outline replaces that with the 2018 Act, Appendix A.) Every block in the notes ends its heading with " (Higher Level only)".
- **Also examined:** no listed years for this item. Past schemes credited "barring or protection orders" under the superseded Domestic Violence Act 1996 (in a 14-mark question on Acts protecting children and in 12-mark marriage-breakdown answers); the current Act must be used instead.
- **Prepared by:** a higher model, 2026-11-23. The Domestic Violence Act 2018 came into operation on 1 January 2019 and consolidated and replaced the earlier domestic violence legislation. Sources checked: irishstatutebook.ie (Act No. 6 of 2018), gov.ie press release on commencement, Citizens Information (safety, protection and barring orders). The Act's name in the heading is "barring orders", but exams and the notes should show the whole family of orders so the barring order is understood in context.

## 1. Must cover

What the Act is
- [ ] D1 The Domestic Violence Act 2018 gives legal protection from domestic violence and abuse. It came into operation on 1 January 2019 and replaced the earlier domestic violence law (do not name the earlier Acts; see Traps). Domestic violence includes physical, sexual and emotional or psychological abuse and threats.
- [ ] D2 Applications are normally made to the District Court. The court can act urgently, including without the respondent being present, where there is immediate risk (assumed).
- [ ] D3 The person who applies is the applicant; the person the order is made against is the respondent (assumed terms, needed to explain the orders).

The orders
- [ ] O1 Barring order: a court order requiring the respondent to leave the home they share with the applicant (the home the applicant lives in) and to stay away from it, for the period the court sets, which is up to three years (it can be renewed). It is the order that removes the abuser from the home.
- [ ] O2 Who can get a barring order: a spouse or civil partner; a cohabitant (a partner living with the respondent in an intimate relationship); a parent against a respondent of full age who is not a dependent person (a non-dependent adult child living in the home). For cohabitants and parents the applicant's interest in the home must not be less than the respondent's; a court cannot make the order if the respondent has an interest and the applicant has none or a lesser one. This property-interest condition is what keeps barring orders narrower than the others.
- [ ] O3 Safety order: a longer-term order that prohibits the respondent from using or threatening violence, from molesting or putting the applicant in fear, and from watching or following them. It does not remove the respondent from the home. It is the widest in who can apply: including people who have lived together, parents who have a child together but never lived together, and adults living together in a non-contractual relationship such as relatives.
- [ ] O4 Protection order: a temporary order with the same effect as a safety order, made while an application for a safety order or barring order is waiting to be heard. It protects the applicant in the meantime; it can be granted quickly, even before the respondent has been heard.
- [ ] O5 Interim barring order: a short-term barring order made where there is an immediate risk of significant harm to the applicant or a dependent person and a protection order would not be enough to protect them. It lasts until the application for the full barring order is decided.
- [ ] O6 Emergency barring order: an order for a person who lived with the respondent but could not get a barring order because they have a lesser interest in the home or none (for example an unmarried partner without ownership, or a parent of an adult respondent). It is for immediate risk of significant harm to the applicant or a dependent person (the same harm test as the interim barring order) and lasts for a very short period (up to eight working days).
- [ ] O7 Compare in one table or short list: safety order (prohibits, respondent stays at home, longer term); protection order (the same, temporary); barring order (respondent must leave); interim and emergency barring orders (urgent, short-term versions).

Other points the Act covers
- [ ] P1 Dependent children: the orders can protect the applicant and also a dependent child or other dependent person; Tusla (the Child and Family Agency) can apply on behalf of a person, including a child, in some cases (assumed link to the next subsection's Act).
- [ ] P2 Breaking an order is a criminal offence. The Gardaí can arrest a person who breaches an order and the respondent can be prosecuted.
- [ ] P3 Coercive control is a criminal offence under the Act: knowingly and persistently behaving in a controlling or coercive way towards a partner or former partner, with a serious effect on them (for example isolating them from family and friends, monitoring them, controlling their money). It applies to people in an intimate or formerly intimate relationship.
- [ ] P5 Exam-friendly steps: apply to the District Court; urgent orders can be made quickly; at the full hearing the court decides on a safety order or barring order; a breach is reported to the Gardaí and prosecuted.
- [ ] P6 Diagram: none.

## 3. Traps

- Do not use: the Domestic Violence Act 1996, the Family Law Act 1976 section 22 barring orders, or the Family Law (Protection of Spouses and Children) Act 1981, because they are superseded by the 2018 Act, even though past marking schemes name them. Never write notes on them.
- Do not use: maximum length of the safety order ("a safety order lasts up to five years"), because it could not be confirmed in searching and the outline asks for no changeable figures. The barring order's three-year limit and the emergency order's eight working days are confirmed and may be used.
- Do not use: "protection order prohibits the respondent without requiring them to leave" as the definition of a protection order on its own, because it leaves out the safety order and what a protection order is for: it is a temporary order pending the hearing. Use O3 and O4 together.
- Do not use: "a person in an intimate relationship can apply for a barring order even if they no longer live together", because only a safety order (and protection order) is open to people who have never lived together or who have separated; a barring order is for a home the applicant lives in and the respondent shares.
- A barring order does not transfer ownership of the home or end a marriage. It removes the respondent temporarily. Do not confuse it with divorce, judicial separation or the Family Home Protection Act (consent to sell, lease or mortgage the home).
- Coercive control is a criminal offence, not an order. Do not list it among the orders.
- Neighbouring subjects: judicial separation (the next subsection) lets a court decide who lives in the family home as part of its decree, which is different; the Child Care Act (the subsection after) protects children by Tusla applications for care or supervision orders.

## 4. What exams ask

- Higher Level only. Expect "name the Act and outline the protection it gives" in a family-law question, or a Section B answer listing the Acts that protect family members. Expect barring order defined as an order to leave the home and stay away; protection from violence or threats; criminal penalty for breaking an order.
- Name the orders available: safety, protection, barring, interim barring, emergency barring, with a one-line difference for each.
- Who may apply, including spouses, civil partners, cohabitants and parents.
- Mention that the Act also covers coercive control and that breaching an order is a criminal offence.
- Expected depth for a 10-14 mark question: name the Act correctly (2018, not 1996), two or three orders with their effect, and the court (District Court).
