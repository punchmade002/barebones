# Package: hom-social-change-s3 — Education and social welfare

- **loId:** `hom-social-change-s3` · **chapterId:** `hom-social-change` · **syllabusRef:** 6.1
- **Syllabus scope:** item 3, education and social welfare [6.1]: improvements in the provision of education and social welfare. Also examined: 2025H, 2021H, 2004O (and 2012O, 2007O for programmes that retain students and payments to families with children). Settings: comparing who attended school a hundred years ago with today. No [HL] items in this subsection. The outline marks special needs education, equality of opportunity and contemporary access initiatives [HL] in the Education chapter, not here.
- **Prepared by:** a higher model, 2026-11-23. Common to both levels. Two linked changes: how education reached more people, and how the State's income support grew; and what each did to family life. The detail of the Irish education system and of named access initiatives is in the Education chapter; the detail of unemployment payments is in the unemployment and poverty chapter and the household income subsection of the Personal Finance chapter. This subsection gives the overview and the family effects.

## 1. Must cover

Improved provision of education (one block)
- [ ] E1 A hundred years ago many children left school at primary level, and second-level and university education were mainly for those who could pay. Today every child has free primary and second-level education and a wide choice of routes after school. (Assumed: comparison, no dates other than E2.)
- [ ] E2 Free second-level education: announced in 1966 by the Minister for Education, Donogh O'Malley, and in effect from 1967 (the free post-primary scheme), removing fees that had kept many children from completing school. School attendance is compulsory from age 6 to 16 or until three years of second-level education have been completed (Education (Welfare) Act 2000).
- [ ] E3 Third level: tuition fees for eligible full-time undergraduates were abolished from 1996 (the free fees initiative), widening access; a student contribution (registration charge) remains, and grants to meet living and other costs are paid by SUSI (Student Universal Support Ireland), based on means.
- [ ] E4 Early years: the free pre-school programme, the Early Childhood Care and Education (ECCE) programme, began in 2010 and now provides two years of free pre-school before primary school, for children of the eligible age.
- [ ] E5 Support within school for children who need it: special needs assistants and resource teaching for pupils with special educational needs in mainstream classes; the School Completion Programme for students at risk of leaving school early (extra supports such as homework and out-of-school clubs); the DEIS programme for schools in disadvantaged areas.
- [ ] E6 Choices and second chances: Transition Year; Leaving Certificate Applied (a practical modular programme); Youthreach for early school leavers; Post Leaving Certificate (PLC) courses; further education and apprenticeships; adult and community education; the Back to Education Allowance for people on certain social welfare payments who take approved courses.
- [ ] E7 Access to third level for students who are disadvantaged or have a disability: HEAR (Higher Education Access Route) and DARE (Disability Access Route to Education).
- [ ] E8 Effects of improved education on family life: parents better able to help with homework and encourage children's progress; better job prospects and earnings raise the family's standard of living; women's education gives financial independence and increased participation in paid work; adult education lets parents return to work after raising children; smaller families and later marriage as education widens choices; less poverty across generations. Negative: cost of books, uniforms, transport and college living away from home; exam pressure; children leaving the area for college or work (link to rural to urban movement).

Improved provision of social welfare (one block)
- [ ] E9 Social welfare: income support from the State (through the Department of Social Protection) to people who cannot earn enough: those who are unemployed, ill or disabled, retired, widowed, parenting alone, caring for others or on low pay. It is funded from taxes and PRSI contributions (assumed: PRSI explained in the Personal Finance chapter).
- [ ] E10 Social insurance payments (contributory): depend on PRSI contributions paid while working (for example Jobseeker's Benefit, the contributory State pension, maternity benefit). Social assistance payments (non-contributory): means-tested, based on household income and savings and paid from taxation (for example Jobseeker's Allowance, the non-contributory State pension, Supplementary Welfare Allowance, One-Parent Family Payment, Disability Allowance, Carer's Allowance). Universal payments: paid to everyone in a category regardless of income, such as Child Benefit.
- [ ] E11 Payments for families with children: Child Benefit (a monthly payment for each child, paid up to age 16, or up to 18 where the child is in full-time education); the Working Family Payment (formerly Family Income Supplement) for employees on low pay with children; One-Parent Family Payment; the Back to School Clothing and Footwear Allowance (once a year, means-tested); maternity, paternity and parent's benefits for workers taking leave.
- [ ] E12 Health supports: medical cards (and GP visit cards) give eligible families free GP visits and free or low-cost prescribed medicines; they depend on means or age. Other supports: Fuel Allowance, Household Benefits Package, free travel pass for older people.
- [ ] E13 Effects of improved social welfare on family life: reduces child poverty by giving a regular income when a parent is out of work, ill or on low pay; old age pensions give older people financial independence so they do not depend on their adult children; carer's payments let a family member care for a relative at home; lone-parent families can support children; lets children stay in school and have more activities. Negative or criticisms: payments can be low relative to living costs; dependence on welfare and a "poverty trap" when extra earnings reduce payments (the poverty trap is Higher Level in the poverty chapter: mention in one clause only); some people feel stigma in claiming; the cost to taxpayers.
- [ ] E14 Conditions on payments: job-seeking and activation requirements for jobseekers; means tests; contribution records. These aim to avoid long-term dependence.

Settings (one short block)
- [ ] E15 Ask an older relative how many years they attended school, how many were in their class and whether they stayed on; record the same for a present-day young person; note which supports (special needs assistants, school completion programme, free pre-school) the older relative did not have.

## 3. Traps

- Do not use: "a free pre-school year", because the programme now provides two free years (the old notes say one year).
- Do not use: Child Benefit payable to 19, or the Department of Social and Family Affairs or Department of Social Welfare, because these are superseded (the 2007 marking scheme uses them); the current body is the Department of Social Protection and Child Benefit currently ends at 16, or at 18 for a child in full-time education.
- Do not use: FÁS as a training provider, because it was replaced by SOLAS and the Education and Training Boards (2007 marking scheme names FÁS).
- Do not use: the Combat Poverty Agency as a current body, because it was dissolved (2009); the 2007 marking scheme names it.
- Do not use: any amount of a payment, the income limit for a medical card, grant values, the age of State pension or the rates of PRSI, because these change.
- Do not use: "institutes of technology" as the third-level sector in general, because many have become technological universities; say "colleges and universities".
- Do not use: details of "individual school retention plans" or other internal workings of the School Completion Programme, because they are unconfirmed; say "extra supports such as homework and out-of-school activities".
- Do not confuse social insurance (contributory, based on PRSI record) with social assistance (means-tested) with universal payments (Child Benefit). The Back to Education Allowance is an income support, not a course.
- Neighbouring subsections: the full structure of Irish education (pre-school, primary, second-level, third-level, adult), the purpose of education, equality of opportunity in education, special needs education and named access initiatives belong to the Education chapter; unemployment payments and community responses belong to the unemployment and poverty chapter; PRSI, tax, pensions and household income belong to the Personal Finance chapter; the effect of women's education on work belongs to the women in the workforce subsection. Give these only as one-line links.
- Do not present welfare only as a benefit: the exam asks for effects, and weaker answers omit the problems and cost.

## 4. What exams ask

- Discuss the impact on family life of improvements in education (Higher Level 2025 and 2021 questions on increased educational requirements; Ordinary Level 2004 and 2007). Expected: parents help with homework, better jobs and incomes, women's independence, smaller families, adult return to education.
- Discuss the impact on family life of improvements in social welfare entitlements: reduced poverty, income for the unemployed and sick, independence for older people, support for lone parents and carers, plus a drawback or two.
- Give three social welfare payments for families with children and details of one (name, who is eligible, how it helps): Child Benefit, Working Family Payment, back-to-school clothing allowance, One-Parent Family Payment.
- Name and give details of programmes that keep young people in second-level education (School Completion Programme, Leaving Certificate Applied, Youthreach, Transition Year) and two benefits to the student of each.
- Short questions: true or false that Child Benefit is universal and Supplementary Welfare Allowance is means tested; define means test and PRSI.
