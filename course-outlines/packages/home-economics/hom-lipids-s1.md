# Package: hom-lipids-s1 — Fatty acids

- **Prepared by:** a higher model, 2026-11-23. Higher and Ordinary Level except where marked [HL]. The triglyceride that these acids build is in the next-but-one subsection ("Triglycerides and the classification of lipids"); where lipids are found in foods is in "Sources"; cholesterol and the health effects of a high-saturated-fat diet are in "Functions and energy value". ## 1. Must cover

What a fatty acid is and the three classes (one block)
- [ ] F1 (assumed) Fatty acids are the building units of fats and oils (lipids). A fatty acid is a chain of carbon atoms with hydrogen attached, with a carboxyl group (–COOH) at one end and a methyl group (–CH₃) at the other.
- [ ] F2 Classification by the number of double bonds (C=C) between carbon atoms in the chain: saturated = none (every carbon holds as much hydrogen as it can); monounsaturated = one; polyunsaturated = two or more.
- [ ] F3 Physical state: saturated fatty acids and fats rich in them are generally solid at room temperature; fats rich in mono- and polyunsaturated acids are generally liquid (oils). Exceptions to mention once: coconut and palm oil are plant oils rich in saturated fat.
- [ ] F4 One named example of each class (marking schemes accept): saturated = stearic acid (or butyric acid); monounsaturated = oleic acid; polyunsaturated = linoleic acid (linolenic acid also accepted).

Structure of fatty acids [HL] (one block)
- [ ] S1 [HL] Saturated: a chain of carbon atoms joined by single bonds only, fully saturated with hydrogen; carboxyl group at one end, methyl group at the other; the straight chains pack closely together, which is why the fat is solid.
- [ ] S2 [HL] Monounsaturated: one double bond, because two neighbouring carbon atoms each hold one hydrogen fewer ("some carbons are unsaturated"); the double bond puts a kink in the chain, so the chains pack less tightly and the fat is liquid.
- [ ] S3 [HL] Polyunsaturated: two or more double bonds, so more than one kink; liquid at room temperature.
- [ ] S4 [HL] Diagram: draw and label a saturated, a monounsaturated and a polyunsaturated fatty acid chain, marking the –COOH end, the –CH₃ end and each C=C double bond. (Draw a zig-zag or a simple row of carbons with C=C marked; the structure question is worth about 6 marks per acid at Higher Level.)
- [ ] S5 [HL] Omega-3 structure (asked 2013H): a polyunsaturated fatty acid whose first double bond lies between the third and fourth carbon atoms counted from the methyl (omega) end. Omega-6: first double bond at the sixth carbon from the methyl end.

Cis and trans fatty acids [HL] (one block)
- [ ] C1 [HL] The two forms occur at a double bond in an unsaturated fatty acid. Cis: the two hydrogen atoms on either side of the double bond are on the same side of the carbon chain (both above or both below), so the chain bends. Trans: the two hydrogen atoms are on opposite sides, so the chain stays nearly straight, like a saturated chain.
- [ ] C2 [HL] Cis fatty acids are the usual natural form in plant oils and fish oils (2006H credits cooking oils, oily fish as a source).
- [ ] C3 [HL] Sources of trans fatty acids: formed when oils are partially hydrogenated in food manufacture; small amounts also occur naturally in the fat of ruminant animals (milk fat and beef/lamb fat).
- [ ] C4 [HL] Health contrast, taught in the "significance" block below: cis unsaturated acids are protective; trans acids behave like saturated fat in the blood.

Essential fatty acids (one block)
- [ ] E1 Essential fatty acids are polyunsaturated fatty acids that the body cannot make (or cannot make in enough quantity), so they must come from food.
- [ ] E2 The two named most often: linoleic acid (an omega-6 fatty acid) and linolenic acid (an omega-3 fatty acid). Marking schemes also list arachidonic acid; strictly the body can make it from linoleic acid, so say that it is formed from linoleic acid when enough linoleic acid is eaten (see Traps).
- [ ] E3 Functions (2025H scheme): support the structure of cell membranes, growth, the nervous system and brain, the immune response, and the formation of hormone-like compounds (prostaglandins) that regulate blood clotting, blood pressure and inflammation.
- [ ] E4 Sources: vegetable oils (sunflower, corn, soya, rapeseed), nuts and seeds, oily fish (see "Sources"; only one or two examples here).

Omega-3 fatty acids: function and sources (one block; examined 2016H, 2015O, 2007H, 2014O)
- [ ] O1 Function (any one earns the mark at Ordinary Level; Higher Level gives several): reduce the risk of heart attack, stroke, circulatory disease and the formation of blood clots; help maintain healthy brain activity; can raise HDL cholesterol; marking schemes also credit reducing inflammation and (2007H, 2025H) possibly reducing the risk of some cancers. Use "may" or "helps reduce the risk" wording, not "prevents".
- [ ] O2 Food sources: oily fish (salmon, mackerel, herring, sardines, trout; tuna is named in one scheme but see Traps), flaxseed (linseed), walnuts, soya beans, rapeseed oil; omega-3-enriched eggs and milk (a hen's or cow's diet is changed) and fish-oil supplements are accepted.
- [ ] O3 True/false facts: omega-3 fatty acids are found in oily fish (True); omega-3 fatty acids help reduce the risk of heart disease (True); essential fatty acids are made in the body (False).

Significance of fatty acids in the diet, LDL and HDL (one block; 2025H, 2018H, 2013H, 2012H)
- [ ] D1 Cholesterol travels in the blood bound to proteins, forming lipoproteins. LDL (low-density lipoprotein) carries cholesterol to the tissues and is the "harmful" form because excess is deposited on artery walls, narrowing them (atherosclerosis) and raising the risk of coronary heart disease (CHD). HDL (high-density lipoprotein) carries cholesterol away from the tissues to the liver for removal and is the "protective" form.
- [ ] D2 Saturated fatty acids raise LDL cholesterol and so raise the risk of CHD.
- [ ] D3 Unsaturated (cis) fatty acids, both mono- and polyunsaturated, help lower LDL and help raise HDL, so are generally good for the heart, especially when they replace saturated fat.
- [ ] D4 [HL] Trans fatty acids raise LDL and lower HDL, increasing the risk of CHD; they are thought to do more harm than saturated fat.
- [ ] D5 Essential fatty acids must be eaten (E1–E3); omega-3 gives the benefits in O1.
- [ ] D6 Exam sentence form: 2018H and 2013H award marks for "unsaturated (cis) good: reduce LDL, increase HDL" and "trans: increase LDL, deposit cholesterol, increase CHD". The full 2025H answer (12 marks) combines essential fatty acids, omega-3, mono/poly/cis and saturated/trans.

## 3. Traps

- Saturated means "saturated with hydrogen", not "high in saturates in the diet". Unsaturated means double bonds are present.
- Cis and trans are about hydrogen positions at one double bond, not the number of double bonds. Do not confuse cis/trans with saturated/unsaturated: trans acids are unsaturated chemically but act like saturated fat in the body.
- LDL and HDL are lipoproteins (carriers of cholesterol), not types of cholesterol itself. "Bad" and "good" cholesterol are shorthand; use the carrier names.
- Arachidonic acid: schemes list it with the essential fatty acids (2004O, 2025H). Teach linoleic and linolenic as the two essential fatty acids and mention arachidonic acid as listed by examiners and made from linoleic acid.
- Omega-3 is not one acid; it is a family (the position of the first double bond defines it).
- Trans fats and hydrogenation: hydrogenation itself is a property of lipids ("Properties"). Here say only that partial hydrogenation forms trans acids.
- The triglyceride, glycerol and ester bond belong to "Triglycerides and the classification of lipids". Cholesterol as a substance, its uses and gallstones belong to "Functions and energy value".
- Do not use: "A polyunsaturated fatty acid has more than one bend in its chain for every double bond", because it is garbled; correct form is one kink per cis double bond, so more double bonds means more kinks.
- Do not use: "Foods rich in trans fatty acids include hard margarines, some shortenings and fat used repeatedly in a deep-fat fryer", because modern margarines and shortenings are reformulated under the EU limit of 2 g trans fat per 100 g fat, and the fryer claim is an overstatement. Say instead: partially hydrogenated fats in some processed foods (biscuits, pastries, fried takeaway foods) and natural traces in dairy and beef fat.
- Do not use: "cis fatty acids are converted to trans during high-temperature cooking" as a general statement, because only small amounts form and it is not examined.
- Do not use: "Omega-3 fatty acids prevent heart attack/cancer", because the evidence supports a reduced risk, not prevention (the schemes say "reduce the risk").

## 4. What exams ask

- Classify fatty acids and give one example of each; Higher Level asks for the structure of each (about 18 marks for three structures plus examples in 2025H, 2018H, 2008H): double-bond count, hydrogen saturation, carboxyl and methyl ends, state at room temperature, named acid. Draw the chain with C=C marked.
- Cis versus trans (6-mark short question 2022H; 2013H as a 12-mark part with omega-3): hydrogens same side versus opposite sides, and the effect on LDL/HDL. 2006H also asks for one source of each.
- Omega-3 (2007H, 2016H, 2015O, 2014O): one function and two named sources for 6 marks; the structure at Higher Level (first double bond third carbon from methyl end).
- Significance of fatty acids (8 to 12 marks): good answer has three points, each with a reason: essential fatty acids needed (cannot be made); cis unsaturated lower LDL and raise HDL; saturated and trans raise LDL, lower HDL, increase CHD risk. Add omega-3 for the 12-mark version.
- LDL and HDL (2012H, 6 marks): each defined as a cholesterol-carrying lipoprotein with its effect on the arteries, 3 marks each.
- True/false items at Ordinary Level: lipids contain nitrogen (False); essential fatty acids are made in the body (False); omega-3 in oily fish (True); omega-3 reduces risk of heart disease (True); polyunsaturated fatty acids in oily fish (True).
- Essential fatty acid questions at Ordinary Level: name the fatty acids and a function.
