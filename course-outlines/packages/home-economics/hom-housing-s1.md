# Package: hom-housing-s1 — Housing choices

- **loId:** `hom-housing-s1` · **chapterId:** `hom-housing` · **syllabusRef:** 2.1.4 (core, Ordinary and Higher Level)
- **Syllabus scope:** factors that determine individual and family housing choices — socio-economic factors, national housing policy (the current national plan is *Delivering Homes, Building Communities 2025–2030*), trends in housing development, availability. No [HL] marks in this subsection.
- **Also examined:** features of national housing policy (2024H, 2014H, 2008H); buying vs renting (2021O, 2018O, 2014H); private rented sector (2014H). Past papers also ask "how do cost, location, availability and trends influence a family's choice" (2004O, 2008H, 2012H, 2014O, 2017H, 2019O, 2023O, 2026O).
- **Settings (default examples):** a case study of a student, a young couple or a retired couple choosing a home.
- **Prepared by:** a higher model, 2026-11-23. Housing finance (mortgages, deposits, protection) is in "Housing finance"; the needs of particular groups and social housing are in "Housing provision"; location choice for a self-build is in "Location, planning and professional services". This subsection is the choice a household makes (what shapes it, what policy and the market offer, and buy against rent).

## 1. Must cover

Factors that shape a household's choice (one block)
- [ ] C1 Socio-economic factors: income and ability to afford a deposit or rent, employment, household size, ages of children and the stage of the family life cycle (young couple, growing family, teenagers, empty nest, older couple), special needs of a member (e.g. an older person or a person with a disability), personal preference (layout, view, garden).
- [ ] C2 Cost: the price or rent decides the size, type and location; the other costs (deposit, legal and lender fees, stamp duty, repayments, insurance, maintenance, heating, travel to work) must be counted as well as the headline price. Parental help with a deposit is an economic factor.
- [ ] C3 Location: closeness to work, schools, childcare, shops, health services, public transport, family and friends; urban against rural (services and jobs against space and quiet); safety, noise, and risks such as flooding or pollution. (Detail of choosing a site for building is in "Location, planning and professional services"; here it is one choice among several.)
- [ ] C4 Type and size of home: bungalow, two-storey, detached, semi-detached, terraced, townhouse, duplex, apartment, bedsit; room for the family, a possible attic conversion or extension, parking, space to work from home, energy efficiency and BER (named only; the BER itself is in "House design and building standards").
- [ ] C5 Availability: the supply of suitable homes in the area and sector at a given time. Private sales and the private rented sector can be short of supply in cities and commuter areas, which raises prices and rents; social housing demand exceeds supply, so there are waiting lists.
- [ ] C6 Setting: apply the factors to a short case (student, young couple, retired couple) and say which matters most.

Trends in housing development (one block)
- [ ] T1 Mixed estates of detached, semi-detached, terraced and apartment or duplex units, with shared open space, play areas, and in larger estates a crèche or shops; many estates and complexes with a gated or controlled entrance.
- [ ] T2 Higher-density development and apartment living in cities and towns, on less land per household; townhouses on small urban sites.
- [ ] T3 Refurbishment and inner-city redevelopment: vacant, derelict and older buildings brought back into use; restoration of period homes; attic conversions and extensions.
- [ ] T4 Energy-efficient, environmentally friendly and passive homes (high insulation, renewable heating, timber frame or other modern methods of construction).
- [ ] T5 Housing development follows employment: commuter towns and suburbs grow around jobs, and commuting distance is a cost to the household. Older marking schemes also name satellite towns and one-off rural houses.
- [ ] T6 Working from home creates demand for a study or home-office space.

Irish national housing policy (one block)
- [ ] P1 Housing policy is the government's plan for how enough suitable homes are provided and who is helped. The current national plan is *Delivering Homes, Building Communities 2025–2030* (it replaced *Housing for All*; see Traps).
- [ ] P2 Features the notes may give (each is a point an exam can credit): increasing the supply of new homes, with investment in the infrastructure (water, wastewater, roads) that housing needs; social housing built by local authorities and approved housing bodies; affordable homeownership supports, including the Starter Homes programme; cost rental homes; measures to reduce and prevent homelessness, including the Housing First programme; national rent controls for tenants; modern methods of construction (factory-built components) to speed building.
- [ ] P3 Policy themes that run through exam answers (give as general aims, not as clauses of the plan): sustainable communities supported by shops, schools, transport and services; energy-efficient homes, supported by the Building Regulations, BER ratings and SEAI grants; accommodation for Travellers, which each local authority plans under a Traveller Accommodation Programme (Housing (Traveller Accommodation) Act 1998).
- [ ] P4 How policy affects a household's choice: it changes what is built and where, what supports and grants are open to a buyer or tenant, and how much a tenant is protected.

Buying a home: advantages and disadvantages (one block)
- [ ] B1 Advantages: the home may rise in value (an investment); security of tenure; freedom to alter, decorate and choose the design; ownership builds equity; repayments may cost less over time than rent; some supports for buyers.
- [ ] B2 Disadvantages: a deposit and a long financial commitment; legal, lender, valuation and survey fees and stamp duty on top of the price; the owner pays all maintenance, repairs, insurance and property tax; the value can fall; tied to one place, so moving is slow and costly.

Renting a home: advantages and disadvantages (one block)
- [ ] R1 Advantages: little capital needed (a deposit and rent in advance); mobility; the landlord normally carries structural repairs; wide choice of location and type; legal protection for tenants, including notice rules.
- [ ] R2 Disadvantages: rent gives no ownership or return ("dead money"); an agency fee, a deposit and rent in advance; the rent can be as high as a mortgage repayment on a similar home; no say over changes; contents insurance is the tenant's cost; no long-term security if the landlord sells.
- [ ] R3 Why renting is popular (examined in Ordinary Level papers): buying is expensive and a deposit is hard to save, the rent is a smaller outlay, no repair costs, mobility for work, and a choice of area.

Private rented sector (one block)
- [ ] S1 What it is: homes (houses, apartments, flats, rooms) let by private landlords, mainly in cities and larger towns; popular with students, young workers and people saving for a deposit; quality varies.
- [ ] S2 Landlords must register tenancies with the Residential Tenancies Board (RTB), and the RTB deals with disputes. Rented homes must meet minimum standards set by the Housing (Standards for Rented Houses) Regulations 2019 (structure and repair, heating, ventilation and lighting, water supply, sanitary facilities, fire safety, gas and electrical safety), enforced by local authorities.
- [ ] S3 The sector gives flexibility to people in temporary work or who want to move, and the State helps some tenants with rent through social housing supports (named in "Housing provision").

Assumed knowledge
- [ ] A1 (assumed) What a mortgage and a deposit are, in one phrase only: the mortgage types and approval rules are in "Housing finance".
- [ ] A2 (assumed) Tenure terms: owner-occupier, tenant, landlord, tenancy; security of tenure means the right to stay in the home without being asked to leave without legal cause.

## 3. Traps

- Do not use: *Housing for All* as the current policy, because it was replaced by *Delivering Homes, Building Communities 2025–2030*. Marking schemes up to 2024 name it; the notes name the new plan only.
- Do not use: mortgage interest relief, tenant purchase scheme, "ghost estates", tax-designated areas or rent allowance as current features, because these appear in 2008–2017 marking schemes and are ended, renamed or out of date.
- Do not use: "PRTB" or "Private Residential Tenancies Board" as the body's name, because it is now the Residential Tenancies Board (RTB), with a wider role.
- Do not use: protection of listed buildings (with grants) and protection of the natural environment as features of the current national plan, because they come from the earlier policy's marking schemes and were not confirmed in the 2025–2030 plan.
- Do not use: the plan's targets (number of homes, social homes a year, VAT rate on apartments, fund sizes), because they are changing figures.
- Do not use: statistics from marking schemes or paper stimuli (percentage of households renting, ownership share, waiting-list totals), because they date quickly.
- Do not use: any detail of rent caps, rent pressure zones, tenancy lengths or notice periods, because the rules for tenants were being reformed and the details are not confirmed.
- Buying a home as an investment is a possibility, not a certainty: values can fall (state both).
- Renting is not always cheaper per month: in high-demand areas rent can match or exceed a mortgage repayment; the saving is in upfront cost and repairs.
- Belongs to a neighbouring subsection: mortgage types, deposits, approval conditions and mortgage protection (housing finance); needs of families, older people, disabled and homeless people and social housing schemes (housing provision); BER detail, grants and building standards (house design and building standards); choosing a site and planning permission (location, planning and professional services). Mention each in one clause only.

## 4. What exams ask

- Name and explain factors influencing housing choice: Higher Level questions name three headings (e.g. socio-economic factors, availability, national housing policy; or economic factors, policy, trends) and want two developed points under each; Ordinary Level questions give a family (e.g. a couple buying) and ask how cost, size and type of house, location and trends influence them.
- A short question: outline two features of national housing policy (Section A, 6 marks); expected answers are the features in P2 and P3, each in a sentence.
- A short question: identify or analyse three recent trends in housing development (6 marks): mixed estates and open space, apartments and higher density, refurbishment and inner-city redevelopment, eco or energy-efficient homes, gated developments, extensions and attic conversions, home-working space.
- Advantages and disadvantages of renting (Ordinary Level 6 or 12 marks, usually two of each, or the reasons renting is popular): capital outlay, mobility, maintenance, deposit and agency fees, no return, insurance.
- Compare the costs of buying and renting for a family (Higher Level 15 marks): deposit, legal and lender fees and stamp duty against deposit and a month's rent; mortgage repayments, protection and home insurance, property tax and repairs against rent; investment and security against flexibility.
- "Informative note" on the private rented sector, three points (Higher Level 2014): who rents, where, quality, flexibility, landlord registration, minimum standards.
- An answer earns most when it names the factor and says its effect on the choice, using a stated family's circumstances.
