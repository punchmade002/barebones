# Package: hom-social-change-s4 — Changing attitudes

- **loId:** `hom-social-change-s4` · **chapterId:** `hom-social-change` · **syllabusRef:** 6.1
- **Syllabus scope:** item 4, changing attitudes [6.1]: to marriage, parenting and traditional roles within the family. Chapter-wide also examined: 2026O (marriage, working hours, parenting), 2023H (marriage, parenting roles, women's work), 2014H (marriage, traditional roles, modern communications technology), 2012O, 2011H, 2008H (decline of the traditional family), 2007O, 2006H. Settings: comparing family life a hundred years ago with today (roles, number of children). No [HL] items in this subsection (the outline marks historical development of the Irish family and role conflict [HL] in the Family and Society chapter).
- **Prepared by:** a higher model, 2026-11-23. Common to both levels. Attitudes are what people think is right or normal. The subsection says what attitudes to marriage, to parenting and to the roles of men, women and children were, how they changed, why, and what that did to family life. Family law is given at the depth needed for the divorce conditions only.

## 1. Must cover

Attitudes to marriage (one block)
- [ ] A1 Marriage used to be expected for everyone, usually early, and the only accepted basis for a family and for living together. Social, family and Church pressure to marry young has fallen; couples now marry later, often after education and building a career; some do not marry at all.
- [ ] A2 Cohabitation (living together without being married) is common and widely accepted; many children are born to unmarried parents. Legislation recognises cohabiting couples: the Civil Partnership and Certain Rights and Obligations of Cohabitants Act 2010. Children born outside marriage are no longer treated differently in law (Status of Children Act 1987).
- [ ] A3 Separation and divorce: divorce became possible after the 1995 referendum, and the Family Law (Divorce) Act 1996 sets out the conditions. The conditions now are: the spouses have lived apart for two of the previous three years (changed by the Family Law Act 2019, from four of the previous five); there is no reasonable prospect of reconciliation; proper provision has been or will be made for the spouses and dependent children. Judicial separation (Judicial Separation and Family Law Reform Act 1989, as amended) is a court order allowing spouses to live apart and dealing with maintenance and property, without ending the marriage (assumed: law detail is in the Family Law subsection of the Family and Society chapter).
- [ ] A4 Same-sex couples: the 2015 referendum allowed same-sex couples to marry (Marriage Act 2015); wider acceptance of different family types.
- [ ] A5 Effects on family life: more lone-parent, blended (reconstituted) and cohabiting families; smaller families; later first births; more family breakdown but also greater freedom to leave an unhappy or abusive marriage; children may live in more than one household; family relationships are chosen rather than expected; more variety in family structure; less stigma for lone parents.

Attitudes to parenting (one block)
- [ ] A6 Parenting has become more child-centred: children's views, rights and wellbeing; open communication and negotiated rules instead of strict obedience. Children's rights are recognised in the Constitution (the Thirty-first Amendment, Children, 2012, which became law in 2015).
- [ ] A7 Physical punishment: the defence of reasonable chastisement was abolished by the Children First Act 2015, so smacking is no longer lawful discipline; attitudes moved to explaining, praising and setting limits. Child protection is the State's concern (Tusla, Child and Family Agency).
- [ ] A8 Smaller families: contraception and family planning let couples choose the number and timing of children; the cost of rearing children; women's work. More attention and money per child.
- [ ] A9 Shared parenting: fathers are more involved in caring and housework; paternity leave (Paternity Leave and Benefit Act 2016), unpaid parental leave (Parental Leave Act 1998) and paid Parent's Leave and Benefit (2019 Act) recognise the father's and both parents' role; some men are the main carers (house husbands).
- [ ] A10 Other carers: grandparents often give regular childcare, especially where both parents work; paid childcare (childminders, creches, after-school services) and pre-school education are widely used.
- [ ] A11 Effects on family life: closer, more open relationships and more equal decisions; greater independence for children; more pressure and guilt for working parents ("time poverty"); costs of childcare; less respect for traditional authority; children may take on more responsibility for chores or care of younger siblings.

Attitudes to traditional roles within the family (one block)
- [ ] A12 Traditional roles: the father was the breadwinner and head of the household, the mother the homemaker and carer, with clearly separated tasks; children obeyed and helped; older children minded younger ones. Egalitarian (shared) roles: partners share earning, housework, childcare and decisions. Pattern now: both partners often work (the dual-earner family).
- [ ] A13 Change in roles: men in housework and childcare; women in paid work and decision-making; a man as main carer or house husband and a woman as main earner are accepted; household tasks are shared with children; family diversity (nuclear, extended, lone-parent, blended, cohabiting, same-sex-parent) is accepted.
- [ ] A14 Effects: more equal relationships; more income; role overload and role conflict, time pressure and stress (developed in the Work and Leisure chapter); arguments about sharing tasks; greater independence for women; some men feel a loss of status.

Causes of changing attitudes (one block)
- [ ] A15 Education, especially for women, widened choices about careers, marriage and children.
- [ ] A16 Contraception and family planning gave control over family size and timing.
- [ ] A17 Women's participation in paid work and equality legislation gave financial independence.
- [ ] A18 Decline in the influence of the Church and of tradition (secularisation), so marriage, sexuality, cohabitation and divorce are seen as personal choices.
- [ ] A19 Urbanisation and mobility loosened the influence of the extended family and local community.
- [ ] A20 Media, travel and international influences, and communications technology, bringing new ideas about relationships and family life.
- [ ] A21 Greater value placed on individual choice and personal freedom over duty to family or community; higher living costs and housing costs requiring two incomes; legislation and referendums reflecting and speeding up change.
- [ ] A22 Modern communications technology and family life (examined 2014H): mobile phones, social media and video calls keep the extended family and emigrants in contact and allow remote working; but screen time can reduce face-to-face time, parents may find it harder to supervise children, and cyberbullying and online safety are concerns.

Settings (one short block)
- [ ] A23 Compare roles, number of children and attitudes to marriage a hundred years ago and today; interview an older relative about who earned, who cared and who made decisions in their childhood home.

## 3. Traps

- Do not use: "lived apart for four of the previous five years" as the divorce condition, because the Family Law Act 2019 reduced it to two of the previous three. The 2006 marking scheme and the old notes give four of five.
- Do not use: the Family Law Act 1976 barring orders or the Family Law (Maintenance of Spouses and Children) Act as the law on separation or divorce, because barring orders are now under the Domestic Violence Act 2018 and maintenance is a different matter.
- Do not use: any statistic such as average age at marriage, the number of divorces, the proportion of births outside marriage or average family size, because they are changing figures or unconfirmed.
- Do not use: "smacking is a criminal offence", because the 2015 Act removed the defence; it did not create a new offence.
- Do not use: a named year for the introduction of contraception or the decline of Church influence, because dates are unconfirmed; family planning law was widened in stages from 1979.
- Judicial separation does not end a marriage and neither spouse can remarry; divorce ends it. Cohabitation gives fewer legal rights than marriage.
- Nuclear, extended, lone-parent and blended families, and the sociological terms (socialisation, kinship, social mobility), belong to the family structures and sociological concepts chapters; here they appear only as outcomes of changing attitudes.
- The functions of the family, family law in full (maintenance, barring orders, guardianship, wills) and the life cycle of the family belong to the Family and Society chapter; reconciling work and family belongs to the Work and Leisure chapter; women's employment belongs to the women in the workforce subsection.
- Do not suggest "traditional" family roles were better or worse; present them as different and say what changed.
- Attitudes are different from laws: say a law reflected or helped to change an attitude.

## 4. What exams ask

- Discuss the impact of changing attitudes to marriage on family life (every level, almost every year in some form: 2007O, 2012O, 2014H, 2023H, 2026O). Expected: later marriage, cohabitation, divorce and separation, lone-parent and blended families, same-sex marriage, smaller families, more choice.
- Discuss changing attitudes to parenting and parenting roles: child-centred approach, shared parenting, fathers' leave, smaller families, grandparents and childcare.
- Discuss changes in traditional roles within the family: breadwinner and homemaker to equal partners; the effects of women working; house husbands; children's roles. Higher Level wants an analysis of the reasons for the decline of the "traditional family" (separation and divorce, births outside marriage, fewer marriages, changing roles, contraception, urbanisation).
- Set out the conditions for granting a divorce under the Family Law (Divorce) Act 1996. Give three: living apart for the required period, no reasonable prospect of reconciliation, proper provision for spouses and dependants. Today's required period is two of the previous three years.
- Impact of modern communications technology on family life (2014H): contact and convenience versus screen time and safety.
- Short questions: define cohabitation, egalitarian roles, judicial separation; give two reasons attitudes to marriage changed.
