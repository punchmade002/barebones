# Package: hom-family-society-s4 — Changes affecting family structures

- **loId:** `hom-family-society-s4` · **chapterId:** `hom-family-society` · **syllabusRef:** 3.1.3
- **Syllabus scope [HL]:** "social, economic and technological changes affecting modern family structures." The whole subsection is Higher Level only [HL]. Also examined: 2024H, 2019H, 2016H.
- **Prepared by:** a higher model, 2026-11-23. This is the causes subsection. The three headings (social, economic, technological) come straight from the question wording in 2019H and 2024H; 2016H asked only for social and economic. For each change, the notes should state the change and then its effect on the family (size, structure, roles, location). The narrative of when things happened is in "The historical development of the Irish family", and the descriptions of the structures are in "Modern family structures"; give only a clause on those here. Role changes and role conflict belong to the chapter on the family as a caring unit.

## 1. Must cover

Social changes (one block)
- [ ] S1 [HL] Decline of the extended family, with urbanisation and movement from rural to urban areas: fewer relatives nearby, smaller nuclear households.
- [ ] S2 [HL] Separation and divorce (divorce possible since the Family Law (Divorce) Act 1996): more lone-parent families and, through new partnerships, more blended families.
- [ ] S3 [HL] Cohabitation and births outside marriage: unmarried couples form families, and marriage is no longer the only basis of a family.
- [ ] S4 [HL] Later marriage, later first births and fewer children (family planning, contraception, women's choices): smaller families.
- [ ] S5 [HL] Gender equality in education and employment: more dual-career couples, shared household roles and, in some families, a father as the main carer.
- [ ] S6 [HL] Declining influence of religion and a more multi-denominational and multicultural society: more choice in how people form families, and a wider range of family types.
- [ ] S7 [HL] Same-sex marriage and civil partnership, and adoption: same-sex parent families are a recognised structure. Surrogacy and assisted reproduction are social issues in the schemes; mention only that they allow new routes to parenthood.
- [ ] S8 [HL] Children dependent on parents for longer (more years in education), so adult children stay at home longer. Increased life expectancy: more generations alive, more older people, more care for them.
- [ ] S9 [HL] Others the schemes list: addiction and its effect on family life; changing attitudes to lone parents.

Economic changes (one block)
- [ ] C1 [HL] The cost of living, housing and childcare has risen: both parents often need to work (dual incomes); couples may delay having children or have fewer.
- [ ] C2 [HL] The recession after 2008: unemployment, mortgage arrears, lower incomes; some families moved in with relatives and adult children stayed at or returned to the family home, giving multi-generation households.
- [ ] C3 [HL] Emigration and mobility for work: parents or adult children living abroad, so the family is spread across countries; a parent may work away from home.
- [ ] C4 [HL] Women's paid employment: many mothers work outside the home, which increases demand for childcare and changes who earns and who cares.
- [ ] C5 [HL] Changes in work: shift and part-time work, flexible hours, hybrid working; farming is no longer the main source of income for farm families.
- [ ] C6 [HL] Unemployment increases the financial burden on families and financial stress, which can bring conflict; families on a low income, lone-parent families and the unemployed depend on State support.
- [ ] C7 [HL] State supports (welfare, Child Benefit, childcare schemes) affect the choices families make; no amounts.

Technological changes (one block)
- [ ] T1 [HL] Household appliances (washing machines, dishwashers, microwaves, smart appliances) save time on housework, freeing parents for paid work, leisure and children; they make shared household roles easier.
- [ ] T2 [HL] Communication technology (mobile phones, email, video calls, social media) keeps families in touch across distance, helps emigrant families and separated parents; but can reduce face-to-face contact and bring cyberbullying and pressure on children.
- [ ] T3 [HL] Home working and online services (banking, shopping, school and health information): parents can combine work and care, saving time.
- [ ] T4 [HL] Contraception and assisted human reproduction (fertility treatment): couples can decide whether and when to have children, and some couples who could not otherwise have children can. This affects family size and timing. The Health (Assisted Human Reproduction) Act 2024 (Act 18 of 2024) regulates it; only some sections are in force, so describe it as enacted in 2024, not as fully in operation.
- [ ] T5 [HL] Educational, entertainment and security technology: computers and the internet support learning; screens and games change how families spend time; home security systems and monitoring give safety.
- [ ] T6 [HL] Medical technology and better healthcare: lower child mortality and longer life, so smaller families and more older family members.

Combined effects (one block)
- [ ] K1 [HL] Changes interact: for example, high housing costs plus recession leads to adult children living at home (an extended household); emigration plus communication technology means a family split across countries but in daily contact; remote working plus childcare costs allows parents to combine work and care; divorce plus communication technology supports shared parenting between two households.
- [ ] K2 [HL] Overall result: families are smaller, more varied (nuclear, lone-parent, blended, same-sex, cohabiting) and more mobile, with more flexible roles and more reliance on State and private services.

## 3. Traps

- Keep the three headings separate. Divorce, religion, gender equality, urbanisation and cohabitation are social; costs, jobs, income, recession, emigration are economic; appliances, communication, home working, contraception and fertility treatment are technological. Where a change fits two headings, choose one and say why.
- Every point must be linked to a family structure or a family consequence (size, who lives together, roles, location). "The recession happened" is not an answer; "adult children stayed at home, giving extended households" is.
- Do not say that divorce or the decline of religion caused family breakdown; say they gave people more choice and changed the structures.
- Do not confuse cause and effect: dual incomes are an effect of women's employment and high costs; they are also a cause of the demand for childcare.
- Neighbouring subsections: the period-by-period history of the Irish family belongs to "The historical development of the Irish family"; the characteristics of nuclear, lone-parent, extended and blended families belong to "Modern family structures"; role conflict, role overload and role changes belong to the chapter on the family as a caring unit; the law of divorce and separation belongs to the family law chapter. Give a clause only.
- Do not use: "Divorce became legal under the Family Law (Divorce) Act 1996" as the whole account, because the constitutional ban was first removed by the 1995 referendum and the Act came into operation in 1997; write "possible since 1997 under the 1996 Act".
- Do not use: figures for housing costs, childcare costs, rent, the number of emigrants, unemployment or births, because they are changing figures.
- Do not use: "women now outnumber men in employment" and similar claims, because they are not stated in any scheme and the figures change.
- Do not use: "the Church no longer has any influence on family life", because the schemes say only that its influence declined.

## 4. What exams ask

- "Analyse / discuss the social, economic and technological changes that affect families today" (2019H 24 marks; 2024H 18 marks; 2016H 20 marks social and economic only). Marking pattern: about four to six developed points, with at least one from each heading; each point states the change and its effect on the family.
- Social points expected: decline of extended family, urbanisation, gender equality, dual careers, separation and divorce, cohabitation, same-sex marriage and adoption, fewer and later children, multiculturalism, shared roles.
- Economic points expected: living, housing and childcare costs, State supports, dual incomes, mobility for work or a parent abroad, hybrid work, unemployment, agriculture no longer the main income for farm families.
- Technological points expected: communication and social media, home working and online services, household appliances and smart technology, educational, entertainment and security technology.
- A variation would be to analyse one heading only or to ask for the effect on a single structure, such as lone-parent or extended families. Prepare for that by linking changes to structures.
- Tip: use the pattern "change, effect on the family, example".
