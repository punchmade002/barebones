# Package: hom-resource-management-s3 — Factors that affect management

- **loId:** `hom-resource-management-s3` · **chapterId:** `hom-resource-management` · **syllabusRef:** 2.1.2
- **Syllabus scope:** [2.1.2] stages in the life cycle, employment pattern, culture, values, standards, sex roles, managing a dual role, lifestyle as determined by socio-economic status and family composition.
  - Also examined: 2023H, 2022O, 2013H, 2011O.
  - No [HL] items in this subsection (common to Ordinary and Higher Level).
- **Prepared by:** a higher model, 2026-11-23. The marking schemes treat each of these as a separate "factor": composition of family, stage in life cycle, employment patterns, socio-economic status, culture, management of dual roles, gender (sex) roles, values and standards. The 2013H 20-mark question asked for any four with an explanation of each; 2011O asked how stage in life cycle, employment patterns and sex/gender roles affect a family (18 marks). The skill is to explain how each factor changes the family's resources, priorities or the way tasks are shared, not just to name it.

## 1. Must cover

Stages in the life cycle
- [ ] L1 The family life cycle is the sequence of stages a family passes through: for example a newly married or partnered couple, a family with young children, a family with school-going children, a family with teenagers, a family whose children are leaving home, and older people. Priorities, income and time change at each stage.
- [ ] L2 Young children: need constant care, time, money and space; routines and childcare dominate management; less free time, and often reduced income if a parent works fewer hours.
- [ ] L3 School-going children and teenagers: costs rise (clothes, school costs, activities, education); children can take on tasks and join decisions, which reduces the parents' workload.
- [ ] L4 Couples without children, or after the children leave: more freedom, and often more money and time for leisure; later, retirement may lower income and may reduce mobility or increase health needs.
- [ ] L5 Summary line: priorities are different for a family with or without children; as children get older they become part of the decision-making process.

Employment patterns
- [ ] E1 Employment pattern is the number of family members working, whether inside or outside the home, the type of job and the hours.
- [ ] E2 It affects income (how much there is to manage) and time (when members are free to do tasks).
- [ ] E3 Dual-income household: more money but less time; needs planning and shared work. Single-income household: less money, so budgeting and prioritising are critical.
- [ ] E4 Hours: full-time, part-time, job-sharing, flexible and seasonal hours each change when people are available. Shift or night work changes meal times and sleep patterns, so routines are built around the shifts. Commuting uses time and money. Working from home changes how tasks are shared.

Managing a dual role
- [ ] DR1 Dual role: both partners work outside the home, so extra planning is needed for childcare, meals and housework.
- [ ] DR2 Lone-parent household: the parent has the extra demand of being both earner and parent, with no second adult to share tasks and decisions.
- [ ] DR3 Management responses: share and delegate tasks, make a weekly plan or list, use childcare and time-saving equipment, communicate regularly.

Culture
- [ ] CU1 Culture is the shared customs, beliefs and way of life of a group. It affects family management through dress code, food eaten, religious practices, and festivals and celebrations.
- [ ] CU2 It also shapes expectations of roles: who earns, who cooks, who cares for children and older relatives.
- [ ] CU3 It can affect spending and use of time, for example around religious festivals and family traditions. Use generic examples; do not describe individual cultures in detail.

Values
- [ ] V1 Values are beliefs about what is important to the family (health, education, family time, faith, sustainability, community, security).
- [ ] V2 Values affect management because they determine the decisions taken (for example buying local or organic food, how much to save, how money and time are shared).
- [ ] V3 Values can change when circumstances change, for example the illness of a family member. (Values also appear as an input in "Components of management and decision-making"; one phrase only here.)

Standards
- [ ] ST1 Standards are the level of quality the family sets for its home, meals, appearance and finances (for example how clean the house is kept, how meals are presented).
- [ ] ST2 Standards decide how much time, money and energy a task takes. High standards can strain a family short of time or money; very low standards can affect health and comfort.
- [ ] ST3 Standards can be reviewed and adjusted when circumstances change.

Sex (gender) roles
- [ ] SR1 Sex or gender roles are the roles and behaviour expected of men and women in the household.
- [ ] SR2 Traditionally men were the earners and women did most household work and caring. Roles are now more equal: partners share earning, childcare, cooking and cleaning, with tasks assigned on fairness rather than sex (a scheme also mentions rotating tasks).
- [ ] SR3 Effect on management: sharing roles reduces overload and gives more equal decision-making; when one partner does everything, management is unfair and strained. Use the syllabus term "sex roles" and note "gender roles" as the equivalent term papers use.

Lifestyle: socio-economic status and family composition
- [ ] LS1 Lifestyle is the way a family lives: its pattern of work, leisure, eating, spending and travel. The syllabus ties it to socio-economic status and family composition.
- [ ] LS2 Socio-economic status is the family's position in society based on income, occupation and education. It affects the resources available and the priorities set. Families on lower incomes tend to emphasise needs (food, heating, housing); families on higher incomes have more scope for wants, luxuries and leisure. Use "tend to".
- [ ] LS3 Socio-economic group also influences educational opportunity and the type of job available, and so future income.
- [ ] LS4 Family composition: the number of people in the family, the presence of members with special needs (for example a family member with a disability or an older relative needing care), one-parent or two-parent family.
- [ ] LS5 Effects: a larger family needs more food, clothing, space and money; a family member with special needs may raise costs and reduce others' time to earn; a lone parent has one income and one adult for all tasks.

Diagram cues
- [ ] D1 None required. Do not add a diagram.

## 3. Traps

- "Dual role" means both partners in paid work (the schemes' wording), or a lone parent combining earner and carer; do not assign it to one sex.
- Do not mix up sex roles (who is expected to do tasks) with employment pattern (hours and type of work); explain each separately.
- Do not describe family types, structures, functions or the history of the family; those belong to the social studies material. Here "family composition" means the size and make-up that affect management.
- The roles and responsibilities of family members through the life cycle as a social topic belong to the sociology material; here keep to the effect on managing resources.
- Household income, budgeting and welfare payments belong to household finance and social studies; say only that income sets the resources.
- The management framework (inputs, throughputs, outputs) belongs to "Components of management and decision-making"; do not restate it.
- Do not present socio-economic generalisations as rules: use "tend to" and keep examples neutral and respectful.
- Do not use: any percentage, hours or cost figure for employment, childcare or household size, because such figures change and are not in the syllabus.

## 4. What exams ask

- Discuss four factors that can influence management of family resources (20 marks, five per factor, 2013H pattern): name each factor and explain its effect with an example. Composition, life-cycle stage, employment patterns and socio-economic status have the clearest effects; culture, dual role, gender roles, values and standards are all credited.
- Discuss how stage in the life cycle, employment patterns and sex/gender roles affect a family managing the home (18 marks, 2011O pattern, three factors at six marks each): young children need time, money and space; older children help and join decisions; employment affects money, travel time, and meal and sleep patterns; roles are more egalitarian and tasks shared.
- Short questions on one factor (2022O, 2023H): for example how shift work or being a lone-parent household affects management.
- Explain how values, culture or standards affect the way a family manages, with an example each.
- Expected answers pair each factor with its effect and one example; a list of names earns few marks.
