Nearby examined content, which this subsection supports but does not replace: the impact of social change on family life (2006, 2012, 2015, 2023H, 2026), taught in the chapter on the family in society (changes affecting family structures, a Higher Level item there) and the chapter on the family as a caring unit (changing roles).

## 1. Must cover

What social change is (one block)
- [ ] C1 Social change is the alteration, over time, in the way a society is organised: its norms, values, institutions, roles and relationships. Marking-scheme sense: change in society caused by law reform, technology or major events.
- [ ] C2 Change can be gradual (changing attitudes to marriage over decades) or sudden (a new law, a war, a pandemic); it can be planned (a law, a policy) or unplanned. It is happening in every society, but speed differs.
- [ ] C3 Cultural lag: material change (a new technology) comes first and norms, values and institutions take time to catch up. The idea was put forward by the sociologist William Ogburn. Optional extra; name him only here.

Causes: social (one block)
- [ ] S1 Changing attitudes and values: more acceptance of cohabitation, separation, divorce, lone parenthood, same-sex relationships and diversity; the declining influence of the Church; less stigma attached to children born outside marriage.
- [ ] S2 Greater equality for women: better education, careers, financial independence and legal equality; changing gender roles at home; the marriage bar (the rule forcing women to leave their jobs on marrying) ended in the civil service in 1973.
- [ ] S3 Urbanisation and rural depopulation: people move from rural areas to towns and cities, and extended family contact weakens; services and facilities improve in towns but there is more pressure, traffic and anti-social behaviour; rural areas can lose services and older people can become isolated.
- [ ] S4 Education: more people reach second and third level; attitudes and expectations change; link to mobility.
- [ ] S5 Demographic change: falling birth rates, smaller families, later marriage and parenthood, longer life expectancy and an ageing population.
- [ ] S6 Migration and globalisation: movement of people into and out of Ireland brings new cultures, languages and religions; ideas, products and fashions spread through travel and media. Optional extra: social movements and campaigns for equality.

Causes: legal and political (one block)
- [ ] L1 Law reform both reflects and drives change. Irish examples: divorce was made possible after a referendum in November 1995 and the Family Law (Divorce) Act 1996; same-sex marriage after a referendum in 2015, with the Marriage Act 2015; civil partnership for same-sex couples under the Civil Partnership Act 2010 (named in the 2012-era schemes); maternity and paternity leave; equality legislation.
- [ ] L2 Because the exams ask for "the most recent developments", state only what each law does in one line and do not give section numbers; the outline's current family law Acts are in the chapter on family law.

Causes: economic (one block)
- [ ] EC1 Growth of female employment and dual-earner households; more income but more need for childcare; role overload and role conflict (defined in the role and status subsection).
- [ ] EC2 Industrialisation and growth of service industries; changing patterns of work; flexible hours, job sharing, working from home.
- [ ] EC3 Unemployment, recession, emigration and returning emigrants; high housing costs delaying home ownership, marriage and parenthood; higher standards of living.
- [ ] EC4 State welfare: improved social welfare reduces dependence on the family (child benefit, pensions; no amounts).

Causes: technological (one block)
- [ ] T1 Modern communications technology: mobile phones, the internet and social media change how families communicate, how children learn and how people work; positive and negative effects (access to information, contact with distant relatives, addiction, cyber-bullying, less face-to-face time). Mark-scheme examples: children skilled in IT, video calls, apps.
- [ ] T2 Labour-saving appliances free time and make shared housework easier; contraception and medical advances affect family size and life expectancy; transport shortens distances.

Effects on society and the family (one block)
- [ ] E1 Examples from the schemes: decline of the extended family; more lone-parent, blended and cohabiting families; later and fewer marriages; fewer children; more equal roles for partners; changing roles of older people; more childcare use; more role conflict and role overload; more diverse family structures.
- [ ] E2 Effects can be positive and negative: more choice and freedom against stress and strain, loss of community, social problems; give one of each.
- [ ] E3 Change affects institutions: the Church loses influence, the State takes on more services, schools adapt to diversity.
- [ ] E4 Resistance and tension: groups who benefit from the existing arrangements, tradition and religious belief, and fear of the unknown can slow change; generations may disagree. Optional extra; one block, three bullets.

Social change in Ireland (one block)
- [ ] IR1 Examples with checked facts only: divorce (referendum 1995, Family Law (Divorce) Act 1996); marriage equality (referendum 2015, Marriage Act 2015); women in paid work (marriage bar in the civil service ended 1973; more married women in employment since); the Celtic Tiger period of rapid economic growth in the late 1990s and 2000s, with increased immigration; emigration in earlier decades and in the 2008 recession years; rural depopulation and urban growth; falling influence of the Church.

How to answer (one block)
- [ ] A1 Structure "discuss the impact of social change" answers by cause (social, economic, technological) then effect, with one Irish example per point, as the schemes award points per cause.
- [ ] A2 Diagram cue: none.

## 3. Traps

- Social change is not the same as social mobility (movement of people between groups) or as personal change; it is change in society.
- Do not present change as always good or always bad.
- Do not confuse causes and effects: women entering paid work is a cause of changing roles and of dual-earner families; role overload is an effect.
- Do not use: "a society in which change is accepted easily has a higher rate of adaptation", because the phrase is invented and has no standard meaning.
- Do not use: that divorce became "available in 1995", because the referendum was in 1995 and the Act of 1996 provided for divorce; say referendum 1995, Act 1996.
- Do not use: any claim that the proportion of women in paid work rose by a stated amount, or any date for when most women worked, because figures change and none is confirmed.
- Do not use: Irish emigration or immigration numbers and the exact years of the Celtic Tiger, because sources differ and the figures change.
- Do not use: Civil Partnership as the current law for same-sex couples' marriage; since 2015 same-sex couples can marry, and the notes should say civil partnership was the earlier arrangement.
- Do not use: the Family Law Act 1976 (and its section 22 barring orders) or the Judicial Separation Act 1989 under their old names as examples of change, because they have been superseded or renamed (the current Acts are in the chapter on family law).
- Belongs to a neighbouring subsection: definitions of norms and values (first subsection); status, role, mobility and role overload definitions (role and status); socialisation and kinship (groups and socialisation); the institutions (social institutions); the historical development of the Irish family and the detailed changes to family structure and roles (the family chapters); poverty, unemployment and technology at work (the social studies elective).

## 4. What exams ask

- Explain "social change" in a set of terms (2019H): change in society caused by law reform, technology or major events, with an example such as divorce legislation or the internet. About 3 marks.
- Discuss or analyse the impact of social, economic and technological change on family life or family structure (2006, 2012, 2015, 2019H, 2023H, 2026). Expected content: social (decline of the extended family, urbanisation, gender equality, dual careers, separation and divorce, same-sex marriage, changing roles, fewer and later children), economic (unemployment, dual incomes, childcare cost, state benefits, housing costs, higher living standards) and technological (communications, social media, labour-saving appliances, medical advances). One cause with one effect earns marks; the schemes allocate points per category.
- Impact of changing attitudes to marriage, parenting and the role of women (2023H and earlier): cohabitation, divorce, blended families, egalitarian parenting, women in the workforce, role conflict and overload.
- Impact of urbanisation and of improved social welfare on family life (2012): services and amenities against pollution, congestion and isolation; welfare reducing financial strain.
- Exam tip: tie every change to its effect on family life with a specific Irish example, and say whether it is a social, economic or technological cause.
