# Package: hom-food-additives-s3 — Contaminants in the food chain

- **loId:** `hom-food-additives-s3` · **chapterId:** `hom-food-additives` · **syllabusRef:** 1.3.5
- **Syllabus scope:** contaminants that may enter at various stages of processing, e.g. antibiotics and chemicals. Also examined: pesticides and metal residues (2013H); 2024H and 2004H (identify two contaminants that may enter the food chain, with a source and an effect on the body for each). Nothing here is Higher Level only; mark nothing HL.
- **Prepared by:** a higher model, 2026-11-23. Exams always ask the same shape: name the contaminant, give a source, give an effect on the body. The notes must make that shape easy. Microbial contamination and food-poisoning bacteria are in the food-safety chapter; they appear here only as one line because the schemes accept "micro-organisms" as a contaminant.

## 1. Must cover

What a contaminant is and where it enters (one block)
- [ ] C1 A contaminant is an unwanted substance that gets into food by accident, or that is not meant to be there, at any stage from farm to fork. It is different from an additive, which is added deliberately and legally.
- [ ] C2 Entry points: primary production (soil, water, air, animal feed, sprays and veterinary treatments), processing (equipment, lubricants, cleaning chemicals), packaging (migration), storage and transport, and the kitchen. Syllabus wording: "at various stages of processing", with antibiotics and chemicals as the examples.
- [ ] C3 A residue is a small amount of a substance left in food after it was used legitimately (a pesticide, a veterinary medicine).
- [ ] C4 The law (outline): General Food Law, Regulation (EC) No 178/2002, says unsafe food must not be placed on the market. The FSAI coordinates enforcement; the official agencies under FSAI service contracts (the Department of Agriculture, Food and the Marine, the HSE including the Public Analyst Laboratories, local authorities, the Sea-Fisheries Protection Authority) sample and test food.

Pesticides (one block)
- [ ] D1 Pesticides are chemicals used on crops: insecticides (insects), herbicides (weeds), fungicides (fungi).
- [ ] D2 Source: crops sprayed, spray drift in the air, run-off into water, uptake by plants and soil organisms; residues on fruit, vegetables and cereals.
- [ ] D3 Possible effects on the body (the 2013 scheme): respiratory problems; heart and circulatory problems; damage to the nervous system; birth defects; may cause cancer.
- [ ] D4 Control: use only approved products at the approved rate; observe the interval between the last spraying and harvest so residues fall; maximum residue limits set in EU law and checked by testing; washing and peeling reduce residues on the consumer's side.

Antibiotics, hormones and other veterinary residues (one block)
- [ ] V1 Source: antibiotics given to treat or prevent disease in animals; if milk or meat is sold before the withdrawal period has passed, residues remain in the food.
- [ ] V2 Effects: antibiotic-resistant bacteria; allergic reactions in sensitive people; antibiotic residues in milk stop the lactic acid bacteria working, so yoghurt and cheese fail to set or ripen (an industry effect).
- [ ] V3 Growth-promoting hormones are banned in EU animal production. Residues from legitimate veterinary treatments are controlled by withdrawal periods and monitored (in Ireland, through the national residue control plan run by the Department of Agriculture, Food and the Marine).

Metals and environmental contaminants (one block)
- [ ] H1 Metal residues named in the schemes: lead, mercury, cadmium (also tin and aluminium in the 2004 scheme, but see Traps).
- [ ] H2 Sources: soil, water and air polluted by industry or traffic; old lead water pipes; food containers and cooking equipment; mercury in shellfish and large predatory fish from industrial pollution of coastal waters.
- [ ] H3 Effects (2013 and 2004 schemes): stomach cramps; damage to kidneys and liver; damage to the nervous system and central nervous system; headaches; effects on the immune system.
- [ ] H4 Bioaccumulation: the contaminant builds up in an organism over time and is more concentrated further up the food chain (mercury in shark and swordfish); repeated exposure is a bigger concern than one exposure.
- [ ] H5 Other environmental contaminants the schemes accept: dioxins and foreign bodies; micro-organisms (bacteria from poor hygiene, undercooked meat, unwashed hands, giving nausea, vomiting and food poisoning).

Chemicals from processing and packaging (one block)
- [ ] P1 Lubricants, cleaning agents and sanitisers can contaminate food if misused or not rinsed off; lubricants from industrial waste can cause nausea and central nervous system damage (2004 scheme).
- [ ] P2 Chemicals can migrate from packaging and food-contact materials into food, especially fatty or acidic foods; food-grade materials reduce the risk. Pest-control chemicals must be stored away from food.

Preventing and controlling contamination (one block)
- [ ] R1 Farm level: good agricultural practice, withdrawal periods, approved products only.
- [ ] R2 Industry: approved suppliers, testing on reception, hygiene and HACCP (food-safety chapter), traceability records and lot codes so a batch can be recalled.
- [ ] R3 Official: sampling and testing by official agencies; recall of unsafe food.
- [ ] R4 Consumer: wash and peel produce, buy from reputable sources, follow storage and cooking guidance; vary the diet so exposure to one contaminant is limited.

## 3. Traps

- Do not use: tin and aluminium (from toothpaste and baking powder) as causes of Alzheimer's disease (2004 scheme), because the link is not established.
- Do not use: "lead is used to seal food cans" (2013 scheme) as a present-day fact, because lead solder in food cans has been phased out; lead from old water pipes is the safe example.
- Do not use: "hormones and antibiotics increase basal metabolism and cause heart disease" (2004 scheme), because it is doubtful; use antibiotic resistance, allergic reaction and the fermentation effect.
- Do not use: any maximum residue limit, tolerable intake or concentration, because they change.
- Do not use: "withdrawal period" as the term for crops. For veterinary medicines it is the withdrawal period; for pesticides, the pre-harvest interval; one sentence can cover both ("the required wait before sale or harvest").
- Contaminant (accidental, unwanted) is not an additive (deliberate). Residue is the legitimate-use case; contaminant is the broader word.
- Organic food reduces synthetic pesticide use but is not "free of all residues".
- Antibiotics, pesticides, metals and packaging chemicals are the chemical contaminants; bacteria cause food poisoning (food-safety chapter). Don't explain bacterial food poisoning here.
- Foreign bodies (glass, metal fragments) are physical contaminants; one line.
- Neighbours: microbial contamination, cross-contamination, HACCP and the hygiene regulations belong to the food-safety chapter; packaging materials belong to the packaging subsection; organic and GM foods belong to the first subsection of this chapter.
- Use only current law: do not cite the Food Hygiene Regulations 1950–1989 (the 2024H scheme names them in another question) or the Sale of Food and Drugs Acts.

## 4. What exams ask

- Identify two contaminants that can enter the food chain, with one source and one effect on the body for each (6 marks, 2004H and 2024H). Best answers: pesticides (crop spraying or run-off; nervous system, respiratory or circulatory problems, cancer, birth defects) and mercury or lead (industrial pollution of shellfish and water or old pipes; central nervous system damage, kidney damage). Antibiotics (treated animals; resistance), hormones, dioxins, micro-organisms and foreign bodies are also accepted.
- Complete a table for pesticides and metal residues: how contamination occurs and the possible effect on the body (2013H, 6 marks).
- Name an example from the syllabus, antibiotics, and explain what goes wrong if milk is sold before the withdrawal period ends (processing effect plus a health effect).
- Longer questions may ask how contamination can be prevented in the food chain: use the controls at farm, factory, official and consumer level.
