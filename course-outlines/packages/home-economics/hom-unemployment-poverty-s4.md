## 1. Must cover

Defining poverty (one block)
- [ ] P1 Poverty: being without enough income and resources (material, cultural and social) for an acceptable standard of living. It means lacking adequate food, clothing, shelter and warmth, and being unable to take part in the activities most people regard as normal. The schemes award marks for "inadequate food, clothing, shelter" and for "exclusion from normal social participation".
- [ ] P2 Poverty is more than low income: it includes the lack of opportunities, comforts and self-respect that other people enjoy, and social exclusion.
- [ ] P3 Two concepts of poverty are used: absolute and relative.

Absolute poverty (one block)
- [ ] A1 Absolute poverty: income too low to meet basic physical needs (food, clothing, warmth, shelter), so health and survival are threatened. The test is a fixed minimum, set regardless of how others in society live.
- [ ] A2 It is rare in a developed country like Ireland, but the schemes give examples of people who may experience it: homeless people, people living without proper housing, clothing or medical care.

Relative poverty and the poverty line (one block)
- [ ] R1 Relative poverty: living below the standard of living that society regards as basic, so that a person cannot take part in activities that are normal for others. It is judged against others in the same society, so it exists even in a wealthy country.
- [ ] R2 The poverty line: the level of income below which a household is counted as poor; the estimated minimum income for basic food, clothing and shelter and an acceptable standard of living (2021H scheme wording). In Irish official statistics the line is 60% of the median equivalised household income (the middle income, adjusted for household size and composition). Because it moves with incomes, relative poverty can persist when incomes rise.
- [ ] R3 Photographs setting: pictures of everyday life from different places or times (for example a child's clothing, a home) show that what counts as an acceptable standard changes with place and time, which is why poverty is relative. One or two bullets.
- [ ] R4 Examples of relative poverty (items the CSO uses, assumed from the deprivation list): unable to afford a warm waterproof coat, two pairs of strong shoes, a meal with meat, chicken or fish (or a vegetarian equivalent) every second day, or to keep the home adequately warm.

Official measures (one block)
- [ ] M1 At-risk-of-poverty rate: the share of the population whose income is below the poverty line (60% of median income). The 2011O term "at risk of poverty" is explained in the scheme as relative poverty.
- [ ] M2 Enforced deprivation: being unable to afford basic items, such as those in R4, that most people regard as normal; the CSO counts a household as deprived if it lacks two or more of 11 listed items.
- [ ] M3 Consistent poverty: being both below the at-risk-of-poverty line and experiencing enforced deprivation (lacking two or more of the 11 items). It is the narrower, more severe measure, because the household has low income and cannot afford the basics.
- [ ] M4 The CSO publishes these measures every year from the Survey on Income and Living Conditions (SILC), an EU-wide household survey. The Social Justice Ireland group also publishes a count of people in poverty (the 2016H and 2017O stems quote it).

Extent and distribution of poverty in Ireland today (one block)
- [ ] X1 Extent over time (2021H scheme): poverty fell from the 1960s and 1970s with rising living standards; rose again in the 1980s recession and high unemployment; fell in the Celtic Tiger years of the 1990s and early 2000s as employment grew; rose after the 2008 downturn as unemployment and living costs rose; the pandemic and rising housing and living costs again raised the risk for some households. The overall pattern follows the economy and unemployment.
- [ ] X2 Extent today: poverty is a real problem for a sizeable minority of people, with children and some adult groups at higher risk; state the pattern and leave out the percentages (they change every year).
- [ ] X3 Distribution: poverty is not spread evenly. Risk differs by household type, by labour-market status, by region (urban black spots and some rural areas) and by age.

Groups at risk of poverty (one block, a good list for fact cards)
- [ ] G1 Lone-parent households (one income, childcare costs, difficulty working).
- [ ] G2 Unemployed people, especially long-term unemployed (welfare payments are lower than wages).
- [ ] G3 Low-paid workers (the "working poor") and people in insecure or part-time work.
- [ ] G4 People with a disability or long-term illness (they may be unable to work and face extra costs).
- [ ] G5 Older people, especially those living alone, living on a State pension in poorly insulated homes that are costly to heat.
- [ ] G6 Large families (more children to feed and clothe on one income).
- [ ] G7 Travellers and other ethnic minorities (discrimination, lower education and employment, poorer accommodation).
- [ ] G8 Homeless people; asylum seekers (restricted right to work while awaiting a decision); refugees (low income, language barriers).
- [ ] G9 Early school leavers and people with few qualifications (low pay, poor job prospects).
- [ ] G10 People in households in a cycle of deprivation (give each group with a reason).
- [ ] G11 Effects on each group are asked in the Ordinary Level question (2017O: name three groups and state the effect of poverty on each, for example elderly people: costly heating, illness; unemployed people: payments too low for needs; lone parents: one income, childcare costs). The general effects are in the next subsection; one effect per group is enough here.

## 3. Traps

- Absolute poverty uses a fixed survival standard; relative poverty uses the living standard of the society. A student who writes "relative poverty means being poorer than your neighbour" has missed the point of exclusion.
- Consistent poverty is not the same as the at-risk-of-poverty rate: it needs both low income and enforced deprivation.
- The causes of poverty, the cycle of poverty, the poverty trap and the effects of poverty belong to the next subsection; statutory schemes and voluntary bodies belong to the responses subsections. Here each group needs only a reason it is at risk and, for the 2017O style, one effect.
- Unemployment is a cause of poverty and unemployed people are an at-risk group; the effects of unemployment are in an earlier subsection.
- Do not use: "the poverty line is 50% of average household income" (the 2022O scheme's wording), because Irish and EU statistics use 60% of median equivalised income; the scheme's figure is out of date and "average" is the wrong measure.
- Do not use: any poverty figure, rate, number of people or children in poverty, or percentage of households quoted in past papers (the 755,570 people of 2016H and 2017O, "14.4% in 2008", "over 20% of households", "one in four children"), because rates are changing figures and the paper figures are from those years.
- Do not use: "older people living alone, particularly women over 65" (current notes), because the claim about women over 65 is unconfirmed; say older people, especially those living alone.
- Do not use: "small farm households ... asset rich but cash poor and cannot access income-based payments" (current notes), because the means-test claim is unconfirmed and no scheme names this group.
- Do not use: "a person in absolute poverty has no viable way of solving the problem alone", because it is unsourced; the definition is about basic needs not being met.
- Do not use: "Poverty is rare ... in modern Ireland" as an unqualified statement; say absolute poverty is rare while relative poverty is the main concern.

## 4. What exams ask

- Define poverty (6-8 marks, Ordinary and Higher): not enough income and resources for an acceptable standard of living; lacks food, clothing, shelter; excluded from normal participation. Two points at 3-4 marks each.
- Explain relative poverty, absolute poverty and the poverty line (12-18 marks): one clear sentence for each with a contrast. Higher Level often adds the poverty trap or cycle of poverty (next subsection).
- Name three groups at risk and state the effect on each (Ordinary, 18 marks: 3 marks for each group, 3 marks for each effect).
- Identify and discuss groups at risk of poverty and give reasons (12 marks).
- Discuss the extent and distribution of poverty in Ireland today (Higher Level, 12-20 marks): one point on how poverty changed over time linked to the economy, one on who is most affected, plus two more.
- Explain "at risk of poverty" (6 marks, 2011O), and consistent poverty.
- Tips: always contrast absolute with relative; define the poverty line; use group + reason; for extent, trace the trend with causes and leave out percentages unless the stem gives them.
