## 1. Must cover

Resources and sustainable use
- [ ] R1 A natural resource is a material or energy source taken from the environment (fuels, water, minerals, timber, wind, sunlight).
- [ ] R2 Resources are classed by how quickly they are replaced. Renewable: replenished naturally and not used up by constant use, provided it is used no faster than replaced. Non-renewable: limited in supply and cannot be replaced within a human lifetime once used (also called finite).
- [ ] R3 Sustainable use means meeting present needs without reducing the ability of future generations to meet theirs. "Sustainability" is a heading in the "one renewable source" questions.
- [ ] R4 The consumer's responsibility: consumers influence resource use through what they buy, how long they use it and how they dispose of it. They conserve resources by buying only what is needed, choosing durable and repairable goods, and using items fully before replacing them.

Renewable energy sources (each source with how it works)
- [ ] N1 Solar: photovoltaic (PV) panels convert sunlight directly to electricity; solar thermal panels use sunlight to heat water. Passive solar design (siting the house and its glazing towards the sun) uses sunlight for heat and light (2026H scheme).
- [ ] N2 Wind: turbines turn the kinetic energy of moving air into electricity; wind farms are sited on exposed upland, coastal land or at sea.
- [ ] N3 Hydroelectric: falling or flowing water turns turbines; needs a steady flow and a height difference.
- [ ] N4 Biomass: wood, straw, energy crops and plant or animal waste are burned, or converted to biogas, to give heat or electricity.
- [ ] N5 Geothermal: heat from the earth's crust is brought up as hot water or steam to heat buildings or generate electricity.
- [ ] N6 Tidal and wave: the rise and fall of tides, and the movement of waves, drive turbines or devices.
- [ ] N7 Renewable heat in the home: heat pumps (take low-grade heat from the air, ground or water), solar thermal panels, and stoves burning wood from managed forests.
- [ ] N8 Hydrogen: the 2025O scheme lists it; mention in one phrase only, as an energy carrier that is renewable only if made with renewable electricity (see Traps).

Benefits and limits of renewable energy
- [ ] B1 Benefits: supply does not run out on human timescales; little or no air pollution or carbon dioxide while generating; reduces dependence on imported fuel (energy security); some can be installed in the home so energy is produced where it is used; creates jobs in building and maintaining systems; running costs are low once installed.
- [ ] B2 Limits: wind and solar output varies with weather and daylight, so storage or backup supply is needed; installation costs can be high; hydro and tidal need suitable sites; wind farms and dams affect landscape and habitats (one point is enough).
- [ ] B3 Sustainability of a renewable source: it is replaced naturally, so supply can last indefinitely if the system is well sited and maintained.

Non-renewable resources
- [ ] F1 Fossil fuels: coal, oil, natural gas and peat, formed from the remains of plants and animals over very long periods (peat over thousands of years; coal, oil and gas over millions). Peat (turf) is cut from bogs for home heating; bogs regrow far too slowly to count as renewable. Other fuels in exam answers: coke, anthracite.
- [ ] F2 Nuclear energy uses uranium, a mined, finite fuel (non-renewable). Ireland has no nuclear power station.
- [ ] F3 Non-renewable materials: iron ore, copper ore and bauxite (the ore of aluminium) are mined and finite; recycling metals extends their life.
- [ ] F4 For "discuss one non-renewable source in the home" (2011H): source; advantage (concentrated, gives a lot of heat, established supply and appliances); disadvantages (finite, price rises as reserves fall, emissions, extraction damage); sustainability (not sustainable).

Environmental effects of using non-renewable resources
- [ ] E1 Burning fossil fuels releases carbon dioxide (the main greenhouse gas: global warming and climate change) and, from coal, oil and peat, sulphur dioxide (acid rain). One line only; the full effects are in the pollution subsection.
- [ ] E2 Mining, quarrying, drilling and turf cutting destroy habitats and landscapes; cutting bogs releases stored carbon.
- [ ] E3 As reserves fall, extraction becomes harder and costlier, so supplies become less secure.

Consumer responsibility in practice
- [ ] C1 Reduce energy demand: insulate, use appliances efficiently, switch off standby (one line; detail is in the household-practices subsection).
- [ ] C2 Choose renewable options where practical: a renewable electricity supplier, solar panels or a heat pump, smokeless or renewable fuel.
- [ ] C3 Buy wood and paper certified by FSC or PEFC, which confirm the timber came from managed forests.
- [ ] C4 Reuse and recycle metals, glass and paper so finite materials are used again; choose products with recycled content.
- [ ] C5 (assumed) greenhouse gas, global warming and acid rain are used as terms here; they are explained in the pollution subsection.

## 3. Traps

- Nuclear is non-renewable. The 2012O scheme's answer list sits nuclear beside renewable examples; do not copy that grouping.
- Peat is a non-renewable fossil fuel (forms over thousands of years). Do not call it renewable because bogs "regrow".
- Biomass is renewable only if the plants are regrown; burning it still releases CO₂, though it is carbon the plants recently took from the air. Do not say it gives "no emissions".
- Renewable does not mean no disadvantages: when asked for advantages and disadvantages, give both.
- "Sustainable" and "renewable" are not the same: a renewable source used faster than it is replaced (an over-felled forest) is not sustainable.
- Hydrogen is an energy carrier, not a primary source; do not give it its own block.
- Boundaries: how pollutants harm the environment belongs to the pollution subsection; recycling and packaging to the waste subsection; insulation, appliance use and heating practices to the household-practices subsection; types of central heating system, fuels in the home and the BER belong to the home services and housing chapters.
- Do not use: "solar thermal supplies 50 to 60 per cent of a household's hot water" (2014H scheme), because it is an unsourced, changing figure.
- Do not use: nuclear listed as a renewable source, because uranium is finite.
- Do not use: any wind, solar or hydro share, capacity, cost, payback or grant amount, because these figures change.

## 4. What exams ask

- Define a renewable and a non-renewable resource and give an example of each (2019H, 2004H: marks for the explanation and for the example). Expected: replaced naturally and not used up by constant use, versus limited and cannot be replaced; solar, wind, hydro, geothermal, biomass, tidal versus coal, oil, gas, peat, uranium.
- Name two renewable sources and explain one benefit to the environment (2025O, 2016O, 2009H). Expected: sustainable (will not run out), clean, low emissions, less imported fossil fuel.
- Name one renewable and one non-renewable source (2012O, 2008O), sometimes with two ways to reduce energy use at home.
- Discuss one renewable source in detail (2014H, 2022H, 2026H, 2007H): how it works, advantages, disadvantages, sustainability. Tip: pick solar, wind or hydro and cover all four headings.
- Discuss one non-renewable source used in the home (2011H): source, advantage, disadvantage, why it is not sustainable.
- List consumer responsibilities (2004O): reduce pollution, choose renewable resources, think about disposal, recycle to save landfill space.
