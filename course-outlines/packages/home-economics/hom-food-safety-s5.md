- **Syllabus scope:** Enzymes and food spoilage [1.3.8]: the role of enzymes in food spoilage; **[HL]** the principles underlying the control of enzymic spoilage.
- **Also examined:** 2022H, 2015H, 2013H, 2009H; also 2005H (one method of controlling enzymic spoilage), 2015O (enzymes in food cause decay: true).
- **Level:** the role of enzymes is common to both levels. The control of enzymic spoilage is Higher Level only: that block's heading ends " (Higher Level only)".
- **Prepared by:** a higher model, 2026-11-23. Exam answers are built on three named types of enzymic spoilage (over-ripening, enzymic browning, enzymic deterioration) at 5 marks each, so the notes must name and exemplify all three.

## 1. Must cover

What enzymes are (assumed, one block)
- [ ] E1 (assumed) Enzymes are protein catalysts that speed up chemical reactions in living cells. Plant and animal foods contain their own enzymes, which keep working after harvest or slaughter. Spoilage by enzymes happens without micro-organisms.
- [ ] E2 (assumed) Enzymes are specific (each acts on one substrate at its active site), work best in warmth and moisture, are slowed by cold and are destroyed (denatured) by heat. Give no optimum temperature or pH.

Role of enzymes in spoilage: three types (exam structure)
- [ ] O1 Over-ripening: ripening enzymes keep acting after fruit is picked, turning starch to sugar and softening cell walls; once past the ripe stage the fruit turns soft, mushy and then rots. Example: a hard, under-ripe banana becomes softer, sweeter and easier to digest, then black and over-ripe.
- [ ] B1 Enzymic browning: when apples, bananas, potatoes and similar foods are cut, bruised or peeled, damaged cells release the enzyme (oxidase, also called polyphenol oxidase) which, with oxygen from the air, turns phenolic compounds to brown pigments on the cut surface. Needs oxygen; changes appearance and flavour but is not a safety hazard.
- [ ] D1 Enzymic deterioration (rotting): (a) enzymes in fish continue to break down the flesh even at low temperatures, producing odours; (b) vegetables that are not blanched before freezing keep active enzymes that spoil colour, texture and flavour in the freezer; (c) oxidase in plant cell walls, activated by oxygen, destroys thiamine (vitamin B1), vitamin C and carotene; (d) lipase in fats and oily foods hydrolyses triglycerides to fatty acids and glycerol, causing rancidity (2019H scheme).
- [ ] D2 Effects of enzymic spoilage: loss of colour, flavour, texture and some nutritive value; shorter shelf life. Distinguish from microbial spoilage (no micro-organisms involved).

Control of enzymic spoilage (HL)
- [ ] C1 [HL] Heat: cooking denatures enzymes; blanching (brief immersion in boiling water or steam, then rapid cooling) inactivates enzymes in vegetables before freezing.
- [ ] C2 [HL] Acid: adding lemon juice or vinegar to cut fruit lowers the pH and inactivates the enzyme (2009H scheme: lemon juice on apples).
- [ ] C3 [HL] Low temperatures: refrigeration and freezing slow enzyme action but do not stop or destroy it, so enzymes become active again on thawing, and frozen vegetables need blanching.
- [ ] C4 [HL] Chemical preservatives: sulphur dioxide in dried fruit and fruit juices and vegetable juices inhibits enzymes and stops browning (2009H scheme).
- [ ] C5 [HL] Excluding oxygen: vacuum packing, covering cut surfaces with water or sugar syrup, antioxidants such as ascorbic acid (vitamin C) prevent oxidase browning.
- [ ] C6 [HL] Removing water: drying makes enzymes inactive (dried herbs, fruit).
- [ ] C7 [HL] Irradiation (the 2009H scheme lists it as a control); name only, detail in the preservation chapter.
- [ ] C8 [HL] The principle in one line: enzymes are controlled by destroying them (heat), changing their conditions (acid, cold, dryness), or removing a reactant (oxygen).

## 3. Traps

- Enzymic spoilage is not microbial spoilage. Do not say enzymes are micro-organisms or that blanching kills bacteria (blanching inactivates enzymes).
- Cold slows enzymes but does not destroy them (unlike heat). That is why freezing does not stop enzymic spoilage and vegetables are blanched first.
- Browning needs oxygen, so excluding oxygen or adding acid or vitamin C prevents it. Browning of cooked foods in the oven (Maillard, caramelisation) is a different process and belongs to cooking methods and carbohydrates.
- Belongs to a neighbouring subsection: microbial spoilage and general causes of spoilage (spoilage overview); freezing, drying, canning and irradiation in detail (preservation chapter); structure of an enzyme, active site and denaturation in depth (food chemistry in the protein chapter).
- Do not use: "Enzyme activity needs warmth and moisture, so drying slows enzyme action" as the only reason for drying, because the reason is that without water the enzyme cannot act; write "drying removes the water enzymes need".
- Do not use: any blanching time or temperature, because no checked figure exists for this chapter (the exam asks only for the principle).
- Do not use: "vitamin C is destroyed by oxidase in all vegetables" as a general rule; say oxidase "can destroy" thiamine, vitamin C and carotene (scheme wording).

## 4. What exams ask

- Discuss the role of enzymes in food spoilage (2013H 15 marks, 2022H 15 marks): three points at 5 marks: over-ripening (banana), enzymic browning (apple, potato; oxidase plus oxygen), enzymic deterioration (fish; unblanched vegetables; vitamin loss). Mention that enzymes act after harvest.
- Explain how enzymic spoilage can be controlled (2009H 20 marks; 2005H 2 marks for one method): acid, heat or cooking, blanching, cold, sulphur dioxide, removing oxygen, drying. Give the reason each works (denaturing, lowering pH, removing water or oxygen).
- Ordinary Level true/false (2015O): enzymes in food cause decay; true.
- Part of general "causes of spoilage" questions (2019H, 2009H, 2005O): enzymes are one cause with a named example.
