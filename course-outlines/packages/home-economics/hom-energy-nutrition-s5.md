# Package: hom-energy-nutrition-s5 — The Irish diet

- **loId:** `hom-energy-nutrition-s5` · **chapterId:** `hom-energy-nutrition` · **syllabusRef:** 1.2.4
- **Syllabus scope:** changes in food and eating patterns in the Irish diet since the beginning of the twentieth century; comparison of the Irish diet with current dietary guidelines; aspects of malnutrition currently identified: low dietary fibre, high saturated fat, relatively low iron and calcium intakes, with causes, effects and corrective measures. Settings: comparing a typical day's menu from the start of the twentieth century with one from today against current guidelines; a visiting speaker on diet and disease (for example Irish Cancer Society, Irish Heart Foundation, a dietitian). Also examined: contemporary trends in Irish eating patterns (2024H, 2021H, 2015O, 2010H); data-based questions on Irish consumption (Section B Q1 most years).
  No item in this subsection is Higher Level only.
- **Prepared by:** a higher model, 2026-11-23. This subsection joins the guidelines to real eating. The detail of fibre, fat, iron and calcium (sources, functions, deficiency) belongs to the nutrient chapters; here each one is treated as an Irish dietary concern with causes, effects and corrections. The guidelines and Food Pyramid themselves are in "Dietary guidelines"; obesity and BMI in "Energy balance".

## 1. Must cover

The Irish diet at the start of the twentieth century (one block)
- [ ] H1 Traditional Irish diet, based on home-grown and home-made foods: potatoes as the staple, eaten in large amounts with milk, buttermilk and butter; oatmeal (porridge) and home-baked breads such as soda bread and brown bread; seasonal home-grown vegetables such as cabbage, turnips and carrots; limited fresh fruit; bacon and salted meat eaten occasionally; eggs and fish where available (coastal areas); tea with bread and butter.
- [ ] H2 Characteristics: food prepared from basic raw ingredients; little processed or convenience food; cooked on an open fire or range; dinner was the main meal, eaten at midday; the family ate together at set times; the diet was high in starch and fibre and relatively low in sugar and fat, although limited in variety, and poorer households could go short of food.
- [ ] H3 A typical-day menu then: porridge or bread and tea for breakfast; potatoes, cabbage and bacon or fish for dinner at midday; bread, butter and tea in the evening (Settings comparison activity).

Changes since then (one block)
- [ ] C1 Refined white flour and white bread replaced much wholemeal bread and oatmeal, lowering fibre intake; processed breakfast cereals replaced porridge for many households.
- [ ] C2 Rice and pasta partly replaced potatoes; the variety of foods and cooking methods increased.
- [ ] C3 More sugar, sweets, biscuits, cakes, soft drinks and fat (butter, cream, processed meats, fried foods); more energy-dense, nutrient-poor food.
- [ ] C4 Processed, frozen, canned and ready-made convenience foods, with microwaves and freezers, shorter preparation times; fewer meals prepared from raw ingredients.
- [ ] C5 Imported fruit and vegetables are available all year round while seasonal home-grown produce fell; supermarkets replaced local shops.
- [ ] C6 Eating out and takeaways became frequent; travel abroad, immigration and television cooking introduced new dishes and ethnic foods.
- [ ] C7 Greater nutritional awareness, food labelling and health campaigns led to low-fat, reduced-sugar, wholegrain and organic products being widely available; also more vegetarian and special-diet choices.
- [ ] C8 Reasons for the changes: rising incomes, urbanisation, women working outside the home, more leisure time pressures, food industry growth and advertising, refrigeration, supermarkets, education.

Contemporary trends in eating patterns (one block; 2015O, 2021H, 2024H, 2010H)
- [ ] E1 Irregular meals and more snacking and grazing; skipped meals, especially breakfast.
- [ ] E2 The main meal is eaten in the evening rather than at midday; family members often eat at different times, in front of the television or on the go (2015O scheme names these).
- [ ] E3 Greater use of convenience and ready meals, microwave cooking and takeaways; some barbecuing.
- [ ] E4 Growing health awareness: interest in low-fat, low-sugar, organic, functional and plant-based foods, allergen and special-diet products, and reading labels.
- [ ] E5 Influences: advertising and social media, especially on children; busy work and study timetables; cost; food trends and celebrity chefs; concern for sustainability and locally produced food.
- [ ] E6 Consequences: more obesity, type 2 diabetes and heart disease linked to poor diet and inactivity; improved choice and food safety.

Comparison with current guidelines (one block)
- [ ] G1 Benchmark: the Department of Health healthy eating guidelines and Food Pyramid ("Dietary guidelines").
- [ ] G2 Typical shortfalls: dietary fibre is low (too few wholegrains, fruit and vegetables); fruit and vegetable servings fall short of five to seven a day; saturated fat, sugar and salt are too high (processed food, sweets, fatty meat); fish, especially oily fish, is eaten less than advised; iron and calcium are low for some groups, such as teenage girls and women of child-bearing age; many people eat from the top shelf too often.
- [ ] G3 Strengths: widespread protein intake; availability of lower-fat products; good milk and potato tradition.
- [ ] G4 Method (menu comparison): list foods and portions; identify sources of fibre, saturated fat, salt, sugar, iron and calcium; compare each with the guidelines as too high, too low or about right; recommend specific changes; rewrite a meal or day.

Low dietary fibre (one block)
- [ ] F1 Causes: refined flour (bran and germ removed), white bread and rice, processed convenience foods, few fruit and vegetables, no wholegrain cereals.
- [ ] F2 Effects: constipation, diverticular disease, bowel disorders and a higher risk of bowel cancer; less fullness, linked to overeating and obesity; higher blood cholesterol; raised risk of heart disease and type 2 diabetes.
- [ ] F3 Corrective measures: choose wholemeal bread and pasta, brown rice, porridge and high-fibre breakfast cereals; eat fruit and vegetables with their skins; add pulses; use nuts and seeds; build up fibre gradually and drink plenty of fluid. (The Irish Cancer Society gives information on diet and cancer.)

High saturated fat (one block)
- [ ] S1 Sources: butter, cream, full-fat dairy, fatty and processed meats, pastries, cakes, biscuits, chocolate, fried and takeaway foods, coconut and palm oil.
- [ ] S2 Causes: fast food and convenience foods, frying, snacking, large portions of fatty meat, advertising.
- [ ] S3 Effects: raised blood cholesterol (LDL), coronary heart disease, stroke, obesity, type 2 diabetes. (The Irish Heart Foundation gives information on heart health.)
- [ ] S4 Corrective measures: choose lean meat, low-fat dairy and spreads; grill, bake or steam instead of frying; use unsaturated oils (rapeseed, olive); eat oily fish; limit the top shelf; read labels.

Low iron (one block)
- [ ] I1 Why it matters: iron makes haemoglobin, the pigment in red blood cells that carries oxygen.
- [ ] I2 Causes: low intake of red meat and iron-rich foods; the blood loss of menstruation; growth spurts in adolescence; pregnancy; dieting; vegetarian diets without careful planning; tea and coffee with meals and phytates reduce absorption (one clause).
- [ ] I3 Effects: iron-deficiency anaemia: tiredness, pale skin, weakness, poor concentration, headaches, breathlessness, poor resistance to infection.
- [ ] I4 Corrective measures: eat lean red meat, liver, eggs, fortified breakfast cereals, wholemeal foods, pulses, dark green vegetables and dried fruit; take vitamin C food (citrus fruit, fruit juice) with the meal; avoid tea with meals; supplements only on medical advice.

Low calcium (one block)
- [ ] K1 Why it matters: bones and teeth, blood clotting, muscle contraction, nerve function.
- [ ] K2 Causes: low intake of milk, yogurt and cheese (dieting, dislike, cost, replacement by soft drinks); low vitamin D (poor sunlight, little oily fish) reduces absorption.
- [ ] K3 Effects: poor bone growth in children; osteoporosis in later life; dental decay; muscle cramps; slow blood clotting.
- [ ] K4 Corrective measures: three servings of milk, yogurt and cheese (lower fat) a day or fortified plant alternatives; fish with edible bones; dark green vegetables, nuts and seeds; vitamin D from oily fish, eggs and safe sunlight; weight-bearing exercise; supplements on medical advice.

Reading data on Irish consumption (one block, method)
- [ ] D1 Read the title, units, years and groups first; quote figures exactly; describe the trend (rise, fall, small change); compare groups (men and women, age groups); link to the guideline and to a health risk; offer reasons (convenience foods, eating out, advertising, income, lifestyle) and a recommendation.
- [ ] D2 Past data questions: 2013H (consumption compared with pyramid shelves, 1998 and 2007); Q1 data questions in most years. Do not reuse their numbers.

## 3. Traps

- "Malnutrition" in this subsection means an unbalanced diet (too much or too little of something), not only undernutrition.
- The syllabus lists four concerns: fibre low, saturated fat high, iron and calcium relatively low. Do not add other concerns (such as vitamin D or salt) as syllabus items; mention salt and sugar only under the guideline comparison.
- Do not say the traditional diet was "healthy" or "unhealthy" without qualification; it was high in fibre and low in fat and sugar but limited in variety and sometimes inadequate.
- Do not describe changes as caused only by "laziness"; give reasons (work patterns, income, availability).
- Iron-deficiency anaemia is not the only effect of low iron, but it is the one the schemes look for.
- Fibre, fat, iron and calcium as nutrients (properties, full lists of sources and functions, other deficiency diseases) belong to their own chapters; keep this subsection to causes, effects and corrections.
- Obesity, BMI and energy balance belong to "Energy balance".
- Food safety and processing belong to later chapters.
- Do not use: "Many people drink sugary drinks instead of water as the main drink" as a stated fact about the Irish population, because it is an unsupported generalisation; say sugary drinks add empty kilocalories and that water is advised as the main drink.
- Do not use: a claim that salt and sugar intakes exceed guidelines for every group, because the survey evidence is mixed (food-source salt intake of some groups is within the 6 g target; discretionary salt is not counted); say "can be too high, especially from processed foods".
- Do not use: "Health Promotion Unit" as a body to visit, because it no longer exists under that name; use the HSE health promotion service, Irish Heart Foundation or Irish Cancer Society.
- Do not use: figures from the 2007 and 1998 consumption chart (percentages of people meeting servings), because they are old survey data.
