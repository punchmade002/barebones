# Package: hom-social-change-s5 — Women in the workforce and equality legislation

- **loId:** `hom-social-change-s5` · **chapterId:** `hom-social-change` · **syllabusRef:** 6.1
- **Syllabus scope:** item 5, women in the workforce and equality legislation [6.1]: increased participation of women in the workforce; legislation on equal pay and employment opportunities. Chapter-wide also examined: 2026O (three reasons for increased participation), 2023H and 2023O (participation of women; equal pay and employment opportunities), 2018O, 2017H (factors influencing participation), 2014O (four factors), 2005O, 2004H. No [HL] items in this subsection.
- **Prepared by:** a higher model, 2026-11-23. Common to both levels. The subsection covers why more women are in paid work, what that did to family life, and the laws that give equal pay and equal opportunity. Use only the legislation in force for June 2027: the Employment Equality Acts 1998 to 2015 for equal pay and treatment; the Acts of the 1970s were repealed and replaced.

## 1. Must cover

Increased participation of women in the workforce: reasons (one block; examiners ask for three or four, each explained)
- [ ] F1 Education: more girls complete second level and third level, giving the qualifications for professional and managerial jobs and raising expectations of a career.
- [ ] F2 Contraception, smaller families and later marriage: women can plan when to have children and return to work after childbirth, and have fewer years of child rearing.
- [ ] F3 Economic need: the cost of housing, mortgages, rent and education means many families need two incomes; a wish for a higher standard of living; lone parents must earn.
- [ ] F4 Changing attitudes: work outside the home accepted for married women and mothers; equal opportunity expected; financial independence and job satisfaction as goals; a career valued for its own sake.
- [ ] F5 Equality legislation and the removal of barriers: equal pay and equal treatment laws; the marriage bar (the rule that women had to leave public-service jobs on marriage) was removed in 1973; maternity leave and protection from dismissal.
- [ ] F6 Childcare and supports: creches, childminders, pre-school and after-school services; the free pre-school programme; the National Childcare Scheme subsidises costs (name only).
- [ ] F7 Flexible work: part-time work, job-sharing, flexitime, term-time working, remote working; the right to request flexible working (Work Life Balance and Miscellaneous Provisions Act 2023).
- [ ] F8 Labour-saving appliances and convenience foods cut housework time; growth of the service sector and of jobs in offices, health and education suited to women; jobs have become less gender-specific (women as engineers, doctors, lawyers; men as nurses and childminders).
- [ ] F9 Participation: the share of women in paid work has risen greatly from the early 20th century and the number of married women in work is far higher than in the past. Say "risen steeply", no percentage.

Effects of women's employment on family life (one block)
- [ ] F10 Positive: dual-earner households have higher incomes and a higher standard of living; financial independence and self-esteem for women; the division of housework and childcare is more equal; children see equal roles; more security if one partner loses a job; women have more choice about leaving an unhappy marriage.
- [ ] F11 Negative: role overload (doing a paid job and most of the housework and childcare) and time pressure, especially for lone parents and parents of young children; childcare costs; reliance on grandparents, relatives or paid childminders; less time with children, less family time together; stress and tiredness; tension about sharing tasks; career moves can require relocation; children may be in care for long hours.
- [ ] F12 Effects on society: bigger labour force and tax revenue; smaller families; more demand for childcare, convenience foods and services.

Equality legislation (one block)
- [ ] F13 The Employment Equality Acts 1998 to 2015 prohibit discrimination in employment on nine grounds: gender, civil status, family status, sexual orientation, religion, age, disability, race, and membership of the Traveller community. They cover recruitment and advertising, access to training and promotion, pay, terms and conditions of work, harassment and dismissal.
- [ ] F14 Equal pay: men and women must receive equal pay for "like work": work that is the same, similar, or of equal value. An employee may claim equal pay on any of the nine grounds.
- [ ] F15 Equal opportunity: employers cannot refuse to hire, train or promote because of gender or marital status; exceptions exist where a gender is a genuine occupational requirement (assumed: one clause only).
- [ ] F16 Family status ground: protects employees who are parents of children under 18 or carers of a person with a disability who needs care, against discrimination.
- [ ] F17 Pregnancy and maternity: the Maternity Protection Acts 1994 and 2004 give the right to maternity leave and protection against dismissal and loss of rights; dismissal because of pregnancy is unlawful (also treated as gender discrimination).
- [ ] F18 Enforcement: complaints are made to the Workplace Relations Commission, with appeals to the Labour Court; the Irish Human Rights and Equality Commission promotes equality and can assist.
- [ ] F19 Related laws: the Gender Pay Gap Information Act 2021 requires larger employers to report differences between men's and women's average hourly pay and explain the gap (the size of employer covered has been widened in stages: give no thresholds); the Equal Status Acts 2000 to 2018 cover discrimination in goods, services and education, not employment.

Barriers that remain (one block)
- [ ] F20 Women are more likely than men to work part-time, often because of caring duties; a gender pay gap persists in many sectors, partly because women and men are concentrated in different jobs; women are under-represented in senior management (the glass ceiling); career breaks for childcare reduce pension entitlements and promotion chances; lack of affordable childcare; women still do most unpaid housework and caring.

Settings (one short block)
- [ ] F21 Compare women's employment and options a hundred years ago with today; interview an older woman about her working life, and whether she left work on marriage.

## 3. Traps

- Do not use: the Anti-Discrimination (Pay) Act 1974 or the Employment Equality Act 1977, because they were repealed and replaced by the Employment Equality Act 1998; marking schemes from the 2000s name them.
- Do not use: the Equality Authority or the Equality Tribunal as current bodies, because they were replaced by the Irish Human Rights and Equality Commission (2014) and the Workplace Relations Commission (2015).
- Do not use: the figure in the 2014 past paper stem (the share of women in work in the early 1960s and "now"), or any other participation or pay gap statistic, because these are changing and unconfirmed.
- Do not use: the Employment Appeals Tribunal or Employment Equality Agency, because they no longer exist.
- Do not use: a start date or the exact thresholds for gender pay gap reporting, because they have been changed in stages.
- Do not use: "family status covers any caring responsibility for dependants" as written in the old notes, because the legal definition is narrower: a parent or in loco parentis of a child under 18, or the carer of a person with a disability who needs care.
- Equal pay means the same pay for like work, not the same pay for all jobs. Equal opportunity covers recruitment, training and promotion, not just pay.
- The Equal Status Acts do not cover employment; the Employment Equality Acts do.
- Maternity leave is a right to time off with job protection; the payment (maternity benefit) comes from the State, not the employer, in most cases (assumed; say "maternity benefit is a social insurance payment").
- Neighbouring subsections: role overload, role conflict and the distribution of home-care duties, and the types of childcare, belong to the Work and Leisure chapter; the effects of attitudes on roles belong to the changing attitudes subsection; the Protection of Young Persons (Employment) Act 1996 belongs to the Work and Leisure chapter; education of women belongs to the education and welfare subsection; family law belongs to the Family and Society chapter.
- Do not write that equality has been achieved: Higher Level answers need the remaining barriers (F20).

## 4. What exams ask

- Give three or four reasons for the increased participation of women in the workforce, each explained (Ordinary Level, 15 to 20 marks: 2005, 2014, 2018, 2026; Higher Level 2004 and 2017 analyse the factors influencing participation, including barriers). Expected: education, contraception and smaller families, economic need, changing attitudes, equality legislation, childcare, flexible work, labour-saving devices.
- Discuss the impact on family life of increased participation of women: income, shared tasks, role overload, childcare, independence, effects on children.
- Name and explain equal pay and employment opportunity legislation: Employment Equality Acts, equal pay for like work, nine grounds, how complaints are made.
- Other legislation protecting people in employment (maternity, working time, minimum wage, young persons) appears across years.
- Higher Level 20-mark social change analysis often includes women's participation as one of three changes: the answer must show cause then family effect.
- Short questions: define participation rate or equal pay; give two reasons more women work; name one law that protects pregnant workers.
