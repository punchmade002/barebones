## 1. Must cover

Classification (one block)
- [ ] CL1 Cheese is made by coagulating milk protein and draining off the whey. It is classified by texture and moisture, and by how it is ripened.
- [ ] CL2 Hard: low moisture, matured longer, firm; Cheddar, Parmesan, Cheshire, Emmenthal (2014H scheme).
- [ ] CL3 Semi-hard or semi-soft: Gouda, Edam, Halloumi; Stilton appears in the 2014H scheme here, but it is blue (see CL5).
- [ ] CL4 Soft: higher moisture; cottage cheese, cream cheese, mozzarella and feta (fresh, unripened, or brined) and Brie and Camembert (surface mould-ripened) (2014H, 2015O schemes).
- [ ] CL5 Mould (blue) cheeses: ripened by a mould that grows through the cheese: Cashel Blue (Irish), Roquefort, Gorgonzola, Stilton.
- [ ] CL6 Processed cheese: natural cheese melted and blended with emulsifying salts and other ingredients, then moulded; cheese slices and spreads; the 2014H scheme also files smoked cheese here. Longer shelf life, uniform, melts easily.
- [ ] CL7 Diagram or table cue: a table with columns class, description, examples, uses.

Nutritive value (one block)
- [ ] NV1 Protein: about 10 to 27% (2017H scheme), HBV, mostly casein; contains all the essential amino acids; soft cheese has less protein than hard.
- [ ] NV2 Fat: about 4 to 35%, mostly saturated, with cholesterol; hard cheese is high in fat, less if made from skimmed milk; the more water in a cheese, the less fat. Cottage cheese is about 4% fat.
- [ ] NV3 Carbohydrate: 0 to about 1.5%, because most lactose drains away in the whey and the rest changes to lactic acid.
- [ ] NV4 Vitamins: A (retinol) and beta-carotene (skin, eyes, membranes), B2 (riboflavin) and B12, some vitamin D (helps absorb calcium); no vitamin C.
- [ ] NV5 Minerals: calcium (about 1.5 to 4% minerals in total) and phosphorus for bones and teeth; sodium from the salt added in production (high in Cheddar and processed cheese); low in iron.
- [ ] NV6 Water varies by type; no fibre. Concentrated energy because of the fat.

Dietetic value (one block)
- [ ] DV1 A relatively cheap protein food, no waste, no cooking needed, wide variety, versatile, suits packed lunches and snacks.
- [ ] DV2 Protein and calcium for growth in children, adolescents, pregnant women and nursing mothers; a high-energy food for active people.
- [ ] DV3 Cottage cheese and reduced-fat varieties suit low-kilocalorie, low-cholesterol and weight-watching diets; full-fat cheeses are high in saturated fat and salt and should be limited by people with heart disease or high blood pressure.
- [ ] DV4 Pregnancy and listeria: pregnant women are advised to avoid mould-ripened soft cheeses such as Brie and Camembert, blue cheeses and soft cheeses made from unpasteurised milk, because of the risk of Listeria; hard cheese, cottage cheese, cream cheese and pasteurised processed cheese are considered safe.
- [ ] DV5 Cheese lacks carbohydrate and fibre, so serve with bread, crackers, pasta, potatoes, vegetables or fruit. Some cheeses can be hard to digest (high fat).
- [ ] DV6 The milk, yoghurt and cheese shelf of the Food Pyramid: cheese in small amounts and in lower-fat forms.

Starter culture and rennet (one block)
- [ ] SR1 Lactic acid bacteria are added to pasteurised milk as a starter culture: they change lactose into lactic acid, which gives flavour, preserves the cheese and helps the curd to form.
- [ ] SR2 Rennet contains the enzyme rennin, which changes caseinogen (soluble milk protein) into casein (insoluble); the casein coagulates and sets, and the milk separates into curds (solids) and whey (liquid, which holds most of the lactose and water).
- [ ] SR3 Rennet traditionally comes from calf stomach; vegetarian rennet is made from microbes or plants.

Production (one block; the exam asks the stages in order, 2 marks each)
- [ ] PR1 A culture of lactic acid bacteria is added to pasteurised milk. 
- [ ] PR2 The milk is heated to about 30 °C and rennet is added; left to stand about 30 minutes, it separates into curds and whey.
- [ ] PR3 The curd is cut or chopped to release more whey, and the whey is drained off.
- [ ] PR4 The curds are heated to about 35 to 40 °C (scalding) to squeeze out more whey and give the correct consistency.
- [ ] PR5 Cheddaring (for Cheddar): the curd is cut into blocks and piled on top of each other, turned, to drain the last whey.
- [ ] PR6 The blocks are milled (cut) and about 2% salt is added for flavour and preservation.
- [ ] PR7 The salted curds are placed in moulds and pressed; moulds may be sprayed with hot water so that a protective rind forms.
- [ ] PR8 The cheese is removed from the moulds, date stamped, stored and ripened (matured) for about 3 to 12 months, then graded. Ripening develops flavour and texture; longer maturing gives a stronger, drier cheese.
- [ ] PR9 Blue cheese has a mould (Penicillium) added and the cheese is pierced to let air in; soft ripened cheeses have a surface mould. (Contrast only; not a full production route.)
- [ ] PR10 Packaging: vacuum packed in polythene or plastic (zip-lock) bags, waxed paper or wax coating, plastic tubs; the pack protects from mould and drying and extends shelf life.
- [ ] PR11 Labelling: type, brand, quantity, nutritional information, date stamp, storage instructions, and allergen information (milk); legal basis Regulation (EU) No 1169/2011 as given effect by the European Union (Provision of Food Information to Consumers) Regulations 2014.
- [ ] PR12 Diagram cue: a flow chart of cheese production.

Effects of cooking (one block)
- [ ] EC1 Cheese browns (colour change). The protein coagulates and shrinks. The fat melts and, if overheated, separates (oily). Overcooked cheese becomes stringy, tough and hard to digest, and may carbonise. There is little loss of nutrients.
- [ ] EC2 So: grate or slice cheese and cook it at a moderate temperature for a short time; add it at the end of a sauce.

Culinary uses (one block)
- [ ] CU1 Sandwiches and snacks; baking (scones, pastry, cheese straws); sauces (white cheese sauce, macaroni cheese); fillings (savoury and sweet); desserts (cheesecake made with cream cheese); cheeseboards; main ingredient (pizza, pasta, potato and salad dishes); dips, fondue; toppings and garnishes (gratin, Parmesan on pasta).
- [ ] CU2 Matching cheese to dish (2025O): Parmesan on pasta carbonara, cream cheese in lemon cheesecake, cheddar slices in a cheese sandwich; mozzarella on pizza; grated hard cheese for toppings that brown.

Irish farmhouse cheese (one block; 2026O, 2019O)
- [ ] FC1 Farmhouse cheese is made on the farm, in small quantities, mainly by hand, usually from the farm's own cow's, goat's or sheep's milk. It is a speciality or artisan food.
- [ ] FC2 Examples: Cashel Blue (Co Tipperary, blue), Gubbeen (West Cork, washed rind), Durrus (West Cork), Coolea (Co Cork, Gouda style).
- [ ] FC3 Reasons for popularity: wide variety and distinctive flavour; high quality and hygiene; seen as less processed and more natural; local and traceable, with fewer food miles, and support for local employment and family farm businesses; availability at farmers' markets, specialist shops and restaurants and food tourism; promotion by celebrity chefs and media and food awards; the cheeseboard and entertaining; also organic and reduced-fat varieties.

## 3. Traps

- Do not use: "soft cheese is not matured", because Brie and Camembert are ripened by a surface mould and only fresh soft cheeses (cottage, cream, mozzarella) are unripened.
- Do not use: "smoked cheese" as a class by itself, because smoking is a flavouring treatment; the 2014H scheme files it with processed cheese, and the notes should follow the scheme.
- Do not use: "pregnant women should avoid all soft cheeses", because safe soft cheeses exist (cottage, cream cheese, mozzarella, pasteurised); the risk is mould-ripened and blue cheeses and cheeses made from unpasteurised milk.
- Do not use: any amount of salt, protein or calcium per 100 g or any RDA for cheese, because the schemes give only the ranges in the table above.
- Do not use: brand names other than the named Irish farmhouse cheeses (Cashel Blue, Gubbeen, Durrus, Coolea), because they are the only ones confirmed here, and do not give years of founding.
- Stilton appears as semi-hard in the 2014H scheme and is a blue cheese; offer Cashel Blue, Roquefort or Gorgonzola as blue examples and Edam, Gouda or Halloumi as semi-hard ones.
- Casein is the protein; caseinogen is the soluble form in milk. Rennin (rennet) is the enzyme; lactic acid is made by the bacteria, not the rennet.
- Curds are solid; whey is liquid and holds most of the lactose. Do not say lactose stays in cheese.
- Cheddaring is for Cheddar and similar cheeses, not every cheese.
- The milk-processing subsection has pasteurisation; the yoghurt manufacture steps are in the cream, butter and yoghurt subsection.

## 4. What exams ask

- Production of cheese (Higher Level, 18 or 20 marks): nine marked points, two marks each: seven stages in order, one packaging point and one labelling point. The stages must include lactic acid bacteria and what they do, rennet and what it does, curds and whey, chopping and draining, scalding, cheddaring, salting, moulding and pressing, ripening and grading.
- Short answers: the role of lactic acid bacteria and of rennet (2024H, 2010H); fill-in on lactic acid, rennin and curds (2016O); classify cheese and name an example in each class (2014H, 2015O); choose a cheese for a dish (2025O).
- Nutritive and dietetic value of cheese (Ordinary and Higher, 20 marks): HBV protein, saturated fat, no carbohydrate, calcium, vitamins A, D, B2, sodium; lacks vitamin C and iron; versatile, cheap, no waste; unsuitable for low-fat diets; avoid soft cheese in pregnancy.
- Effects of heat on cheese (9 marks, 2019O); culinary uses (three uses, 9 marks).
- Irish farmhouse cheese (2026O, 2019O): three reasons for growing popularity, each explained.
- Menus: place cheese in the main meal (2026O).
