# Package: hom-cooking-methods-s2 — Heat transfer and the principles of cooking

- **loId:** `hom-cooking-methods-s2` · **chapterId:** `hom-cooking-methods` · **syllabusRef:** 1.3.4
- **Syllabus scope:** principles underlying the cooking of food and their correct application to the commodities in the syllabus's food-commodity section; conduction, convection and radiation. Also examined: 2025H, 2024O, 2018H, 2012H. No [HL] marks in this entry; mark nothing.
- **Prepared by:** a higher model, 2026-11-23. Common to both levels. The three methods of heat transfer are examined as a definition plus a cooking example almost every few years; the "principles" half is examined as the aims of cooking and the application to food types.

## 1. Must cover

Why we cook (one block)
- [ ] A1 Aims of cooking: make food safe by destroying harmful micro-organisms; make it easier to chew and digest (softens cellulose, gelatinises starch, denatures protein); improve flavour, colour, texture and appearance (palatability); give variety in the diet; extend keeping time by destroying enzymes and micro-organisms.
- [ ] A2 (assumed) Starch, protein and cellulose are taught in the nutrient chapters; use the terms only.

Principles of cooking (one block)
- [ ] B1 Heat has to reach the centre of the food, and the cook controls the temperature, the time and whether moisture or fat is present. Too little heat or time leaves food undercooked and unsafe; too much dries, toughens or burns it.
- [ ] B2 Size and thickness: thin or small pieces cook faster and evenly; a large joint needs a lower heat for longer so the centre cooks before the outside burns.
- [ ] B3 Moist methods stop food drying out; dry methods need added fat, basting or a cover to keep it juicy.
- [ ] B4 Match the method to the food: tough cuts need long slow moist cooking; tender, delicate foods need short, gentle cooking.
- [ ] B5 Cooked food should be eaten soon or cooled quickly, because bacteria multiply fastest between 5 °C and 63 °C (the danger zone). (Food safety detail belongs to the hygiene and safety chapters.)

Conduction (one block)
- [ ] C1 Heat travels through a solid from molecule to molecule: the molecules at the hot end vibrate faster and pass energy on to their neighbours; the material itself doesn't move.
- [ ] C2 Cooking use: a cooker heats the pan base, which heats the fat or liquid in the pan and then the food (boiling, poaching, stewing, shallow frying and stir-frying all begin with conduction through the pan). A hot pan conducts heat into the surface of a steak, and heat then passes to the centre by conduction.
- [ ] C3 Metals (aluminium, copper, steel) are good conductors, so saucepans have metal bases; wood, plastic, glass and air are poor conductors (insulators), so handles and oven gloves use them.
- [ ] C4 Diagram: heat flame, pan base, food, arrows showing direction, labelled "conduction".

Convection (one block)
- [ ] V1 Heat travels through a liquid or a gas by currents: the heated fluid expands, becomes less dense and rises; cooler, denser fluid moves in to take its place and is heated in turn.
- [ ] V2 Cooking use: boiling and stewing (water currents), deep-fat frying (hot oil), steaming (hot steam rising), baking and roasting in an oven (hot air).
- [ ] V3 A fan oven uses a fan to force the hot air round (forced convection), so food cooks faster and more evenly.
- [ ] V4 Diagram: a pan of water with arrows showing hot water rising from the base and cooler water sinking at the sides.

Radiation (one block)
- [ ] R1 Heat travels as infrared rays in straight lines from a hot source and heats the first solid surface it strikes; no solid, liquid or gas is needed to carry it.
- [ ] R2 Cooking use: grilling (the element or flame heats the surface of the food, sealing and browning it), barbecuing and toasting over hot coals, flame or electric element; the inside then cooks by conduction.
- [ ] R3 Dark, dull surfaces absorb radiant heat; shiny surfaces reflect it (why foil reflects heat). Heat falls off with distance, so the grill rack height is adjusted.
- [ ] R4 Diagram: arrows from a grill element to the food surface, labelled "radiation".

Combined heat transfer and the microwave (one block)
- [ ] M1 Most methods use more than one: roasting uses radiation from the oven walls and element, convection by hot air and conduction inside the food; boiling uses conduction through the pan then convection in the water.
- [ ] M2 Microwave cooking: a magnetron produces microwaves (electromagnetic waves) that make water molecules in the food vibrate, which makes heat inside the outer layer of the food; heat then spreads inwards by conduction. The 2012H scheme listed microwave cooking as an example of conduction, so say that the heat spreads by conduction once made.

Applying the principles to food commodities (one block)
- [ ] F1 Meat: tough cuts, rich in collagen, need long, slow, moist cooking; tender cuts can be grilled or fried quickly.
- [ ] F2 Fish: little connective tissue, so it cooks quickly at gentle heat; poaching, steaming, grilling, baking and frying suit it.
- [ ] F3 Eggs and milk dishes: protein coagulates at moderate temperatures, so gentle heat is used; fierce heat makes eggs rubbery and custard curdle; milk scorches on direct heat.
- [ ] F4 Vegetables and fruit: short cooking in little water, or steaming, keeps colour, texture and water-soluble vitamins.
- [ ] F5 Cereals, rice and pasta: starch needs water and heat to gelatinise, so they cook in boiling water or steam.
- [ ] F6 Pastry and cakes: dry oven heat sets the structure; keep the fat cold for pastry; do not open the oven early for cakes.
- [ ] F7 Fats and oils: overheating makes them smoke and break down; choose a fat with a high smoke point for deep-fat frying and do not reuse it too often.

## 3. Traps

- Three definitions get matched in the ordinary-level paper: radiation = straight lines, first solid object it hits; convection = currents in liquid or gas; conduction = molecule to molecule through a solid. Make them clear and distinct.
- Radiation needs no medium; convection and conduction do.
- Grilling is radiation (the 2012 scheme lists it also under conduction because the food conducts inside, but a one-method answer should be radiation).
- Steaming is convection (hot steam rises and carries heat) but the steam also condenses on the food and gives up heat; don't present it as radiation.
- Cooking methods themselves (moist, dry, fat-based and how to do each) belong to the next subsection, "Choice of cooking method"; here use methods only as examples of a heat-transfer type.
- Browning and the loss of nutrients belong to the first subsection of this chapter.
- Meat tenderising belongs to the meat chapter, egg cookery to the dairy and eggs chapter, and food safety temperatures to the hygiene chapters.
- Do not use: "FSAI guidance on safe cooking applies in Irish kitchens" with no content, because it names a body without a fact; if a safe core temperature is wanted it must be checked, so leave it out.
- Do not use: "solids conduct heat more slowly than liquids" as a general rule, because it is false (metals conduct better than water); say instead that large solid pieces of food take longer to heat through because the heat has to travel inwards.
- Do not use: any oven temperature or fan-oven reduction figure, because manufacturers vary.

## 4. What exams ask

- Name and describe one method of heat transfer with a cooking example (2025H); explain conduction and convection (2018H); name three methods with an example of each (2012H); match three definitions (2024O). Expected: the definitions above, then boiling, frying or stewing for conduction, boiling, baking, roasting or steaming for convection, and grilling or barbecuing for radiation.
- State why food is cooked (safety, digestibility, flavour, colour and texture, keeping time) and the factors that affect cooking time (size, thickness, temperature, food type).
- Choose the cooking principle for a given food: tough meat slow and moist; fish and eggs gentle heat; vegetables short time and little water.
