# Package: hom-meat-preparation-s4 — Meat processing and meat products

- **loId:** `hom-meat-preparation-s4` · **chapterId:** `hom-meat-preparation` · **syllabusRef:** 1.3.2

- **Also examined:** 2014H (details of one process to extend shelf life: name, method with underlying principle, and effect on meat); 2010O (two types of processed meat with an advantage of each).
- **Prepared by:** a higher model, 2026-11-23. Preservation in general (principles, fermentation, irradiation, jam and bottling) is taught in the food preservation chapter; here the processes are applied to meat, and one or more must be worked in full (method, principle, effect) because that is how the exam asks.

## 1. Must cover

What processed meat is and the main products (one block)
- [ ] P1 Processed meat is meat that has been changed from its fresh state by mincing, salting, curing, smoking, cooking, canning, freezing or adding ingredients, usually to make it convenient, to extend shelf life, or to use cheaper cuts and trimmings.
- [ ] P2 Examples (any two with an advantage each for 2010O): burgers, sausages, puddings (black and white pudding), nuggets (frozen); rashers, bacon, gammon, cooked ham and smoked meats (cured); luncheon meat, cold cuts and salami; canned corned beef, stews and meat in sauce; dehydrated or freeze-dried meat in soups and meals; ready meals and pies.
- [ ] P3 Typical ingredients: minced meat and trimmings (sometimes offal), fat, water, salt, herbs and spices, rusk or breadcrumbs as binder, additives (preservatives such as nitrite, colours, flavour enhancers, antioxidants) and sometimes textured vegetable protein (TVP, a dried soya product) as a meat extender. Additives must be approved under Regulation (EC) No 1333/2008 and are listed by E number.
- [ ] P4 Advantages: low cost per portion; uses all parts of the carcass (little waste); convenient and quick (little preparation, skill or fuel); long shelf life; variety, uniform portions, appeal to children; some products (canned, frozen) need no refrigeration or can be stored for months.
- [ ] P5 Disadvantages: often high in salt (linked to high blood pressure) and saturated fat; may contain additives, fillers, added water and less meat than expected; lower nutritional quality than fresh lean meat; some nutrient losses (B vitamins in canning and dehydration). Health guidelines advise limiting processed meat.
- [ ] P6 Labelling: ingredients in descending order of weight, with the meat content declared where the name stresses meat; allergens emphasised (e.g. cereals containing gluten, soya, mustard, celery).

Why meat spoils (assumed knowledge, one block, kept short)
- [ ] W1 (assumed) Meat spoils through micro-organisms (bacteria, moulds), the meat's own enzymes, oxidation of fat (rancidity) and drying. Moisture, warmth, air, nutrients and time are what bacteria need; processes work by removing or controlling one of these.

Processes that extend the shelf life of meat (one block; give each as name, how, principle, effect)
- [ ] S1 Refrigeration (5 °C or below): slows enzyme action and bacterial growth, adding days; does not kill; cover or wrap. Effect: nutrients retained, small drip loss. Listeria monocytogenes can multiply at fridge temperatures, so ready-to-eat meats need good hygiene and use-by dates.
- [ ] S2 Freezing (blast freezing at about -30 °C, storage -18 °C or below): meat is boned, trimmed, portioned, wrapped in moisture-proof packaging and frozen fast. Principle: low temperature inhibits enzyme activity and microbial growth; water turns to ice, so it is unavailable; fast freezing gives small ice crystals that damage cells less; wrapping prevents entry of microbes and freezer burn. Effect: colour, flavour, texture and nutrients mostly retained; some loss of extractives and B vitamins and minerals in drip; freezer burn and rancidity if badly packed.
- [ ] S3 Vacuum packing: air is removed and the meat sealed in strong plastic. Principle: aerobic micro-organisms (and oxidation) need oxygen; sealing stops re-entry. Effect: keeps longer in the fridge, nutritive value, flavour and texture largely unchanged, has a use-by date; treat as fresh once opened.
- [ ] S4 Modified atmosphere packaging (MAP): air is replaced by a gas mix (carbon dioxide and nitrogen; oxygen is used for fresh red meat to keep the colour) in a sealed tray. Principle: carbon dioxide inhibits aerobic bacteria; nitrogen is inert and prevents the pack collapsing. Effect: longer life in the fridge; use-by date; treat as fresh when opened.
- [ ] S5 Curing (salting, brining): meat cooled, then cured by dry-rubbing with salt (and curing salts) or injecting or soaking in brine (salt, sugar and sodium nitrite or nitrate), held in a cold room for a few days, then smoked or sold "green" (unsmoked). Principle: high salt concentration draws water out of micro-organisms by osmosis, dehydrating them and lowering the water available; nitrite inhibits spoilage bacteria including Clostridium botulinum and fixes the pink colour. Effect: little loss of nutritive value; sodium content is raised; colour (pink) and flavour (salty) change; firmer.
- [ ] S6 Smoking: after curing, hung in smoke from burning hardwood sawdust. Principle: smoke contains antibacterial and antioxidant chemicals and the surface dries; adds flavour. Effect: flavour and colour change; may extend life only modestly; smoking is usually combined with curing.
- [ ] S7 Canning: meat is prepared and cooked, packed in cans with brine, sauce or liquid, air is removed, cans are sealed and heat-sterilised under pressure. Principle: high temperature destroys micro-organisms and enzymes; the airtight seal stops re-entry of microbes. Effect: long shelf life (years) at room temperature; meat tender and cooked; loss of B vitamins, especially thiamine; texture and flavour change.
- [ ] S8 Dehydration and freeze-drying: water is removed (hot air or freeze-drying under vacuum). Principle: micro-organisms need water to grow. Effect: light, long-lasting; rehydrated in dishes; some loss of B vitamins and texture change. (Typical of dried meat in soups and ready meals.)
- [ ] S9 Pick one process and be able to write it in full as a 15-mark answer: name, how it is carried out, the underlying principle, and its effect on meat (colour, texture, flavour, nutrients).
- [ ] S10 Diagram optional: flow diagram for curing or freezing.

## 3. Traps

- Do not use: "140 °C" (or any sterilising temperature) for canning, because the marking-scheme figure is not a standard canning temperature and is unconfirmed. Say "heated to a high temperature under pressure".
- Do not use: any curing times or numbers of days (such as "4 days in brine, 4-5 days in a cold room"), because they vary with product and are unchecked; say "several days".
- Do not use: "modified atmosphere packaging uses only carbon dioxide and nitrogen", because oxygen is also used in fresh red-meat packs.
- Do not use: market share, per-capita consumption or salt-content figures for particular products, because they change.
- Wording: say health advice is to limit processed meat for its salt, saturated fat and bowel-cancer risk.
- Refrigeration and freezing are the cheapest; "extend shelf life" means slowing spoilage, not sterilising (except canning).
- Salt does not kill by poisoning; it dehydrates microbial cells by osmosis. Nitrite is the key to the pink colour and to botulism control.
- A processed meat is not necessarily unhealthy for every nutrient; state both advantages and disadvantages when asked to evaluate.
- Vacuum-packed meat is not sterile: it still needs refrigeration and a use-by date.
- Freeze-drying and dehydration remove water; canning uses heat; do not confuse the principles (water removal vs. heat sterilisation vs. cold vs. salt).
- Belongs to neighbouring subsections: selecting and storing fresh meat at home to the second subsection; cooking effects and tenderising to the third; irradiation and fermentation (which are Higher Level preservation methods) and jam-making to the food preservation chapter; the bacteria in detail to the food poisoning chapter.

## 4. What exams ask

- One process that extends shelf life (2014H, 15 marks; scheme gives four marks per component): name the process; method with underlying principle; effect on meat. Accepted: freezing, refrigeration, vacuum or modified atmosphere packing, curing and salting, canning (also smoking, dehydration). Full marks need all three components for one process, not a list of processes.
- Processed meats (2010O, 16 marks): name two types (burgers, sausages, bacon, ham, cooked meats, canned corned beef, dehydrated TVP products) and give one advantage of each (economy, little waste, convenience, long shelf life, speed of cooking, variety).
- Evaluate processed meats: advantages (cost, convenience, shelf life, uses less tender cuts) against disadvantages (salt, saturated fat, additives, lower meat content).
- TVP as an extender: dried soya protein replacing some meat.
- Linked topics: packaging materials for perishable foods (2023H, plastic or vacuum packs, suitability and environmental impact), preservation in general.
- Tip candidates: for any process always write "principle" as a separate sentence (low temperature, removal of air, osmosis, high heat, removal of water); mention the effect on nutrients and on texture or flavour; name the product examples; link vacuum or MAP to the need for refrigeration and a use-by date.
