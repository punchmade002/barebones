# Package: hom-family-society-s3 — Modern family structures

- **loId:** `hom-family-society-s3` · **chapterId:** `hom-family-society` · **syllabusRef:** 3.1.3
- **Syllabus scope:** "characteristics of lone-parent, nuclear, extended and blended families." Settings: comparing Irish families with families in other European countries by structure, size and roles. Common to Ordinary and Higher Level; no [HL] items. Past papers also examine it: 2021O (one-parent, nuclear, blended), 2017O and 2013O (nuclear, extended, blended), and 2019H (characteristics of contemporary family structures, which also lists same-sex-parent and childless families).
- **Prepared by:** a higher model, 2026-11-23. Four structures, each given by a short definition and a list of characteristics, because the exams mark "describe" with about five marks per structure. Same-sex-parent, childless and cohabiting families are a short extra block, since the 2019H scheme names them. Why structures have changed belongs to "Changes affecting family structures" and "The historical development of the Irish family"; what families do belongs to "Family functions".

## 1. Must cover

Nuclear family (one block)
- [ ] N1 A nuclear family is one or two parents, married or cohabiting, and their children, living in a self-contained household. (The marking schemes accept "parent or parents and children".)
- [ ] N2 Characteristics: small and self-contained; mobile, so members can move for work; number of children usually small; tasks and decisions are usually shared between the parents; often both parents work; the family is independent of relatives day to day, but may lack nearby relatives' support; children depend on the parents and on State supports.
- [ ] N3 Advantages and drawbacks (one line each): privacy, independence and mobility; but less help with childcare and care of ill members, and more pressure on the two adults.

Lone-parent (one-parent) family (one block)
- [ ] L1 A lone-parent family is one parent living with one or more dependent children.
- [ ] L2 Causes: separation or divorce; death of a partner (bereavement); desertion; the parent's choice to rear a child alone; the parent never having lived with a partner.
- [ ] L3 Characteristics: the parent carries the full economic, emotional and practical responsibility; often a single income and financial pressure; heavy workload and little time; need for reliable and affordable childcare; may depend on relatives, friends and State supports; lone parenthood may be temporary, ending when a new partner joins the household (a blended family) or the children grow up. Most lone parents are women, but fathers can be lone parents. (Schemes: financial, emotional and workload pressures.)
- [ ] L4 Supports: the State supports lone parents through social welfare payments and schemes such as the One-Parent Family Payment and the Working Family Payment, medical cards and childcare supports; voluntary organisations offer information and support. Name and explain how they help, without amounts or eligibility rules.

Extended family (one block)
- [ ] X1 An extended family includes the nuclear family plus other relatives such as grandparents, aunts, uncles and cousins, who live in the same household or very close by (the schemes: "living with or in close proximity").
- [ ] X2 Types (assumed): vertical (three or more generations, e.g. grandparents, parents, grandchildren) and horizontal (relatives of the same generation, e.g. brothers with their wives and children).
- [ ] X3 Characteristics: members are interdependent, sharing childcare, housework, income and care of older relatives; may work together as one economic unit (family farm or business); strong family tradition, with respect and values passed between generations; relationships may be more traditional, with roles divided by gender and age; larger and more crowded households; grandparents often provide much childcare.
- [ ] X4 Advantages and drawbacks: wide support network and shared costs; but less privacy, possible conflict between generations and the authority of older members. Extended families are the usual form in many cultures and were the usual form in rural Ireland in the past.

Blended (reconstituted, step) family (one block)
- [ ] B1 A blended family is formed when a parent with children from an earlier relationship has a new partner, through remarriage or cohabitation, so that two family units join. The old marking schemes also accept "reconstituted family" and "step-family".
- [ ] B2 Members and relationships: step-parents, step-siblings, half-siblings (when the new couple have a child), and extended step-relatives; children may split time between two households.
- [ ] B3 Characteristics and difficulties: the adults must build new relationships and agree rules and discipline; children may find it hard to accept a step-parent or step-siblings; rivalry or jealousy; different expectations and routines; financial obligations to children of an earlier relationship as well as the new family; contact arrangements with the non-resident parent; it takes time and patience. Advantages: more adults to share income and care, new relationships and support.
- [ ] B4 Blended families have become more common in Ireland since divorce became possible and lone-parent families formed new partnerships (one line; the history is in a neighbouring subsection).

Other structures the examiners accept (one short block)
- [ ] O1 Same-sex-parent families: two parents of the same sex and their children (by adoption, fostering, previous relationships or assisted reproduction). Childless (couple) families: a couple with no children. Cohabiting-couple families: an unmarried couple with or without children. Foster families (assumed): children placed in a family's care by the State on a temporary basis, no further detail needed. Mark 2019H lists nuclear, extended, blended, lone-parent, same-sex-parent and childless.

Comparing Irish and other European families (settings; one block)
- [ ] E1 How to compare: by structure (the proportions of nuclear, lone-parent, extended and blended households), by size (the number of children and members), and by roles (who works, who cares for children and older people). The notes should explain how to compare, using published statistics from Eurostat and the Central Statistics Office, but quote none.
- [ ] E2 Nordic countries (e.g. Sweden, Denmark, Norway): State-funded parental leave and childcare, supporting shared parenting and mothers' employment.
- [ ] E3 Southern European countries (e.g. Italy, Spain): the family, including grandparents, plays a larger part in childcare and care of older people; adult children stay in the parental home longer.
- [ ] E4 Ireland: a mixture, with the nuclear family the most common household structure and relatives, especially grandparents, still important for childcare; and a family-based approach to care. Ireland has historically had a higher birth rate and larger families than most of Europe, a gap that has narrowed. State support (childcare, parental leave) has grown but still relies on families and on private spending.
- [ ] E5 The cause of differences: culture, religion, the economy and how far the State supports families.

## 3. Traps

- A lone-parent family is not always a mother with children, and is not always the result of separation; list all causes.
- A nuclear family can be a single-parent unit in the schemes' wording ("parent or parents"), but the standard textbook nuclear family is two parents and children; keep the two structures distinct by their characteristics: nuclear has two adults sharing tasks, lone-parent has one.
- An extended family is not just any family with relatives; relatives live in the same household or very close by and are interdependent.
- A blended family is not the same as a lone-parent family that gets a new partner; it begins when the new partner and the children live as one family. Step-siblings (no blood link) differ from half-siblings (one shared parent).
- Do not describe any structure as "better" than another. Say each has advantages and challenges.
- Neighbouring subsections: causes of the shift from extended to nuclear, and the effects of divorce, recession and technology, belong to "Changes affecting family structures" and "The historical development of the Irish family". The five functions belong to "Family functions". Roles of parents, role conflict and care of older people belong to the chapter on the family as a caring unit. Divorce and separation law belongs to the family law chapter.
- Do not use: any amount, rate, age limit or eligibility rule for the One-Parent Family Payment, Child Benefit or Working Family Payment, because these change; name the payment and what it is for.
- Do not use: figures for the share of lone-parent, nuclear or extended households in Ireland or Europe, or the number of children per family, because they change with each census or survey.
- Do not use: named countries' rankings (for example "Ireland has the largest families in the EU"), because the figures change and the notes cannot confirm them.
- Do not use: "Extended families have all but disappeared in Ireland", because many families still have close relatives nearby and grandparents are heavily involved in childcare.

## 4. What exams ask

- "Describe each of: nuclear, extended, blended / one-parent family" (2013O 15 marks, 2017O and 2021O 18 marks; 5 to 6 marks each): expect a definition plus two or three characteristics each. Nuclear: parents and children, self-contained, small, mobile, shared tasks. Extended: grandparents, aunts, uncles, cousins, close together, interdependent, shared roles. Blended: step-parents, step-siblings, remarriage or cohabitation, adjustment and rules. One-parent: one parent and children, from separation, bereavement, desertion or choice, with financial, emotional and workload pressure.
- "Describe the characteristics of contemporary family structures" (2019H, 12 marks): at least four types, each with a defining feature; same-sex-parent and childless families give extra credit.
- Compare Irish and European families (settings, so a possible question): structure, size and roles, with one contrast for each.
- Tip: give a defining sentence and then characteristics; do not write only "mother, father and children".
