## 1. Must cover

Sources of monosaccharides (one block)
- [ ] M1 Glucose: fruit (grapes, for example), some vegetables, and glucose syrup, a refined sugar used in sweets and processed foods.
- [ ] M2 Fructose: fruit and honey (honey contains fructose and glucose; fructose is the larger part). Fruit sugar.
- [ ] M3 Galactose: not usually eaten free. It is released when lactose, the sugar in milk, is digested ("digested milk" in the schemes).

Sources of disaccharides (one block)
- [ ] D1 Sucrose (table sugar): extracted from sugar beet and sugar cane and sold as white and brown sugar; it also occurs naturally in many fruit and vegetables. Added to biscuits, cakes, sweets, jam, fizzy drinks, breakfast cereals and other processed foods.
- [ ] D2 Lactose (milk sugar): in milk and milk products such as yoghurt.
- [ ] D3 Maltose (malt sugar): made when starch is broken down in germinating cereal grains, especially barley; used to make malt (found in malted drinks, malt extract and malt loaf, and used in brewing). The schemes give "barley".
- [ ] D4 Added (free) sugars in processed food: biscuits, cakes, sweets, chocolate, jam, fizzy drinks, ice cream, breakfast cereals and sauces; check the ingredients list. Natural sugars in whole fruit and vegetables and in milk are packaged with fibre and other nutrients; added sugars supply energy with few other nutrients.

Sources of starch (one block)
- [ ] S1 Cereals and cereal products: bread, breakfast cereals, porridge oats, pasta, rice, flour, wheat, oats, barley, maize. The schemes list "potatoes, cereals", "rice, pasta, potatoes, bread, breakfast cereals or flour".
- [ ] S2 Potatoes and root vegetables; pulses (peas, beans, lentils).
- [ ] S3 Starch is the main carbohydrate in the Irish diet and the main energy-supplying carbohydrate in staple foods. Unripe bananas are optional (omit).
- [ ] S4 Glycogen (a polysaccharide): present in small amounts in meat (the 2015 scheme lists "meat"); not a significant dietary source. One line.

Sources of non-starch polysaccharides (dietary fibre) (one block)
- [ ] N1 Non-starch polysaccharides (NSP) are the plant polysaccharides, mainly cellulose and pectin, that human digestive enzymes cannot break down; they make up the dietary fibre of food. Gloss "dietary fibre".
- [ ] N2 Cellulose: skins of fruit and vegetables, leafy and other vegetables, wholegrain cereals, bran, wholemeal bread, brown rice, wholemeal pasta, nuts and seeds.
- [ ] N3 Pectin: fruit, especially apples and citrus fruit (the peel and pith of citrus), also soft fruits such as gooseberries and blackcurrants. Unripe fruit has more.
- [ ] N4 Wholemeal and wholegrain products have more fibre than white because the bran (the outer layer of the grain) is kept; white flour and polished (white) rice have the bran removed in refining.
- [ ] N5 Foods named in the marking schemes as good fibre sources: skins of fruit and vegetables, fruit and vegetables, peas, beans and lentils, wholegrain cereals, brown bread, nuts, seeds (sunflower, sesame, pumpkin and linseed), brown rice and pasta, bran, porridge oats, high-fibre breakfast cereals.
- [ ] N6 Fibre comes only from plants; meat, fish, eggs and dairy foods contain none.
- [ ] N7 Fibre sources have two kinds: insoluble fibre (cellulose and wheat bran: wholemeal bread, bran, vegetable skins) and soluble fibre (pectin, plus the soluble fibre in oats, beans and lentils: fruit, oats, pulses). One short block; the effects of each belong to the functions subsection.

Putting it together (one block)
- [ ] T1 A summary table of foods by carbohydrate: sugars, starch and fibre. This is the usual exam shape: "give one source of sugar, starch and fibre".
- [ ] T2 Poster or collage (Settings): a poster headed by classification groups (sugars, starch, fibre) with pictures of foods. Give one tip in the notes; no diagram.
- [ ] T3 Many foods contain more than one: bread has starch, some sugar and fibre (more in wholemeal); fruit has sugars, pectin and cellulose; potatoes in their skins have starch and fibre; milk has lactose only.

## 3. Traps

- Do not use: "cheese" as a source of lactose (the old notes list yoghurt and cheese), because most of the lactose in cheese is lost in the whey or fermented in making it, so hard cheese contains almost none. Name milk, yoghurt and other milk products; if cheese is mentioned, say only that hard cheese has almost no lactose (fresh and soft cheeses keep some).
- Do not list galactose as a food sugar you eat in quantity; it comes from lactose, not from a plant food.
- Maltose is not a source in table sugar; the sugar in sugar beet and cane is sucrose. Honey is not table sugar.
- Cellulose, pectin and the other NSP are not digested by people; they are not a source of energy and they are not a "source of carbohydrate" in the energy sense (energy is in the energy value subsection). The schemes still list them under carbohydrate.
- Do not call wholemeal bread "high in sugar" or white bread "fibre-free"; white bread has little fibre.
- "Brown" is not the same as "wholemeal": brown bread or rice may be coloured; the safe advice is "wholemeal", "wholegrain" and "brown rice". Do not turn this into a claim that colouring is common.
- Do not name a fibre content, a grams-per-100 g figure or a recommended intake in this subsection; those belong to the energy value and dietary fibre targets subsection and labelling.
- Smoothies and juice: fruit juice has little fibre; do not list it as a fibre source. (A blended smoothie keeps some fibre; the targets subsection covers the sugar point.)
- Do not describe how fibre works in the bowel here (functions subsection) or give the properties of starch and sugars (properties subsection).
- Glycogen is stored in animals and is mentioned in the table only; do not teach animal carbohydrate stores here.

## 4. What exams ask

- One source each of sugar, starch and fibre (Ordinary Level, 6 marks): table sugar, fruit, honey, sweets, jam; rice, pasta, potatoes, bread, flour, cereals; wholegrain cereals, fruit and vegetable skins, nuts, seeds, brown rice.
- Name three sources of fibre, or three ways of getting fibre: skins, pulses, wholegrain products, nuts, seeds (Ordinary Level, 6 marks).
- Dietary sources as one heading in the long accounts (14–30 marks): a good answer gives sources for each class: sugars (fruit, honey, table sugar, sweets, cakes, biscuits), starch (cereals, bread, rice, pasta, potatoes) and fibre (wholegrain cereals, fruit and vegetables, nuts, seeds), at about three sources per class.
- The classification table's "food source" column at Higher Level: glucose, fructose and galactose from fruit, honey and digested milk; sucrose, lactose and maltose from table sugar, milk and barley; starch, cellulose and pectin from potatoes and cereals, vegetables and fruit.
- Matching and sorting: cellulose from vegetables, glucose from fruit, sucrose as table sugar.
