# Package: hom-work-leisure-s4 — The Protection of Young Persons (Employment) Act (1996)

- **loId:** `hom-work-leisure-s4` · **chapterId:** `hom-work-leisure` · **syllabusRef:** 6.3.1
- **Syllabus scope:** "brief outline" of the Protection of Young Persons (Employment) Act (1996). Also examined: other legislation protecting people in employment (2026O, 2019H, 2011H). Also asked directly as an informative note or description (2005O, 2007H, 2016O, 2023H). Common to both levels; nothing is [HL]. The Act is in force (as amended) and keeps its syllabus name (Appendix A).
- **Prepared by:** a higher model, 2026-11-23, checked against the revised text of the Act on the Law Reform Commission's revisedacts site (revised to July 2022) and the WRC guidance. Neighbours: parental leave and flexible hours belong to the changing patterns subsection; the part-time job advantages and disadvantages for students belong to the first subsection; minimum wage rates and unemployment are not here.

## 1. Must cover

What the Act is and who it protects
- [ ] A1 The Protection of Young Persons (Employment) Act 1996 protects young people in work by setting minimum ages, limits on hours, rest and break rules and night-work limits, so that work does not harm their health, safety, education or development. Its aim is to stop exploitation and to protect schooling.
- [ ] A2 Two age groups: a child is under 16; a young person is 16 or 17. Rules differ for each. (Say "under 18" only for the combined group.)
- [ ] A3 It is enforced by the Workplace Relations Commission (WRC) inspectors; employers who break it commit an offence and can be prosecuted and fined.

Minimum age
- [ ] M1 Children under 16 may not be employed full-time (it is an offence).
- [ ] M2 Children under 14 may not be employed at all, apart from licensed cultural, artistic, sporting or advertising work (e.g. film, theatre, modelling, sport), which requires authorisation from the relevant Minister with conditions, in individual cases.
- [ ] M3 Children of 14 may do light work during school holidays and approved work experience; children of 15 may in addition do light work during the school term. Light work means work that is not harmful to health, safety or development and does not interfere with schooling.
- [ ] M4 Employers must not give children work that is unsuitable or dangerous.

Hours of work, children (14 and 15)
- [ ] H1 During school holidays: light work only, maximum 7 hours in a day and 35 hours in a week.
- [ ] H2 During the school term: only 15-year-olds may do light work, a maximum of 8 hours a week. 14-year-olds cannot work during the term, apart from approved work experience.
- [ ] H3 Approved work experience as part of a school programme (at 14 or over) and approved training or work experience (at 15): up to 8 hours a day and 40 hours a week.
- [ ] H4 During the summer holidays, a child must have at least 21 days free from any work.

Hours of work, young persons (16 and 17)
- [ ] Y1 Maximum 8 hours a day and 40 hours a week.

Rest, breaks and night work
- [ ] R1 Children: at least 14 consecutive hours' rest in every 24 hours; two days off in every seven; a break of at least 30 minutes after 4 hours' work.
- [ ] R2 Young persons (16 and 17): at least 12 consecutive hours' rest in every 24; two days off a week, consecutive where practicable; a 30-minute break after 4.5 hours' work. Breaks are unpaid.
- [ ] R3 Night work: children may not work between 8 p.m. and 8 a.m.; young persons may not work between 10 p.m. and 6 a.m. (a later window of 11 p.m. to 7 a.m. applies only in prescribed limited cases, and not before a school day during term).

Employer duties
- [ ] E1 Before employing anyone under 18, the employer must see a copy of the birth certificate or other satisfactory evidence of age.
- [ ] E2 Before employing a child (under 16), the employer must get the written permission of a parent or guardian.
- [ ] E3 The employer must keep a record (register) of each young person's name, date of birth, daily start and finish times and pay, and keep it for at least three years.
- [ ] E4 Employers may be inspected, and must give inspectors access to the records.
- [ ] E5 Employers are expected to be aware of the Act's rules; the Act also applies in most cases to work done for family employers, with some limited exceptions (do not give detail of exceptions).

Other legislation protecting people in employment (also examined)
- [ ] L1 Safety, Health and Welfare at Work Act 2005: employers must provide a safe workplace, assess risks, supply safety training and equipment, have a safety statement; workers have duties too; the Health and Safety Authority (HSA) enforces.
- [ ] L2 National Minimum Wage Act 2000: sets a minimum hourly pay rate; reduced rates for young workers (youth rates are under review and may change, so no figures or percentages).
- [ ] L3 Employment Equality Acts 1998 to 2015: prohibit discrimination in employment on nine grounds (gender, civil status, family status, age, disability, sexual orientation, race, religion, membership of the Traveller community). Covers access to work, pay, conditions, training, promotion and dismissal.
- [ ] L4 Organisation of Working Time Act 1997: limits on weekly hours, rest breaks and daily and weekly rest, paid annual leave and public holidays, Sunday and night work provisions.
- [ ] L5 Other examples to name in a line each (not detail): Unfair Dismissals Acts 1977 to 2015 (protection against unfair dismissal); Maternity Protection Acts 1994 and 2004; Parental Leave Act 1998; Terms of Employment (Information) Acts (written terms of employment). Disputes go to the Workplace Relations Commission.
- [ ] L6 Assumed (assumed): trade unions represent workers and negotiate pay and conditions.

## 3. Traps

- Do not use: "A young person is generally under 18" as the definition, because the Act splits the group: a child is under 16, a young person is 16 to 17. Teaching it as one group loses the different rules.
- Do not use: "14- and 15-year-olds may do light work for up to 8 hours a week during school term", because only 15-year-olds may do term-time light work; 14-year-olds may not (other than approved work experience).
- Do not use: "Children under 14 may not work during school term" as the only under-14 rule, because under-14s may not be employed at all (other than licensed cultural, artistic, sporting and advertising work); the term-time rule is for 14-year-olds.
- Do not use: the 2005 marking scheme's "39 hours a week for 18-year-olds" and "35 hours for 14 to 15-year-olds" as general rules, because 18-year-olds are adults and are outside the Act; 35 hours is the holiday limit for 14 and 15-year-olds only.
- Do not use any figure for minimum wage rates (changing) or fines (changing). Do not use "lower rates for workers under 20" with percentages.
- Do not give a 7 hours a day holiday limit for 16 and 17-year-olds; theirs is 8.
- The Organisation of Working Time Act 1997 limits adults' hours; the 1996 Act protects under 18s. Keep them separate.
- Do not list the Equality Authority (replaced by the Irish Human Rights and Equality Commission in 2014) as the enforcing body; the WRC deals with employment equality claims and the IHREC has a wider equality role. If a body is named, give only the WRC.
- Leave minimum wage and unemployment supports to other subsections; do not describe pay rates.

- Describe how the Act protects young people, or write an informative note on it (2005O, 2007H, 2016O, 2023H; 8 to 12 marks): under-16s not full-time; under-14s not in work (licensed exceptions); 14 and 15 light work in holidays (35 hours) and 15-year-olds 8 hours in term; breaks and rest; no night work; employer must hold birth certificate and written parental permission; records. A few correct limits are enough.
