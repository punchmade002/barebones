## 1. Must cover

Criteria for selection
- [ ] C1 Criteria for choosing any material: durability (resistance to wear, impact, moisture, heat); cost (purchase, fitting, upkeep over its life); appearance and texture (suits style, colour scheme and décor); ease of cleaning and maintenance; safety in use (slip resistance, safe edges, safety glass); insulation and warmth to touch; environmental impact (source, distance travelled, recyclability, life span); suitability for the job (strength, resistance to water and heat).

Wood
- [ ] WD1 Solid hardwoods: ash, beech, elm, oak, mahogany, rosewood, walnut. Properties: expensive, durable, resistant to decay and warping, attractive grain. Uses: floors, stairs, furniture, doors, architraves, windows, skirting boards, decking. For "one solid wood", give a single named wood with its type, properties and uses (e.g. oak: hardwood, hard-wearing, durable, expensive, used for floors, stairs and furniture).
- [ ] WD2 Softwoods: pine and spruce (deal); cheaper, easy to work, dent and scratch more readily; used for floorboards, joists, roof timbers, doors, windows, furniture, shelving.
- [ ] WD3 Wood is natural: it shrinks, swells and may warp with changes in humidity, so it is seasoned (dried) before use; it is protected and kept easy to clean with varnish, lacquer, stain, paint or sealant; it is a poor conductor so feels warm; it is renewable if sustainably sourced.
- [ ] WD4 Manufactured boards: veneer (thin layer of attractive hardwood on a cheaper core: look of solid wood at lower cost); plywood (thin layers glued with the grain of each layer at right angles to the next: strong, stable, resists warping); medium-density fibreboard, MDF (wood fibres and resin, smooth, takes paint, swells when wet); chipboard (wood chips and resin, cheap, used for kitchen units and flooring, weak when wet).

Metal
- [ ] M1 Metals used in the home: steel, stainless steel, aluminium, copper, brass, cast iron. General properties: strong, hard-wearing, good conductors of heat and electricity, feel cold to touch.
- [ ] M2 Ordinary steel rusts unless coated or painted. Stainless steel contains chromium, resists corrosion, is hygienic and easy to clean: sinks, worktops, cooker hoods, appliance fronts, cutlery.
- [ ] M3 Aluminium: lightweight, resists corrosion (protective oxide layer): window frames, saucepans, fittings. Copper: malleable, good conductor: hot water pipes, cylinders, saucepan bases. Brass: alloy of copper and zinc, resists corrosion: taps, door handles, decorative fittings. Cast iron: heavy, brittle, retains heat: fireplaces, radiators, cooking pots.

Glass
- [ ] G1 Glass is made mainly from sand heated with other materials; it is transparent, non-porous, hygienic, easy to clean, hard but brittle.
- [ ] G2 Types and uses: float glass (smooth and clear: windows, mirrors, which have a reflective coating); toughened glass (heat treated: shatters into small blunt pieces, withstands heat and stress better: shower screens, glass doors, low-level glazing, oven doors); laminated glass (two sheets bonded with a plastic interlayer: holds together when broken, resists break-ins: windscreens, security glazing); double glazing (two panes with a sealed gap of air or argon: reduces heat loss and noise); low-emissivity glass (thin coating reduces heat loss, lets light in); frosted or patterned glass (light passes, view obscured: bathrooms, hall doors); glass bricks (refract light and let light through partitions and walls).

Plastics
- [ ] PL1 Plastics are made from polymers (long chains of molecules, usually from petroleum); they are lightweight, waterproof, easy to clean, easily coloured and moulded, and poor conductors of heat and electricity. Two groups: thermoplastic and thermosetting.
- [ ] PL2 Thermoplastics: soft and flexible; soften when heated; can be moulded and remoulded; harden on cooling; damaged by solvents; not damaged by acids and alkalis. Examples and uses: polyethylene (bottles, food containers, toys, bowls); polytetrafluoroethylene, PTFE or Teflon, a fluorocarbon (non-stick finish on saucepans and frying pans); PVC (vinyl floor and wall coverings, pipes, window frames).
- [ ] PL3 Thermosetting plastics: hard and rigid; set permanently on first moulding and cannot be remoulded; withstand high temperatures; blister and crack if overheated; damaged by strong acids and alkalis, not by solvents. Examples and uses: melamine formaldehyde (cups, plates, saucers, laminated worktops); phenol formaldehyde (interior paddles of top-loading washing machines); Bakelite (light switches, telephones, utensil handles, cooker control knobs).
- [ ] PL4 Comparison for exam answers: three headings (properties, examples, uses), with at least two properties and two named examples with uses for each type. Diagram: none needed; a two-column table is the best revision form.

Fabric
- [ ] FB1 Fabrics in the home are used for curtains, upholstery, carpets, bed linen and cushions. Criteria: durability, drape, colour fastness and fade resistance, cleanability, flame resistance, comfort, cost.
- [ ] FB2 Natural fibres: cotton (absorbent, washable, cool, creases, shrinks unless pre-shrunk); linen (strong, absorbent, cool, creases); wool (warm, resilient, insulating, may shrink or felt); silk (soft, lustrous, drapes well, delicate, expensive). Man-made: polyester (strong, crease resistant, quick drying, often blended with cotton); nylon (very strong, abrasion resistant: carpets, upholstery); acrylic (wool-like, takes bright colours, resists fading). Velvet is a pile fabric, made from several fibres, not a man-made fibre. Blends combine the advantages of each fibre.
- [ ] FB3 Woven fabrics are stable and strong; knitted fabrics stretch and recover. Upholstery fabric: durable, comfortable, resilient, stain resistant, hard-wearing, fire resistant.

## 3. Traps

- Do not use: "single glazing conducts heat poorly", because it is the wrong way round: glass itself is a poor conductor, but a single thin pane loses heat quickly; write "single glazing loses heat easily; double glazing traps a layer of air or argon and reduces heat loss".
- Thermoplastic means "softens with heat" and thermosetting means "sets hard and stays hard". Students reverse these. Thermosetting plastics withstand high temperatures; thermoplastics do not.
- Chemical resistance is the opposite way round for the two groups: thermoplastics are damaged by solvents but not by acids and alkalis; thermosetting plastics by strong acids and alkalis but not by solvents.
- Bakelite is itself a phenol-formaldehyde resin; list it as the scheme does (a separate example with its own uses) and do not claim it is chemically different from phenol formaldehyde.
- PTFE is a fluorocarbon thermoplastic; the heat-resistant non-stick pan coating is thermoplastic in the scheme even though it tolerates cooking heat.
- Hardwood and softwood describe the type of tree, not how hard the wood is. Do not say all hardwoods are harder than all softwoods; say hardwoods are usually denser and more durable.
- MDF and chipboard are manufactured boards, not solid wood; do not offer them as the "one solid wood" in an answer.
- Do not use: the Fire Safety (Domestic Furniture) Order or its label as a source for upholstery fabric, because it was revoked in July 2026; write only "fire-resistant fabric" (see the furniture and soft-furnishings subsection for the consumer-protection point).
- Fibre origins, spinning, weaving and knitting methods, fabric finishes and care labels belong to the textiles chapters; give here only the household properties and uses.
- Floor and wall products made from these materials (vinyl floors, wood flooring, tiles, laminate) are in the subsection on floors, walls, furniture and soft furnishings. Recycling and plastic waste belong to the environmental-issues chapter; one phrase on recyclability is enough.
- Do not state prices or carbon figures for any material.

## 4. What exams ask

- Differentiate between thermoplastic and thermosetting plastics, referring to properties, examples and uses (2015H, 2005H). Expected: two properties of each type from PL2 and PL3, and two named examples with a use each. This is the most reliably repeated item.
- Describe one solid wood used in the home: type, properties, uses (2009H). Expected: name one wood (e.g. oak or beech), say it is a hardwood, give durable, resistant to decay and warping, expensive, then at least two uses from WD1.
- Not yet asked directly but possible at Higher Level: criteria for selecting a material for a stated use (e.g. for a kitchen worktop, bathroom window or child's furniture); properties and uses of glass (toughened, laminated, double glazing); a metal for a given fitting; a fabric for upholstery or curtains.
- Often these items are combined with a room context: justify a material choice by durability, cost, safety, ease of cleaning, insulation and environmental impact.
