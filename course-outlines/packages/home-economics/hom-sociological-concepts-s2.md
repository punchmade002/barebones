## 1. Must cover

Status (one block)
- [ ] ST1 A status is a person's position in society or in a group, and the standing that goes with it. One person holds several statuses at once (daughter, student, goalkeeper, citizen).
- [ ] ST2 Ascribed status: fixed at birth or given without choice (sex, age, ethnic origin, family background, being born into a royal or titled family). Achieved status: earned through effort, choice or ability (a degree, a job title, being a parent or a spouse, a sporting medal).
- [ ] ST3 Master status: a status that outweighs the others in how people see you (for example, being a prisoner or a celebrity). Optional extra.
- [ ] ST4 Status can be based on occupation, education, wealth, family background or achievement, and in a society that rewards achievement a person can raise their status; link to mobility.
- [ ] ST5 Open societies allocate status mostly by achievement; closed societies mostly by birth. Optional extra.

Role (one block)
- [ ] R1 A role is the pattern of behaviour expected of a person who holds a status: the rights, duties and expectations that go with it. Marking-scheme wording: the behaviour considered appropriate for a person according to their position in society; examples: teacher, student, adult, child, parent.
- [ ] R2 Status and role go together: status is the position, role is what you do and are expected to do in it. A teacher (status) is expected to prepare lessons, mark work and treat pupils fairly (role).
- [ ] R3 A person plays many roles at once (mother, employee, daughter, club member) and roles change through the life cycle (child, adolescent, adult, parent, grandparent, retired person) and over time (gender roles within the family). Roles are learned through socialisation.
- [ ] R4 Role conflict: the expectations of one role clash with those of another (a sick child and a work deadline; being a loyal friend and an honest employee). Marking-scheme sense: the expectations of a person in one role clash with what is expected in another role.
- [ ] R5 Role overload (role strain): a person tries to play too many roles at one time and cannot meet all the demands, which reduces leisure time and increases stress (for example, a working parent who also cares for an older relative). Marking-scheme sense: attempting to play several roles at once.
- [ ] R6 Role models: people whose behaviour others copy (parents, teachers, sports stars). Optional extra, one line.

Socio-economic groupings (one block)
- [ ] G1 A socio-economic group (social class) is a category of people with a similar economic and social position, defined mainly by occupation, and linked to income, education, wealth and lifestyle. Marking-scheme sense: classification by economic or social position into strata.
- [ ] G2 Social stratification: society is arranged in layers, a hierarchy of unequal ranks. A socio-economic scheme is one way of describing the layers.
- [ ] G3 Irish example: the Central Statistics Office (CSO) groups the population by the occupation of each person into social classes for the census. The groups run from professional workers, managerial and technical, non-manual, skilled manual, semi-skilled to unskilled (with a further group for all others gainfully occupied and unknown). Education is not used to assign a person to a class; occupation is.
- [ ] G4 Examples of occupations: doctor or solicitor (professional); manager or technician (managerial and technical); electrician or carpenter (skilled manual); factory worker (semi-skilled); labourer (unskilled). Offer one example per group at most, and say "for example".
- [ ] G5 Socio-economic group affects life chances: educational attainment, health, housing, income, and access to opportunities. Exams link it to equality of opportunity in education (past questions on how socio-economic status affects equality of opportunity) and to housing choices and household expenditure.
- [ ] G6 Factors that determine socio-economic group: occupation, income, education level, wealth, housing and family background.

Social mobility (one block)
- [ ] M1 Social mobility is movement of a person or family between socio-economic groups (up or down). Marking-scheme sense: movement between socio-economic groups, enabled by education, income and occupation.
- [ ] M2 Upward mobility (a move to a higher group: a labourer's child becomes a teacher) and downward mobility (a move to a lower group: loss of a job and no work at the same level).
- [ ] M3 Intergenerational mobility (comparing a child's position with their parents') and intragenerational mobility (a change within one person's career, for example from apprentice to manager). Optional extras.
- [ ] M4 Structural mobility (the occupational structure itself changes, for example more professional and service jobs and fewer farm jobs, so many people move) is an optional extra, one line.
- [ ] M5 What helps upward mobility: education and qualifications, training and skills, promotion in a job, a good income, free second-level education and access to third level (grants, free fees schemes), equality legislation, ambition and support from family. What blocks it: low family income, poor access to education, early school leaving, discrimination, unemployment and living in an area with few jobs.
- [ ] M6 Open societies, where status is achieved, allow more social mobility than closed societies, where status is ascribed. Optional extra.
- [ ] M7 Education is the main route to upward mobility in Ireland; link to the role of education in the subsection on social institutions (one clause only).

## 3. Traps

- Role versus status: status is the position; role is the behaviour expected. Do not use them as synonyms.
- Role conflict versus role overload: conflict is two roles making incompatible demands (a clash); overload is too many roles to cope with (too much). A question that asks for both needs both separately; the family-chapter exam questions credit each point separately.
- Ascribed versus achieved: being a parent or spouse is usually treated as achieved; sex, age and ethnicity are ascribed. "Social class at birth" is ascribed, but class in adulthood is mostly achieved through occupation.
- Do not use: the claim that socio-economic groups are "the most common measure" of stratification, because this is an unsourced generalisation; say only that a socio-economic scheme is one way of describing social layers.
- Do not use: any claim that the CSO scheme includes education, because the CSO states that education is not used to assign class.
- Do not use: any census percentage, income band or earnings figure for a socio-economic group, because the figures change.
- "Social class" and "socio-economic group" are used almost interchangeably in Irish textbooks; do not insist on a distinction in the notes, but note that the CSO uses both a "social class" and a separate "socio-economic group" classification (the second has more groups, such as farmers and employers); the notes should describe the six-group social class scheme as an example only.
- Social mobility does not mean geographical mobility (moving house or country). Say so in one clause.
- Do not describe Ireland as having no class system or as a rigid class society; keep to "life chances differ".
- Belongs to a neighbouring subsection: society, culture, norms, values (the first subsection of the chapter); family and kin groups and socialisation (groups and socialisation); education as an institution (social institutions); causes of change, such as women entering paid work (social change); changing family roles, role conflict in dual-earner families and gender issues in roles (the chapter on the family as a caring unit); poverty, deprivation and unemployment (the social studies elective on poverty and unemployment).

## 4. What exams ask

- Explain-the-terms questions: role (2013H) as the behaviour expected of someone in a position, with an example such as teacher or parent; socio-economic group (2019H) as a category of people by economic and social position, with occupation, income or education as the basis; social mobility (2019H) as movement between groups, with education and occupation as the means. Each is worth about 3 marks: definition plus an example or an enabling factor.
- Role overload and role conflict in a dual-earner family question (2016, 2021H, 2023H and a later year): the expected answers are the definitions above with family examples, but that application belongs to the family chapter; here the notes need the definitions and one example each.
- Socio-economic status and equality of opportunity in education: higher-status families have more resources and expectations, lower-status families face costs for books, uniforms and trips, higher early school leaving; State supports (grants, free schemes, DEIS) narrow the gap. This is a link to the education institution, so only a one-line bridge here.
- Socio-economic factors in housing choices and household expenditure: income, family size, stage of the life cycle, occupation. One-line bridge.
- A useful exam tip: always give an example from everyday life, because a bare definition gets half the marks.
