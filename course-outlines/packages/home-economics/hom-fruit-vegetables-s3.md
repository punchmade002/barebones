# Package: hom-fruit-vegetables-s3 — Preparation and cooking

- **loId:** `hom-fruit-vegetables-s3` · **chapterId:** `hom-fruit-vegetables` · **syllabusRef:** 1.3.2
- **Syllabus scope:** the effects of preparation and cooking on nutrients, colour, texture and flavour; guidelines that retain nutrients. No [HL] marks on this subsection (caramelisation and dextrinisation are Higher Level in the carbohydrates chapter, not here).
- **Also examined (outline):** 2026H, 2021O, 2019H, 2016O, 2012O, 2011O, 2008O, 2005O. In the papers this is the "guidelines for preparing and cooking vegetables (or fruit) to retain nutrients" question, set almost every second year, plus "outline the effects of cooking on vegetables" (2021O).
- **Prepared by:** a higher model, 2026-11-23. This is the most predictable question in the chapter: examiners give 4 to 12 marks for three or four guidelines, each worth a mark or two for the guideline and a mark for the reason. The notes should teach every guideline as "do this because that" so that students can write the reason. The marking-scheme guidelines are the Must-cover core; the colour and texture science supports the "effects" question.

## 1. Must cover

Why vegetables lose nutrients (one block; assumed knowledge from the vitamins chapter)
- [ ] W0 (assumed) Vitamin C and the B vitamins (thiamine, riboflavin, folate) are water-soluble and are destroyed by heat and oxidised by air; they leach into water. Vitamin C is the least stable and the one to protect. Carotene (fat-soluble) is stable to heat and is not lost into water. Minerals dissolve in water too.
- [ ] W1 The three ways nutrients are lost in preparation and cooking: dissolving in water (soaking, boiling), oxidation (air, cut surfaces, enzymes), and heat (long cooking). Each guideline below prevents one of them.

Preparation guidelines (one block)
- [ ] P1 Use fresh vegetables, stored for the shortest time possible (vitamin C falls on storage; see the Selection, storage and spoilage subsection for storage).
- [ ] P2 Eat raw where possible (salads, raw fruit): no cooking losses.
- [ ] P3 Prepare immediately before cooking, because cut surfaces are exposed to air and enzymes and vitamin C is oxidised; do not leave prepared vegetables standing.
- [ ] P4 Use a sharp knife and avoid chopping into small pieces: a blunt knife crushes cells and releases oxidase, and small pieces expose more surface to air and water; cut into larger pieces.
- [ ] P5 Peel thinly, scrape, or leave edible skins on (potatoes, carrots, apples), because the nutrients and fibre are close to the skin.
- [ ] P6 Wash before cutting, never after, and do not steep or soak in water: water-soluble vitamins and minerals dissolve.
- [ ] P7 Remove only inedible or damaged parts; keep outer green leaves of greens if edible (more vitamin C and carotene).

Cooking guidelines (one block)
- [ ] K1 Cook for the shortest time that makes them tender but slightly crisp ("al dente"): overcooking destroys vitamins and spoils texture and flavour.
- [ ] K2 Boiling: use the minimum amount of water, bring the water to the boil before adding the vegetables, and use a tight-fitting lid: less water means less dissolving and the lid keeps steam in and shortens cooking time.
- [ ] K3 Prefer steaming, pressure cooking, microwaving or stir-frying to boiling: less contact with water (steaming and microwaving), a shorter time (pressure cooking, microwaving, stir-frying). In stir-frying, small pieces cooked quickly in a little oil keep colour and crispness, and fat-soluble vitamins (A, carotene, K) are absorbed better with fat.
- [ ] K4 Do not add bicarbonate of soda (bread soda) to keep greens green: it destroys vitamin C and thiamine and makes the texture mushy.
- [ ] K5 Avoid copper and brass pans, which speed the destruction of vitamin C.
- [ ] K6 Cook frozen vegetables from frozen (no thawing), to avoid loss of vitamins in the drip.
- [ ] K7 Serve immediately and do not keep hot for long (vitamin C falls on holding), and use the cooking liquid in soups, sauces and gravy so the dissolved vitamins and minerals are eaten.

Effects of cooking: nutrients, colour, texture, flavour (one block each, short)
- [ ] N1 Nutrients: heat and water destroy or remove vitamin C and the B vitamins; minerals dissolve into cooking water; carotene and fibre are kept; cooking makes some nutrients more available (carotene is released from cell walls, and starch is cooked).
- [ ] N2 Digestibility and safety: cooking softens cellulose and pectin in the cell walls, gelatinises starch (in potatoes and pulses, granules swell and absorb water), makes food easier to digest, and destroys many micro-organisms and denatures enzymes.
- [ ] N3 Colour, green vegetables: chlorophyll turns olive-green or brown (pheophytin) when overcooked or cooked in acid; alkali (bicarbonate) keeps the green but is harmful to vitamins.- [ ] N4 Colour, orange, yellow and red: carotenoid pigments are stable in heat, so carrots, peppers and tomatoes keep their colour.
- [ ] N5 Colour, red cabbage and beetroot: red cabbage contains anthocyanin pigments: red in acid, blue or purple in alkaline conditions, so vinegar or apple helps keep red cabbage red when cooked.
- [ ] N6 Texture: softens as cell walls (cellulose, pectin) break down and water is lost or taken up; overcooked vegetables become mushy; acid keeps fruit and vegetables firm; vegetables cooked briefly keep a crisp texture.
- [ ] N7 Flavour: volatile flavour compounds and water-soluble flavour pass into the cooking water or escape in steam, so long boiling loses flavour; overcooked cabbage and other brassicas release sulphur compounds (strong smell); roasting or frying brings out sweetness and browning, as natural sugars brown in dry heat (caramelisation, taught in the cooking-methods chapter).

Blanching (one block)
- [ ] B1 Blanching means putting prepared vegetables into boiling water for a short time (a minute or a few) and then cooling quickly in cold water.
- [ ] B2 Reasons: it inactivates (denatures) enzymes, fixes colour, removes some micro-organisms and air, and is required before home or commercial freezing. Some water-soluble vitamins are lost, which is a necessary cost.

Preparing and cooking fruit (one block; examined 2008O as "three guidelines")
- [ ] FR1 Eat fruit raw wherever possible, with the skin on if edible (apples, pears), because vitamin C and fibre are kept; wash rather than peel.
- [ ] FR2 Prepare just before cooking or eating, using a sharp knife; peel thinly.
- [ ] FR3 Stew in as little water as possible, with a lid, for the shortest time, and use the juice; add sugar at the start of stewing, because it helps the fruit keep its shape (assumed practical point).
- [ ] FR4 Avoid copper pans; do not overcook; serve promptly.
- [ ] FR5 Cut apples, pears and bananas brown through enzymic browning; lemon juice or other acid, a sugar syrup or cold water slows browning; a fruit salad is dressed with lemon or orange juice.

## 3. Traps

- Do not use: cooking greens with the lid off for the first minutes, because it conflicts with the tight-fitting lid advice in K2 and the marking scheme.
- Wash before cutting, not after cutting; the old note says so and it is correct.
- Large pieces lose less than small pieces: do not say "chop finely to cook faster" as a nutrient tip.
- Carotene is not lost into water; vitamin C is. Don't say "all vitamins are lost in water".
- Do not use: "red cabbage anthocyanin pigments turn blue-green in alkaline conditions" (the old note), because anthocyanins turn blue or purple in mild alkali; use "blue or purple".
- Do not use: "starch gelatinisation" as a definition to be taught in full here, because it belongs to the carbohydrates chapter; one clause on potatoes and pulses softening only.
- Do not use: "caramelisation improves flavour in roasted onions and carrots" as an explanation to be taught in full, because caramelisation is Higher Level carbohydrate chemistry (cooking-methods chapter); name the browning in one clause with no mechanism.
- Do not use: "microwaving retains much of the vitamin C" without the conditions, because microwaving only retains vitamin C when used with little water and a short time; say so.
- Do not use: "blanching reduces the number of micro-organisms" as a main reason, because the schemes credit enzyme inactivation; micro-organism reduction is a small extra.
- Do not use: "stainless steel or enamel pans should be used instead" as a rule that stainless is better for all nutrients; say only that copper and brass pans speed up vitamin C loss.
- Neighbouring subsections: how fruit and vegetables are stored before cooking, and why produce spoils, is in Selection, storage and spoilage; freezing, canning and drying on a commercial scale is in Processing. The physical principles of conduction, convection and radiation, choice of cooking method for other foods, and enzymic and non-enzymic browning chemistry are in the cooking-methods chapter; here give only the application to fruit and vegetables. Home freezing (including blanching for freezing) and jam-making are in the food-preservation chapter.
- Pulses need soaking and long cooking; do not teach this as a nutrient-retention guideline.

## 4. What exams ask

- "Outline four guidelines when preparing and cooking vegetables to maximise nutrients" (12 marks, Higher; also Ordinary, three guidelines at 4 marks each, with one reference to preparation and one to cooking): fresh and used soon; prepare just before cooking; sharp knife; large pieces; peel thinly or leave skins on; wash before cutting; no soaking; minimum water and tight lid; short time to al dente; steam, pressure cook, microwave or stir-fry; no bicarbonate; no copper pans; keep cooking liquid; serve at once.
- The same for fruit (6 marks, Ordinary, three guidelines).
- "Compile a set of guidelines for cooking vegetables to maintain nutritive value" (10 marks, Higher 2019).
- "Outline the effects of cooking on vegetables" (9 marks, Ordinary 2021): starch gelatinises, cellulose softens, microbes destroyed, enzymes denatured, colour, flavour and texture change, water-soluble vitamins lost, vitamin C destroyed, digestibility improved.
- "List two guidelines to reduce vitamin loss when preparing vegetables" (4 marks): sharp knife, peel thinly, no soaking, prepare just before use.
- Short items on blanching, the effect of bread soda, and why copper pans are avoided.
- Expect each answer to explain why a guideline works, not just list it.
