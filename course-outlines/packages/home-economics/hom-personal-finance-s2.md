# Package: hom-personal-finance-s2 — Household expenditure and budgeting

- **loId:** `hom-personal-finance-s2` · **chapterId:** `hom-personal-finance` · **syllabusRef:** 2.1.3
- **Syllabus scope (outline):** patterns of household expenditure at varying levels of income; essential and discretionary expenditure; planning personal and family budgets, including credit. Settings: case studies of sample budgets for families on varying incomes.
- **Also examined (outline):** a budget plan for a stated household and net income (2025O, 2021O, 2015H, 2004H); MABS (2026H, 2025O, 2019H, 2010H).
- **Higher Level only:** nothing marked [HL]. Do not mark anything HL.
- **Prepared by:** a higher model, 2026-11-23. Common to both levels. The budget is always built on net income. Sample budgets use made-up incomes; the notes must say they are samples.

## 1. Must cover

Patterns of household expenditure
- [ ] P1 Expenditure is the money a household spends. Income sets the limit: spending that exceeds income leads to debt, so spending must be matched to income.
- [ ] P2 Income level: low-income households spend most of their income on essentials (housing, food, heat) and a larger share on food than richer households; they have little or no discretionary spending or saving. Higher-income households spend more in total and a bigger share on discretionary items (leisure, holidays, eating out), and can save and pay for credit.
- [ ] P3 Family size and composition (number of children, one- or two-parent household).
- [ ] P4 Stage of the family life cycle: young children raise spending on food, clothing, childcare and schooling; teenagers cost more for food, clothing and education; once children are independent and at retirement, spending falls and shifts to health, leisure and heating.
- [ ] P5 Employment pattern: working outside the home adds travel, convenience food and childcare costs; shift work changes meal and sleep patterns and transport costs.
- [ ] P6 Location (urban or rural): housing, transport and heating costs differ (rural households depend more on cars; urban rents and mortgages are higher).
- [ ] P7 Other influences: socio-economic group and the sex of the earner, personal values and priorities, health needs or special needs, advertising and peer pressure. One line each; Higher Level answers give a reason and a consequence.

Essential and discretionary expenditure
- [ ] E1 Essential expenditure: spending on necessities needed to manage family life, such as housing (rent or mortgage), food, heating and electricity, transport, clothing, insurance, medical costs, loan repayments, household bills. Discretionary expenditure: spending on non-essentials made after essentials and savings, such as holidays, entertainment, alcohol, cosmetics, eating out, gym membership, streaming.
- [ ] E2 Essential spending is fixed (same amount at regular times: rent, mortgage, insurance, phone), or irregular/variable (different amounts or times: clothing, doctor's bills, school costs, car repairs), and some is seasonal (heating, Christmas, back to school).
- [ ] E3 Some spending is a choice of priority: the same item can be essential for one household and discretionary for another (a car for a rural family compared with a city family).
- [ ] E4 Credit repayments are fixed commitments in the budget; credit used for essentials adds to future spending.

Planning a personal or family budget
- [ ] B1 A budget is a plan that sets expected net income against planned spending for a period (week, month or year).
- [ ] B2 Steps: 1 list all net income (wages, welfare, maintenance, other); 2 list all expenditure: fixed, irregular and discretionary, and credit repayments; 3 choose the time period; 4 put essentials first; 5 include savings (short- and long-term, emergency fund) and some personal spending; 6 allow for annual events such as Christmas and for changes in circumstances; 7 keep bills and receipts and record actual spending for a month to find overspending; 8 compare income with expenditure and adjust; 9 review regularly.
- [ ] B3 A surplus (income greater than spending) can be saved; a deficit (spending greater than income) means cutting discretionary spending, raising income or seeking advice, rather than borrowing.
- [ ] B4 Advantages of budgeting: shows where money goes, identifies overspending, avoids impulse buying and unplanned debt, reduces reliance on credit, ensures bills are paid, reduces stress and worry, gives security, provides for emergencies and savings, helps reach goals, teaches money-management skills, supports a good credit record.
- [ ] B5 Ways to reduce expenditure: shopping list and sticking to it, own-brand goods, comparing prices, buying second-hand or in charity shops, using sales, buying in season, cooking from scratch, cheaper cuts, cutting energy use, switching supplier, cancelling unused subscriptions.
- [ ] B6 Sample budget plan: for a family of two adults and two children, a weekly guide often split by percentages of net income: housing 25%, food 25%, household bills 15%, education 10%, and clothing, leisure, savings, health and discretionary spending 5% each. Worked sample: net income 900 euro a week gives 225, 225, 135, 90 and five lots of 45. A second sample: 800 euro gives 200, 200, 120, 80 and five lots of 40. Each allocation comes with a reason (housing: the largest fixed cost; food: needs rise with growing children; education: school costs; savings: emergencies). The totals must add to the net income.
- [ ] B7 Fitting the plan to different incomes: a lower income cannot cover the same percentages, so essentials come first and discretionary and savings shrink; a higher income allows more savings.

MABS
- [ ] M1 The Money Advice and Budgeting Service: a free, confidential, independent service funded by the State through the Citizens Information Board, for people in debt or at risk of debt.
- [ ] M2 What it does: helps a household work out its income and spending, draws up a realistic budget and repayment plan, negotiates with lenders (including moneylenders and mortgage lenders) for the client, advises on mortgage arrears, personal insolvency and income maximisation (claiming entitlements), advises on financial products and interest rates, and points to cheaper credit such as credit unions. It teaches money-management skills.

## 3. Traps

- Essential versus discretionary depends on the household: do not classify a car as always essential or always a luxury.
- A budget starts from net income, never gross.
- The percentages are a guide for a given sample. Do not present 25/25/15/10/5 as a rule that applies to every family, and do not state it for a different income without saying it is a sample.
- A budget is a plan; a record of actual spending is a check on it. Both are needed.
- Do not say MABS lends money or pays debts for the client. It advises, helps budget and negotiates.
- Credit and credit types belong to the Methods of payment and credit subsection; here only that repayments are a fixed commitment. Saving schemes belong to the Saving subsection. The family management framework (inputs, throughputs, outputs) belongs to the Resource management chapter.
- Household income sources and deductions belong to the previous subsection (Household income).
- Do not use: MABS helpline numbers and the number of MABS offices, because they change and sources disagree.
- Do not use: the Abhaile scheme or named mortgage-arrears schemes, because their status is unconfirmed; say only "MABS mortgage arrears service".
- Do not use: the 2010 marking scheme statement about fuel allowance advice as a named MABS service, because it is a dated detail; "income maximisation" covers it.
- Do not use: any claim that low-income families spend a stated percentage on food, because proportions change and no source figure is checked.
- Do not use: euro amounts for wages, rent or bills in the samples other than the two samples in B6, because these change.

## 4. What exams ask

- Differentiate essential and discretionary expenditure with an example of each (6 marks, very common: 2025H, 2021O, 2013H, 2011O). Add the fixed and irregular essential split for the full marks.
- Discuss four advantages of household budgeting (Ordinary Level 20 marks, 2025O, 2021O, 2016O): reduces overspending, avoids debt and impulse buying, provides for emergencies, reduces stress, builds money skills. Each needs a developed sentence, not a list.
- Set out a weekly budget for a stated family and income (20 marks): allocations that add to the net income, in the usual headings with percentage and euro amount; savings and discretionary lines present; at Higher Level a reason for each allocation (2015H) and a note on review.
- Outline three steps in planning a household budget (2018O): estimate net income, list all expenditure, allocate and review.
- Strategies for planning a family budget (Higher Level 20 marks, 2019H, 2010H): list all net income and fixed, irregular and discretionary expenditure, weekly or monthly totals, allow for Christmas and changes, short- and long-term savings, personal spending, review.
- Describe ways a consumer could reduce expenditure (12 marks, 2012O): shopping list, own brand, second-hand, bulk, cheaper cuts, energy saving.
- Discuss how income, family stage and personal values affect household spending (2026H): income sets the total; the life-cycle stage changes needs; values shape priorities.
- Role of MABS (2026H, 2025O, 2019H, 2010H): free, independent, confidential; budgets and repayment schedules; debt advice; negotiating with lenders; mortgage arrears; income maximisation; credit unions and interest rates.
