## 1. Must cover

Historical development (Higher Level only; use that heading suffix; the marking scheme gives six periods, so give six blocks or sub-blocks)
- [ ] H1 Early nineteenth-century rural housing: labourers and small farmers lived in a single-storey thatched cottage, usually two rooms (kitchen and bedroom), with solid stone (or mud) walls, small windows, a half-door opening into the kitchen, and an open turf fire used for cooking and heating. The thatch was reeds, straw or hay. Some cottages had a loft directly under the thatch, reached by a ladder, where children slept. Wealthier farmers lived in two-storey slate-roofed houses with several bedrooms and a parlour. Wealthy landowners lived in large estate houses, with servants' quarters generally in the basement.
- [ ] H2 Nineteenth-century urban housing: Georgian terraces in towns, two, three or four storeys, with basements, high ceilings and prominent doors; the middle classes in small terraced houses in the suburbs; shopkeepers lived over their shops; workers lived in single-storey cottages or, in the towns, crowded tenements.
- [ ] H3 Late nineteenth century: better transport brought a wider variety of building materials; houses were more ornate, with Gothic and Tudor styles; two-storey slate-roofed stone houses were the commonest rural dwelling; in towns the better-off moved to the suburbs and many Georgian houses were divided and let to poorer families.
- [ ] H4 Early twentieth century: detached and semi-detached houses; council housing and housing estates; brick with plastered walls replaced stone and tiles replaced slate; two-storey houses where land was dear; in the countryside, rural electrification (mid-twentieth century) and government housing grants improved homes, and the slate-roofed bungalow gradually replaced the thatched cottage.
- [ ] H5 Late twentieth century: private estates built to a common design, while individual private houses varied between classical and modern designs; high-rise blocks in some cities; the Building Regulations greatly improved standards; new materials and methods such as double glazing, PVC and fibreglass; indoor sanitation improved.
- [ ] H6 Twenty-first century: estates are usually of one style but include single-storey detached, dormer and two-storey semi-detached houses; apartments, townhouses and mixed-density schemes, with gated complexes; old dilapidated buildings and thatched cottages restored; custom-designed houses on private sites; attic conversions with roof windows, extensions, conservatories and sunrooms; home-office space; open-plan living; attention to insulation, glazing, security, accessibility and renewable energy.
- [ ] H7 Diagram (optional, one cue): a thatched cottage in cross-section with the thatched roof, the loft reached by a ladder, the open hearth and the half-door. Keep it to the nineteenth-century cottage only.
- [ ] H8 Why styles changed: materials and transport, rising incomes, population moving from country to town, government housing policy and grants, building regulations, family size, land values and environmental awareness. One line of linking text per period is enough.

Popular housing styles in Ireland today (one block)
- [ ] P1 Bungalow: single-storey; suits older people, people with mobility needs and rural sites; needs a large site; the dormer bungalow adds bedrooms in the roof space.
- [ ] P2 Two-storey detached house: space for a large family and a garden; privacy; costs more to buy and to heat; needs a larger site.
- [ ] P3 Semi-detached and terraced houses: use land efficiently, cost less, lose less heat because walls are shared; less privacy and noise from neighbours; a small garden.
- [ ] P4 Apartments and duplexes: suit first-time buyers, singles, downsizers and city living; low maintenance, communal space and security; limited space and no private garden; management fees.
- [ ] P5 Townhouse: multi-storey on a small urban site; limited outdoor space.
- [ ] P6 Other styles: traditional, neo-Georgian or restored period houses (character and proportion), modern minimalist homes (light and easy to maintain), custom-designed one-off houses, timber-frame and energy-efficient or passive houses, and old cottages kept for holiday and local use.
- [ ] P7 For each style, give a reason for its popularity (2011H asks for three styles with reasons).

Factors influencing choice of style (one block per heading, or one block)
- [ ] F1 Social: family size and stage, the ages of children, special needs or disability, wanting a garden or a home office, wanting to be near family and community, the sense of belonging.
- [ ] F2 Cultural: personal taste and fashion, regional building traditions and local materials (stone, slate) that shape the look of a place, respect for heritage and the character of the area.
- [ ] F3 Economic: income and available money, the price of land, the cost of building and running costs, resale value and investment potential, and whether to build, buy new or buy second-hand.
- [ ] F4 Environmental: aspect (south-facing rooms give light and solar heat), shelter from wind and rain, site slope, energy efficiency (insulation and glazing), fit with the landscape and neighbouring buildings, and planning rules on scale and materials.
- [ ] F5 Exam links: Higher Level questions ask the economic and environmental factors with two developed points each; Ordinary Level questions give photographs of three styles and ask for four factors.

## 3. Traps

- Do not use: a precise year or percentage for rural electrification, because the scheme ran over decades; the marking scheme says "1950s" and the notes should say "mid-twentieth century".
- Do not use: the marking scheme's "triple A rated" or other BER wording in this subsection, because the BER is dealt with in "House design and building standards" and its scale has changed.
- The existing notes mark the historical blocks [HL] correctly, and mark the popular styles and the factors as not [HL]. Keep it so: a blanket "Higher Level only" label on the whole subsection would be wrong.
- Do not describe the thatched cottage as one used by all people in the nineteenth century: farmers and landowners lived in larger two-storey and estate houses.
- Georgian terraces and tenements are urban; thatched cottages and farmhouses are rural; keep the two columns separate, as the marking scheme does.
- Council housing appears in the early twentieth-century block, not the late; social housing schemes today are in "Housing provision".
- Belongs to a neighbouring subsection: orientation, insulation and energy-efficiency design (house design and building standards); planning controls on scale and materials (location, planning and professional services); general housing choice (housing choices); needs of groups (housing provision).

## 4. What exams ask

- "Outline the historical development of housing styles in Ireland" (Higher Level, 15 to 20 marks; 2013, 2019, 2026): five to six developed points, one per period, each with a rural and urban contrast where possible. Examiners credit specific features (half-door, loft, open hearth, basements, tenements, slate replacing thatch, bungalows, estates, double glazing, apartments, restored period houses) rather than general statements.
- Economic and environmental factors affecting style (Higher Level 18 to 24 marks; 2009, 2019, 2023): cost, income, land price, resale value; aspect, shelter, energy efficiency, fit with landscape, planning. Social, cultural and environmental (2004): three headings with two points each.
- Factors that may influence a young person's choice of style from photographs (Ordinary Level 2017, four factors) and comment on social, economic and environmental factors (2011O).
- Identify three styles found in Ireland today and give reasons for their popularity (2011H): bungalow, two-storey detached or semi-detached, apartment or townhouse, with reasons.
- Photograph questions: name the style, give two features, and state who it suits.
- The best answers tie each style to a type of household and each factor to an effect.
