## 1. Must cover

Cream (one block)
- [ ] CR1 Cream is the fat-rich layer of milk, separated from the skim milk. It is mostly fat (high in energy, mostly saturated, with cholesterol), with little protein and lactose.
- [ ] CR2 Vitamins A and D are carried in the fat; it has no vitamin C and no fibre. Calcium and protein are low compared with milk because most of them stay in the skimmed milk.
- [ ] CR3 Dietetic points: a concentrated energy food, so use in small amounts; not suited to low-fat, low-cholesterol or slimming diets; adds richness and flavour; easily digested fat in tiny globules.
- [ ] CR4 Types: single cream (lower fat, about 18% fat, pours, does not whip; for coffee, sauces, desserts); whipping cream (about 35 to 38% fat, whips to a foam); double cream (highest fat, about 48% fat, whips to stiff peaks, pours thick, can be piped, used for fillings, toppings and rich sauces); sour cream (cultured with lactic acid bacteria, tangy; dips, toppings, baked potatoes); also crème fraîche and soured cream as cultured creams, and clotted cream (one mention, optional).
- [ ] CR5 Processing: milk is warmed and separated by a centrifugal separator (spinning, so the lighter fat rises and is drawn off); the cream is standardised to its fat level, then pasteurised (or UHT treated for long life), cooled, packaged and labelled. Cream is homogenised only lightly or not at all if it is to be whipped.
- [ ] CR6 Selecting and storing: choose by the type on the label (fat content) and by the use-by date; check the carton is sealed and cold in the shop; refrigerate at once at about 4 °C; keep covered and away from strong-smelling foods; once opened use within the time on the label; whipped cream keeps only a short time. Long-life (UHT) cream keeps unopened without refrigeration.
- [ ] CR7 Culinary uses: poured over desserts and fruit, whipped as filling and topping (aeration: air trapped in the fat network), in soups and sauces, in coffee, in ice cream, in mashed potato, in baking and dishes such as trifle; cream stirred into hot dishes is added at the end.

Butter (one block)
- [ ] B1 Production: cream is pasteurised, cooled and ripened (soured by a lactic culture for flavour in many butters), then churned (agitated). Churning breaks the fat-globule membranes so the fat clumps together into butter grains and separates from the liquid buttermilk; the butter is washed, salted if wanted, worked (kneaded) to remove excess water, then shaped, wrapped and labelled.
- [ ] B2 Composition: about 80% fat, mostly saturated, with water (up to about 16%) and traces of milk solids; high in energy and cholesterol; vitamins A and D in the fat (and carotene, which gives the yellow colour); negligible protein, calcium and carbohydrate; no vitamin C, no fibre. Salted butter also supplies sodium.
- [ ] B3 Dietetic points: concentrated energy and vitamins A and D; use in small amounts; for people cutting saturated fat, cholesterol or kilocalories, choose reduced-fat spreads or use less; unsalted butter for those limiting salt. Butter is a pleasing flavour and can be spread straight from the fridge in spreadable forms.
- [ ] B4 Types: salted, unsalted (lightly salted), spreadable butter (blended with a vegetable oil to soften it), reduced-fat dairy spreads, flavoured butters (garlic), cultured butter, and butter in portion packs.
- [ ] B5 Culinary uses: spreading; frying and sautéing (flavour; burns at lower heat than oils); making sauces (roux, white sauce, hollandaise, melted butter sauces); rubbed in for shortcrust pastry and scones (shortening); creaming with sugar for cakes (aeration); icings and fillings; adding flavour and shine to vegetables.
- [ ] B6 Selecting and storing: choose intact, clean wrappers, a clean shop, use-by date, the type needed (salted or unsalted) and the best value; refrigerate, covered or in its wrapper, away from strong odours (butter absorbs smells easily); it may be frozen for long storage.

Yoghurt (one block)
- [ ] Y1 Yoghurt is milk fermented by lactic acid bacteria: lactose is changed to lactic acid, which gives the sharp taste, sets the milk and preserves it.
- [ ] Y2 Nutritive value (schemes 2025O): HBV protein (growth and repair; slightly more concentrated than milk because milk solids are often added); saturated fat in full-fat yoghurts, less in low-fat; lactose and often added sugars for energy; vitamins A, D and B group (B2, B12); calcium and phosphorus (bones and teeth); traces of minerals such as potassium; about 87% water; lacks vitamin C and iron.
- [ ] Y3 Dietetic value: suits all ages; easily digested (lactic acid, and part of the lactose already broken down, so many people with lactose intolerance can take it); versatile; can be fortified; an inexpensive, convenient snack; a low-fat cream substitute; should be balanced with starch, fibre and iron foods; check sugar content and fat on the label.
- [ ] Y4 Manufacture (2024H, 2006H): milk is homogenised, then pasteurised at 80 to 90 °C, cooled to about 37 °C; a culture (Lactobacillus bulgaricus, usually with Streptococcus thermophilus) is added; incubated for 6 to 8 hours, during which lactose changes to lactic acid and the milk proteins coagulate and thicken; cooled to about 5 °C to stop the fermentation; flavours, fruit, nuts, sweeteners, colours or stabilisers may be added; weighed, packaged (plastic tubs or pots with lids, or cartons, sealed) and labelled.
- [ ] Y5 Packaging and labelling for yoghurt: sealed plastic pots with foil or plastic lids; label has name and type, brand, ingredients, quantity, nutrition information, allergens (milk, and any nuts), use-by date and storage (refrigerate); the legal basis is Regulation (EU) No 1169/2011 as given effect by the European Union (Provision of Food Information to Consumers) Regulations 2014.
- [ ] Y6 Set yoghurt (incubated in the pot, firm) and stirred or drinking yoghurt (set in a vat, then stirred and poured into pots); Greek-style yoghurt is strained and thicker; plain (natural) yoghurt has no sugar added.
- [ ] Y7 Bio-yoghurt (2008O): contains added live cultures such as Lactobacillus acidophilus and bifidobacteria, added after pasteurisation because heat would kill them. Benefit to the consumer: aids digestion and helps balance the bacteria in the gut. Probiotic yoghurts carry live bacteria intended to help the gut; they must be refrigerated to stay alive.
- [ ] Y8 Selecting and storing: buy from a clean shop, check use-by date and that the lid is sealed and the pot undamaged; look at the ingredients list for sugar, fruit and allergens; check origin and value; refrigerate promptly at about 4 °C; rotate stock (older first); reseal with the lid and use within the time on the label.
- [ ] Y9 Culinary uses: eaten alone or as a drink; in desserts; in salads and dressings; in smoothies; as a topping or garnish; on cereal; as a dip (tzatziki or raita); in curries and marinades; in baking; as a low-fat substitute for cream or mayonnaise.

Dairy industry and consumer trends (one block)
- [ ] T1 Responses by the dairy industry (2021H, 2025O, 2006H): milk: low-fat, semi-skimmed and skimmed, fortified, lactose-free, varied sizes; cheese: low-fat, grated, sliced, cubed, snack and flavoured, family and resealable packs, individually wrapped portions; yoghurt: flavours, functional and probiotic (plant stanols, omega-3), drinking, set, stirred, frozen, Greek, high-protein, lactose-free, sugar-free, unsweetened, plant-based alternatives; butter: spreadable, low-fat spreads, unsalted, flavoured, sustainable packs; cream: single, double, sour, whipped, flavoured, varied sizes.
- [ ] T2 Reasons for the trends: growing nutritional awareness and healthy eating guidelines; obesity and heart disease concerns; demand for convenience; busy lifestyles; lactose intolerance and allergy; interest in functional foods; supermarket competition; sustainability and packaging concern.

## 3. Traps

- Do not use: "low-fat butter" as a literal butter type, because a product with less than about 80% fat cannot be sold as butter; say "reduced-fat spread" or "dairy spread" (a 2021H scheme uses "low-fat butter" loosely).
- Do not use: brand names such as Benecol, Flora ProActiv, Danone or Müller, because brands change and some products differ (plant stanols are in Benecol, plant sterols in other spreads); say "plant stanol or sterol products".
- Do not use: claims that probiotic yoghurt prevents or treats any illness, because only "aids digestion" is in the schemes and health claims are tightly regulated.
- Yoghurt is made from milk, so its nutrients come from milk; do not credit yoghurt with extra nutrients unless added (fortified, fruit).
- The culture is added after pasteurisation (the heat would kill it); the cooling to about 37 °C is to let the culture work. Do not say yoghurt is pasteurised after culture is added.
- Churning separates butter from buttermilk; separation (centrifugal) separates cream from skimmed milk. Do not mix the two.
- Sour cream and crème fraîche are cultured; soured cream is not made by curdling milk with lemon in the dairy industry.
- Cream and cream substitutes: yoghurt may replace cream in cooking but it curdles if boiled.
- Cheese (starter, rennet, curds and whey) belongs to the cheese subsection; the heat treatments of milk belong to the milk-processing subsection; the general rules on food labelling belong to the food legislation chapter.

## 4. What exams ask

- Yoghurt is the most examined item here. Ordinary Level: nutritive and dietetic value of yoghurt, with culinary uses, buying and storing, and three reasons for the variety of yoghurts and drinks (nutrition awareness, lactose-free, probiotic, plant stanol, high-protein, sugar-free, omega-3, convenience).
- Higher Level: the stages of manufacture of yoghurt with production, packaging and labelling (the culture named; temperature of 80 to 90 °C pasteurisation, 37 °C, 6 to 8 hours and 5 °C all earn marks); what happens in incubation (lactose to lactic acid, protein coagulates).
- Short items: the benefit of acidophilus in bio-yoghurt (aids digestion); cheese and yoghurt are made with bacteria (not yeast).
- Choosing a yoghurt from a table for an obese person or a teenager (2021O): lowest energy, saturated fat and salt, with protein for growth; compare per 100 g.
- Measures by the dairy industry to meet trends (2021H, 12 marks): at least three products with a trend each; link to a reason.
- Butter and cream are asked in part answers: nutritive value (high in saturated fat and cholesterol, A and D, no vitamin C), culinary uses (pastry, creaming, sauces, whipping), selection and storage.
