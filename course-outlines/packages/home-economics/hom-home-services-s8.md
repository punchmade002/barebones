## 1. Must cover

Properties of light and their application [HL]
- [ ] L1 [HL] Light is a form of energy that travels in straight lines as waves; it is made of the colours of the spectrum (white light splits into colours). Transparent materials let it through, translucent materials scatter it, opaque materials block it.
- [ ] L2 [HL] Reflection: light bounces off shiny surfaces (mirrors, glazed tiles, gloss paint, white ceilings), so a room looks brighter and larger. Application: mirrors, pale walls and reflective finishes, fitting reflectors.
- [ ] L3 [HL] Refraction: light bends as it passes into glass or another transparent material. Application: glass bricks, cut crystal, thick or ridged glass spread light and give decorative effects.
- [ ] L4 [HL] Diffusion (scattering): translucent materials scatter light so it is softer and glare is reduced. Application: frosted glass, net curtains, fabric and opal lampshades.
- [ ] L5 [HL] Absorption: dark, matt surfaces take in light, so the room looks darker and smaller and needs more light; pale surfaces absorb less. Application: choose wall, ceiling and floor colour with the light level in mind; dark colours for a cosy mood.
- [ ] L6 [HL] Dispersion: a prism or crystal splits white light into its component colours. Application: crystal chandeliers.
- [ ] L7 [HL] Colour of light: warm (yellowish) and cool (bluish) light; coloured surfaces and shades change how colours look; this is described by the lamp's colour temperature.

Principles for planning a lighting system
- [ ] P1 Plan at the design or building stage so that outlets, switches, wiring and fittings are in the right places and the cost is lower.
- [ ] P2 Function of the room: a kitchen and study need bright task lighting; a living room needs softer, flexible light; a bedroom needs general and bedside light; bathrooms need safe, moisture-protected fittings. Think about who uses the room (children, elderly, reading, homework).
- [ ] P3 Types of lighting: general (ambient) lighting for the whole room; task lighting for an activity (reading, cooking, desk); accent lighting to highlight a picture, feature or focal point. Combine all three; add a focal point for atmosphere.
- [ ] P4 Natural light: window size, position and aspect of the room (south-facing gets most); maximise it with large windows, skylights, glass bricks, pale colours, clear glass, no net curtains or heavy blinds, cut back trees, mirrors. Balance natural and artificial light; it saves electricity and helps wellbeing.
- [ ] P5 Colour and size of the room: pale walls and ceiling reflect light; dark ones absorb; size and shape of the room decide the number of fittings.
- [ ] P6 Avoid glare and shadows: shades, indirect light, fittings out of the line of sight (TV and screens), several light sources.
- [ ] P7 Flexibility and mood: dimmers, movable lamps, switching in groups, two-way switches on stairs.
- [ ] P8 Safety: correct voltage and fuse; fittings installed by an electrician; enough space between bulb and shade; no flexes trailing; bathroom fittings suited to the zone.
- [ ] P9 Maintenance and convenience: fittings easy to clean; bulbs easy to reach and replace; switches in sensible places.
- [ ] P10 Energy efficiency and cost: efficient lamps (LED), sensors, timers; a choice of fittings to suit the décor and budget.

Fittings (for examples)
- [ ] F1 Central or general: ceiling pendant, semi-flush and flush fittings, chandelier, rise-and-fall light, recessed downlights, track and spotlights.
- [ ] F2 Wall: wall brackets, picture lights, uplighters and downlighters, swivel lights.
- [ ] F3 Task and decorative: table and desk lamps, floor lamps (pivot or flexible stem), under-cabinet and under-counter lights, pelmet lighting.
- [ ] F4 Outdoor: sensor lights, solar lights, column, step and decking lights.

Contemporary lighting developments (outline)
- [ ] D1 LED lighting: bulbs, strips, rope lights and tiny fittings; many colour temperatures; can be dimmable; long life, low heat.
- [ ] D2 Sensor (movement) and timer lighting: switches on when someone is present; useful in halls, stairs, porches and outdoors; saves energy and improves security.
- [ ] D3 Smart lighting: controlled by phone, app or voice; schedules and dimming; some change colour.
- [ ] D4 Solar-powered lights: a small photovoltaic panel charges a battery in daytime for garden and path lights.
- [ ] D5 Colour-changing and LED rope lighting for mood and accent; recessed and under-counter lights for kitchens; low-voltage lighting; fibre-optic lighting.
- [ ] D6 Practical context: collect examples from magazines, brochures and shops; for each note the fitting, the lamp, the type of lighting (general, task, accent), the energy efficiency, safety and suitability for a named room.

Energy-efficient lighting: principles and uses
- [ ] EE1 Filament lamp (for comparison): current heats a tungsten filament until it glows; most of the energy is given off as heat, not light, so it is inefficient. It has been phased out of sale in the EU under efficiency rules.
- [ ] EE2 Compact fluorescent lamp (CFL): a coiled glass tube coated inside with phosphor, containing argon and mercury vapour; current heats electrodes at each end, the mercury vaporises and the gas gives off ultraviolet light, which the phosphor converts into visible light. Uses less energy and lasts longer than a filament lamp; cost more; contains mercury, so must be recycled; slow to reach full brightness; may cause glare without a shade.
- [ ] EE3 LED (light-emitting diode): electricity passes through a semiconductor chip; electrons combine with "holes" and release energy as light (electroluminescence); the colour depends on the semiconductor material. Uses far less energy than a filament lamp, little heat, instant full brightness, long life, many shapes and colours, can be dimmable with a compatible dimmer; higher cost to buy, saved on bills.
- [ ] EE4 Energy-efficient use: dimmers, timers, sensors, switching off lights, task lighting rather than lighting a whole room, using daylight, lamp with a high rating on the energy label.
- [ ] EE5 Choosing a lamp: brightness is in lumens, not watts; colour temperature; compatibility with fitting and dimmer; life; energy label rating.

## 3. Traps

- Only the properties of light are Higher Level. The planning principles, contemporary developments and energy-efficient lamps are common to Ordinary and Higher Level: do not label them HL.
- Reflection, refraction and diffusion are different: bouncing, bending, scattering. Do not swap them.
- Watts measure the power used, lumens measure the light given; a lower wattage LED can be as bright as a higher wattage filament bulb.
- Name the types of lighting (general, task, accent) and give a fitting for each; do not list fittings without saying what job they do.
- The CFL principle needs: gas and mercury in a glass tube, electrodes, ultraviolet light, phosphor. The LED principle needs: semiconductor, electrons and holes, light released. Do not describe an LED as heating a filament.
- Electrical safety, fuses and wiring belong to the electricity subsection; the energy-saving strategy list to the efficiency subsection; the BER to the efficiency and insulation subsections.
- Do not use: "(Higher Level only)" on the planning, developments, fittings or energy-efficient lamps blocks, because only the properties of light are Higher Level (Appendix C).
- Do not use: "CFLs last about eight times longer than a filament bulb" or any "times longer", wattage or hours figure (2009O scheme), because lifetimes are changing figures.
- Do not use: "CFL" as the modern or recommended lamp without noting that CFLs are being replaced by LEDs, because the EU mercury exemption ended in February 2023; marking schemes still accept the CFL principle.
- Do not use: "halogen lamps" as a contemporary energy-efficient development (2013 scheme lists halogen), because halogen filament lamps are being phased out and are not energy-efficient compared with LEDs.
- Do not use: "touch-sensitive lights" and "pool and pond lights" as headline developments, because they are minor scheme extras; give LED, sensor, smart and solar lighting.

## 4. What exams ask

- Planning a lighting system (2004O, 2009O, 2011H, 2014O, 2016H, 2019O, 2022O, 2025H): three or four principles for a named room (open-plan kitchen/living room, study bedroom, home office, kitchen/dining room): function and enough light, natural light and aspect, colour and size, combination of general, task and accent light, focal point and mood, glare, dimmers and movable lamps, safety, bulb access, efficient lamps.
- Contemporary developments in lighting (2006H, 2013H, 2019O, 2021H, 2022O): two or three, named with detail or evaluation: LED, sensor, smart, solar, recessed, rise-and-fall, under-cabinet, colour-changing, picture and track lights.
- Underlying principle of one type of energy-efficient lighting (2013H, 2023H): CFL (looped tube, phosphor, argon, mercury, electrodes) or LED (semiconductor, electrons and holes).
- Advantages and disadvantages of CFLs (2004O): low wattage, long-lasting, bright; expensive, glare without shade, mercury; one modern energy-efficient fitting (2009O).
- Properties of light and application (Higher Level): reflection, refraction, absorption, diffusion, dispersion with an example of each.
- Maximising natural light (2014O): window position and size, aspect, skylights, glass bricks, colours, clear glass, tree cutting, furniture placing.
- Practical: design a room layout with lighting (2005H); investigate contemporary lighting.
