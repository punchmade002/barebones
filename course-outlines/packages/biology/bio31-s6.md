## 1. Must cover

Population curves (one short block)
- [ ] C1 A population curve is a graph of population size (number of individuals, y-axis) against time (x-axis). It is a model of how a population changes, used to explain and predict the effects of limiting factors.
- [ ] C2 Biotic potential is the maximum rate at which a population could grow with unlimited resources and no limiting factors. Environmental resistance is the combined effect of all the limiting factors (food shortage, predation, disease, competition, lack of space, harsh conditions) that stop a population reaching its biotic potential. (assumed)

J-shaped curve (one block)
- [ ] J1 A J-shaped curve shows exponential growth: when resources are unlimited and there is little environmental resistance, the population grows at an ever-increasing rate, because each new generation adds more breeders, e.g. 2 → 4 → 8 → 16 → 32. The curve starts slowly (few breeders), then rises ever more steeply.
- [ ] J2 Conditions for J-shaped growth: a new habitat with plenty of food and space, few competitors, and no predators or diseases, often a species arriving in a new area, or a sudden large supply of nutrients.
- [ ] J3 J-shaped growth can't continue: resources eventually run out or the population damages its environment, and the population usually crashes suddenly (a "boom and bust"), often falling below the carrying capacity because the habitat has been damaged.
- [ ] J4 Examples: an algal bloom in a lake enriched with phosphate and nitrate, which grows explosively in warm, still weather and then dies back, the decomposing algae using up oxygen (e.g. the blue-green algal bloom on Lough Neagh in 2023; eutrophication detail in "Ethics and sustainability of nutrient cycling"); the zebra mussel's rapid spread through Lough Derg after it arrived in the 1990s, with no native predators or competitors adapted to it; rabbits introduced to Australia in 1859, which, with abundant food and few predators, grew into plague numbers.
- [ ] J5 Diagram: a J-shaped curve of population size against time, rising slowly then steeply, with a sudden vertical crash at the end; label "exponential growth", "resources unlimited", "crash".

S-shaped (sigmoid) curve (one block, one bullet per phase)
- [ ] S1 An S-shaped (sigmoid) curve shows the growth of a population in an environment with limited resources. It has four phases.
- [ ] S2 Lag phase: slow growth at first, because there are few individuals to breed, they may still be settling in or reaching breeding age, and they are spread out.
- [ ] S3 Exponential (log) phase: rapid growth, because resources are plentiful and there are now many breeders; births greatly exceed deaths.
- [ ] S4 Deceleration (transitional) phase: growth slows as resources begin to run short and density-dependent factors (competition for food and space, predation, disease, build-up of waste) increase; the death rate rises and the birth rate falls.
- [ ] S5 Stationary (plateau) phase: the population levels off at the carrying capacity (K), where births plus immigration equal deaths plus emigration. In nature the population fluctuates up and down around K rather than staying exactly level, because there is a time lag between a change in numbers and the effect on resources (e.g. a large population overgrazes, then falls, then the food recovers).
- [ ] S6 Examples: yeast cells growing in a flask of sugar solution; a population of animals colonising a new habitat, e.g. grey squirrels spreading into a woodland, which rise rapidly and then level off when food (seeds and nuts) limits them; barnacles settling on a newly cleared rock, which fill the space and then stop increasing.
- [ ] S7 Diagram: an S-shaped curve with the four phases labelled (lag, exponential, deceleration, stationary), a horizontal dashed line labelled "carrying capacity (K)", small oscillations about K, and the gap between the J-curve and the S-curve labelled "environmental resistance".

J- and S-curves compared (one comparison block, paired points)
- [ ] K1 Resources: unlimited (J) vs limited (S). Environmental resistance: little until the crash (J) vs increasing as the population grows (S). Growth: ever faster until a crash (J) vs fast, then slowing to zero (S). End point: crash, often below K (J) vs stable around K (S). Typical of: invasive species, pests and blooms (J) vs established populations in balance with their environment (S).

How carrying capacity varies with limiting factors in Irish ecosystems (one block; each bullet describes the curve)
- [ ] V1 When a limiting factor improves, K rises and the curve rises to a new, higher plateau: e.g. rewetting a drained bog restores breeding habitat for bog species; a large crop of beech nuts or acorns (a "mast year") raises the number of squirrels and wood mice a woodland can support the following year.
- [ ] V2 When a limiting factor worsens, K falls and the population declines to a new, lower plateau, or to extinction if K falls to zero: e.g. drainage of wet grassland and bogs has lowered the carrying capacity for breeding curlew so far that the population has collapsed (see "Biodiversity loss and conservation"); the arrival of grey squirrels and squirrelpox lowered K for red squirrels in eastern Ireland.
- [ ] V3 A new predator or the recovery of a predator lowers K for its prey: the recovery of the pine marten has reduced grey squirrel populations in the midlands, which in turn has allowed red squirrel numbers to rise towards a higher K.
- [ ] V4 Removing a predator can let a prey population overshoot K: without wolves, deer in Co. Wicklow increase until they overbrowse; damaged vegetation then lowers the K of the habitat for deer and for other species. Culling holds them below this level.
- [ ] V5 Density-independent events cause sudden falls at any population size, after which the population climbs back along an S-shaped curve: e.g. a severe winter kills many wrens and other small birds, whose numbers then recover over the following years.
- [ ] V6 Predator–prey cycles: predator and prey populations rise and fall in linked cycles, with the predator peak following the prey peak. More prey → more food for predators → predators increase → prey decrease → predators starve and decrease → prey recover. Classic example: the snowshoe hare and lynx in Canada, from fur-trading records over many decades. An Irish link: fox and rabbit numbers.
- [ ] V7 Diagram: (a) a curve that plateaus at K1, then, after a habitat change, falls to a lower plateau K2 (label "habitat loss lowers carrying capacity"); (b) linked predator and prey curves, the predator curve lower and lagging behind the prey curve.

Human population (one short block)
- [ ] H1 The world's human population has shown J-shaped growth over the last few centuries, because farming, medicine, sanitation and technology have repeatedly raised the carrying capacity of the Earth for humans, though growth is now slowing in many countries. Whether it will level off along an S-curve depends on birth rates and on resources such as food, water and energy.
- [ ] H2 Ireland illustrates a crash when a key resource fails: the population of Ireland reached about 8.2 million in the 1841 census, with many people depending on the potato; potato blight from 1845 caused the Great Famine, and death and emigration reduced the population sharply (link to "Variation").
