## 1. Must cover

A balanced diet (one block)
- [ ] B1 A balanced diet contains all the nutrients (carbohydrate, protein, lipid, vitamins, minerals), with water and dietary fibre, in the right amounts and proportions to meet a person's needs, and with enough but not too much energy.
- [ ] B2 Malnutrition is any diet that does not meet the body's needs: undernutrition (too little energy or too little of a nutrient, e.g. iron-deficiency anaemia, rickets) or overnutrition (too much energy or of a nutrient, e.g. obesity, or too much salt).
- [ ] B3 Energy balance: if energy taken in from food equals energy used (in metabolism and activity), body mass stays the same. Excess energy is stored as fat, causing weight gain; too little leads to the body using its glycogen, then fat and eventually muscle protein, and weight loss.
- [ ] B4 Ireland's Food Pyramid (Department of Health, 2016) is a guide to a balanced diet: most servings should come from the lower shelves (vegetables, salad and fruit, 5 to 7 servings a day; wholemeal cereals, breads, potatoes, pasta and rice), with milk, yoghurt and cheese (3 servings a day, 5 for ages 9 to 18), meat, poultry, fish, eggs, beans and nuts (2 servings), fats, spreads and oils in very small amounts, and the top shelf (foods and drinks high in fat, sugar and salt) not every day. No other serving figures.
- [ ] B5 Body mass index (BMI) is mass in kilograms divided by the square of height in metres. For adults, the World Health Organization classes 18.5–24.9 as a healthy weight, 25–29.9 as overweight and 30 or more as obese; below 18.5 is underweight. Limitations: BMI does not distinguish fat from muscle (a muscular athlete can have a high BMI) or show where fat is stored; children and teenagers are assessed with growth charts for their age and sex instead. Worked example: 70 kg ÷ (1.75 m × 1.75 m) = 70 ÷ 3.06 = 22.9, a healthy weight.

How needs vary: health, age, gender, activity (one block; one bullet per factor, each with the biological reason)
- [ ] N1 Health: illnesses change needs. People with coeliac disease must avoid gluten (see "Emerging diseases and society"); people with type 1 diabetes match carbohydrate intake to their insulin; people with high blood pressure are advised to eat less salt (see "Minerals"); people with anaemia need more iron; people recovering from injury or surgery need more protein for repair; people with lactose intolerance (see "Chemical breakdown") avoid or limit lactose and need other calcium sources.
- [ ] N2 Age, children and teenagers: growing, so they need more protein, calcium and vitamin D (for bones) and more energy per kilogram of body mass than adults. The teenage growth spurt raises energy, protein, calcium and iron needs; this is why the Food Pyramid gives 5 dairy servings for ages 9 to 18. Babies' needs are met by breast milk or formula.
- [ ] N3 Age, older people: metabolic rate and activity usually fall, so less energy is needed, but protein (to limit muscle loss), calcium and vitamin D (to limit bone loss and osteoporosis), fibre (constipation is more common) and water (the sense of thirst weakens with age) remain important.
- [ ] N4 Gender: on average males have more muscle and a larger body, so they need more energy and protein. Females who menstruate lose iron in blood each month, so they need more iron. Pregnancy and breastfeeding raise the need for energy, protein, calcium and iron; folic acid is advised before and in early pregnancy (see "Vitamins"); pregnant women should avoid alcohol and liver (vitamin A, see "Vitamins").
- [ ] N5 Activity: the more active a person, the more energy they use, so active people need more energy, mainly from starchy carbohydrates (which build glycogen stores), more protein for muscle repair, and more water and salt to replace sweat. Inactive (sedentary) people need less energy; eating as though active leads to weight gain.

Variety of foods (one block, two bullets)
- [ ] V1 No single food contains every nutrient in the right proportions, so a varied diet from all the food groups is the most reliable way to get all the vitamins, minerals, essential amino acids and essential fatty acids.
- [ ] V2 Variety within groups also matters: different fruits and vegetables supply different vitamins, minerals and fibre types (e.g. citrus fruit for vitamin C, green leafy vegetables for iron, calcium and vitamin K, carrots for beta-carotene), and combining plant proteins (e.g. beans with cereal) supplies all the essential amino acids (see "Metabolic roles and nutritional sources").

Sufficient water (one block)
- [ ] W1 Water is lost every day in urine, sweat, breath and faeces, and must be replaced from drinks and food (roles of water in "Water"). Needs rise in hot weather, during exercise, with fever, vomiting or diarrhoea, and when breastfeeding.
- [ ] W2 Signs of dehydration: thirst, dark-yellow urine in small volumes (ADH; see "ADH and osmoregulation" at Higher Level), headache, tiredness and poor concentration; severe dehydration is dangerous, especially in babies and older people.
- [ ] W3 Water is the best drink; sugar-sweetened drinks supply water but also free sugars (D7). No daily litre figure.

The role of dietary fibre (one block)
- [ ] F1 Dietary fibre (roughage) is plant material that human enzymes cannot digest, mainly cellulose from plant cell walls (see "Structures of biomolecules"). Sources: wholegrain cereals and wholemeal bread, brown rice, vegetables, fruit (especially with skins), beans, peas and lentils, nuts and seeds. There is no fibre in meat, fish, eggs or dairy produce.
- [ ] F2 Fibre adds bulk and absorbs water, so faeces are soft and large; this gives the gut muscles something to push against, keeps peristalsis effective (see "Mechanical breakdown") and prevents constipation.
- [ ] F3 Fibre makes food more filling and slows the absorption of sugar, which helps control body mass and blood glucose; high-fibre diets are linked to a lower risk of heart disease, type 2 diabetes and bowel cancer (bowel cancer detail in "Causes, prevention and treatment of cancer").
- [ ] F4 Bacteria in the colon ferment some fibre, which supports a healthy gut microbiome (detail in "The microbiome").
- [ ] F5 Raising fibre intake suddenly can cause wind and bloating, and fibre needs extra water to work; very large amounts of bran can reduce the absorption of some minerals such as iron and calcium. One bullet.

Researching dietary choices: how (one Skills block)
- [ ] R1 1. Pose a specific, testable question (e.g. "Does a vegan diet supply enough iron for teenage girls?"). 2. Find several reliable sources: public health bodies, peer-reviewed reviews, textbooks (see "Recognising bias and evaluating sources"). 3. Check who funded and wrote each source and whether it sells a product. 4. Judge the type of evidence: most diet evidence comes from observational studies of large populations, which show links (correlations) but not cause, because people who choose a diet often differ in other ways (e.g. vegetarians on average smoke less and exercise more); controlled trials give stronger evidence of cause but are usually short and small. 5. Explain the biological mechanism behind each claim (which nutrient, which body process). 6. Reach a balanced, justified conclusion for a particular group of people.
- [ ] R2 Example of funding bias (Nature of Science link): in 1967 a review of diet and heart disease published by Harvard scientists in the New England Journal of Medicine played down the role of sugar and blamed dietary fat; in 2016 researchers revealed that the review had been paid for by the sugar industry's trade group, the Sugar Research Foundation, without this being disclosed.

Biological implications of dietary choices (one block per choice; each block: what it is, arguments for, arguments against or risks, with the biological reason, and a one-line judgement)
- [ ] D1 Vegetarian and vegan diets, what they are: a vegetarian diet excludes meat and fish; most vegetarians eat eggs and dairy produce (lacto-ovo vegetarians). A vegan diet excludes all animal products, including eggs, dairy produce and honey. Pescatarians eat fish but not meat.
- [ ] D2 Vegetarian and vegan diets, benefits: usually higher in fibre, fruit and vegetables and lower in saturated fat, so on average blood cholesterol and BMI are lower; observational studies link them to a lower risk of heart disease and type 2 diabetes. They avoid processed and red meat, which are linked to bowel cancer (see "Causes, prevention and treatment of cancer"). Producing plant food generally uses less land and water and releases less greenhouse gas than producing meat (sustainability; one phrase, as it is in "Metabolic roles and nutritional sources"). Ethical reasons: animal welfare.
- [ ] D3 Vegetarian and vegan diets, risks (each needs planning, not avoidance): vitamin B12 occurs naturally only in animal foods, so vegans need fortified foods or supplements to prevent anaemia and nerve damage (see "Vitamins"); iron from plants is less easily absorbed than iron from meat, so eat iron-rich plants (beans, lentils, green leafy vegetables, fortified cereals) with vitamin C (see "Minerals"); vegans must get calcium from fortified plant milks, tofu and green vegetables instead of dairy produce; iodine comes mainly from milk and seafood, so vegans may be short of it; the long-chain omega-3 fatty acids are found mainly in oily fish; plant proteins must be combined to supply all essential amino acids. Teenagers, pregnant women and young children have the highest needs, so a vegan diet for them needs careful planning.
- [ ] D4 Vegetarian and vegan diets, evidence (one bullet): a large UK study of people's diets and health over many years (EPIC-Oxford) found that vegetarians had a lower rate of heart disease but a slightly higher rate of stroke than meat eaters, and vegans had more bone fractures; observational findings like these show links that may have other causes. Judgement: a well-planned vegetarian or vegan diet can meet all needs, but it must replace the nutrients that meat, fish and dairy produce supply.
- [ ] D5 High-sugar diets, what it means: free sugars are sugars added to food and drink, plus the sugars in honey, syrups and fruit juice; the sugar inside whole fruit, vegetables and milk is not counted as free sugar. The World Health Organization (2015) recommends that free sugars make up less than 10% of daily energy intake, and ideally less than 5%.
- [ ] D6 High-sugar diets, risks: tooth decay (plaque bacteria ferment sugar to acid; see "Mechanical breakdown"); excess energy is stored as fat, causing weight gain and obesity, which raises the risk of type 2 diabetes, heart disease, high blood pressure and some cancers; sugary foods often supply energy with few other nutrients ("empty" energy) and replace more nutritious foods; sugary drinks add energy without making a person feel full; sugar triggers dopamine release, which can drive overeating (see "Dopamine and endorphins").
- [ ] D7 High-sugar diets, counter-arguments: glucose is the brain's main fuel and sugar is a quick energy source, useful e.g. during long endurance exercise or for a person with diabetes whose blood glucose is too low; sugar in whole fruit comes with fibre and vitamins and is not the problem; the risk comes from the amount and how often it is eaten. Policy link: Ireland introduced a tax on sugar-sweetened drinks in 2018. Judgement: small amounts of free sugar are harmless; frequent, large amounts harm teeth and raise the risk of obesity and type 2 diabetes.
- [ ] D8 High-fat diets: saturated fats (butter, lard, fatty meat, cheese) raise the type of blood cholesterol (LDL) that is deposited in artery walls, narrowing them (atherosclerosis) and raising the risk of heart attack and stroke; artificial trans fats, once common in processed foods, are worse. Replacing saturated fats with unsaturated oils (olive oil, nuts, oily fish) lowers this risk. Counter-argument: fat is needed for energy, insulation, cell membranes, essential fatty acids and the absorption of vitamins A, D, E and K, so a very low-fat diet can cause deficiencies (see "Vitamins"). Fat has about twice the energy per gram of carbohydrate (see "Metabolic roles and nutritional sources"), so fatty food makes weight gain easy.
- [ ] D9 High-protein diets (e.g. for body-building): extra protein beyond needs is not stored as muscle; excess amino acids are deaminated and the urea excreted (see "Absorption, transport and storage"), and the rest is used for energy or stored as fat. Muscle growth depends on training, with enough protein and energy. High-protein diets based on processed and red meat bring more saturated fat; they are often low in fibre.
- [ ] D10 Low-fibre and highly processed diets: many processed and fast foods are high in fat, sugar and salt and low in fibre, vitamins and minerals. Implications: constipation, a higher risk of bowel cancer, heart disease, type 2 diabetes and obesity, and high salt intake linked to high blood pressure (see "Minerals"). Counter-points: some processed foods are useful and nutritious (e.g. frozen vegetables, tinned beans, fortified cereals), so the label, not the word "processed", is what matters.
- [ ] D11 Dairy-free and gluten-free diets: necessary for people with milk allergy, lactose intolerance (lactose-free milk is another option) or coeliac disease; for others they bring no proven benefit and need care, since dairy produce is the main calcium and iodine source in many Irish diets and gluten-free products are often lower in fibre and more expensive.
- [ ] D12 Skipping meals and very low-energy (crash) diets: glycogen stores run down, leading to tiredness and poor concentration; the body breaks down muscle protein as well as fat; nutrients are likely to be missed; weight lost quickly is often regained. One bullet. Eating disorders are not on the specification; don't teach them.

## 3. Traps

- Don't repeat food sources and roles from "Metabolic roles and nutritional sources", "Minerals" and "Vitamins": refer to them by title and give only the diet argument here. E.g. B12 for vegans is one clause, with the cross-reference.
- "Balanced" means right proportions, not equal amounts of each food group.
- Fibre is not digested and supplies (almost) no energy to humans; don't call it a nutrient that is absorbed. Don't say fibre "cleans" the gut.
- Observational studies show links, not cause: write "linked to" or "associated with", never "vegetarian diets prevent heart disease" or "sugar causes diabetes". Obesity is the main link between sugar and type 2 diabetes.
- Don't say vegans can't get enough protein: combined plant proteins supply all essential amino acids.
- Don't invent studies, percentages or named diets (no keto, paleo, intermittent fasting or named commercial diets). Only the EPIC-Oxford study and the 1967 review are named.
- Free sugars exclude the sugar inside whole fruit; fruit juice counts as free sugar.
- Eating disorders, food additives (E-numbers), food labelling law and food hygiene are not on the specification.
- Coeliac disease as an autoimmune condition belongs to "Emerging diseases and society"; lactase persistence to "Variation"; fibre and bowel cancer to "Causes, prevention and treatment of cancer". One phrase each here.
- No diagram cues. Diet needs none.

## 4. What exams ask (topics only)

- What is meant by a balanced diet.
- Explain why the dietary needs of a teenager, an older person, a pregnant woman or an athlete differ.
- Explain the importance of dietary fibre (e.g. how wholegrain foods help peristalsis).

## 5. Practice questions to write

1. 6 marks: "What is a balanced diet? Give two reasons why dietary fibre is important." Model answer: B1; two of F2–F4 (bulk and peristalsis, prevents constipation, linked to lower bowel-cancer risk, feeds gut bacteria).
2. 9 marks: "Explain why the dietary needs of a 15-year-old girl differ from those of a 70-year-old man." Model answer: N2 (growth: protein, calcium, energy), N4 (iron for menstruation), N3 (less energy, but protein, calcium, vitamin D, fibre, water still important), with the biological reason for each.
3. 12 marks: "A teenager is considering a vegan diet. Research the biological implications of this choice, giving the benefits and the nutrients she would need to plan for." Model answer: D1; at least two benefits from D2; at least four risks from D3 with the reason and solution (B12, iron with vitamin C, calcium, iodine, omega-3, protein combining); one point on the nature of the evidence (D4 or R1 step 4); a justified judgement. Must not say vegan diets lack protein.
