# Package: bio5-s2 — Levels of organisation

- **loId:** `bio5-s2` · **chapterId:** `bio5` · **syllabusRef:** 1.3.1
- **Syllabus:** "organisation of life in increasing complexity from individual cells to organism
  level." Outcome 1: "describe the complexity of multicellular organisms." Ordinary and Higher
  level. "Describe" needs the levels with examples, plus why multicellular organisms need
  specialised cells and systems.
- **Prepared by:** a higher model, 2026-10-22.

## 1. Must cover

The levels (one block)
- [ ] V1 Living things are organised in levels of increasing complexity: cell → tissue → organ → organ system → organism. Each level is made of units of the level below working together.
- [ ] V2 (assumed) Below the cell, cells are built of organelles, which are built of biomolecules (e.g. proteins, lipids). One bullet; organelles are in "Structures and organelles of animal and plant cells".
- [ ] V3 Cell: the basic unit of life. In a multicellular organism cells are specialised (differentiated): their structure suits one job.
- [ ] V4 Tissue: a group of similar cells with the same structure working together to carry out a particular function.
- [ ] V5 Organ: a structure made of several different tissues working together to carry out one or more functions.
- [ ] V6 Organ system: a group of organs working together to carry out a major function.
- [ ] V7 Organism: an individual living thing, whose organ systems work together to keep it alive.
- [ ] V8 Diagram: a five-step flow chart from cell to organism with a human example at each level (muscle cell → muscle tissue → stomach → digestive system → human) and a plant example (xylem vessel → vascular tissue → leaf → shoot system → plant).

Specialised cells (one block)
- [ ] S1 Unicellular organisms, e.g. Amoeba, yeast and bacteria, carry out every life process in one cell, so that cell is not specialised for a single job.
- [ ] S2 Differentiation: in a multicellular organism, all body cells contain the same DNA, but each cell type uses (expresses) only some of its genes, so cells become specialised. Unspecialised cells that can still become several cell types are called stem cells (their uses are in "Genetic Engineering").
- [ ] S3 Red blood cell: no nucleus and a biconcave disc shape, giving more room for haemoglobin and a large surface area for carrying oxygen.
- [ ] S4 Nerve cell (neuron): long thin extensions carry impulses over long distances quickly.
- [ ] S5 Sperm cell: a tail for swimming and many mitochondria in the middle section to release energy.
- [ ] S6 Root hair cell: a long extension gives a large surface area for absorbing water and minerals.
- [ ] S7 Palisade cell of a leaf: packed with chloroplasts for photosynthesis. Guard cells: change shape to open and close stomata.

Tissues, organs and systems in humans and plants (one block each, or one combined)
- [ ] T1 The four main animal tissue types: epithelial (lines and covers surfaces, e.g. the lining of the gut and skin surface); connective (supports and connects, e.g. bone, cartilage, blood); muscle (contracts to cause movement: skeletal, smooth and cardiac); nervous (carries impulses: brain, spinal cord, nerves).
- [ ] T2 Plant tissue types: dermal (the epidermis, a protective outer layer); vascular (xylem carries water and minerals, phloem carries food); ground (the bulk of the plant, carrying out photosynthesis and storage, e.g. the mesophyll of a leaf); meristematic (dividing cells at root and shoot tips, where growth happens).
- [ ] T3 Organ examples: the stomach contains epithelial tissue lining it, smooth muscle that churns food, connective tissue and nervous tissue; other human organs include the heart, lungs, kidneys and brain. Plant organs: root, stem, leaf and flower; a leaf contains dermal, ground and vascular tissue.
- [ ] T4 Human organ systems with one function each: digestive (breaks down and absorbs food), circulatory (transports materials), breathing (gas exchange), nervous (fast coordination), endocrine (hormonal coordination), musculoskeletal (support and movement), urinary (excretion and water balance), reproductive, immune (defence). Plants have two systems: the root system (anchorage, absorbing water and minerals) and the shoot system (stem, leaves and flowers: photosynthesis, transport, reproduction).

The complexity of multicellular organisms (one block)
- [ ] X1 (assumed) As an organism gets bigger its volume increases faster than its surface area, so its surface area to volume ratio falls. Worked example: a 1 cm cube has surface area 6 cm² and volume 1 cm³ (ratio 6:1); a 2 cm cube has surface area 24 cm² and volume 8 cm³ (ratio 3:1).
- [ ] X2 A unicellular organism has a large surface area to volume ratio, so diffusion across its membrane is enough to supply oxygen and food and remove wastes. In a large multicellular organism most cells are far from the surface, so diffusion alone is too slow.
- [ ] X3 So multicellular organisms need specialised exchange surfaces (lungs, gut lining, root hairs, leaves), transport systems (blood and heart; xylem and phloem), and coordination systems (nervous and hormonal) that link all the parts and keep conditions constant (homeostasis).
- [ ] X4 Division of labour: each cell type does one job very efficiently, but no cell can survive alone; the cells depend on each other.
- [ ] X5 Advantages of being multicellular: larger size (fewer predators, can reach light or food), cells inside are protected from the environment, efficiency from specialisation, and the organism can survive the death of individual cells, which are replaced.
- [ ] X6 Disadvantages: more energy and resources are needed to build and maintain the body; damage to one vital organ or system can kill the whole organism; growth to reproductive age takes longer than for unicellular organisms, which can divide within hours.

## 3. Traps

- The levels above the organism (population, community, ecosystem, biosphere) belong to "Ecology, Ecosystems and Biodiversity". Don't teach them here.
- Tissue culture and micropropagation were on the previous syllabus, not this one.
- An organ is made of several tissue types; students often call blood an organ or the heart a tissue. Blood is a tissue.
- Don't give organ-system detail (heart chambers, nephrons, etc.); each system has its own chapter. T4 gives one function each.
- Organelle structure belongs to "Structures and organelles of animal and plant cells"; the cell theory and "cellular basis" belong to "Characteristics of living things" (refer to it, don't repeat).
- Diffusion as a process is defined in "Transport Across Membranes"; X2 only uses the word.
- Diagram cues: only V8. No other diagram cues.

## 4. What exams ask (topics only)

- Define tissue, organ and organ system, and give an example of each in humans and in plants.
- Put the levels of organisation in order.
- Name the plant tissue types (dermal, vascular, ground) and say where each is found.
Expect also: explain why large organisms need transport systems (surface area to volume); describe how a named specialised cell suits its function.
