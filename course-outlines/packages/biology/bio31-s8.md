## 1. Must cover

What a pyramid of numbers is (one block; Ordinary and Higher)
- [ ] P1 A pyramid of numbers is a diagram showing the number of organisms at each trophic level of a food chain (or food web) in an area at one time. Each level is drawn as a horizontal bar whose width (length) is proportional to the number of organisms; the producers form the bottom bar, then primary, secondary and tertiary consumers above in order.
- [ ] P2 In most ecosystems the pyramid is upright: there are many producers, fewer primary consumers, fewer still secondary consumers and very few top consumers, e.g. many grass plants → fewer grasshoppers → fewer frogs → one heron; or grass → rabbits → foxes.
- [ ] P3 Why the numbers usually decrease up the pyramid: about 90% of energy is lost at each trophic level, so each level can support fewer organisms than the one below; also, predators are usually larger than their prey, so each needs to eat many prey.
- [ ] P4 Why pyramids (and food chains) are usually restricted to three or four levels: so little energy reaches the top level that it can't support a further level of consumers (from "Energy and nutrient transfer").

Using and drawing a pyramid of numbers (one block; Ordinary and Higher)
- [ ] U1 Uses in the study of ecosystems: it shows the feeding relationships in a food chain quantitatively (how many of each organism there are); it lets two ecosystems, or one ecosystem at different times, be compared; it shows how many prey each predator depends on; it helps predict the effect of a change in one level on the others (e.g. if a disease reduces the primary consumers, the secondary consumers will decline); and it shows why top predators are few and vulnerable.
- [ ] U2 How the numbers are found: by sampling the area, e.g. counting plants in quadrats and animals caught in traps or nets, and scaling up to the whole area (sampling methods are in "Investigating an Ecosystem"). Each organism is assigned to a trophic level.
- [ ] U3 How to draw one: 1. write the food chain and count (or take from the data) the organisms at each level; 2. draw a horizontal bar for each level, producers at the bottom, all centred on a vertical line; 3. make each bar's width proportional to the number (or use a scale, or a logarithmic scale for very large numbers, or draw it "not to scale" and say so); 4. label each bar with the trophic level, the organism and the number.
- [ ] U4 Diagram: an upright pyramid of numbers for grass → grasshopper → frog → heron, with the trophic levels (producer, primary, secondary, tertiary consumer) labelled.

Shapes of pyramids of numbers (Higher Level only) (one block)
- [ ] S1 Upright pyramid: the usual shape, wide at the base and narrowing to the top, when the producers are small and numerous and each consumer is larger than its prey (P2).
- [ ] S2 Inverted pyramid (narrow base): when one large producer supports many small herbivores, the base is narrower than the level above, e.g. one oak tree → thousands of caterpillars → a few blue tits → one sparrowhawk; or one rose bush → many caterpillars (or aphids) → a few blackbirds → one hawk. Because the upper levels still narrow, this pyramid is spindle- or diamond-shaped.
- [ ] S3 Inverted at the top: when the top level is made of parasites, which are smaller and more numerous than their host, the top bar is wider than the one below, e.g. grass → rabbits → fleas; one tree → birds → many lice and fleas on each bird.
- [ ] S4 Diagram: three pyramids side by side: upright (grass → grasshopper → frog → heron), narrow-base (oak tree → caterpillars → blue tits → sparrowhawk) and top-heavy with parasites (grass → rabbits → fleas), each level labelled.

Limitations of pyramids of numbers (Higher Level only) (one block; one bullet per limitation)
- [ ] L1 They take no account of the size of organisms: one oak tree counts the same as one blade of grass, so the shape can be inverted and misleading about how much food each level provides.
- [ ] L2 The numbers at different levels can differ so much (e.g. millions of grass plants and one fox) that the pyramid can't be drawn to scale.
- [ ] L3 It is hard to decide what counts as an individual for some organisms, e.g. grass and clover plants spread by runners, or a clump of moss.
- [ ] L4 Organisms that feed at more than one trophic level (omnivores, e.g. a fox eating rabbits and beetles, or a badger) are hard to place.
- [ ] L5 Counting is difficult and usually based on samples: small, hidden, nocturnal or very mobile animals are hard to count accurately, so numbers are estimates.
- [ ] L6 It shows the numbers at one moment only, so it changes with the seasons (e.g. many more caterpillars in early summer).
- [ ] L7 It does not show the amount of biomass or the energy at each level, and it usually leaves out decomposers.
- [ ] L8 The fix for L1 is a pyramid of biomass (see "Pyramids of biomass").

- Draw a pyramid of numbers for a given food chain (e.g. one with a large producer at the base).
- Name the organisms at the top of a food chain; explain why pyramids are usually restricted to three or four levels.
- Explain why a pyramid of numbers may be inverted; give a limitation.
