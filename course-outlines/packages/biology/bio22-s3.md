## 1. Must cover

Active and passive immunity (one block)
- [ ] T1 Active immunity: the person's own immune system makes antibodies and memory cells in response to an antigen. It takes days to weeks to develop, but it is long-lasting (often years or for life) because memory cells remain.
- [ ] T2 Passive immunity: ready-made antibodies from another person or animal are given to the person. Protection is immediate, but short-lived (weeks to a few months), because the antibodies are gradually broken down and no memory cells are made. A later infection triggers a primary response as if for the first time.
- [ ] T3 Natural vs artificial: natural immunity comes from normal life events (infection, pregnancy, breastfeeding); artificial immunity comes from deliberate medical intervention (vaccines, antibody injections).

The four ways immunity is acquired (one block, one bullet each with an example)
- [ ] W1 Active natural, by infection: catching a disease and recovering leaves memory cells against that pathogen, e.g. most people who have had chickenpox or measles don't get it again.
- [ ] W2 Active artificial, by vaccination: a vaccine stimulates the immune system to make antibodies and memory cells without causing the disease, e.g. the MMR vaccine against measles, mumps and rubella.
- [ ] W3 Passive natural, from the mother: antibodies (IgG) cross the placenta into the fetus during pregnancy; after birth, breast milk, especially the first milk (colostrum), contains antibodies (mainly IgA) that protect the baby's gut and breathing passages. This protects the newborn for its first few months while its own immune system develops.
- [ ] W4 Passive artificial, by donor transfer of antibodies: an injection of antibodies collected from donors (human immunoglobulin) or from immunised animals gives instant protection when there is no time for active immunity to develop. Examples: tetanus immunoglobulin for an unvaccinated person with a dirty wound; rabies immunoglobulin after a bite from a possibly rabid animal; hepatitis B immunoglobulin for babies born to infected mothers; antivenom (antibodies made in horses or sheep) for snake bites.
- [ ] W5 Comparison bullet, active vs passive: own antibodies vs donor antibodies; memory cells vs none; slow onset vs immediate; long-lasting vs short-lived; used for prevention in advance vs emergency protection or protecting newborns.

Vaccination (one block)
- [ ] V1 A vaccine contains antigens of a pathogen in a form that can't cause disease. Given by injection (or by mouth or nasal spray), it triggers a primary response, so memory cells form; a later infection meets a fast, strong secondary response and the disease doesn't develop (see "Innate and adaptive immunity").
- [ ] V2 Types of vaccine (one bullet each, with an example): live attenuated (a weakened pathogen that reproduces only slightly, e.g. MMR, and BCG against TB); inactivated (killed pathogen, e.g. the injected polio vaccine and most injected flu vaccines); toxoid (an inactivated toxin, e.g. tetanus and diphtheria vaccines); subunit (only a purified antigen, often made by genetically modified cells, e.g. the hepatitis B and HPV vaccines); mRNA (the genetic instructions for a pathogen protein, which the body's cells use to make the antigen, e.g. some COVID-19 vaccines); viral vector (a harmless virus carrying an antigen gene; see "Viruses").
- [ ] V3 Booster doses: a second or later dose of the same vaccine produces a secondary response, raising antibody levels and the number of memory cells, so immunity lasts longer (e.g. tetanus boosters). A vaccine booster is active immunity; an injection of donor antibodies is passive (W4).
- [ ] V4 Herd (community) immunity: when a high enough proportion of a population is immune, a pathogen can't find enough susceptible hosts to keep spreading, so the people who can't be vaccinated (newborns, people with weakened immune systems, people allergic to a vaccine component) are protected indirectly. The proportion needed depends on how infectious the disease is (calculated in "Emergence and spread of infectious disease").
- [ ] V5 Nature of science: in 1796 Edward Jenner showed that infecting a boy, James Phipps, with cowpox (a mild disease) protected him from smallpox; the word vaccine comes from vacca, Latin for cow. Worldwide vaccination led the World Health Organization to declare smallpox eradicated in 1980, the first human disease to be eradicated.
- [ ] V6 Benefits and concerns (paired points): vaccines prevent illness, disability and death and are cheaper than treatment; they protect others through herd immunity. Concerns: rare side effects (most are mild, e.g. a sore arm or low fever); live vaccines are not given to people with weakened immune systems; misinformation lowers uptake, and outbreaks follow when coverage falls (the MMR case is in "Evaluating evidence").

Strategies to prevent microbial disease (one block, one bullet per strategy: how it works, example)
- [ ] P1 Hygiene: handwashing with soap removes and kills microorganisms on hands, breaking transmission by contact (the Semmelweis case is in "The nature of scientific knowledge"); covering coughs and sneezes; cleaning surfaces.
- [ ] P2 Clean water and sanitation: treating drinking water (filtering and chlorination) and safe disposal of sewage stop the spread of waterborne diseases such as cholera.
- [ ] P3 Food safety: cooking food thoroughly kills pathogens (e.g. Salmonella in chicken); refrigeration slows their growth; pasteurisation (heating milk to about 72 °C for 15 seconds, then cooling quickly) kills pathogens without spoiling the milk; avoiding cross-contamination between raw and cooked food.
- [ ] P4 Vaccination (see V1–V4).
- [ ] P5 Vector control: insecticide-treated bed nets, insecticide spraying and draining standing water reduce mosquitoes that carry malaria; controlling ticks reduces Lyme disease.
- [ ] P6 Barrier and blood safety: condoms reduce the spread of sexually transmitted infections such as HIV; donated blood is screened for HIV and hepatitis viruses; needles are sterile and used once.
- [ ] P7 Sterilisation and aseptic technique: autoclaving (steam under pressure at 121 °C) kills microorganisms and spores on equipment; disinfectants and antiseptics are used on surfaces and skin; aseptic technique in surgery prevents wound infection.
- [ ] P8 Isolation and quarantine: isolation separates people who are ill; quarantine separates people (or animals, plants) who may have been exposed until it is clear they aren't infected; contact tracing finds people exposed to a case. (Population-level use is in "Emerging diseases and society".)
- [ ] P9 In plants and farm animals: resistant varieties and breeds, certified disease-free seed and stock, crop rotation, fungicide sprays (e.g. against potato blight), and good animal housing and hygiene. Prion diseases are prevented by keeping prions out of feed and food (see "Pathogens").

Strategies to treat microbial disease (one block, one bullet per drug type)
- [ ] D1 Antibiotics: chemicals, originally made by microorganisms, that kill bacteria or stop them growing, without harming human cells, because they target structures bacteria have and human cells lack. Penicillin stops bacteria building their peptidoglycan cell wall, so growing bacteria burst; tetracycline and streptomycin bind to bacterial 70S ribosomes and stop protein synthesis (human cytoplasmic ribosomes are 80S). Antibiotics have no effect on viruses.
- [ ] D2 Responsible antibiotic use: take antibiotics only when prescribed for a bacterial infection, take the full course as directed, and never share them, because unnecessary use selects for resistant bacteria (detail in "Emergence and spread of infectious disease").
- [ ] D3 Antiviral drugs interfere with a stage of virus replication inside the host cell, so they are specific to one virus or a few (examples and how they act are in "Viruses: replication"). There are far fewer antivirals than antibiotics, because viruses use the host cell's own machinery, which a drug must not damage.
- [ ] D4 Antifungal drugs target structures of fungal cells, e.g. clotrimazole cream for athlete's foot and thrush. Antiprotozoal drugs treat protist infections, e.g. artemisinin-based drugs for malaria. Anthelmintics kill parasitic worms, e.g. ivermectin.
- [ ] D5 Antibody treatments: monoclonal antibodies (identical antibodies made in the laboratory against one antigen) are used to treat some infections and cancers; donor antibodies are W4. One bullet.
- [ ] D6 Supportive treatment: rest, fluids and rehydration (e.g. oral rehydration solution of water, salts and sugar for cholera), and reducing fever, while the immune system clears the infection.

## 3. Traps

- "Acquired" immunity in the specification means the specific (adaptive) immunity; passive immunity is a way of acquiring it temporarily. Don't call passive immunity "innate".
- The specification's "antibody booster injections" means injections of donor antibodies (passive). A vaccine booster dose is active. Keep W4 and V3 distinct.
- Breastfeeding gives passive natural immunity; it doesn't make the baby produce its own memory cells.
- Vaccines contain antigens, not antibodies.
- Antibiotics don't work on viruses or fungi. Don't say bacteria "become immune" to antibiotics: bacterial populations become resistant by natural selection (in "Emergence and spread of infectious disease").
- How antibodies and memory cells work belongs to "Innate and adaptive immunity"; R₀ and the herd-immunity threshold calculation to "Emergence and spread of infectious disease"; biosecurity, surveillance and outbreak control to "Emerging diseases and society"; microorganisms that make antibiotics and vaccines in industry to "Microorganisms in industry"; HPV vaccination and cancer to "Causes, prevention and treatment of cancer"; Rhesus factor and anti-D to "Blood".
- No diagram cues. (A graph of antibody level after a first and booster vaccine dose is the L6 graph of "Innate and adaptive immunity"; don't redraw it.)
