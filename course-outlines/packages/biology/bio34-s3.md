## 1. Must cover

What the issues are (one short block)
- [ ] I1 Humans now move huge amounts of carbon and nitrogen between stores: burning fossil fuels and clearing forests and bogs moves carbon into the air ("The carbon cycle"); making and spreading fertiliser, and keeping large numbers of livestock, adds fixed nitrogen to soils and water ("The nitrogen cycle"). A sustainability issue arises when a practice damages the environment's ability to support future generations; an ethical issue arises when people disagree about what is right, especially who gets the benefits and who bears the costs.
- [ ] I2 Ethical questions to ask of every issue (one bullet): who benefits and who is harmed (now, elsewhere, and in the future); who should pay to prevent or repair harm (the "polluter pays" principle, which is part of EU environmental law); is the harm fair (justice); should we act before harm is proven (precautionary principle); and do people affected have a say.

Eutrophication (one process block)
- [ ] E1 Eutrophication is the enrichment of rivers, lakes or coastal water with nutrients, mainly nitrate and phosphate, leading to excessive growth of algae and plants and loss of oxygen.
- [ ] E2 Sources: nitrate leached from fertilised fields after rain (nitrate is very soluble; see "The nitrogen cycle"); slurry and farmyard manure washed off land, especially if spread before heavy rain or on wet or frozen ground; untreated or poorly treated sewage and septic tanks; phosphate-containing detergents; runoff from forestry and peat extraction.
- [ ] E3 The sequence, numbered: 1. extra nitrate and phosphate enter the water; 2. algae grow very rapidly (an algal bloom), turning the water green and covering the surface; 3. the bloom blocks light, so rooted plants and algae below die; 4. algae have short lives, so huge numbers die; 5. aerobic decomposers (bacteria) feed on the dead algae and plants, multiply and respire, using up dissolved oxygen (the water's biochemical oxygen demand rises); 6. dissolved oxygen falls, so fish (especially salmon and trout, which need high oxygen) and invertebrates die; 7. their decomposition uses still more oxygen; anaerobic decomposition may release foul-smelling gases.
- [ ] E4 Some blooms are of blue-green algae (bacteria) that make toxins harmful to animals, pets and people, so lakes may be closed for swimming, and drinking-water treatment costs rise.
- [ ] E5 Example: in summer 2023, Lough Neagh in Northern Ireland, the largest freshwater lake in Ireland and Britain, had widespread blue-green algal blooms, linked to excess nutrients (from farming and wastewater) and an unusually warm summer.
- [ ] E6 Diagram: a flow chart of the eutrophication sequence in E3 (nutrients in → algal bloom → plants below die → algae die → decomposers increase → oxygen falls → fish die).

Issue 1: nitrogen fertilisers and slurry (one evaluation block, paired points)
- [ ] F1 Benefits (for): nitrogen is usually the nutrient that limits crop and grass growth, so fertiliser greatly raises yields; food grown with fertiliser made by the Haber process feeds about half of the world's people. Higher yields mean less land must be cleared for farming, sparing forests and wild habitats. In Ireland, grass-based livestock farming depends on nitrogen for grass growth, and farming supports rural incomes and exports.
- [ ] F2 Costs (against), water: leached nitrate and washed-off slurry cause eutrophication (E1–E6), harming fisheries, angling, tourism and wildlife. Nitrate in drinking water above the EU limit of 50 mg per litre is a health risk, especially to bottle-fed babies (it reduces the blood's ability to carry oxygen, "blue baby syndrome"), so water must be treated or sources abandoned.
- [ ] F3 Costs (against), air and climate: making fertiliser by the Haber process uses a lot of energy, mainly from natural gas, and releases CO₂; extra soil nitrate increases denitrification, which releases nitrous oxide (N₂O), a powerful greenhouse gas (see "Climate change and biological strategies"); ammonia escapes from slurry and urine, and in Ireland agriculture produces about 99% of ammonia emissions. Ammonia falling on bogs, heaths and woods acts as a fertiliser that lets a few fast-growing plants crowd out species adapted to low nutrients, reducing biodiversity; it also forms fine particles in the air that harm human health.
- [ ] F4 Ethical arguments: the farmer gains the yield but others pay the cost (downstream communities, anglers, people drinking the water, future generations), so the polluter-pays principle argues farmers should bear the cost of prevention; against this, farm incomes are often low, food must stay affordable, and consumers who want cheap food share responsibility. Rules must be fair to small farmers as well as large ones. Globally, many poor farmers use too little fertiliser and suffer low yields and hunger, while richer regions use too much: the same chemical is a benefit in one place and a pollutant in another (justice).
- [ ] F5 Regulation (one bullet): the EU Nitrates Directive (1991) requires member states to protect water from farm nitrate. Under it, livestock manure may not add more than 170 kg of nitrogen per hectare per year (Ireland has had a derogation allowing some intensive farms more, under stricter conditions); slurry and fertiliser may not be spread in a winter closed period or near watercourses; farms must have enough slurry storage. Ireland applies these through its Nitrates Action Programme.
- [ ] F6 Sustainable responses (one bullet each, each with its biological reason): test soil and apply only the nitrogen the crop needs, at the time it is growing, not before heavy rain; grow clover or other legumes with grass, and legumes in crop rotation, so fixation replaces some fertiliser ("The nitrogen cycle"); leave uncultivated buffer strips of vegetation beside rivers to absorb nutrients before they reach the water; spread slurry close to the ground (low-emission methods such as a trailing shoe) to cut ammonia loss; protect wetlands, where denitrification removes nitrate; treat sewage fully.
- [ ] F7 Judgement (one bullet): nitrogen fertiliser is needed to feed the world, so banning it would be unethical, but applying more than crops can use wastes money and transfers the costs to others and to the future; the sustainable and fair position is to use less, more precisely, and to recycle nitrogen in manure and legumes.

Issue 2: recycling nutrients from waste (one evaluation block)
- [ ] R1 Sustainable nutrient cycling means returning nutrients from wastes to the soil instead of losing them: slurry and manure used as fertiliser at the right time and rate; composting of garden and food waste, where decomposers (bacteria and fungi) turn organic matter into a nutrient-rich soil improver; treated sewage sludge spread on land; anaerobic digestion of slurry and food waste, which produces biogas (methane) for fuel and a digestate that is used as fertiliser.
- [ ] R2 For: it cuts the need for manufactured fertiliser and the fossil energy used to make it; it keeps wastes out of landfill, where they would decompose anaerobically and release methane; it improves soil structure and soil organic matter (a carbon store).
- [ ] R3 Against or limitations: manure and sludge can carry pathogens, and sludge may contain heavy metals and other pollutants, so it must be treated and tested; spreading at the wrong time causes the same pollution as fertiliser; anaerobic digesters are costly to build and leaks of biogas release methane. Many people are uneasy about food grown on land treated with sewage sludge.

Issue 3: releasing stored carbon from forests and bogs (one evaluation block)
- [ ] C1 Deforestation: tropical forests are cleared, often by burning, mainly for cattle ranching (e.g. in the Amazon in Brazil), soya grown largely for animal feed, and oil-palm plantations (e.g. in Indonesia and Malaysia). This turns a carbon sink into a source (see "The carbon cycle"), removes nutrients with the crop, exposes soil to erosion, and destroys habitats of very high biodiversity.
- [ ] C2 Ethical arguments on deforestation: for clearing, poorer countries argue they have the same right to develop their land as richer countries had when they cleared their own forests, and farming gives jobs and food; against, the losses (carbon released, species extinct, rainfall changed) affect the whole world and future generations, and indigenous people who live in the forests lose their homes and way of life. Consumers in richer countries share responsibility, because they buy the beef, soya-fed meat and palm oil. Responses: protected areas, payments to countries for keeping forests, and EU rules that ban imports of products linked to recent deforestation.
- [ ] C3 Peat in Ireland: peatlands cover about a fifth of Ireland's land area. For centuries turf was cut by hand for household fuel, and in the twentieth century bogs were drained and milled by Bord na Móna for electricity and horticultural compost. Drained peat decomposes aerobically and releases CO₂, and is lost as a carbon store (see "The carbon cycle"); raised bogs are also rare habitats for specialised species such as sphagnum moss.
- [ ] C4 Ethical arguments on peat: for continuing, families have traditional rights to cut turf on their bog (turbary rights), it is a cheap local fuel and part of rural culture, and peat extraction gave jobs to Midlands communities; against, peat burning releases more CO₂ per unit of energy than coal, the habitats can't be replaced on any human timescale, and the climate cost falls on everyone and on the future. Responses: Bord na Móna ended peat harvesting in January 2021; cutting on protected raised bogs (Special Areas of Conservation) is restricted, which has caused conflict with turf-cutting families; bogs are being rewetted; and a Just Transition fund supports workers and communities that depended on peat. Fairness to the people who lose income is part of the judgement.
- [ ] C5 Fossil fuels as an ethical issue (one bullet): the countries that industrialised first have released most of the CO₂ in the atmosphere, while many of the people most harmed by the resulting climate change (low-lying and poor countries) have contributed least, and future generations will bear costs they did not choose (intergenerational justice). The biological strategies to respond are in "Climate change and biological strategies".

## 3. Traps

- Eutrophication: the fish die from lack of oxygen used by decomposers, not because the algae themselves "use up the oxygen" or because nitrate poisons them. Algae add oxygen by day; the oxygen crash comes from decomposition.
- Nitrate, not nitrogen gas, is leached. Phosphate is also a major cause of eutrophication (often the main one in lakes); don't say only nitrogen causes it.
- Don't say blue-green algae are true algae without saying they are bacteria (one phrase in E4); don't name species.
- Don't name nitrogen-cycle microorganisms.
- Greenhouse effect mechanism, methane's and nitrous oxide's warming power, carbon sinks as a climate strategy, rewetting as a climate strategy, afforestation, low-methane feeds and the post-fossil-fuel world all belong to "Climate change and biological strategies": here give only the ethical and nutrient-cycling side, in a phrase.
- Biodiversity loss in general and conservation belong to "Biodiversity loss and conservation"; C1 and F3 give the nutrient-cycling cause only.
- The ethics of genetic modification belong to "Ethics of genetic technologies"; don't raise GM crops here.
- Don't present one side only: every issue block must give arguments for and against, then a judgement.
- Diagram cue E6 only. No other diagram cues.

## 5. Practice questions to write

1. 9 marks: "Explain how the spreading of slurry before heavy rain can lead to the death of fish in a nearby river." Model answer: E2 (leaching or runoff of nitrate and phosphate) and the E3 sequence through algal bloom, death, decomposers respiring, oxygen falling, fish dying.
2. 12 marks: "Evaluate the ethical and sustainability issues associated with the use of nitrogen fertilisers in agriculture." Model answer: F1 benefits; F2 and F3 costs (water, drinking water, climate, ammonia and biodiversity); F4 ethical arguments including who pays; F6 at least two responses with reasons; F7 a clear judgement.
3. 9 marks: "Peat was once widely harvested in Ireland for fuel. Discuss the arguments for and against protecting Irish bogs from turf cutting." Model answer: C3 biology (aerobic decomposition of drained peat releases CO₂; rare habitat); C4 arguments on both sides (turbary rights, cheap fuel, culture, jobs; carbon, habitat, future generations); responses and a judgement.
