## 1. Must cover

What variation is (one block)
- [ ] V1 Variation is the differences between individuals of the same species, e.g. in height, eye colour, blood group, or resistance to a disease.
- [ ] V2 Genetic (inherited) variation is caused by differences in alleles and genes; it can be passed to offspring. Environmental (acquired) variation is caused by the conditions an organism lives in, e.g. a scar, a suntan, muscles built by exercise, or a plant's size in poor soil; it is not passed on through the DNA. Most characteristics depend on both (phenotype = genotype + environment, in "Key concepts of information transfer").
- [ ] V3 Only genetic variation matters for evolution, because only it can be inherited and acted on by natural selection (see "The theory of evolution by natural selection").
- [ ] V4 (assumed) Discontinuous variation: individuals fall into a few distinct groups with no in-between forms, e.g. human ABO blood group, round or wrinkled pea seeds, albinism; usually controlled by one or a few genes, with little effect of the environment; shown in a bar chart.
- [ ] V5 (assumed) Continuous variation: a smooth range between two extremes, e.g. human height, mass, skin colour, leaf length; usually controlled by many genes together with the environment; most individuals are near the middle, giving a bell-shaped (normal) distribution in a histogram.
- [ ] V6 Diagram: a bar chart of the four ABO blood groups (discontinuous) beside a bell-shaped histogram of height in a class (continuous); label axes (blood group or height class; number of people).

Variation from sexual reproduction (one process block; one bullet per source)
- [ ] S1 Sexual reproduction does not create new alleles; it produces new combinations of existing alleles in every generation, so every offspring (except identical twins) is genetically unique.
- [ ] S2 Independent assortment in meiosis: which chromosome of each homologous pair goes into a gamete is random and independent of the other pairs, so one person can make 2²³, about 8.4 million, different combinations of chromosomes in their gametes. The cell-level reason is taught in "Mitosis and meiosis"; give it here in one bullet as a source of variation, without re-explaining meiosis. (Mendel's law of independent assortment is in "Mendel's Laws".)
- [ ] S3 Random fertilisation: any one of the father's millions of sperm types can fertilise any one of the mother's egg types, so two parents can produce about 8.4 million × 8.4 million, about 70 trillion (7 × 10¹³), different combinations of chromosomes, even before other sources of variation are counted.
- [ ] S4 Two parents: each offspring combines alleles from two different individuals, so alleles that arose in different family lines come together. The choice of mate also varies.
- [ ] S5 Contrast with asexual reproduction: one parent, no gametes, division by mitosis, so offspring are genetically identical clones (e.g. bacteria dividing by binary fission, potato tubers, strawberry runners). Their only source of genetic variation is mutation. A population of clones may all be destroyed by the same disease or change in the environment.
- [ ] S6 Example of the risk (one bullet; the same case appears as a disease-spread factor in "Emergence and spread of infectious disease"): the potatoes grown in Ireland before the Great Famine were mostly a single variety, the Lumper, propagated from tubers, so the plants were almost genetically identical; when potato blight (caused by Phytophthora infestans) arrived in 1845, none was resistant and the crop failed across the country.
- [ ] S7 Diagram: two homologous pairs (one long, one short, coloured to show the chromosome from the mother and from the father) in a cell, the two possible ways they can line up before separating, and the four resulting kinds of gamete; label homologous pair, gamete, maternal chromosome, paternal chromosome.

Variation from mutation (one block)
- [ ] U1 A mutation is a change in the DNA: in the base sequence of a gene, or in the structure or number of chromosomes. Mutation is the only source of new alleles, so it is the original source of all genetic variation.
- [ ] U2 Causes: mutations happen spontaneously, mainly as rare copying errors when DNA is replicated; their rate is increased by mutagens, such as ionising radiation (X-rays, gamma rays), ultraviolet light, and certain chemicals, e.g. some in tobacco smoke. (Mutagens as causes of cancer are in "Causes, prevention and treatment of cancer".)
- [ ] U3 Gene mutation (point mutation): a change in the base sequence of one gene, e.g. one base replaced by another, or a base added or lost. It creates a new allele of that gene. Example: sickle-cell anaemia is caused by a change of a single base in a gene for haemoglobin. (Substitution, insertion and deletion, with modelled examples, are in "Mutations", Higher Level.)
- [ ] U4 Chromosome mutation: a change in the structure of a chromosome, such as a section lost (deletion), copied (duplication), turned around (inversion) or moved to another chromosome (translocation), affecting many genes at once (detail in "Mutations"). Changes in chromosome number also cause variation: e.g. Down syndrome is caused by an extra copy of chromosome 21 (47 chromosomes); in plants, whole extra sets of chromosomes (polyploidy) are common, e.g. bread wheat has six sets.
- [ ] U5 Effects: most mutations are neutral (no effect, e.g. in non-coding DNA, or a change that gives the same amino acid) or harmful; a few are beneficial in a particular environment. Chromosome mutations are usually harmful in animals but polyploidy is often tolerated in plants, and many crop plants are polyploid.
- [ ] U6 Beneficial example: lactase persistence. Most mammals stop making lactase, the enzyme that digests milk sugar, after weaning. A mutation in the DNA that controls the lactase gene keeps it switched on in adults; it became common in populations with a long history of dairy farming, such as in northern Europe, including Ireland, because adults who could digest milk had an advantage.
- [ ] U7 Only mutations in cells that make gametes (or in a gamete) are inherited. A mutation in a body cell (somatic mutation), e.g. a skin cell damaged by ultraviolet light, affects only the cells descended from it and may cause cancer, but is not passed to offspring.
- [ ] U8 Although each gene mutates rarely, there are many genes and many individuals, so new mutations arise in every generation. Organisms that reproduce very fast, such as bacteria, accumulate mutations quickly (antibiotic resistance is in "Evidence for evolution").

Sexual reproduction and mutation compared (one comparison block, paired points)
- [ ] C1 What it does: shuffles existing alleles into new combinations vs creates new alleles (or new chromosome arrangements).
- [ ] C2 How often: in every generation, in every offspring vs rarely, at random.
- [ ] C3 Effect on the individual: every offspring different, but each allele already worked in a parent vs most mutations neutral or harmful, a few beneficial.
- [ ] C4 Organisms: only in sexually reproducing organisms vs in all organisms, including those that reproduce asexually.
- [ ] C5 Together: mutation supplies new alleles, and sexual reproduction spreads them into new combinations, giving the variation on which natural selection acts.

## 3. Traps

- Don't say sexual reproduction creates new alleles; only mutation does (S1, C1).
- Crossing over: the specification says "knowledge of crossing over not required". Don't describe it. If mentioned at all, one phrase only: "a further shuffling between homologous chromosomes, not required".
- Mutations are random; they are not caused by the organism's needs or by the environment "to help it adapt". Mutagens increase the rate but not the direction.
- Somatic mutations are not inherited (U7); acquired characteristics such as muscles from exercise are not inherited (V2). Epigenetic inheritance is a limited exception, in "Genetic and epigenetic mechanisms"; one phrase at most.
- Down syndrome is a change in chromosome number, not one of the four structural chromosome mutations (U4 keeps them separate).
- The full list of point and chromosomal mutation types with modelled examples (cystic fibrosis, cri du chat, the Philadelphia translocation) belongs to "Mutations" in "DNA Replication, Protein Synthesis and Mutations" (Higher Level); don't model them here.
- Meiosis, independent assortment at cell level and random fertilisation are taught in "Mitosis and meiosis"; S2–S3 summarise them as sources of variation and add the two-parent figure, without re-explaining the division. Mendel's law of independent assortment belongs to "Mendel's Laws"; no crosses.
- Natural selection acting on variation belongs to "The theory of evolution by natural selection"; V3 and C5 name it only.
- Tongue rolling is not a reliable single-gene example; don't use it.
- No other diagram cues beyond V6 and S7.

## 4. What exams ask (topics only)

- Give two causes of variation within a species.
- Define mutation; name two mutagens; distinguish gene and chromosome mutations, with an example.
- Explain why sexual reproduction produces variation and asexual reproduction does not.
- Explain why a mutation in a skin cell is not passed to offspring.
- A legacy statement that "a mutation always has a negative impact" (true/false): the answer is false (U5).

2. 9 marks: "Define mutation. Distinguish between a gene mutation and a chromosome mutation, giving one example of each." Model answer: U1; U3 with sickle-cell anaemia; U4 with a structural change or Down syndrome correctly described as a change in number.
3. 12 marks: "Explain how mutation and sexual reproduction together provide the variation needed for evolution." Model answer: U1 (only source of new alleles), U5 (most neutral or harmful, some beneficial, e.g. U6), U7 (must be in gametes), S1–S3 (new combinations), C5, V3.
