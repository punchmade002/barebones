## 1. Must cover

The Higher Level model of DNA replication (one process block, numbered)
- [ ] R1 [HL] Replication takes place in the nucleus during the S phase of interphase (see "The cell cycle"). In eukaryotes it begins at many points along each chromosome at once, so the very long molecules are copied quickly; bacteria start at one point on their circular chromosome.
- [ ] R2 [HL] 1. The enzyme DNA helicase unwinds the double helix and separates the two strands by breaking the hydrogen bonds between complementary bases, forming a Y-shaped replication fork.
- [ ] R3 [HL] 2. Each exposed strand acts as a template. Free DNA nucleotides, made in the cell and present in the nucleus, pair with the exposed bases by complementary base pairing (A–T, C–G).
- [ ] R4 [HL] 3. The enzyme DNA polymerase joins each new nucleotide to the growing strand, forming the sugar–phosphate backbone of the new strand. It adds nucleotides only where they pair correctly with the template.
- [ ] R5 [HL] 4. DNA polymerase also proofreads: it checks each new base pair and removes and replaces most wrongly paired nucleotides; other repair enzymes correct many of the rest. A mistake that is not corrected becomes a permanent change in the base sequence, a point mutation (see "Mutations").
- [ ] R6 [HL] 5. Each new DNA molecule (one template strand plus its new complementary strand) twists into a double helix. The two molecules are identical to each other and to the original, and remain joined as two sister chromatids at a centromere until they separate at anaphase of mitosis.
- [ ] R7 [HL] Energy: replication needs energy, supplied by ATP (see "ATP") and by the nucleotides themselves as they are added. One bullet.
- [ ] R8 [HL] Diagram: a replication fork, labelled: parent (original) DNA double helix, DNA helicase at the fork, hydrogen bonds being broken, the two template strands, free nucleotides, DNA polymerase adding nucleotides, the new complementary strands, and the two daughter molecules, each with one old strand (dark) and one new strand (light).

The semi-conservative model and its evidence (one block)
- [ ] S1 [HL] Semi-conservative replication: each new DNA molecule contains one strand from the original molecule (conserved) and one newly made strand. Watson and Crick predicted this in 1953 from the base-pairing model (see "Models").
- [ ] S2 [HL] Two other models were possible: conservative replication (the original double helix stays intact, and a completely new molecule is made) and dispersive replication (each new molecule is a patchwork of old and new pieces in both strands).
- [ ] S3 [HL] The Meselson–Stahl experiment (Matthew Meselson and Franklin Stahl, 1958) tested the three models. 1. They grew the bacterium E. coli for many generations in a medium whose only nitrogen was the heavy isotope ¹⁵N, so all its DNA was "heavy". 2. They moved the bacteria to a medium containing only normal, light ¹⁴N. 3. After each generation they extracted the DNA and spun it at high speed in a caesium chloride solution, which forms a density gradient, so DNA settles as a band at a level that matches its density.
- [ ] S4 [HL] Results and conclusions: before the transfer, one heavy band. After one generation: one band of intermediate density, so conservative replication (which would give one heavy and one light band) was ruled out. After two generations: one intermediate band and one light band, of equal size, so dispersive replication (which would give a single band, becoming lighter) was ruled out. Only the semi-conservative model fits.
- [ ] S5 [HL] Diagram: three centrifuge tubes side by side (generation 0, 1, 2) showing band positions heavy; intermediate; intermediate and light; with a strand diagram under each showing heavy strands dark and light strands pale.

Using the model (one skills block, with worked examples)
- [ ] M1 [HL] Predicting daughter molecules: given a parent molecule with strands ATGCCA and TACGGT, the strands separate; ATGCCA is the template for a new TACGGT, and TACGGT for a new ATGCCA; the two daughter molecules are identical to the parent, each with one old strand and one new strand.
- [ ] M2 [HL] Counting nucleotides: copying a DNA molecule of 1,000 base pairs makes two new strands of 1,000 nucleotides each, so 2,000 free nucleotides are used. If 30% of the parent's bases are adenine, 30% of the nucleotides used are A, 30% T, and 20% each C and G (because A = T and C = G in double-stranded DNA; see "Nucleotides, DNA and RNA").
- [ ] M3 [HL] Generations: one DNA molecule replicated three times gives 2³ = 8 molecules; in a Meselson–Stahl-type experiment, only 2 of these 8 still contain an original heavy strand (each original strand is kept, never broken up).
- [ ] M4 [HL] Physical model: build a short double strand from paper or plastic nucleotide pieces in four colours (one per base) that fit only their correct partners; separate the strands, add free pieces to each, and compare the two products with the original; use two shades for old and new pieces to show semi-conservation. Strength: shows templates, base pairing and identical products. Limitations: it is flat and short; it does not show the helix, the enzymes, the energy needed or the speed and accuracy in cells (models in general are in "Models").

The flow of genetic information: an overview (one block)
- [ ] F1 [HL] Genetic information flows from DNA to mRNA to protein: DNA → (transcription) → mRNA → (translation) → protein. Replication copies DNA → DNA. Francis Crick called this one-way flow from nucleic acid to protein the "central dogma" of molecular biology.
- [ ] F2 [HL] Transcription: in the nucleus, the base sequence of one gene is copied into a molecule of messenger RNA (mRNA), using one DNA strand as the template.
- [ ] F3 [HL] The mRNA leaves the nucleus through a nuclear pore and travels to a ribosome in the cytoplasm (free or on rough endoplasmic reticulum). The DNA stays in the nucleus.
- [ ] F4 [HL] Translation: at the ribosome, the mRNA's codons are read, transfer RNA molecules bring the matching amino acids, and these are joined in the order set by the codons to make a specific polypeptide, which folds into a functional protein. (Steps and roles in "Transcription and translation".)
- [ ] F5 [HL] Why an mRNA copy is used: the DNA is kept safe in the nucleus and is not damaged by being used directly; many mRNA copies can be made from one gene and each read by many ribosomes, so a lot of protein can be made quickly; mRNA is broken down after use, so the cell can switch protein production on and off.
- [ ] F6 [HL] Exception: retroviruses such as HIV carry RNA and use the enzyme reverse transcriptase to make DNA from their RNA, so information flows from RNA to DNA (detail in "Viruses: replication"). Information never flows from protein back to nucleic acid.
- [ ] F7 [HL] Diagram: a flow diagram of a eukaryotic cell showing a gene on DNA in the nucleus → transcription → mRNA passing through a nuclear pore → ribosome in the cytoplasm → translation → polypeptide folding into a protein. Label: nucleus, DNA, gene, mRNA, nuclear pore, ribosome, tRNA bringing amino acid, polypeptide.

## 3. Traps

- Every block is Higher Level only; mark every heading.
- Don't repeat the Ordinary-level steps word for word: refer to "DNA replication and mitosis in the cell cycle" for the template idea and its role in the cell cycle, and give here the named enzymes, proofreading, energy, the semi-conservative model and its evidence.
- Don't teach antiparallel strands, 3′ and 5′ ends, leading and lagging strands, Okazaki fragments, primase, RNA primers or DNA ligase; they are not on the specification. The R8 diagram shows both new strands simply growing along their templates.
- Helicase separates strands; DNA polymerase builds the new strand. RNA polymerase is used in transcription, not replication. Don't swap them.
- Replication copies the whole DNA once per cycle; transcription copies one gene at a time, many times. Replication uses T; mRNA uses U.
- In S4, conservative would give heavy + light bands after one generation; dispersive would give a single band at every generation. Don't reverse which result ruled out which model.
- The roles of DNA, mRNA, tRNA, rRNA, RNA polymerase and ribosomes, tRNA structure and the steps of translation belong to "Transcription and translation"; F2–F4 are one bullet each. Codons and anticodons are in "The genetic code". Mutation types are in "Mutations".
- Diagram cues: R8, S5 and F7 only. No other diagram cues.
