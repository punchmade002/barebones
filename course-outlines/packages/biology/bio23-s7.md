## 1. Must cover

Prenatal and postnatal care (one short block)
- [ ] C1 Prenatal (antenatal) care is the health care of a pregnant woman and her developing baby before birth; postnatal care is the care of the mother and baby after birth (usually the first six weeks). Its aims are to keep mother and baby healthy, to find problems early, when they can be treated or managed, and to give the parents information to make decisions.
- [ ] C2 Screening vs diagnosis: a screening test is offered to everyone in a group (e.g. all pregnant women, all newborns) to find those at higher risk of a condition; it is quick, cheap and safe but not certain. A diagnostic test confirms whether a person actually has the condition; it is more accurate and may carry some risk. A positive screening result leads to a diagnostic test. (The same distinction is used for cancer in "Causes, prevention and treatment of cancer".)

Monitoring the mother's health (one block)
- [ ] M1 At the first (booking) visit early in pregnancy, the mother's blood is tested for: her blood group and Rhesus factor (a Rhesus-negative mother carrying a Rhesus-positive baby may be given anti-D injections to protect this and later babies; see "Blood"); haemoglobin, to detect anaemia, which is common in pregnancy and treated with iron; immunity to rubella; and infections that can pass to the baby, including HIV, hepatitis B and syphilis, so that treatment can greatly reduce the chance of passing them on.
- [ ] M2 At every visit her blood pressure is measured and her urine tested. High blood pressure with protein in the urine is a sign of pre-eclampsia, a condition in pregnancy that can be dangerous to mother and baby, so she is monitored closely and the baby may be delivered early. Glucose in the urine, or a raised blood glucose in a glucose tolerance test, may show gestational diabetes (diabetes that develops in pregnancy), which can make the baby grow too large; it is managed by diet and, if needed, insulin.
- [ ] M3 Advice and prevention: take folic acid (a B vitamin) daily from before conception until the 12th week, which reduces the risk of neural tube defects such as spina bifida (see "Vitamins"); don't smoke or drink alcohol; avoid foods that may carry harmful microorganisms (e.g. unpasteurised cheese and pâté, which can carry Listeria bacteria; undercooked meat, which can carry Toxoplasma); take medicines only on medical advice (see "Pregnancy" for substances that cross the placenta); eat a balanced diet.
- [ ] M4 Vaccines in pregnancy: the flu vaccine and the whooping cough (pertussis) vaccine are recommended. The mother's antibodies cross the placenta, so the baby is protected from whooping cough in the first weeks of life, before it can be vaccinated itself (passive immunity; see "Acquired immunity, prevention and treatment").

Monitoring the foetus (one block)
- [ ] F1 Ultrasound scanning: high-frequency sound waves are sent into the body and their echoes from tissues of different density form a moving image on a screen. It uses no ionising radiation (unlike X-rays, which are avoided in pregnancy), so it is regarded as safe for mother and foetus.
- [ ] F2 Uses of ultrasound: a dating scan early in pregnancy (around 11–14 weeks) confirms the pregnancy, the number of babies and the expected date of delivery, and checks for an ectopic pregnancy; an anomaly scan at about 20 weeks checks the baby's organs (e.g. heart, spine, brain, kidneys) and growth, and the position of the placenta; later scans check growth and the baby's position before birth. Finding a problem such as a heart defect before birth allows the birth to be planned in a hospital ready to treat the baby at once.
- [ ] F3 During labour the baby's heart rate and the mother's contractions are monitored (e.g. by a cardiotocograph, CTG), so that signs that the baby is short of oxygen are noticed and the birth can be speeded up, e.g. by Caesarean section (see "Pregnancy").

Genetic screening and diagnosis before birth (one block)
- [ ] G1 Genetic screening tests for genetic conditions or for carriers of them. Before or during pregnancy, couples with a family history of an inherited condition (e.g. cystic fibrosis) can have carrier testing to find out their chance of having an affected child (Punnett squares in "Genetic Inheritance"; the CF mutation in "Mutations").
- [ ] G2 Screening for chromosome conditions such as Down syndrome (trisomy 21; see "Mutations"). Combined test: an ultrasound measurement of the fluid at the back of the baby's neck (nuchal translucency) together with a blood test of the mother, at about 11–14 weeks, gives the chance (a probability) of Down syndrome, taking the mother's age into account. Non-invasive prenatal testing (NIPT): from about 10 weeks, a sample of the mother's blood is tested for the small fragments of DNA from the placenta that circulate in it; it is a much more accurate screening test for trisomy 21, 18 and 13, but a positive result still needs a diagnostic test. Neither test harms the pregnancy.
- [ ] G3 Diagnostic tests take cells from the baby's side of the pregnancy so its chromosomes or DNA can be examined (e.g. a karyotype; "Generating a DNA profile" and "DNA sequencing" for DNA methods). Chorionic villus sampling (CVS), at about 11–14 weeks: a sample of placental tissue is taken with a fine needle through the abdomen or a thin tube through the cervix. Amniocentesis, from about 15 weeks: a fine needle, guided by ultrasound, takes a sample of amniotic fluid, which contains cells shed by the foetus. Both give a definite result, but both carry a small risk of miscarriage (well under 1 in 100), so they are offered only when the chance of a condition is raised.
- [ ] G4 Preimplantation genetic testing: in IVF, one or a few cells are taken from each early embryo and tested for a known serious genetic condition, and only unaffected embryos are placed in the uterus. IVF is in "Fertility control and infertility"; the ethical questions in "Ethics of genetic technologies". One bullet.

Postnatal checks and screening (one block)
- [ ] P1 Apgar score: one minute and five minutes after birth, the baby is scored 0, 1 or 2 on each of five signs: heart rate, breathing, muscle tone, reflex response and skin colour (maximum 10). A low score shows that the baby needs help, e.g. with breathing.
- [ ] P2 Vitamin K is offered to every newborn, usually by injection, to prevent a rare but serious bleeding disorder (why newborns need it is in "Vitamins"). One bullet.
- [ ] P3 A full physical examination of the newborn checks, for example, the heart, the hips (for hip dysplasia, a loose or dislocated hip joint, treatable if found early), the eyes and, in boys, whether the testes have descended (see "The male reproductive system"). Newborn hearing screening is offered to all babies in Ireland, so deafness is found early and language development supported.
- [ ] P4 Later postnatal care: weight, length and head size are recorded on growth charts; a public health nurse visits the family at home in the first weeks; the mother's recovery and her mental health are checked (postnatal depression is common and treatable); breastfeeding is supported (benefits in "Hormones in reproduction" and "Acquired immunity, prevention and treatment"); and the baby's routine vaccinations begin at 2 months of age.

Newborn heel-prick screening in Ireland (one block; the Setting for PKU)
- [ ] H1 Every baby born in Ireland is offered newborn bloodspot screening, the "heel-prick test", with the parents' consent. When the baby is 3 to 5 days old (72–120 hours after birth), a midwife or public health nurse pricks the baby's heel and places drops of blood on a special card, which is sent to the national screening laboratory at Children's Health Ireland at Temple Street, Dublin.
- [ ] H2 Why the test is done: the conditions screened for are rare, serious, and show no signs at birth, but early treatment prevents or greatly reduces lasting harm. A positive screen leads to diagnostic tests.
- [ ] H3 Why at 3–5 days: by then the baby has been feeding on milk for a few days, so a substance that the baby can't break down (e.g. phenylalanine in PKU) has had time to build up in the blood to a detectable level; testing too early can miss it.
- [ ] H4 Phenylketonuria (PKU): screened for in Ireland since the 1960s, and the condition for which the blood-spot test was first developed (by Robert Guthrie in the USA; the card is often called a Guthrie card). The test measures phenylalanine in the blood spot; a high level shows the baby can't convert phenylalanine (cause in "Enzymes as catalysts"). PKU is inherited as a recessive condition. Treatment: a special diet low in phenylalanine, which means restricting high-protein foods and using special protein substitutes, from the first weeks of life and continued for life; the sweetener aspartame contains phenylalanine, which is why food labels warn people with PKU. Treated early, children develop normally; untreated, phenylalanine builds up and causes severe intellectual disability. Women with PKU need very strict diet control before and during pregnancy, because high phenylalanine harms the developing foetus.
- [ ] H5 Cystic fibrosis: also screened for by the heel-prick test (cause and mutation in "Mutations"). A raised level of a protein made by the pancreas in the blood spot is followed by DNA testing of the same spot for common CFTR mutations. Early diagnosis allows early treatment (physiotherapy to clear mucus, antibiotics, pancreatic enzymes with food, and newer drugs), which improves growth and lung health.
- [ ] H6 Congenital hypothyroidism: the baby's thyroid gland makes too little thyroxine (see "Glands, locations and hormones"). Untreated, it causes poor growth and intellectual disability. Treatment is a daily dose of thyroxine by mouth, which allows normal development.
- [ ] H7 Classical galactosaemia: the baby can't break down galactose, one of the two sugars in lactose (milk sugar; see "Structures of biomolecules"), because an enzyme in the pathway is missing. Galactose builds up as soon as the baby drinks milk and can quickly cause serious illness; treatment is a diet free of lactose and galactose (e.g. a soya-based formula instead of breast or cow's milk). The heel-prick programme also screens for a small number of other rare inherited conditions; name no others.

Evaluating the impact of modern technology (one comparison block; benefits and limits as paired points)
- [ ] E1 Benefits for health: problems in the mother (anaemia, pre-eclampsia, gestational diabetes, infections) and the baby (growth problems, heart and other defects, the baby's position) are found early and managed, so pregnancy and birth are now far safer for mothers and babies than in the past; newborn screening prevents intellectual disability and early death in PKU, galactosaemia and congenital hypothyroidism, and gives children with cystic fibrosis early treatment; parents and doctors can plan the birth and treatment in advance.
- [ ] E2 Technology after birth: neonatal intensive care allows many premature babies to survive. Incubators keep the baby warm and protected from infection; ventilators help the baby breathe; surfactant (a substance that stops the air sacs in the lungs collapsing, which premature lungs lack) can be given into the lungs; and phototherapy (blue light) breaks down bilirubin in babies with jaundice. These link to the late maturing of the lungs (see "Pregnancy").
- [ ] E3 Limits and costs: no screening test is perfect, so a false positive causes anxiety and further tests, and a false negative gives false reassurance; screening gives a chance, not a certainty, which can be hard to understand; invasive diagnostic tests (CVS, amniocentesis) carry a small risk of miscarriage; finding an untreatable condition can leave parents with difficult decisions; equipment, staff and intensive care are expensive, so access is unequal between and within countries; some very premature babies who survive have long-term health problems.
- [ ] E4 Ethical points (a few bullets, both sides): parents have the right to information about their pregnancy and to decide whether to have tests (informed consent; see the principles in "Science, scientists and society"); genetic tests raise questions about who can see the results and how they are stored (detail in "Ethics of genetic technologies"); some people with disabilities and their families argue that screening for conditions such as Down syndrome can send a negative message about people living with them, while others argue that information lets families prepare. Newborn blood-spot cards contain the baby's DNA, which raises questions about how long they are stored and who may use them. State these as arguments; don't take a side.

## 3. Traps

- Screening is not diagnosis (C2). The combined test and NIPT are screening tests; CVS and amniocentesis are diagnostic.
- Ultrasound is sound, not X-rays or radiation; don't say it "uses radiation".
- The heel-prick test is postnatal, not antenatal; it is a blood test, not a genetic test of the parents. (For cystic fibrosis a DNA test is done on the same blood spot.)
- PKU is treated by diet, not by medicine or enzyme injections; the diet is for life, not only in childhood.
- Don't re-explain the enzyme defect in PKU, or the CFTR mutation; point to "Enzymes as catalysts" and "Mutations".
- Rhesus disease is explained in "Blood"; give the antenatal test and anti-D in one phrase only.
- Don't describe abortion or Irish law on pregnancy; E3 and E4 refer to "difficult decisions" only.
- NIPT DNA comes mainly from the placenta, not from the baby's blood; say "DNA from the placenta".
- Harmful substances crossing the placenta are in "Pregnancy"; M3 gives the advice, not the list again.
- "Vitamins" already teaches that folic acid before and in early pregnancy reduces neural tube defects and that newborns are given vitamin K; M3 and P2 state these as parts of care in one phrase each, without re-explaining.

Model answer: two technologies described with their biology (e.g. F1–F2 ultrasound, H3–H4 heel-prick, G2 NIPT, E2 neonatal intensive care), at least two benefits from E1–E2 and at least two limits from E3, and one ethical point from E4.
