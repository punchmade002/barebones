## 1. Must cover

Novel viruses in animals and humans (one case-study block; one bullet per virus: what, where from, how it spread, what was learned)
- [ ] NV1 A novel virus is one not previously known to infect a species, usually because it has jumped from another host (spillover) or has changed by mutation or mixing of genes. Most new human viruses come from animals (zoonoses), especially bats, birds and other mammals; contact increases when people clear forests, hunt or trade wild animals, or farm animals intensively.
- [ ] NV2 HIV: crossed into humans from a related virus (SIV) in chimpanzees in central Africa in the early twentieth century, probably through hunting and butchering them for meat. It was identified in 1983 by Françoise Barré-Sinoussi and Luc Montagnier at the Institut Pasteur, Paris (Nobel Prize 2008). Its long symptom-free period let it spread worldwide unnoticed for decades. There is still no vaccine, but antiretroviral therapy turned it from a fatal disease into a long-term condition (see "Viruses: replication").
- [ ] NV3 SARS-CoV-2 (COVID-19): a coronavirus first identified in Wuhan, China, in December 2019. Spread by droplets and aerosols, including from people without symptoms, and carried worldwide by air travel. Its genome sequence was shared online in January 2020 (see "Science as a global enterprise"), and vaccines were developed and approved within a year. Mutations produced more transmissible variants, so vaccines were updated.
- [ ] NV4 Avian influenza (H5N1): a highly pathogenic bird flu virus that kills poultry and wild birds. A strain that spread worldwide from about 2020, carried by migrating wild birds, has infected many mammals, and in 2024 was found in dairy cattle in the USA. Human infections are rare and mostly in people in close contact with infected birds or animals. The concern is that mutation or mixing with a human flu virus could produce a strain that spreads between people. Control: culling infected flocks, keeping poultry housed when risk is high, and surveillance.
- [ ] NV5 Ebola virus: causes severe haemorrhagic fever; fruit bats are thought to be the natural reservoir. The 2014–2016 epidemic in West Africa killed more than 11,000 people. It spreads by contact with body fluids of sick or dead people, so caring for the sick and traditional burial practices spread it; it was controlled by isolation, contact tracing, safe burials and, later, a vaccine.
- [ ] NV6 Nipah virus: emerged in Malaysia in 1998–1999, passing from fruit bats to pigs (on farms near fruit trees) and from pigs to pig farmers, causing brain inflammation (encephalitis). It was controlled by culling over a million pigs. One bullet; it shows how farming changes create new routes from wildlife to people.
- [ ] NV7 Schmallenberg virus (an animal virus): identified in 2011 in Germany and named after the town where it was found. Spread by biting midges, it causes birth defects in lambs and calves when the mother is infected in early pregnancy; it spread across northern Europe, including Ireland, within about a year. Bluetongue virus (also midge-borne) is in "Emergence and spread of infectious disease".

Novel viruses in plants (one block)
- [ ] PV1 Tomato brown rugose fruit virus (ToBRFV): a tobamovirus (related to tobacco mosaic virus) first identified in the Middle East in the mid-2010s, which then spread to many countries. It infects tomatoes and peppers, causing wrinkled, discoloured fruit that can't be sold. It spreads by contact (hands, tools, clothing), in infected seed and in the trade in plants and fruit, and stays infectious on surfaces for long periods. It is serious because it overcomes the resistance genes bred into tomato varieties to protect them against related viruses. Control: EU plant-health rules on imported seed and plants, testing seed, hygiene and disinfection in glasshouses, destroying infected crops, and breeding new resistant varieties.
- [ ] PV2 Why plant viruses emerge: global trade in seeds and plants; monocultures of genetically uniform crops; insect vectors spreading into new areas as the climate warms; and mutation overcoming plants' resistance genes. Plants have no adaptive immune system and can't be vaccinated or cured of viral infection, so control depends on prevention: clean seed and stock, controlling vectors, removing infected plants, quarantine and resistant varieties.
- [ ] PV3 Economic and food-security importance: crop losses raise food prices and threaten farmers' incomes; diseases of staple crops are most serious for poor countries that depend on one crop. Tobacco mosaic virus and barley yellow dwarf virus are in "Viruses"; don't repeat them.

Autoimmune diseases (one block)
- [ ] AI1 An autoimmune disease is one in which the immune system attacks the body's own cells or tissues as if they were foreign, because it fails to tell self from non-self. Normally, lymphocytes that react to self are removed during maturation and the rest are held in check by suppressor (regulatory) T cells; in autoimmune disease this tolerance breaks down, and antibodies or killer T cells attack a self antigen.
- [ ] AI2 Examples (tissue attacked → effect → treatment): type 1 diabetes: T cells destroy the insulin-producing cells of the pancreas → high blood glucose → insulin injections for life (see "Glands, locations and hormones"); multiple sclerosis: the myelin sheath around nerve fibres in the brain and spinal cord is damaged → slowed or blocked impulses (see "Neurons"); rheumatoid arthritis: the lining of joints becomes inflamed → painful, swollen, damaged joints; coeliac disease: eating gluten (a protein in wheat, barley and rye) triggers an immune attack that damages the villi of the small intestine → poor absorption → a lifelong gluten-free diet; Graves' disease: antibodies stimulate the thyroid → too much thyroxine.
- [ ] AI3 Causes: a combination of inherited susceptibility (some alleles make it more likely, so these diseases run in families) and environmental triggers. Infections can trigger autoimmunity when a pathogen's antigen resembles a self antigen, so antibodies made against the pathogen also attack the body: e.g. rheumatic fever, in which antibodies against Streptococcus bacteria (from a throat infection) attack the heart valves; Guillain–Barré syndrome, a paralysis that can follow some infections, including Campylobacter food poisoning and Zika virus.
- [ ] AI4 Why they are grouped with emerging diseases: the number of people with several autoimmune diseases has been rising in many countries. One proposed explanation (the hygiene hypothesis) is that less early-life exposure to microorganisms leaves the immune system poorly regulated; it is supported by some evidence but not proven (the gut microbiome's role in immune signalling is in "The microbiome").
- [ ] AI5 Treatment: there is usually no cure. Treatments replace what is lost (insulin, thyroid hormone), avoid the trigger (gluten-free diet), or suppress the immune system with drugs (e.g. corticosteroids), which relieves symptoms but makes infections more likely.

Using knowledge of the factors to control increase (one block, one bullet per strategy, each linked to the factor it targets)
- [ ] S1 Surveillance and early detection: doctors and vets must report notifiable diseases; laboratories sequence pathogens' genomes to detect new variants and resistance; national and international agencies (e.g. the World Health Organization, the European Centre for Disease Prevention and Control) share data. Early detection lets control start while case numbers are low.
- [ ] S2 Reducing R (targets R₀, contact and transmission): isolation of cases, contact tracing and quarantine (reduce the infectious period in the community); masks, ventilation, hand hygiene and physical distancing (reduce the chance of transmission per contact); limiting mass gatherings (reduce contacts). The aim is to bring R below 1.
- [ ] S3 Raising immunity (targets the immunity level): vaccination campaigns aimed at the herd-immunity threshold, with priority for the most vulnerable; updating vaccines to match new variants (targets mutation).
- [ ] S4 Limiting mobility (targets movement): travel restrictions and screening; animal movement bans and culling during outbreaks of foot-and-mouth or avian influenza; disinfecting vehicles and footwear at farms.
- [ ] S5 Plant biosecurity: plant passports and import inspections, quarantine of imported plants, bans on importing high-risk plants (e.g. ash from infected areas), certified disease-free seed and planting material, removing and destroying infected plants, and breeding resistant varieties (counters genetic uniformity).
- [ ] S6 Fighting resistance (targets resistance): antimicrobial stewardship (prescribing antibiotics only when needed), infection control in hospitals, restricting antibiotic use in farm animals, rotating pesticides and fungicides, and developing new drugs.
- [ ] S7 Reducing spillover: controlling vectors; regulating wildlife trade and markets; protecting habitats; biosecurity on farms. The One Health approach treats human, animal and environmental health as linked and coordinates doctors, vets and ecologists.
- [ ] S8 Research and public information: understanding a new pathogen's transmission and R₀ tells authorities which measures will work; clear public information increases vaccine uptake and cooperation and counters misinformation (see "Evaluating evidence").

Why knowledge of emerging diseases matters to society (one discussion block: arguments, then tensions, then a judgement)
- [ ] D1 Health: early recognition and control save lives and prevent disability; knowing the factors lets health systems prepare (stockpiles, hospital capacity, vaccine platforms) before the next outbreak; protecting vulnerable groups depends on high immunity in everyone else.
- [ ] D2 Economic: epidemics close businesses, schools and travel and cost health systems heavily (e.g. COVID-19 from 2020); animal and plant diseases cause culling, lost exports and crop losses (e.g. the 2001 foot-and-mouth epidemic in Britain); prevention and early control usually cost far less than a large outbreak.
- [ ] D3 Food security and the environment: plant and animal diseases threaten food supply, especially where people depend on one crop (the Irish Great Famine shows the human cost, in "Emergence and spread of infectious disease"); diseases of wild species threaten biodiversity (chytrid in amphibians, ash dieback removing a native tree species and the organisms that depend on it).
- [ ] D4 Informed citizens: understanding how diseases spread and why vaccines and antibiotics must be used carefully lets people make informed choices, follow public-health advice and judge claims in the media.
- [ ] D5 Global cooperation: pathogens ignore borders, so a disease anywhere is a risk everywhere; sharing data, genome sequences and vaccines between countries benefits all (see "Science as a global enterprise").
- [ ] D6 Tensions and costs (arguments on the other side): control measures limit personal freedom (quarantine, travel bans, lockdowns), and their social and economic costs (lost income and education, isolation and poor mental health) fall unequally; culling destroys farmers' livelihoods and healthy animals; vaccine requirements raise questions of consent; poorer countries have less access to vaccines and drugs; acting early on uncertain evidence may later look like over-reaction, while waiting for certainty lets a disease spread (the precautionary principle; see "Science, scientists and society").
- [ ] D7 Judgement (one bullet): knowledge of emerging diseases matters because it allows targeted, proportionate measures, which cost less and do less harm than either doing nothing or applying blanket restrictions; the more is known about a disease's factors, the more precisely its spread can be limited.

## 3. Traps

- Don't re-explain the factors or R₀; refer to "Emergence and spread of infectious disease".
- An autoimmune disease is not an infectious disease and isn't caught from another person; an infection can trigger one (AI3). Type 1 diabetes is autoimmune; type 2 is not.
- AIDS is an immune deficiency caused by a virus, not an autoimmune disease.
- Don't state where SARS-CoV-2 came from (debated). Say "first identified in Wuhan".
- The hygiene hypothesis is a hypothesis, not established fact. Don't say vaccines cause autoimmune disease.
- Don't present H5N1 as currently spreading between humans.
- Plant viruses can't be cured or vaccinated against; don't suggest antibiotics or vaccines for plants.
- Ash dieback and chytrid are fungal, not viral: use them only as examples of emerging diseases in D3, not as "novel viruses".
- Individual prevention (hygiene, food safety, vaccination types) is in "Acquired immunity, prevention and treatment"; ethics principles and the precautionary principle in "Science, scientists and society"; media claims in "Evaluating evidence". Refer, don't restate.
