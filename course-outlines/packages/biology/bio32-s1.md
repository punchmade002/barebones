# Package: bio32-s1 — Collection and identification

- **loId:** `bio32-s1` · **chapterId:** `bio32` · **syllabusRef:** 3.1.11
- **Syllabus:** "ecological investigation: observation and collection methods (traps, pooter,
  nets, etc.); identify organisms." Outcome 11: "use primary data gathered from a chosen
  ecosystem to: model a habitat in the ecosystem including its size, species, relevant biotic
  and abiotic factors; investigate quantitatively the impact of variation in abiotic factors on
  the distribution and abundance of a species; explain the feeding and symbiotic relationships
  that occur between organisms." This subsection supplies the observation, collection and
  identification parts. Ordinary and Higher level. No Higher Level only material.
- **Prepared by:** a higher model, 2026-10-29. The chosen ecosystem is a grassland (a
  permanent pasture, school playing field edge or meadow), as the outline directs; one block
  says how the methods change for a woodland, rocky shore and freshwater habitat. Write it as
  the first part of one ecological investigation, in the NOTES_STANDARD "Investigation" style,
  in these blocks: planning the study; observation; collection methods (two blocks:
  ground and soil animals; animals in vegetation and flying animals); handling collected
  organisms; identification (two blocks: keys, with a worked example; other aids and typical
  grassland species); other habitats; safety and the code of conduct; sources of error. The
  terms ecosystem, habitat, population, community, biotic and abiotic factors are taught in
  "Biosphere, ecosystems, habitats and biodiversity"; use them without redefining them.

## 1. Must cover

Planning the ecosystem study (one block)
- [ ] P1 Aim: to find out which organisms live in a chosen grassland habitat, to collect and identify them, and so to build a species list that the surveys, abiotic measurements and feeding-relationship work are based on. Data gathered first-hand in the field are primary data.
- [ ] P2 The full study has these stages, each in its own note: 1. a preliminary visit to choose and map the study area (mapping in "Surveys"); 2. observe, collect and identify the organisms (this note); 3. a qualitative survey (species list) and a quantitative survey (how many of each) ("Surveys"); 4. measure abiotic factors and relate them to the distribution and abundance of a species ("Abiotic factors"); 5. work out feeding and symbiotic relationships ("Biotic factors and relationships"); 6. present the results and draw conclusions.
- [ ] P3 Preliminary visit: walk over the site; note its boundaries, main features (hedges, trees, paths, wet patches, slope, gate); get permission from the landowner; check access and hazards; choose suitable dates (organisms change with the season, so collect over several visits if possible, e.g. late spring to early autumn, when most plants flower and most invertebrates are active).
- [ ] P4 Equipment list (one bullet): pitfall traps (jars, trowel, flat stones or lids), pooter, sweep net, beating tray and stick, Tullgren funnel, collecting jars and labelled specimen tubes, white trays, hand lens, identification keys and field guides, camera or phone, notebook and pencil, labels, gloves, tape measures, quadrats (for "Surveys").
- [ ] P5 Variables (to make collection a fair comparison when two areas or times are compared): the independent variable is the area or time sampled; the dependent variable is the kinds and numbers of organisms caught; controlled variables are the type and number of traps, the trapping time, the number of sweeps, the time of day and the weather. No true control is possible in a field study, because the conditions can't be held constant.

Observation (one block)
- [ ] O1 Direct observation is used for organisms that are large, fast or that should not be caught, e.g. birds, rabbits, foxes and butterflies, and for all plants, which are identified where they grow and not dug up. Use binoculars for birds and mammals at a distance and a hand lens (about ×10) for small features such as the hairs or veins of a leaf.
- [ ] O2 Indirect signs show animals that are rarely seen: droppings, tracks in mud, burrows, nests, feathers, feeding signs (e.g. nibbled leaves, holes in leaves made by slugs or caterpillars, broken snail shells beside a stone used by a song thrush), molehills and wormcasts.
- [ ] O3 Record each observation at once: the organism (or "unknown species A" with a sketch or photograph until it is identified), where it was found (microhabitat, e.g. under a stone, on a dandelion leaf), the time, the weather and what it was doing (e.g. feeding on what).
- [ ] O4 Photographs record organisms without removing them and can be checked later against keys; record the location and date with each image.

Collection methods: ground and soil animals (one block, one item per method: what it catches, how it is used, care needed)
- [ ] C1 Pitfall trap: a jar or plastic cup sunk into a hole in the ground so its rim is exactly level with the soil surface, so that animals walking across the ground fall in and can't climb the smooth sides. Cover it with a flat stone or lid raised on small stones, which keeps out rain and stops birds and other predators taking the catch. Bait (e.g. a small piece of meat or fruit) can be added. Catches ground-dwelling (crawling) invertebrates: ground beetles, spiders, woodlice, slugs, ants, millipedes. Check traps at least every 24 hours (ideally early morning), or predators such as ground beetles and spiders eat the other animals in the trap; remove all traps at the end of the study so no animal is trapped later.
- [ ] C2 Diagram: a pitfall trap in section: jar in the soil with rim level with the surface, raised flat stone cover on small supports, bait, and animals inside; label soil surface, jar, cover, bait.
- [ ] C3 Tullgren funnel: extracts small animals from a sample of soil or leaf litter. The sample sits on a wire mesh (gauze) in a funnel under a lamp (a light bulb about 10–20 cm above it). The heat and light dry the sample from the top, and the animals move down away from the heat, light and dryness, falling through the mesh into a collecting beaker below. The beaker holds damp tissue paper to keep them alive (or ethanol if they are to be preserved for identification). Leave it for several hours to a day. Catches mites, springtails, small beetles and their larvae, small worms. The mesh must be fine enough to hold back the soil; too hot a lamp kills the animals before they leave.
- [ ] C4 Diagram: a Tullgren funnel: lamp, soil or leaf-litter sample, wire gauze, funnel, collecting beaker with damp tissue (or ethanol), and an arrow showing animals moving down away from the heat and light.
- [ ] C5 Earthworms are collected by digging a measured block of soil and sorting it by hand on a white tray, or by watering a marked area with a mustard-powder solution, which irritates them so they come to the surface. Rinse them in clean water at once.
- [ ] C6 Small-mammal trap: a baited box with dry bedding and food inside; the door closes behind the animal, which is unharmed. Used only under teacher supervision; check it at least every few hours and release the animal where it was caught. Some mammal species are protected by law, so trapping may need a licence. Mammals in grassland are more often recorded by observation and signs (O1–O2).

Collection methods: animals in vegetation and flying animals (one block)
- [ ] V1 Sweep net: a strong net with a fine cloth bag on a stout frame and handle, swept quickly through long grass and low plants in a series of side-to-side arcs while walking slowly, so animals on the plants are knocked into the bag. Then fold the bag over to close it and empty it onto a white tray or into a jar. Catches grasshoppers, aphids, flies, beetles, spiders, caterpillars and leafhoppers. Use a fixed number of sweeps (e.g. ten) in each area so that catches can be compared. It doesn't work in wet grass, which soaks the bag and damages the animals.
- [ ] V2 Beating tray: a white sheet or tray (or an upturned umbrella) held under a branch of a hedge, shrub or tree at the grassland edge; the branch is struck sharply with a stick a set number of times, and animals living on it fall onto the tray, where they are easy to see against the white. Catches caterpillars, beetles, spiders, ladybirds, shield bugs. They are then collected with a pooter.
- [ ] V3 Pooter: a clear container (jar or tube) closed by a stopper through which two tubes pass. The user places the end of the long inlet tube next to a small animal and sucks on the mouthpiece tube; the animal is drawn up the inlet tube into the container. A piece of fine gauze (muslin) over the inner end of the mouthpiece tube stops the animal or debris being sucked into the mouth. Used for small, fast-moving animals (small insects, spiders, springtails) from a beating tray, sweep net, the ground or bark. Empty it often, as animals inside can harm each other.
- [ ] V4 Diagram: a pooter: container, stopper, inlet tube, mouthpiece tube, gauze over the inner end of the mouthpiece tube, arrows for the air flow and the animal entering.
- [ ] V5 Butterfly net (a light, fine-meshed net on a long handle) catches flying insects such as butterflies and bees; hold the net closed with a twist of the bag and release the insect after identification. Bees and wasps can sting, so many are identified by observation instead.
- [ ] V6 Light trap (moth trap): a bright lamp (often ultraviolet) above a box with egg trays inside, left on overnight; night-flying insects, mainly moths, are attracted to the light, fall into the box and settle in the trays. They are identified and released next morning, away from birds.

Handling and recording collected organisms (one block)
- [ ] H1 Place animals in labelled jars or specimen tubes with a little vegetation, keep them cool and shaded, and identify them as soon as possible on a white tray with a hand lens.
- [ ] H2 Record for each: the name (or "species A"), the number caught, the method and place of capture, and the date and time. This is the raw primary data used in "Surveys".
- [ ] H3 Return every animal to the place where it was caught, after identification; collect plants only when they are common and can't be identified where they grow, taking a single leaf or flower rather than the whole plant.

Identification: keys (one block, with a worked example)
- [ ] K1 An identification key is a tool for naming an unknown organism by working through a series of questions or statements about its visible features. A dichotomous key ("dichotomous" means dividing in two) gives a pair of contrasting statements at each step (a couplet); the user chooses the statement that fits the organism and follows the number or name it leads to, until a name is reached.
- [ ] K2 Good keys use features that are easy to see and don't vary between individuals or with age or season: number of legs, number of body parts, wings present or absent, shell present or absent, leaf shape and arrangement, flower colour and number of petals. They avoid size, which varies with age, and vague words such as "large".
- [ ] K3 Worked example: a key to five grassland invertebrates.
  1. Legs present → go to 2. No legs → go to 4.
  2. Three pairs of legs (6 legs); body in three parts (head, thorax, abdomen) → insect (e.g. a ground beetle). More than 6 legs → go to 3.
  3. Four pairs of legs (8 legs); body in two parts → spider. Seven pairs of legs (14 legs); body flattened and covered with overlapping plates → woodlouse.
  4. Body made of many ring-like segments → earthworm. Body not segmented, soft and slimy → go to 5.
  5. Shell present → snail. No shell → slug.
  Using it: an animal with 14 legs goes 1 → 2 → 3 → woodlouse. Write the key as numbered couplets in the notes.
- [ ] K4 Making a key: list the organisms; choose a feature that splits them into two groups; keep splitting each group with a new feature until each organism stands alone; check the key by keying out each organism in turn. A tree diagram (branching chart) of the same splits is another way to show a key.

Identification: other aids and grassland species (one block)
- [ ] I1 Other aids: illustrated field guides and identification charts (e.g. of wild flowers, grasses, insects and birds); image-recognition apps on a phone, whose suggestions must be checked against a key or guide because they are often wrong for small invertebrates; comparison with labelled specimens or photographs; asking an expert (a teacher or naturalist). A hand lens or a stereo (dissecting) microscope shows small features.
- [ ] I2 Identify each organism to species if possible. Where that isn't possible, name the group (e.g. "a ground beetle") and give it a code (species A, B …) used consistently throughout the study, so it can still be counted in the surveys.
- [ ] I3 Typical plants of an Irish grassland: grasses (e.g. perennial ryegrass in pasture), daisy, dandelion, creeping buttercup, white clover, ribwort plantain, and, in damp patches, rushes. Daisy and dandelion grow as flat rosettes of leaves close to the ground.
- [ ] I4 Typical animals of an Irish grassland: invertebrates such as earthworms, slugs, snails, ground beetles, spiders, woodlice (in damp places under stones or logs), ants, aphids, grasshoppers, ladybirds, caterpillars, butterflies and bumblebees; vertebrates such as rabbits, the Irish hare, foxes, badgers, hedgehogs, wood mice, pygmy shrews, and birds such as the song thrush, blackbird and kestrel.

How methods change for other habitats (one block, one bullet per habitat)
- [ ] X1 Woodland: beating trays under branches and pitfall traps in the leaf litter are the main methods; leaf litter samples go in a Tullgren funnel; trees are identified by leaf, bark and fruit; the layers (canopy, shrub, herb and ground layers) are recorded separately.
- [ ] X2 Rocky shore: animals are mostly collected by hand from under seaweed, in rock pools and under stones (stones turned back the right way up), or simply counted where they are, as many are attached (barnacles, limpets, mussels); seaweeds are identified with a key; work at low tide, ideally a spring low tide, checking tide tables first and starting at the lowest point as the tide goes out.
- [ ] X3 Freshwater (stream or pond): a pond net is swept through the water and vegetation; in a stream, kick sampling is used: the net is held on the bed just downstream while the stones upstream are disturbed with the feet for a fixed time, so animals are washed into the net. The catch is emptied into a white tray of water and identified (e.g. mayfly, stonefly and caddis fly larvae, freshwater shrimps, snails, leeches). A plankton net (very fine mesh) is towed slowly through the water to collect microscopic organisms. Animals are kept in water from the same habitat and returned quickly.

Safety and the code of conduct (one block)
- [ ] S1 Safety: work in groups, never alone, and tell someone where you are going; wear suitable clothes and footwear for the weather; take care near livestock, electric fences, nettles and brambles; wear gloves when handling soil, leaf litter and traps, and cover cuts; wash hands before eating; check for ticks after working in long grass, because ticks can carry Lyme disease (see "Pathogens"); be aware of anyone with an allergy to bee or wasp stings.
- [ ] S2 Safety near water: never work alone near water; check the depth and the current before entering a stream; at the shore, know the tide times, watch for incoming tides and wet, slippery rocks covered with seaweed; cover cuts and wash hands after contact with fresh water, which can carry the bacteria that cause leptospirosis (Weil's disease), spread in the urine of rats.
- [ ] S3 Code of conduct (protecting the habitat and its organisms): get permission from the landowner; close gates; keep disturbance and trampling to a minimum; replace stones and logs the way they were found; handle animals gently and return them to where they were caught; don't pick or uproot plants unnecessarily; never collect protected or rare species; remove all traps and litter at the end of the study.

Sources of error and limitations (one block)
- [ ] E1 Each collection method catches only some kinds of organism (pitfall traps catch only animals that walk on the ground; sweep nets only animals on tall plants), so several methods must be used to build a complete species list.
- [ ] E2 Catches depend on the weather, the time of day and the season: many animals are nocturnal or are active only in warm, dry or damp conditions, so one visit misses many species. Repeat collections on several days and at different times, under similar conditions when comparing areas.
- [ ] E3 Animals in traps may escape or be eaten by others before they are counted (check traps often); birds may take the catch from uncovered traps.
- [ ] E4 Misidentification, especially of small invertebrates and of grasses that are not in flower; check identifications with a second key or guide, or with the teacher.
- [ ] E5 Disturbance by the collectors (trampling, noise) changes animals' behaviour and damages the habitat.

## 3. Traps

- The pooter's gauze is on the mouthpiece tube, not the inlet tube; students often draw it on the wrong tube.
- A pitfall trap's rim must be level with the ground, not sticking up; a raised rim stops animals falling in.
- In a Tullgren funnel the animals move down away from the lamp; the lamp doesn't "attract" them.
- Plants are identified where they grow, not collected by pulling them up; animals are returned.
- Quadrats, percentage cover and frequency, transects, capture–recapture and the habitat map belong to "Surveys"; give only the stage list in P2. Measuring abiotic factors belongs to "Abiotic factors". Feeding relationships, predation, competition and symbiosis belong to "Biotic factors and relationships".
- Food chains, food webs, trophic levels and pyramids are theory in "Energy and nutrient transfer", "Pyramids of numbers" and "Pyramids of biomass" (Ecology, Ecosystems and Biodiversity); don't teach them here.
- The previous syllabus required students to collect and identify a set number of plants and animals and to write up one adaptation per organism; the specification sets no number and asks only for collection methods and identification. Don't state a required number.
- Diseases spread by ticks and in water get one phrase each (S1, S2); pathogens are in "Pathogens".
- The diagram cues in C2, C4 and V4 are the only ones wanted. No other diagram cues.

## 4. What exams ask (topics only)

Past papers (previous syllabus) asked these, and they remain on the specification:
- What could help ecologists identify organisms (a key); use or make a simple key.
- Name and describe a collection method for a named animal; draw and label a pitfall trap or a pooter; give a precaution when using it.
New on this specification, expect: link each method to the type of organism it collects; explain how methods change for a different habitat; safety and the ethical code of conduct for fieldwork; limitations of collection methods.

## 5. Practice questions to write

1. 6 marks: "Name a piece of apparatus used to collect (i) animals that crawl on the ground and (ii) small insects from a beating tray, and give one precaution taken when using each." Model answer: C1 (pitfall trap; rim level with soil, cover, check every 24 h) and V3 (pooter; gauze on mouthpiece, empty often).
2. 9 marks: "Describe how a Tullgren funnel is used to collect animals from leaf litter, and explain why the animals move into the collecting vessel." Model answer: C3 set-up and method in steps, with the C4 diagram; must say the animals move down away from heat, light and drying; damp tissue keeps them alive.
3. 12 marks: "Explain what a dichotomous key is. Construct a key to separate a spider, a woodlouse, an insect and a slug, and give two other ways an ecologist might identify an organism found in a grassland." Model answer: K1 definition, a key in numbered couplets using K3 features (legs present or not; number of legs), and two aids from I1. Must not use size or colour as the separating features.
