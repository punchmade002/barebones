## 1. Must cover

Water in living things and its properties (one block)
- [ ] W1 Water is the most abundant substance in living things: it makes up about 60% of the mass of an adult human body and most of the mass of cytoplasm; many plant tissues and jellyfish are over 90% water. Organisms die quickly without it.
- [ ] W2 (assumed) A water molecule (H₂O) is polar: the oxygen end carries a slight negative charge and the hydrogen ends slight positive charges. Neighbouring molecules are attracted to each other by hydrogen bonds.
- [ ] W3 (assumed) Because it is polar, water is an excellent solvent: ions (e.g. Na⁺, Cl⁻) and polar molecules (e.g. glucose, amino acids) dissolve in it. Lipids are non-polar and don't dissolve.
- [ ] W4 (assumed) Hydrogen bonding gives water a high specific heat capacity (it takes a lot of energy to raise its temperature) and a high latent heat of vaporisation (a lot of energy is absorbed when it evaporates). It also makes water molecules stick to each other (cohesion) and to other surfaces (adhesion).
- [ ] W5 Diagram: two or three water molecules with δ+ on the hydrogens, δ− on the oxygen, and a dotted hydrogen bond between them.

Medium for chemical reactions (one block)
- [ ] R1 Almost all metabolic reactions take place in solution in water, in the cytoplasm and inside organelles, because dissolved molecules can move, collide and meet enzymes.
- [ ] R2 Water is also a reactant: it is used in photosynthesis (split to release oxygen) and in every hydrolysis reaction, including digestion. It is a product of respiration and of every condensation reaction.

Medium for transport (one block)
- [ ] T1 Animals: blood plasma is mainly water and carries dissolved glucose, amino acids, mineral ions, hormones, urea and carbon dioxide around the body; lymph and tissue fluid are also watery. Urea and excess salts are excreted dissolved in water as urine.
- [ ] T2 Plants: water carries dissolved minerals up the xylem from the roots to the leaves, and sucrose and amino acids through the phloem. Cohesion between water molecules holds the column together in the xylem (detail in "Transport in Plants").
- [ ] T3 Substances move into and out of cells by diffusion through the watery fluid around them (detail in "Transport Across Membranes").

Regulation of temperature (one block)
- [ ] H1 High specific heat capacity: water heats up and cools down slowly, so the water in an organism's body buffers it against sudden temperature changes, and lakes and seas give aquatic organisms a stable temperature.
- [ ] H2 Cooling by evaporation: because water absorbs a lot of heat when it evaporates, sweating in mammals cools the skin (detail in "Homeostasis"), panting cools dogs, and transpiration cools leaves.
- [ ] H3 Ice is less dense than liquid water, so it floats and forms an insulating layer on ponds and lakes, letting organisms survive beneath it in winter. One bullet.

Regulation of pH (one block)
- [ ] P1 pH depends on the concentration of hydrogen ions (H⁺) in a solution; pure water is neutral (pH 7). Acids and bases exert their effects only when dissolved in water.
- [ ] P2 Buffers (e.g. hydrogencarbonate and phosphate ions; see "Minerals") work dissolved in body water, keeping the pH of cells and blood almost constant, so enzymes keep their shape and activity.
- [ ] P3 Water is the medium by which excess H⁺ ions are removed: the kidneys excrete them dissolved in urine, and carbon dioxide (which forms an acid in solution) is carried in the blood plasma to the lungs and breathed out.

Osmoregulation (one block)
- [ ] O1 Osmoregulation is the control of the water and salt (solute) concentration of body fluids. Water moves into or out of cells by osmosis, from a region of higher water concentration to lower water concentration through a selectively permeable membrane (detail in "Transport Across Membranes").
- [ ] O2 Animal cells placed in pure water swell and may burst; in strong salt solution they shrink. So the concentration of the blood and tissue fluid must be kept steady. In humans the kidneys do this, adjusting the volume of water in urine under the control of ADH (detail in "The Urinary System").
- [ ] O3 Plant cells have a strong cell wall, so water entering by osmosis makes them turgid (swollen and firm) instead of bursting. Turgor pressure supports non-woody plants and leaves and opens stomata; when water is lost, cells become flaccid and the plant wilts.
- [ ] O4 Single-celled freshwater organisms such as Amoeba constantly gain water by osmosis and expel it with a contractile vacuole.

Other roles (one bullet)
- [ ] X1 Lubrication (synovial fluid in joints, mucus, saliva helping swallowing); support (turgor in plants; the fluid pressure of a hydrostatic skeleton, e.g. in earthworms); a habitat for aquatic organisms; medium for fertilisation in some organisms (e.g. moss and fern sperm swim in water).

## 3. Traps

- Say "about 60%" for the human body; don't give other percentages.
- Osmosis moves water, not solutes; write "higher to lower water concentration" (or "lower to higher solute concentration"), not "high to low concentration" unqualified.
- Plant cells don't burst in pure water: the cell wall prevents it.
- Osmosis detail and its investigation belong to "Transport Across Membranes"; kidney and ADH detail to "The Urinary System"; xylem and transpiration to "Transport in Plants"; sweating as a homeostatic response to "Homeostasis". Each appears here as one sentence.
- Germination's need for water was on the previous syllabus; don't use germination as an example.
- The diagram cue in W5 is the only one wanted. No other diagram cues.

- Explain how water helps regulate body temperature.
