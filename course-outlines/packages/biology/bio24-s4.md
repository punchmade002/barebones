## 1. Must cover

Dispersal and why it matters (one block)
- [ ] D1 Dispersal is the carrying of fruits and seeds away from the parent plant. Plants can't move, so dispersal is how they spread their offspring.
- [ ] D2 Reasons: it reduces competition between the young plants and the parent, and among the young plants themselves, for light, water, minerals and space; it lets the species colonise new areas and new habitats; it spreads the population, so a local disaster (e.g. disease, fire or flooding) is less likely to destroy all of it.
- [ ] D3 Seeds that fall directly under the parent are usually shaded and short of water and minerals, so few survive. Plants produce many seeds because most are lost during dispersal or eaten.
- [ ] D4 Sustainability link (one bullet): the ability of some plants to spread their seeds widely makes them invasive when introduced to new countries, e.g. Himalayan balsam (an invasive plant along Irish rivers, with exploding seed pods).

Fruits (assumed; one block)
- [ ] F1 A fruit is a mature ovary containing seeds; its wall is the pericarp. It protects the seeds and is adapted to disperse them. Botanically, many foods called vegetables are fruits, e.g. tomatoes, cucumbers and pea pods.
- [ ] F2 Fleshy (succulent) fruits have a soft, juicy pericarp, often brightly coloured and sweet when ripe, e.g. blackberry, tomato, cherry; they are mostly dispersed by animals that eat them. Dry fruits have a hard or papery pericarp, e.g. pea pod, hazelnut, sycamore; they are dispersed by wind, water, animals carrying them, or by splitting open.
- [ ] F3 A false fruit is one in which other parts of the flower, not only the ovary, form the fruit, e.g. strawberry, where the fleshy part is the receptacle. One bullet.

Wind dispersal (one block)
- [ ] W1 Adaptations: fruits or seeds are small and light, or have wings or parachutes that increase their surface area so they fall slowly and are carried further by the wind.
- [ ] W2 Parachutes: dandelion and thistle fruits each carry a parachute of fine hairs that lets them float long distances.
- [ ] W3 Wings: sycamore has paired winged fruits and ash has single winged fruits; the wing makes the fruit spin like a helicopter blade as it falls, slowing it so the wind carries it away from the parent.
- [ ] W4 Censer (pepper-pot) mechanism: the poppy's dry fruit (capsule) has a ring of pores near the top; when the wind sways the stalk, the tiny seeds are shaken out a few at a time.
- [ ] W5 Dust-like seeds: orchid seeds are so tiny and light that they are blown like dust.

Water dispersal (one block)
- [ ] A1 Adaptations: fruits or seeds that float, because they contain air spaces or have a light, waterproof covering, so they can be carried by streams, rivers or the sea.
- [ ] A2 Coconut: the fruit has a thick, fibrous, air-filled husk and a waterproof outer layer, so it floats for long periods and is carried by ocean currents to other shores and islands.
- [ ] A3 Alder: a tree of riverbanks whose small fruits contain air and float, and are carried downstream to settle on new banks.

Animal dispersal, internal (one block)
- [ ] I1 Fleshy fruits are eaten by birds and mammals. The animal digests the fleshy pericarp, but the seeds have a tough testa (or a hard stone) that resists digestion; they pass out in the faeces, often far from the parent, with the droppings acting as a supply of minerals.
- [ ] I2 Adaptations: bright colours (often red, black or purple) when ripe, attracting birds; sweet, juicy, nutritious flesh as a reward; seeds that are unripe and the fruit green and sour until the seeds are mature, so the fruit isn't eaten too early.
- [ ] I3 Examples: blackberry, holly berries and rowan berries (eaten by birds); tomato.
- [ ] I4 Animal caching (one bullet): squirrels and jays bury acorns and hazelnuts as winter stores and fail to recover some, which then grow into new trees.

Animal dispersal, external (one block)
- [ ] E1 Fruits have hooks or sticky surfaces that catch on the fur or feathers of passing animals (or on human clothing); they fall or are brushed off later, away from the parent.
- [ ] E2 Examples: burdock has hooked burs; goosegrass (cleavers) has small fruits covered in hooked hairs.
- [ ] E3 Technology link: the hooked burs of burdock inspired Velcro, invented by the Swiss engineer George de Mestral after he found burs stuck to his dog's coat.

Self-dispersal (one bullet)
- [ ] S1 Some dry fruits split open forcefully as they dry, flinging the seeds out: e.g. gorse and pea pods twist and burst in dry weather; Himalayan balsam pods explode when touched.

Summary (one comparison block, one bullet per method)
- [ ] M1 Wind: wings, parachutes, light seeds (sycamore, ash, dandelion, poppy). Water: floating, air-filled fruits (coconut, alder). Animal internal: fleshy, coloured, edible fruits with indigestible seeds (blackberry, holly). Animal external: hooks (burdock, goosegrass). Self: exploding pods (gorse, pea).
- [ ] M2 Diagram: labelled drawings of one fruit for each method: sycamore (wing, seed), dandelion (parachute of hairs, fruit containing a seed), burdock bur (hooks), blackberry (fleshy fruit, seeds); a coconut in section (fibrous air-filled husk, seed) is optional.

## 3. Traps

- Dandelion, sycamore and ash are dispersed as fruits (each with a seed inside), not as bare seeds; write "fruit".
- Dispersal is not pollination: dispersal moves fruits and seeds; pollination moves pollen.
- Internal animal dispersal depends on seeds surviving digestion; don't say the animal "plants" the seed.
- Germination, dormancy and vegetative propagation (runners, tubers, bulbs, cuttings) are not on this specification. Dispersal by runners is vegetative, not seed dispersal; don't include it.
- Fruit formation from the ovary and growth regulators belong to "Flower structure and pollination" and "Seeds"; F1 to F3 give only what dispersal needs.
- The diagram cue M2 is the only one wanted. No other diagram cues.

## 4. What exams ask (topics only)

- Define dispersal and give reasons why it is important to plants.
- Name a method of dispersal and give a named example, with the adaptation that suits it.
- Explain how fleshy fruits are dispersed by animals.
- Classify given fruits (from diagrams) by their method of dispersal.
