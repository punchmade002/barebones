## 1. Must cover

Why industry uses enzymes (one block)
- [ ] W1 Specificity: an enzyme catalyses one reaction, so it makes the wanted product with few unwanted by-products, giving a purer product that needs less purification.
- [ ] W2 Mild conditions: enzymes work at moderate temperatures (often 30–60 °C), normal pressure and near-neutral pH, whereas many chemical catalysts need high temperatures and pressures. This saves energy and money, reduces greenhouse gas emissions, and makes the process safer.
- [ ] W3 Enzymes are efficient catalysts that are not used up, and they are biodegradable, so they leave less harmful waste than many chemical catalysts.
- [ ] W4 Sources: most industrial enzymes are made by microorganisms (bacteria and fungi) grown in large vessels called fermenters or bioreactors, because microorganisms grow fast, can be grown on cheap nutrients, and many release (secrete) their enzymes into the medium, where they are easy to collect. Many are now made by genetically modified microorganisms carrying the gene for the enzyme (see "Genetic engineering"). A few come from plants (e.g. papain from papaya) or animals (e.g. rennet from calves' stomachs).
- [ ] W5 Limitations: enzymes are denatured by high temperatures and extreme pH, so conditions must be carefully controlled; they can be expensive to produce and purify; free enzymes are lost in the product after one use (solved by immobilisation, I1); and enzyme dust can cause allergies (in the early years of biological detergents, some factory workers developed allergies, so enzymes are now sealed in granules). Enzymes from microorganisms that live in hot springs or alkaline conditions are chosen or engineered for processes that need tougher enzymes.

Food and beverages (one block, one bullet per use)
- [ ] F1 Cheese-making: chymosin (rennin), the active enzyme in rennet, clots the milk protein casein so the milk separates into solid curds (made into cheese) and liquid whey. Rennet was traditionally extracted from the stomachs of young calves; much of the chymosin used today is made by genetically modified microorganisms, which suits vegetarians and needs no animals.
- [ ] F2 Lactose-free milk: lactase breaks lactose down into glucose and galactose, so people with lactose intolerance (see "Enzymes as catalysts") can drink the milk. It also tastes sweeter, as glucose and galactose are sweeter than lactose. Lactase is often used immobilised.
- [ ] F3 Fruit juice: pectinase breaks down pectin, which helps hold plant cell walls together, so more juice is released from crushed fruit and the juice is clearer (less cloudy).
- [ ] F4 Sugar syrups: amylases break starch (e.g. from maize) into glucose syrup; immobilised glucose isomerase then converts glucose into fructose, which is much sweeter, giving high-fructose syrup used to sweeten soft drinks and processed foods, so less is needed for the same sweetness.
- [ ] F5 Brewing and baking: amylases break starch in grain into maltose and glucose, which yeast ferments to make ethanol and carbon dioxide (fermentation is in "Respiration"); in baking this helps the dough rise; proteases are used to clear haze from beer.
- [ ] F6 Other uses: proteases (e.g. papain) tenderise meat by breaking down muscle proteins; invertase (sucrase) converts sucrose into glucose and fructose to make the soft centres of chocolates and other sweets.
- [ ] F7 Household link (one bullet): biological washing powders contain proteases and lipases (and amylases) that digest protein stains (blood, egg, grass), fat and grease, and starch; they work at low washing temperatures (e.g. 30–40 °C), saving energy. They are not used at very high temperatures, where the enzymes would be denatured.

Biofuels (one block)
- [ ] B1 Bioethanol from starch crops: amylases break the starch of crops such as maize and wheat into glucose, which yeast ferments to ethanol; the ethanol is blended with petrol as a fuel.
- [ ] B2 Bioethanol from cellulose ("second-generation" biofuel): cellulases break down the cellulose in straw, wood chips and other crop waste into glucose for fermentation. Cellulose is tightly packed and hard to digest (see "Structures of biomolecules"), so these enzymes are slow and costly and are an active area of research.
- [ ] B3 Biodiesel: lipases can catalyse the conversion of vegetable oils and waste cooking oils into biodiesel, an alternative to chemical catalysts that works at lower temperatures and leaves less waste.
- [ ] B4 Sustainability arguments for: biofuels are renewable; the carbon dioxide released when they burn was taken in by the crop during photosynthesis, so they add less to atmospheric CO₂ than fossil fuels; using waste materials (straw, waste oil) adds value to waste. Arguments against: growing fuel crops uses land that could grow food, which can raise food prices; clearing forests for fuel crops destroys habitats and releases carbon; fertilisers, machinery and processing use fossil-fuel energy, so the saving is smaller than it first appears. Cellulose-based and waste-based biofuels avoid most of the competition with food. (Carbon cycle and climate detail is in "Nutrient Cycling".)

Medicine (one block)
- [ ] M1 Diagnosis: glucose oxidase is used in blood-glucose test strips and electronic biosensors used by people with diabetes, and in urine test strips. It is specific to glucose, so other sugars don't interfere; the reaction produces a colour change or an electrical signal in proportion to the amount of glucose.
- [ ] M2 Dissolving blood clots: clot-dissolving enzymes such as streptokinase (from bacteria) and tissue plasminogen activator (tPA) are given soon after a heart attack or a stroke caused by a clot, to break down the clot and restore blood flow.
- [ ] M3 Enzyme replacement: people with cystic fibrosis often cannot release enough digestive enzymes from the pancreas, so they take capsules of pancreatic enzymes (lipase, protease and amylase) with meals; lactase tablets help people with lactose intolerance.
- [ ] M4 Cancer treatment: the enzyme asparaginase is used to treat a type of childhood leukaemia (acute lymphoblastic leukaemia). The leukaemia cells cannot make enough of the amino acid asparagine and take it from the blood; the enzyme breaks down asparagine in the blood, starving the cancer cells.
- [ ] M5 Medical technologies built on enzymes (name only, cross-refer): restriction enzymes and DNA ligase in genetic engineering (see "Genetic engineering"); Taq polymerase in PCR for DNA profiling and diagnostic testing (see "Generating a DNA profile").

Pharmaceuticals (one block)
- [ ] D1 Making antibiotics: immobilised penicillin acylase converts natural penicillin, made by the Penicillium mould, into a starting molecule (6-APA) from which semi-synthetic penicillins, such as amoxicillin, are made; these can treat infections the natural form does not.
- [ ] D2 Making single-form drugs: many drug molecules exist in two mirror-image forms, of which only one works (the other may be useless or harmful). Because enzymes are specific, they make only the wanted form, avoiding wasteful separation; e.g. an engineered enzyme (a transaminase) is used to manufacture the diabetes drug sitagliptin.
- [ ] D3 Enzymes allow drug manufacture at mild temperatures with less toxic solvent waste than chemical methods ("green chemistry"), linking to W2 and W3. Human insulin made by genetically modified bacteria depends on enzymes used to insert the gene (see "Genetic engineering"). One bullet.

Immobilised enzymes (two blocks: what and why; method)
- [ ] I1 An immobilised enzyme is an enzyme (or a whole cell containing it) that is fixed to, or trapped in, an inert, insoluble material, so it is held in place while the substrate solution passes over or through it and does not mix freely with the product.
- [ ] I2 Methods of immobilisation: (a) entrapment, trapping the enzyme in a gel bead such as calcium alginate; (b) adsorption, sticking the enzyme to the surface of an insoluble support such as glass beads, resin or charcoal; (c) covalent bonding of the enzyme to a support; (d) enclosing the enzyme behind a partially permeable membrane.
- [ ] I3 Advantages: the enzyme can be recovered and reused many times, cutting costs; the product is not contaminated with enzyme, so it needs no purification; the enzyme can be packed in a column for a continuous-flow process, with substrate fed in at the top and product collected at the bottom; immobilised enzymes are often more stable (less easily denatured by changes in temperature or pH); the reaction can be stopped at once by removing the enzyme.
- [ ] I4 Disadvantages: immobilising costs money and equipment to set up; some activity is lost, as part of the active sites may be blocked or altered; the reaction can be slower, because the substrate must diffuse into the bead to reach the enzyme; enzyme may leak out of the support; it is unsuitable where the substrate molecules are too large to enter the beads.
- [ ] I5 Examples (refer to the uses above, don't repeat them): glucose isomerase for high-fructose syrup (F4); lactase for lactose-free milk (F2); penicillin acylase for semi-synthetic penicillins (D1).
- [ ] I6 School method of immobilising yeast (or an enzyme) in alginate beads: 1. Mix sodium alginate solution with a suspension of yeast (which contains the enzyme sucrase, or invertase) or with an enzyme solution. 2. Draw the mixture into a syringe and drop it, one drop at a time, into calcium chloride solution: each drop forms a bead of insoluble calcium alginate gel trapping the yeast. 3. Leave the beads to harden for about 15–20 minutes. 4. Sieve them out and rinse with distilled water to remove the calcium chloride and any free yeast. 5. Place the beads in a separating funnel (or a syringe barrel with a tap) as a column.
- [ ] I7 Using the beads: pour sucrose solution through the column and collect the liquid that drips out; test it with a glucose test strip, which shows glucose present (the sucrose has been converted to glucose and fructose), while the sucrose solution tested before entering gives a negative result. Control: beads made without yeast (alginate only), whose product gives no glucose. The product from the beads is clear, while sucrose mixed with free yeast becomes cloudy with yeast cells that must be removed, showing the advantage of immobilisation. The beads can be rinsed and used again.
- [ ] I8 Safety for I6–I7 (one bullet): calcium chloride irritates the eyes; wear safety glasses and wash spills.
- [ ] I9 Diagram: (a) a syringe dripping alginate–yeast mixture into a beaker of calcium chloride solution, with beads forming, labelled; (b) a separating funnel (or column) packed with beads, sucrose solution added at the top, a tap, and product (glucose and fructose) collected below, labelled.

Bioreactors (one bullet)
- [ ] R1 A bioreactor is a vessel in which cells or enzymes carry out a reaction under controlled conditions: temperature, pH, oxygen (or its absence), nutrient supply, mixing and sterility are monitored and kept at the values that suit the enzyme. A column of immobilised enzyme (I3) is a simple bioreactor. Growing microorganisms in bioreactors, and batch and continuous culture, belong to "Microorganisms in industry".

Cross-cutting themes (one bullet each, may close the relevant blocks)

- [ ] K2 Sustainability: lower energy use and less chemical waste (W2–W3); low-temperature washing (F7); biofuels and their arguments (B4); more juice from the same fruit (F3).
- [ ] K3 Technology: genetic modification of microorganisms to make enzymes, engineered enzymes (D2), immobilisation and continuous-flow bioreactors.

## 3. Traps

- Immobilised enzymes are not denatured or "dead"; they are held in place and still active.
- The beads are calcium alginate, formed when sodium alginate meets calcium chloride. Don't reverse the two solutions.
- Yeast contains sucrase (invertase), which acts on sucrose; don't say yeast "contains glucose".
- Glucose isomerase converts glucose to fructose; it does not break down starch.
- Biological detergents don't work better at very high temperatures: their enzymes would be denatured.
- Cheese-making is an enzyme use; yoghurt-making and most brewing use whole microorganisms and belong to "Microorganisms in industry" (F5 mentions yeast only as the fermenting organism).
- Fermenters, batch and continuous culture, and microbial growth curves belong to "Microorganisms in industry" (the continuous-culture part of an old bioreactor question was removed for this reason). R1 is one bullet.
- Restriction enzymes, ligase and Taq polymerase belong to "Genetic engineering" and "Generating a DNA profile"; M5 names them only.
- The immobilisation method (I6–I7) is a short practical method, not one of the specification's required "investigate" outcomes; don't add variables tables to it.
- The diagram cues in I9 are the only ones wanted. No other diagram cues.
