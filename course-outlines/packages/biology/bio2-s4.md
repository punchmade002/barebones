# Package: bio2-s4 — Producing and analysing data

- **loId:** `bio2-s4` · **chapterId:** `bio2` · **syllabusRef:** U2.4
- **Syllabus:** Outcome 4: "produce and select data (qualitatively/quantitatively), critically
  analyse data to identify patterns and relationships, identify anomalous observations, draw
  and justify conclusions." The strand overview adds that students "obtain and evaluate primary
  data (i.e., collected by themselves) and secondary data (data collected by somebody else)."
  Ordinary and Higher level (no Higher Level only blocks).
- **Prepared by:** a higher model, 2026-10-22. The spec glossary defines "analyse" as
  breaking data down to identify parts and relationships and interpret them to reach
  conclusions, and "justify" as "give valid reasons or evidence to support an answer or
  conclusion". This subsection owns the mean, range, tables, graphs, patterns, correlation vs
  causation, anomalies and conclusions.

## 1. Must cover

Types of data (one block)
- [ ] D1 Qualitative data: descriptions or categories that are not numerical, e.g. a colour change from blue to brick-red in a Benedict's test; presence or absence of a species; "cloudy" or "clear".
- [ ] D2 Quantitative data: numerical measurements or counts with units, e.g. volume of oxygen in cm³, mass in g, number of daisies per quadrat. Quantitative data are more objective and can be analysed mathematically, so they are preferred where possible; a qualitative result can often be made quantitative (e.g. a colorimeter reading instead of a colour).
- [ ] D3 Continuous data can take any value in a range (mass, temperature, time); discrete data are whole-number counts (number of seeds, number of stomata); categoric data are named groups (eye colour, type of habitat).
- [ ] D4 Primary data are collected by the investigator; secondary data are collected by somebody else (published results, databases, other groups' results). Selecting data means choosing the data that are relevant to the question and of good enough quality; secondary data can extend or check primary data. How to evaluate a secondary source is in "Recognising bias and evaluating sources".

Recording results in a table (one skills block)
- [ ] T1 The independent variable goes in the first column, the dependent variable (with columns for each repeat and the mean) in the following columns.
- [ ] T2 Each column heading gives the quantity and its unit, e.g. "Temperature (°C)", "Volume of oxygen (cm³)"; units go in the heading, not after every number.
- [ ] T3 Record every reading to the same number of decimal places, matching the resolution of the instrument (a balance reading to 0.01 g gives 2.45 g, not 2.4 g or 2.453 g). Values of the independent variable are listed in order.
- [ ] T4 Record raw results straight into the table during the investigation, including any that look wrong.

Processing data (one skills block with worked examples)
- [ ] M1 Mean: add the values and divide by the number of values. Anomalous values are left out of the mean (and the fact stated). Worked example: final masses of four replicate potato cylinders are 2.45 g, 2.44 g, 2.46 g and 2.10 g; 2.10 g is anomalous; mean of the other three = (2.45 + 2.44 + 2.46) ÷ 3 = 7.35 ÷ 3 = 2.45 g.
- [ ] M2 Range: the largest value minus the smallest. It shows the spread of repeat readings: in the example the range is 2.46 − 2.44 = 0.02 g, a small spread, so the readings are precise. The range of the independent variable is the lowest to highest value tested (e.g. 0 °C to 60 °C).
- [ ] M3 Percentage change = (final value − initial value) ÷ initial value × 100. It allows samples that started with different masses to be compared. Worked example: a cylinder of initial mass 2.50 g and final mass 2.45 g: (2.45 − 2.50) ÷ 2.50 × 100 = −2.0%. A negative value is a loss, a positive value a gain.
- [ ] M4 Rate = amount of change ÷ time, e.g. 24 cm³ of oxygen in 4 minutes is a rate of 6 cm³ per minute. When the time taken for something to happen is measured (e.g. time for starch to disappear), rate can be calculated as 1 ÷ time: 50 s gives 1 ÷ 50 = 0.02 s⁻¹. A shorter time means a faster rate.

Choosing and drawing graphs (one skills block)
- [ ] G1 Line graph: when both variables are continuous, e.g. rate of enzyme activity against temperature. Bar chart: when the independent variable is categoric or discrete, e.g. number of a species in three habitats; bars are separate and of equal width. Histogram: frequency of a continuous variable divided into classes, e.g. number of students in each height range; bars touch. Scatter graph: to look for a correlation between two measured variables, e.g. light intensity and number of a plant species in quadrats. Pie chart: proportions of a whole, e.g. percentage of each blood group in a class.
- [ ] G2 Axes: the independent variable on the horizontal (x) axis, the dependent variable on the vertical (y) axis. Label both with the quantity and unit.
- [ ] G3 Scales: even, linear scales that use more than half of the grid in each direction; they need not start at zero if the data don't come near zero (show this clearly).
- [ ] G4 Plot points accurately as small crosses or circled dots; plot the means. Then draw a line or smooth curve of best fit that shows the trend, with points balanced on either side, or join points with straight ruled lines where no trend line can be assumed. Don't force the line through an anomalous point. Give the graph a title ("the effect of [independent variable] on [dependent variable]").
- [ ] G5 Diagram: a line graph of rate of enzyme activity against temperature, with labelled axes and units, plotted points, a smooth curve rising to a peak (optimum) and falling, and one circled anomalous point off the curve.

Identifying patterns and relationships (one block)
- [ ] P1 Describe the trend in words and quote data: e.g. "the rate increased from 2 to 8 cm³ per minute as temperature rose from 10 °C to 40 °C, then fell to zero by 60 °C". Name key features: a peak or optimum, a plateau (levelling off), the point where a line crosses zero.
- [ ] P2 Positive correlation: as one variable increases, the other increases. Negative correlation: as one increases, the other decreases. No correlation: no pattern. A straight line through the origin shows the variables are directly proportional.
- [ ] P3 Diagram: three small scatter graphs showing positive, negative and no correlation.
- [ ] P4 Interpolation: reading a value from the graph between plotted points. Extrapolation: extending the line beyond the data range to predict a value; it is less reliable, because the pattern may not continue (e.g. an enzyme's rate does not keep rising with temperature).
- [ ] P5 The gradient of a line graph of amount against time is the rate; a steeper line means a faster rate, and a line levelling off means the reaction is slowing (e.g. as substrate is used up).

Correlation and causation (one block)
- [ ] K1 A correlation shows that two variables change together; it does not show that one causes the other.
- [ ] K2 Other explanations of a correlation: chance (especially with a small sample); a third variable (confounding variable) that affects both; or the cause runs the other way. Illustrative example: people who carry cigarette lighters are more likely to develop lung cancer, but the lighter doesn't cause cancer; smoking, the third variable, causes both.
- [ ] K3 Evidence that one variable causes a change in another comes from a controlled experiment (a fair test with a control), together with a biological mechanism that explains it. Surveys and secondary data alone usually show correlation (strategies compared in "Questioning and predicting").

Anomalous results (one block)
- [ ] A1 An anomalous result (outlier) is one that doesn't fit the pattern of the other results: far from the other repeats at the same value, or off the line of best fit on a graph.
- [ ] A2 Possible causes: a mistake in measuring or recording, an uncontrolled variable, faulty equipment, or an unusual biological sample.
- [ ] A3 What to do: check the reading and the calculation; repeat that measurement if possible; leave it out of the mean and the line of best fit; keep it in the results table and report it with a possible reason (integrity, in "Designing and conducting investigations").
- [ ] A4 A result is not anomalous just because it disagrees with the hypothesis; if repeats confirm it, it may show the hypothesis is wrong or reveal something new.

Drawing and justifying conclusions (one block)
- [ ] C1 A conclusion states the relationship found between the independent and dependent variable, e.g. "As sucrose concentration increased, the percentage change in mass of the potato cylinders decreased."
- [ ] C2 It is justified with evidence: quote values or describe the graph's trend, and say whether the data support or do not support the hypothesis (never "prove").
- [ ] C3 It explains the result using biological knowledge, e.g. water left the cells by osmosis into the more concentrated solutions.
- [ ] C4 It is limited to what was tested: the range of the independent variable, the organism or tissue used, and the conditions. It doesn't claim more than the data show.
- [ ] C5 Confidence in a conclusion depends on whether the test was fair and valid, the sample size, the number of repeats, the spread of the data, the number of anomalies and whether results are repeatable and reproducible (see "Objectivity and data quality"). A conclusion is valid when it is supported by valid, reliable data.

## 3. Traps

- Mean excludes anomalies; range uses the highest minus the lowest, not the two numbers written as "2.44 to 2.46" only (either form is accepted, but the subtraction is clearer).
- Independent variable on the x-axis, always. Units on every axis label.
- Bar chart vs histogram: bars separate for categories, touching for continuous classes.
- Correlation ≠ causation; never write that a survey "proves" a cause.
- "Prove" is never used for a hypothesis or conclusion.
- Repeatability and reproducibility, random and systematic error belong to "Objectivity and data quality"; C5 refers to them only.
- Evaluating secondary sources for bias belongs to "Recognising bias and evaluating sources".
- Don't explain osmosis or enzyme action beyond the one-line reasons in C3 and P4; they have their own chapters.
- Diagram cues: G5 and P3 only. No other diagram cues.

## 4. What exams ask (topics only)

- After designing an experiment: carry it out, gather data, repeat, analyse and interpret the data, draw a conclusion, compare with other results (the steps of the scientific method that remain on the specification; publication and peer review belong to "Science as a global enterprise").
- Draw a graph of given data; name the independent variable for the x-axis; calculate a mean, percentage change or rate.
- Describe the trend shown by a graph and draw a conclusion.
- New to this specification, expect: identify an anomalous result in a table and say how to treat it; explain why a correlation in given data does not show cause; justify a conclusion with data.

## 5. Practice questions to write

1. 6 marks: "Calculate the mean and the range of these four repeat readings: 2.45 g, 2.44 g, 2.46 g and 2.10 g, and explain how you treated any anomalous result." Model answer: M1, M2, A1, A3 with the checked numbers.
2. 9 marks: "Explain why a line graph, not a bar chart, is used to show the effect of temperature on enzyme activity, and state three features of a well-drawn graph." Model answer: G1 (both continuous), then three of G2–G4.
3. 12 marks: "A survey finds that areas with more lichen species have lower levels of air pollution. (a) Describe the correlation. (b) Explain why this does not on its own show that pollution reduces lichen diversity. (c) Describe how a conclusion about cause could be justified." Model answer: negative correlation (P2); K1–K2 (chance, third variable such as humidity or tree type); K3 controlled experiment plus mechanism, and C2, C4 for justifying.
