# Package: bio21-s2 — Homeostasis as a stimulus-response model

- **loId:** `bio21-s2` · **chapterId:** `bio21` · **syllabusRef:** 2.4.5
- **Syllabus:** "homeostasis as a model of stimulus-response in resistance to external or
  internal change, appropriate responses occur via negative feedback, nervous and hormonal
  pathways link a control centre with receptors and effectors in vertebrates." Homeostasis is
  also one of the characteristics of life [1.1.1]. Ordinary and Higher level. The specification
  names no example, so give the four worked examples below.
- **Prepared by:** a higher model, 2026-10-20.

## 1. Must cover

What homeostasis is
- [ ] W1 Homeostasis is the maintenance of a relatively constant internal environment despite changes in external or internal conditions. The internal environment is the tissue fluid and blood around the cells.
- [ ] W2 Factors kept constant include body temperature, blood glucose concentration, water and salt balance, blood pH and carbon dioxide level.
- [ ] W3 Why it matters: enzymes work best within a narrow range of temperature and pH; cells gain or lose water by osmosis if the concentration of the surrounding fluid changes; and steady conditions let cells function whatever the conditions outside the body.
- [ ] W4 Values fluctuate slightly around a set point (normal level), rather than staying perfectly fixed; e.g. normal core body temperature is about 37 °C.

The model
- [ ] M1 Components: the stimulus is a change from the set point; a receptor detects it; a control centre (often in the brain, e.g. the hypothalamus, or a gland) compares it with the set point and sends instructions; an effector (a muscle or gland) carries out the response; the response returns the factor towards the set point.
- [ ] M2 In vertebrates the control centre is linked to receptors and effectors by nervous pathways (neurons, fast) and hormonal pathways (hormones in the blood, slower).
- [ ] M3 Negative feedback: the response reverses the original change. Once the factor returns to normal, the stimulus is removed and the response stops. This keeps the factor oscillating close to the set point.
- [ ] M4 (assumed) Positive feedback is the opposite: the response increases the change, e.g. oxytocin causing stronger and stronger contractions during labour. It is rare and is not used in homeostasis.
- [ ] M5 Diagram: a negative-feedback loop: set point → change → receptor → control centre → effector → response → back towards set point, with a second branch for a change in the opposite direction.

Worked examples (one block each, labelling stimulus, receptor, control centre, effector, response)
- [ ] E1 Body temperature (mainly nervous): receptors in the hypothalamus detect blood temperature, and receptors in the skin detect skin temperature. If too hot: sweat glands produce sweat, which cools the skin as it evaporates; arterioles in the skin widen (vasodilation), so more blood flows near the surface and loses heat. If too cold: muscles shiver, releasing heat from respiration; skin arterioles narrow (vasoconstriction), reducing heat loss; behaviour changes (adding clothes, seeking shelter).
- [ ] E2 Blood glucose (hormonal): after a meal glucose rises → cells of the pancreas detect the rise → the pancreas releases insulin → cells take up glucose and the liver stores it as glycogen → glucose falls back to normal → less insulin is released. In type 1 diabetes this feedback fails because too little insulin is made.
- [ ] E3 Thyroxine (hormonal, the feedback acting on the hormone itself): low thyroxine → hypothalamus and pituitary detect it → the pituitary releases TSH → the thyroid releases thyroxine → high thyroxine inhibits TSH release, so thyroxine production falls.
- [ ] E4 Water balance (hormonal): the hypothalamus detects the water concentration of the blood and the pituitary adjusts ADH release, changing how much water the kidneys reabsorb. One block only; ADH detail is Higher Level material in The Urinary System.
- [ ] E5 Breathing: a rise in blood carbon dioxide is detected by the brain, which increases breathing rate. One bullet; the Higher Level detail is in The Breathing System.

## 3. Traps

- Don't teach glucagon: the specification names insulin only.
- Don't give TRH or other hypothalamic releasing hormones.
- Kidney and nephron detail belongs to The Urinary System; breathing control detail to The Breathing System.
- The comparison of nervous and hormonal coordination belongs to `bio21-s3`; M2 is one sentence.

## 4. What exams ask (topics only)

- Define homeostasis; explain why it is important.
- Explain negative feedback with an example (thyroxine and TSH is a common example).
- Identify the receptor, control centre and effector in a described homeostatic process.
- Describe how the body responds to overheating or cooling (without skin structure).
