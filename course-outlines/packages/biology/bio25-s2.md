## 1. Must cover

Osmosis in food preservation: the principle (one block)
- [ ] P1 Food spoils mainly because bacteria and fungi (moulds and yeasts) grow on it, digesting it and making it unsafe or unpleasant to eat. Like all cells, they need water for their enzymes to work and to grow and reproduce.
- [ ] P2 Mechanism: 1. A high concentration of salt or sugar is added to the food. 2. The food's water is now a concentrated (hypertonic) solution compared with the cytoplasm of any microorganism on it. 3. Water leaves the microbial cells by osmosis. 4. The cells are dehydrated and plasmolysed (bacteria and fungi have cell walls), so their enzymes can't work and they can't grow or reproduce; many die.
- [ ] P3 The salt or sugar also binds water in the food, so less free water is available to any microbe (one bullet). Preserving by drying (e.g. dried fruit, dried fish) works by removing water directly, which has the same effect on microbes; it is related to osmosis but is not osmosis itself.

Salting and sugaring (one block)
- [ ] S1 Salting: meat and fish are rubbed with dry salt or soaked in strong brine (salt solution), e.g. bacon, ham, salted fish. Salt also draws water out of the food itself by osmosis, drying it further.
- [ ] S2 Sugaring: fruit is preserved in a high concentration of sugar, e.g. jam, marmalade and candied fruit; fruit is also stored in heavy syrup. Honey keeps for a very long time because its sugar concentration is so high.
- [ ] S3 Pickling (one bullet): vegetables kept in vinegar are preserved mainly by the low pH of the acid (ethanoic acid), which stops enzymes and microbes working, not by osmosis; brine pickles (salt solutions) combine osmosis with acid made by fermentation.

Examining osmotic preservation: limits and health (one block, points for and against)
- [ ] L1 Advantages: cheap, simple and traditional; needs no refrigeration, so it was used for long journeys and winter stores; it works against both bacteria and fungi.
- [ ] L2 Limits: some microorganisms tolerate high salt or sugar concentrations, especially moulds and yeasts, so jam can still grow mould on its surface once opened and should be refrigerated; preservation fails if the salt or sugar concentration is too low (e.g. reduced-sugar jams spoil faster).
- [ ] L3 Osmotic preservation slows microbial growth but doesn't sterilise the food; it is usually combined with other methods (cooking, sealing, refrigeration, smoking, acids).
- [ ] L4 Health link: salted foods add much salt to the diet, and high salt intake is linked to high blood pressure, which increases the risk of heart disease and stroke; sugary preserves add sugar, linked to tooth decay and obesity (see "Diet"). So salting and sugaring are used less today, and refrigeration and freezing more.
- [ ] L5 Osmosis also affects food preparation: salting sliced vegetables (e.g. cucumber, aubergine) draws water out of them; limp vegetables (e.g. celery, lettuce) become crisp again when placed in cold water, because their cells take in water and become turgid. One bullet.

Osmosis and plant health: turgor and support (one block)
- [ ] T1 Healthy plants keep their cells turgid by taking in water by osmosis into the vacuole of each cell. The turgor pressure of cells against their walls supports non-woody plants, leaves and flowers, holding them upright and spreading leaves to the light for photosynthesis.
- [ ] T2 Turgor pressure drives cell enlargement: young cells expand as water enters their vacuoles, so plant growth depends on water uptake by osmosis.
- [ ] T3 Guard cells open the stomata when they become turgid and close them when they become flaccid, controlling gas exchange and water loss (detail in "Transport in Plants").
- [ ] T4 Wilting: when water is lost from the leaves (by transpiration) faster than the roots take it up, the cells lose water, become flaccid, and the plant wilts. Wilting closes stomata, so it reduces photosynthesis and growth. It is reversed if water is supplied soon enough; prolonged wilting kills the plant.

Osmosis and plant health: damage by salt and fertiliser (one block)
- [ ] D1 Roots take in water by osmosis because the soil water is normally more dilute than the cell sap of the root cells (root hair detail in "Transport in Plants").
- [ ] D2 If the soil water becomes more concentrated than the root cells' contents, water stops entering the roots and can leave them by osmosis. The root cells become plasmolysed, the plant can't take up enough water, it wilts and may die, even in moist soil.
- [ ] D3 Causes of salty soil water: flooding of coastal land by seawater (e.g. storm surges); salt spread on roads in icy weather, which damages roadside trees and hedges; irrigation in hot, dry regions, where water evaporates and leaves salts behind in the soil (salinisation), lowering crop yields.
- [ ] D4 Fertiliser burn: applying too much fertiliser, especially concentrated fertiliser close to roots or on dry soil, makes the soil solution too concentrated, so water leaves the roots and the leaves turn brown and scorched. Advice: apply the recommended amount, spread it evenly and water it in. Salt was used in the past as a weedkiller for the same reason.
- [ ] D5 Some plants of salty habitats (e.g. salt marshes and seashores) survive because their root cells hold a higher solute concentration than the salty soil water, so water still enters by osmosis. One bullet; no named species.
- [ ] D6 Sustainability link: soil salinisation from irrigation, and seawater flooding linked to rising sea levels and stronger storms, reduce the land available for crops; careful irrigation and drainage reduce salinisation.

## 3. Traps

- Salt and sugar preserve food by drawing water out of microbes by osmosis; they don't "kill microbes by poisoning them" or "remove oxygen".
- Vinegar (pickling) works mainly by acidity; don't present it as an osmosis method.
- Drying is water removal, not osmosis; one sentence only (P3).
- Plant cells don't burst in pure water; damage in plants comes from water loss (plasmolysis), not bursting.
- Wilting is caused by water loss exceeding uptake; don't say it is caused by plasmolysis alone. Most wilting leaves are flaccid, not plasmolysed.
- The mechanism of transpiration, the root hair and xylem pathway, and how guard cells change shape belong to "Transport in Plants"; T3, T4 and D1 are one sentence each.
- The investigation of solute concentration and microbial growth belongs to "Investigation: growth of microorganisms"; don't describe it.
- Don't add oral rehydration or kidney osmoregulation; they are not part of this outcome (osmoregulation is in "Water" and "ADH and osmoregulation").
- No diagram cues are wanted in this subsection; the cell diagrams are in "Diffusion, osmosis and active transport".
