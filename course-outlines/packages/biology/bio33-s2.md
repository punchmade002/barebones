## 1. Must cover

Growth and how it is measured (one block)
- [ ] G1 Growth of a microorganism population means an increase in the number of cells (bacteria and yeasts dividing), or in the size of the mycelium (moulds), not the growth of one cell.
- [ ] G2 A colony is a visible mass of cells on a solid medium, grown by repeated division from a single cell (or spore); bacterial colonies are usually small, round, shiny or matt spots; mould colonies are fuzzy and spread outwards. Counting colonies estimates how many living cells were on the plate.
- [ ] G3 Ways to measure growth: count the colonies on each plate (if they are separate); measure the diameter of a mould colony with a ruler under the base of the plate; estimate the percentage of the plate's surface covered (using a grid of squares drawn on the base); in liquid (broth) cultures, measure the cloudiness (turbidity) with a colorimeter, since cloudier broth has more cells and absorbs more light; for yeast, count cells with a microscope and a counting chamber, or measure the CO₂ produced.
- [ ] G4 Microorganisms need for growth: a source of nutrients (energy and raw materials), water, a suitable temperature and pH, and (for most) oxygen; harmful chemicals and high external solute concentrations inhibit them. Each is one factor tested below.

Aseptic technique and safety (one block, or two)
- [ ] A1 Aseptic technique is the set of procedures that keep unwanted microorganisms out of cultures (so that only the intended organism grows and results are valid) and keep the cultured microorganisms from escaping onto people and surfaces (for safety). Any culture may contain or become contaminated with harmful microorganisms, so every culture is treated as a possible pathogen.
- [ ] A2 Before starting: tie back long hair, cover cuts with a waterproof plaster, wash hands with soap and water, and wipe the bench with disinfectant. Don't eat, drink or touch the face during the practical.
- [ ] A3 Sterilise all equipment and media before use: nutrient agar, broths and glassware are heated in an autoclave (or pressure cooker) at 121 °C for 15 minutes, which kills all microorganisms including bacterial endospores; plastic Petri dishes are bought pre-sterilised and sealed.
- [ ] A4 Inoculating loop: heat the wire loop in the hottest part of a Bunsen flame until it glows red-hot, before and after every use, to kill any microorganisms on it. Let it cool for a few seconds (without putting it down) before touching the culture, so that the heat doesn't kill the microorganisms being transferred.
- [ ] A5 Work close to a lit Bunsen burner: the rising hot air carries airborne microorganisms upwards and away from the open plates and bottles.
- [ ] A6 When opening a culture bottle or tube, hold the cap in the little finger (never put it down on the bench), and pass the neck of the bottle briefly through the flame after opening and before closing it, to kill microorganisms at the mouth and create an upward air current.
- [ ] A7 Open a Petri dish lid only slightly (tilted up like a clam shell) and for as short a time as possible, to stop airborne microorganisms landing on the agar.
- [ ] A8 After inoculating, secure the lid to the base with two or four short strips of sticky tape, not sealed all the way round, so oxygen can still enter and conditions don't become anaerobic, which would favour the growth of harmful anaerobic bacteria. Label the base (not the lid, which could be swapped) around the edge with name, date and treatment.
- [ ] A9 Incubate plates upside down (lid at the bottom), so condensation forming on the lid doesn't drip onto the agar and spread the colonies, and the agar doesn't dry out.
- [ ] A10 Incubate school cultures at about 25 °C, never at 37 °C: body temperature favours the growth of microorganisms that are pathogenic to humans.
- [ ] A11 Never open plates after incubation: examine and count colonies through the closed lid. Never culture samples from the human body, toilets or animal waste.
- [ ] A12 Disposal: put used plates and cultures into an autoclave bag and autoclave them (121 °C, 15 minutes) before putting them in the bin, or soak them in strong disinfectant. Disinfect the bench again and wash hands thoroughly at the end.
- [ ] A13 Spills: cover with paper towels soaked in disinfectant, leave for the time stated on the disinfectant, then clear up wearing gloves, and tell the teacher.
- [ ] A14 Ethanol (used to sterilise a glass spreader) is highly flammable: keep the beaker of ethanol away from the Bunsen flame, and pass the spreader through the flame only briefly to burn off the ethanol, holding it pointing down.

Media and preparing plates (one block)
- [ ] P1 A culture medium supplies microorganisms with nutrients and water. Nutrient agar contains agar (a jelly extracted from seaweed, which sets solid and which most microorganisms can't digest) plus nutrients such as meat or yeast extract and peptone (partly digested protein), giving amino acids, sugars, vitamins and minerals. Nutrient broth is the same without agar (liquid).
- [ ] P2 Fungi such as yeasts and moulds grow better on malt agar or potato dextrose agar, which are sugar-rich and slightly acidic.
- [ ] P3 Pouring a plate: 1. melt the sterile agar in its bottle in a boiling water bath; 2. cool it to about 50 °C (it stays liquid until it cools below about 40 °C), which is also cool enough not to kill the organism if it is added to the agar; 3. flame the neck of the bottle; 4. lift the Petri dish lid slightly and pour in enough agar to cover the base (about 15–20 cm³); 5. replace the lid, swirl gently and leave on a level surface to set.
- [ ] P4 Inoculating a plate: streak (draw the flamed, cooled loop dipped in the culture across the agar in a zigzag) to get separate colonies; or spread a set volume (e.g. 0.1 cm³) of a culture or diluted suspension evenly with a sterile glass spreader to make a "lawn" covering the whole plate (needed for the antibacterial test, C items).
- [ ] P5 Suitable microorganisms: baker's yeast suspended in sterile water or sugar solution; moulds such as Rhizopus from bread; or non-pathogenic bacterial cultures supplied for schools. The source must be known, so the risk is low.
- [ ] P6 Diagram: a Petri dish after inoculation: base labelled with name, date and treatment, lid held with two short strips of tape, shown upside down in the incubator; and a flamed inoculating loop held in a Bunsen flame.

The general plan for every factor (one block)
- [ ] V1 Aim: to investigate how a named factor affects the growth of a named microorganism. Each investigation changes one factor (the independent variable) and measures growth (the dependent variable: colony count, colony diameter, percentage cover or turbidity) after a fixed time.
- [ ] V2 Controlled variables: the same microorganism, culture and volume of inoculum on every plate; the same medium and volume of agar; the same incubation time and, except when it is the factor tested, the same temperature, pH, nutrient level and water availability; the same method of measuring.
- [ ] V3 Controls: an uninoculated plate (sterile medium only) incubated with the others. No growth on it shows the medium and technique were sterile, so any growth on the test plates came from the inoculum. In addition, a plate with the normal conditions (e.g. normal pH, no added salt, a water disc in the antibacterial test) is the reference against which the tested conditions are compared.
- [ ] V4 Replicates: set up at least three plates for each value of the factor and calculate the mean, so that one contaminated or unusual plate doesn't mislead. Worked example: colony counts of 42, 38 and 46 at 25 °C give a mean of 126 ÷ 3 = 42 colonies.
- [ ] V5 Record results in a table (value of the factor, growth on each replicate, mean); plot a bar chart for categories or a line graph for a continuous factor (mean growth on the y-axis).
- [ ] V6 Conclusion: describe the trend in the mean results, say whether they support the hypothesis, explain them using the biology given for each factor, and compare them with secondary data (D items).

Temperature (one block)
- [ ] T1 Hypothesis: "Growth increases with temperature up to an optimum, then decreases."
- [ ] T2 Method: inoculate identical plates with the same volume of the same culture (e.g. yeast); incubate sets of three at about 4 °C (refrigerator), 15 °C, 20 °C (room) and 25 °C (incubator) for the same time (e.g. 2–3 days); count the colonies or measure colony diameter. To test high temperatures safely, heat tubes of the culture in water baths at 40 °C, 60 °C and 80 °C (or boil one) for 10 minutes before plating them, then incubate all at 25 °C.
- [ ] T3 Expected results: little or no growth at 4 °C; growth increases with temperature over the range tested; cultures heated to about 60 °C or more grow poorly or not at all.
- [ ] T4 Explanation: microorganisms' growth depends on enzyme-controlled reactions. At low temperatures molecules move slowly, so reactions and growth are slow, but the cells are not killed: growth resumes when they are warmed (why refrigerated food still goes off slowly, and frozen food starts to go off when thawed). Above the optimum, enzymes and other proteins are denatured, so cells die (enzyme detail in "Investigation: rate of enzyme activity"). Bacterial endospores survive higher temperatures than active cells.

pH (one block)
- [ ] H1 Hypothesis: "Bacteria grow best at about neutral pH and poorly in strongly acidic or alkaline conditions."
- [ ] H2 Method: make nutrient agar (or broth) at different pH values, e.g. 3, 5, 7, 9 and 11, by adding buffer solutions (or dilute acid or alkali, checked with a pH meter) before pouring; inoculate each with the same volume of culture; incubate all at the same temperature for the same time; measure growth.
- [ ] H3 Expected results: most bacteria grow best near neutral (about pH 6–8) and little in strongly acidic conditions; fungi such as yeasts and moulds tolerate, and often grow best in, slightly acidic conditions (about pH 5–6). Repeating with a fungus and a bacterium shows the difference.
- [ ] H4 Explanation: pH outside the optimum changes the charges on enzymes and other proteins, so their shape changes, and they work slowly or are denatured. Acid conditions are used to preserve food (e.g. pickling in vinegar), and yogurt keeps longer because the lactic acid made by its bacteria lowers the pH.

Nutrients (one block)
- [ ] N1 Hypothesis: "Microorganisms grow more when more nutrients are available, until another factor limits growth."
- [ ] N2 Method: compare plain agar (agar and water only, no nutrients) with nutrient agar; or use broths (or agar) with increasing concentrations of glucose, e.g. 0%, 1%, 2%, 5%, for yeast; inoculate equally; incubate together; measure growth.
- [ ] N3 Expected results: little or no growth on plain agar; growth increases with nutrient concentration and then levels off (another factor, such as space or the build-up of wastes, becomes limiting); at very high sugar concentrations growth falls again (S items).
- [ ] N4 Explanation: nutrients supply the energy (by respiration of sugars) and the raw materials (sugars, amino acids, minerals such as nitrate and phosphate, vitamins) for new cells. A variation: starch agar shows that only microorganisms that secrete amylase can use starch; flooding the plate with iodine after incubation leaves a clear zone around such colonies where the starch has been digested.

Water (one block)
- [ ] W1 Hypothesis: "Moulds grow only on food that is moist."
- [ ] W2 Method: 1. take slices of the same bread (without preservatives); dry some fully in an oven at a low temperature and moisten others with the same volume of sterile water; 2. expose each slice to the air of the same room for the same time (e.g. 30 minutes) so that mould spores land on it; 3. seal each in a clear plastic bag or sealed dish and label it; 4. keep all in the same warm, dark place (about 25 °C) for 5–7 days; 5. estimate the percentage cover of mould on each, using a transparent grid of squares held over the bag (e.g. 37 of 100 squares covered = 37%). Don't open the bags.
- [ ] W3 Expected results: mould grows on the moist bread and not (or very little) on the dried bread.
- [ ] W4 Explanation: microorganisms need water because their enzymes work only in solution; nutrients are absorbed in solution after extracellular digestion; and water is needed for the cells to grow and divide. Dried foods (e.g. dried fruit, pasta, powdered milk, cereals) keep for a long time for this reason.
- [ ] W5 Safety: mould spores can trigger allergies and asthma; keep the bags sealed throughout and dispose of them unopened.

External solute concentration (one block)
- [ ] S1 Hypothesis: "Growth decreases as the concentration of salt (or sugar) outside the cells increases."
- [ ] S2 Method: make nutrient agar containing increasing concentrations of sodium chloride (e.g. 0%, 2%, 5%, 10%, 20%) or of sucrose; inoculate each with the same volume of culture; incubate together; measure growth.
- [ ] S3 Expected results: growth decreases as the solute concentration rises, with little or none at the highest concentrations.
- [ ] S4 Explanation: when the solution outside a cell has a higher solute concentration (lower water concentration) than the cytoplasm, water leaves the cell by osmosis, so the cell loses water, its enzymes can't work and it stops growing or dies. In a very dilute solution water enters, but the bacterial or fungal cell wall stops the cell bursting. This is the basis of preserving food with salt (salted fish and bacon) and sugar (jam) (see "Osmosis in food preservation and plant health"; osmosis in "Diffusion, osmosis and active transport").

Antibacterial and antifungal chemicals: method (one block)
- [ ] C1 Antibacterial chemicals kill bacteria or stop them growing: antibiotics (e.g. penicillin, streptomycin), antiseptics (used on living tissue, e.g. on skin) and disinfectants (used on surfaces and objects). Antifungal chemicals (e.g. the antifungal creams used for athlete's foot, or fungicides) act on fungi. Antibiotics don't affect fungi, and antifungals generally don't affect bacteria, because the two groups have different structures (see "Bacteria and fungi").
- [ ] C2 Hypothesis: "Different antiseptics (or antibiotics) inhibit the growth of a bacterium to different extents."
- [ ] C3 Method (disc diffusion): 1. spread a set volume of the culture evenly over a nutrient agar plate with a sterile spreader, to make a lawn; 2. using sterile forceps (flamed and cooled), dip paper discs of the same size (e.g. 6 mm, cut with a hole punch and sterilised) in each chemical for the same time, drain them, and place them on the lawn, well spaced; 3. include a control disc dipped in sterile distilled water; ready-made antibiotic discs (or a multi-disc with several antibiotics on one ring) can be used instead; 4. tape and label the plate; 5. incubate upside down at about 25 °C for 2–3 days; 6. measure the diameter of the clear zone around each disc with a ruler on the base of the plate.
- [ ] C4 For an antifungal chemical, make a lawn of yeast on malt agar and use discs of the antifungal (with a water control and, to show the difference in C1, an antibiotic disc).
- [ ] C5 Diagram: a lawn plate with four discs, each labelled with its chemical, showing clear zones of inhibition of different sizes and none around the water control.

Antibacterial and antifungal chemicals: results and worked calculation (one block)
- [ ] C6 Expected results: a clear zone (the zone of inhibition) forms around discs whose chemical stops the bacterium growing; there is no clear zone around the water control. The larger the zone, the more effective the chemical is against that organism at that concentration. On the yeast lawn, the antifungal gives a clear zone and the antibiotic gives none.
- [ ] C7 Worked calculation: a clear zone of diameter 18 mm around a 6 mm disc. Radius = 9 mm; area of the zone = π r² = 3.14 × 9 × 9 ≈ 254 mm². Subtracting the disc (radius 3 mm, area ≈ 28 mm²) gives a clear area of about 226 mm². Areas let zones be compared fairly, because doubling the diameter quadruples the area.
- [ ] C8 Explanation: the chemical diffuses outwards from the disc into the agar, so its concentration falls with distance; bacteria can't grow where the concentration is high enough to kill or inhibit them. A zone's size also depends on how fast the chemical diffuses, so it is a guide, not an exact measure of strength.
- [ ] C9 A bacterium that grows right up to an antibiotic disc is resistant to that antibiotic. In hospitals the same test is used to find which antibiotic will work against the bacterium from a patient (antibiotic resistance in "Emergence and spread of infectious disease").

Secondary data and applications (one block)
- [ ] D1 Secondary data are results collected by others (e.g. other class groups, textbooks, published research or food-safety guidance). The specification requires conclusions supported by both primary and secondary data: compare your own results with the class's pooled results and with published findings, e.g. that most human pathogens grow best near body temperature and neutral pH, and that most bacteria grow little below refrigerator temperature.
- [ ] D2 Applications in food preservation (one bullet each factor): refrigeration and freezing slow or stop growth (low temperature) but don't kill; pasteurisation (heating milk briefly to a temperature below boiling, then cooling quickly) kills most microorganisms; canning and ultra-heat treatment (UHT) heat food enough to sterilise it, and sealing keeps microorganisms out; drying removes water; salting and sugaring use high solute concentration; pickling uses low pH; chemical preservatives inhibit growth; vacuum packing removes oxygen (slowing aerobic microorganisms).
- [ ] D3 Applications in health and hygiene: antiseptics on wounds and skin, disinfectants on surfaces, and aseptic technique in surgery (see "Acquired immunity, prevention and treatment"); in industry, bioreactors are kept at the optimum conditions found by such investigations to maximise growth (see "Microorganisms in industry").

Sources of error (one block)
- [ ] E1 Contamination by airborne or skin microorganisms gives colonies of a different appearance; check with the uninoculated control, and repeat with better aseptic technique.
- [ ] E2 Unequal inoculum (different volumes or numbers of cells) on different plates; use a measured volume from a well-mixed culture.
- [ ] E3 Colonies that grow together can't be counted; dilute the culture so colonies are separate, or measure percentage cover instead.
- [ ] E4 Agar or broth that is not at the intended pH or concentration; check with a pH meter and measure accurately.
- [ ] E5 Temperature not constant (e.g. the "room temperature" plates near a radiator).
- [ ] E6 Discs not equally soaked or chemicals not of known concentration; soak all discs for the same time.
- [ ] E7 Too few replicates; use at least three and calculate means.

## 3. Traps

- Refrigeration slows growth but doesn't kill microorganisms; boiling kills active cells but not all endospores (only autoclaving does).
- Flame the loop red-hot, then let it cool before touching the culture.
- Tape, don't fully seal, the lid; label the base, not the lid; incubate upside down at about 25 °C.
- The uninoculated plate is the control for sterility; the water disc is the control in the disc test. Don't call the uninoculated plate a "negative result".
- Antibiotics act on bacteria, not fungi or viruses.
- A larger zone of inhibition means the bacterium is more sensitive to that chemical, not that the chemical "grew" anything.
- Growth means more cells, not bigger cells (G1).
- The lag, log, stationary and decline phases and the growth curve belong to "Microorganisms in industry" (Higher Level); don't draw or name them here.
- Osmosis theory belongs to "Diffusion, osmosis and active transport" and its food-preservation use to "Osmosis in food preservation and plant health"; S4 explains it in two sentences only.
- The previous syllabus's "leaf yeast" experiment is not required by the specification; don't teach it.
- The diagram cues in P6 and C5 are the only ones wanted. No other diagram cues.
