## 1. Must cover

Why biodiversity matters (one block)
- [ ] W1 Ecosystem services are the benefits people get from ecosystems. Provisioning: food (crops, fish, game), timber, fibre, clean water, and medicines (many drugs come from organisms, e.g. penicillin from a mould, aspirin's ancestor from willow bark). Regulating: pollination of crops by insects; purification of water by wetlands and soil; flood control, as bogs and wetlands soak up and slowly release rain; carbon storage in bogs, forests and the sea; pest control by predators. Supporting: soil formation and nutrient cycling by decomposers. Cultural: recreation, tourism, education, heritage and wellbeing.
- [ ] W2 Resilience: an ecosystem with many species and much genetic diversity is more stable, because if one species declines, others with similar roles can take its place, and some individuals are likely to survive a new disease or change in climate. Low-diversity systems (e.g. a single crop variety) can collapse from one disease.
- [ ] W3 Ethical argument: many people argue that species have a right to exist regardless of their usefulness, and that this generation has a duty to pass on a living world to future generations (sustainability; see "The cross-cutting themes"). Extinction is permanent.

Causes of biodiversity loss (one block; one bullet per cause, each with an Irish example)
- [ ] C1 Habitat loss and fragmentation, the biggest cause: land drained, built on, cut or converted to intensive farming or plantation forestry. Fragmentation leaves small, isolated patches that support only small populations, which are more easily wiped out. Irish examples: drainage and cutting of raised bogs; drainage of wet grassland; removal of hedgerows.
- [ ] C2 Agricultural intensification: earlier and more frequent mowing for silage, reseeding with a few grass species, and more fertiliser reduce the variety of wild plants and destroy nests of ground-nesting birds (curlew, corncrake).
- [ ] C3 Pollution: excess nitrogen and phosphorus from fertiliser, slurry and sewage cause eutrophication of rivers and lakes; silt from drainage, forestry and land works smothers river beds; pesticides harm insects and their predators. (The eutrophication sequence is in "Ethics and sustainability of nutrient cycling"; one phrase here.)
- [ ] C4 Invasive species: non-native species introduced by people that spread and harm native species and ecosystems, often because they have no natural predators or diseases in their new home. Irish examples: grey squirrel, zebra mussel, Rhododendron ponticum (see the invasive-species block).
- [ ] C5 Overexploitation: harvesting faster than a population can replace itself, e.g. overfishing, peat extraction, over-collection.
- [ ] C6 Climate change: species must shift their range or timing; cold-adapted species and those in fragmented habitats can't move. Detail in "Climate change and biological strategies".
- [ ] C7 Disease: new pathogens spread by trade, e.g. ash dieback (a fungal disease, recorded in Ireland in 2012) killing ash trees in hedgerows and woodland (detail in "Emerging diseases and society").

Case study: raised bogs (one block)
- [ ] R1 Raised bogs are dome-shaped peatlands, mainly in the Irish midlands, formed over thousands of years as Sphagnum moss grew over waterlogged ground. They are wet, acidic and low in nutrients, and support specialised species: Sphagnum mosses, sundews (insect-eating plants), bog cotton, heather, and animals such as the curlew, red grouse and Irish hare.
- [ ] R2 Loss: centuries of hand turf cutting, then large-scale industrial peat extraction for fuel and horticulture in the twentieth century, plus drainage for farming and forestry. Only a tiny fraction of the original area (less than about 1%) is still active raised bog that is forming peat.
- [ ] R3 Impacts. Environmental: loss of a rare habitat and its specialised species; drained peat releases stored carbon (one phrase; carbon detail in "Climate change and biological strategies"); loss of flood control and water purification. Economic: peat fuelled power stations and supported jobs in the midlands, so ending extraction cost jobs, while restoration can bring tourism and "green" jobs. Social and cultural: turf cutting for home fuel is a centuries-old rural tradition and many families hold turbary (turf-cutting) rights; bogs preserve archaeology and are part of Irish heritage and literature.
- [ ] R4 Conservation: many raised bogs are protected as Special Areas of Conservation under the EU Habitats Directive, with turf cutting on them banned and compensation offered to turf cutters, which has been controversial; Bord na Móna ended industrial peat harvesting in 2021 and drained bogs are being rewetted (blocking drains to raise the water table) so that Sphagnum can regrow. Arguments: restoration protects species and stores carbon; but it removes a traditional right and a cheap fuel, so it needs compensation and local support to succeed.

Case study: the curlew and the corncrake (one block)
- [ ] B1 The curlew is a large wading bird with a long, down-curved bill that breeds on bogs and wet grassland. A national survey in 2015–2017 found only 138 breeding pairs in the Republic of Ireland, a decline of at least 96% since the 1980s; a 2021 survey found about 105 pairs. The curlew is now one of Ireland's most threatened breeding birds.
- [ ] B2 Causes of the curlew decline: loss and drainage of bogs and wet grassland; plantation forestry on breeding sites; intensive farming with early mowing; and predation of eggs and chicks by foxes and crows, made worse because small breeding areas are surrounded by forest and farmland that support many predators.
- [ ] B3 Curlew conservation: the Curlew Conservation Programme, run by the National Parks and Wildlife Service (NPWS) since 2017, employs local nest-finders and works with farmers to protect nests (e.g. electric fencing against foxes), manage habitat and control predators.
- [ ] B4 The corncrake is a secretive grassland bird with a rasping call that was once heard across rural Ireland in summer. Its population has fallen by more than 96% since the 1970s and it now breeds mainly in Co. Donegal (including offshore islands) and on the western coast of Mayo and Galway. Main cause: mechanised mowing of hay and silage during the nesting season, which destroys nests; mowing from the edge of a field inwards traps chicks in the centre.
- [ ] B5 Corncrake conservation: paying farmers to delay mowing until chicks can fly, to mow from the centre of the field outwards so chicks can escape to the edges, and to leave tall vegetation as cover; the Corncrake LIFE project (EU-funded, launched in 2022) uses payments based on results.
- [ ] B6 Cultural impact of both: the sounds of the curlew and the corncrake are part of rural Irish life, folklore, music and place names, so their loss is felt as a cultural loss, not only an ecological one.

Case study: invasive species in Ireland (one block)
- [ ] I1 Grey squirrel: introduced from North America; the Irish population began with a few animals released in 1911 at Castle Forbes, Co. Longford. It spread across the eastern half of the island, and red squirrels declined where it arrived. Reasons: it is larger, competes for food (it can feed efficiently on acorns, which red squirrels digest poorly) and carries squirrelpox virus, which does not harm grey squirrels but is usually fatal to red squirrels. It also damages trees by stripping bark.
- [ ] I2 Pine marten recovery as natural control: the native pine marten, protected under the Wildlife Act 1976, has recovered, and where pine martens are common (e.g. the Irish midlands) grey squirrels have declined or disappeared and red squirrels have returned. The heavier grey squirrel is more easily caught. This shows how restoring a native predator can help a native species.
- [ ] I3 Zebra mussel: a small freshwater mussel native to the Ponto-Caspian region (around the Black and Caspian Seas), first recorded in Ireland in Lough Derg on the Shannon in 1997, probably brought on boats. It spreads attached to boat hulls and in bilge water. Impacts: it filters huge volumes of water, removing phytoplankton (the base of the food chain), so water becomes clearer but food for zooplankton and young fish falls; it smothers native mussels; and it blocks water-intake pipes, an economic cost. It has itself been displaced in Lough Derg by a related invader, the quagga mussel, first recognised there in 2021.
- [ ] I4 Rhododendron ponticum: a shrub introduced to Killarney for ornamental planting in the nineteenth century. It forms dense thickets in the native oak woodlands of Killarney National Park; its evergreen leaves let very little light reach the woodland floor, so oak seedlings and ground plants can't grow and the wood can't regenerate. Clearing it by cutting and treating regrowth is slow and costly and must be repeated.
- [ ] I5 Other named invasives, one phrase each: Elodea canadensis (Canadian pondweed) in fresh water; Japanese knotweed, which spreads from small root fragments and damages buildings and riverbanks. Prevention: biosecurity, e.g. "check, clean, dry" boats and angling gear between waters; never releasing aquarium plants or pets into the wild.

Case study: water pollution and freshwater species (one block)
- [ ] P1 Freshwater pearl mussel: a long-lived mussel of clean, fast-flowing rivers. Its larvae (glochidia) must attach to the gills of young native salmon or trout to develop, and the juveniles need clean, well-oxygenated gravel. Silt and nutrient enrichment from drainage, forestry and farming clog the gravel, so in many Irish rivers few or no young mussels survive and populations consist only of old adults. Its decline is also linked to the decline of salmon and trout, its hosts.
- [ ] P2 Eutrophication: in summer 2023 Lough Neagh (in Northern Ireland, the largest lake on the island of Ireland) had a massive bloom of blue-green algae (cyanobacteria), linked mainly to excess phosphorus from agriculture and wastewater built up over decades, along with warm, still weather and zebra mussels. Impacts: toxic water unsafe for people, pets and livestock; harm to fish and birds; damage to the eel fishery and tourism; and anger in local communities.

Impacts of biodiversity loss, summarised under the four headings (one comparison block; paired points, each with an example from above)
- [ ] M1 Environmental: food webs are disrupted when a species is lost, affecting predators and prey; ecosystem services such as pollination, water purification and flood control are lost; ecosystems become less resilient; extinction is irreversible. Example: one third of Ireland's wild bee species are threatened with extinction, mainly through loss of flowers and nesting sites, which threatens pollination of wild plants and crops.
- [ ] M2 Economic: farming depends on pollinators and healthy soil; fisheries and angling depend on clean rivers and lakes; tourism (e.g. Killarney National Park, the Burren, bogs) depends on landscapes and wildlife; invasive species cost money to control (e.g. clearing rhododendron, unblocking pipes fouled by zebra mussels); polluted water costs more to treat. Conservation also has costs: compensation to farmers and turf cutters and lost income from land use.
- [ ] M3 Social: health and wellbeing benefit from access to nature; polluted water threatens drinking supplies and recreation (e.g. Lough Neagh); communities that depend on fishing, farming or peat can lose livelihoods either from biodiversity loss or from conservation rules, which causes conflict.
- [ ] M4 Cultural: species and landscapes are part of identity, heritage, folklore, music, literature and place names (curlew, corncrake, bogs); traditional practices such as turf cutting are themselves cultural heritage, which is why some conservation measures are resisted.

Conservation (one block on approaches; one block evaluating them)
- [ ] K1 Conservation is the protection, management and restoration of the natural environment to maintain biodiversity and prevent extinction, while allowing sustainable use.
- [ ] K2 In situ conservation (in the natural habitat): protected areas, such as national parks (Killarney National Park was Ireland's first, in 1932) and Natura 2000 sites across the EU: Special Areas of Conservation (SACs) under the EU Habitats Directive (1992) and Special Protection Areas (SPAs) for birds under the EU Birds Directive (1979). Species protection under the Wildlife Act 1976, which made it illegal to kill or disturb protected species.
- [ ] K3 Habitat management and restoration: rewetting bogs; planting native trees and hedgerows; farming for nature through agri-environment schemes that pay farmers for results such as species-rich meadows (e.g. in the Burren); controlling invasive species.
- [ ] K4 Species recovery programmes: e.g. the Curlew Conservation Programme and Corncrake LIFE (above); the All-Ireland Pollinator Plan (launched 2015), which asks councils, farmers, schools and gardeners to provide flowers and nesting sites for bees (e.g. mowing road verges less often).
- [ ] K5 Ex situ conservation (outside the natural habitat): seed banks, captive breeding and reintroduction, e.g. captive rearing of freshwater pearl mussels to release young mussels into rivers.
- [ ] K6 Policy and public action: in May 2019 the Dáil declared a climate and biodiversity emergency, making Ireland the second country to do so; the Citizens' Assembly on Biodiversity Loss reported in April 2023 with 159 recommendations; Ireland's fourth National Biodiversity Action Plan covers 2023–2030; the EU Nature Restoration Law (adopted 2024) requires member states to restore damaged ecosystems. Individuals can record species for citizen-science surveys (e.g. the National Biodiversity Data Centre), garden for pollinators, and avoid spreading invasive species.
- [ ] K7 Evaluating conservation, arguments for its effectiveness: protection has brought recoveries, e.g. the pine marten after 1976 (and with it, red squirrels); rewetting can restore bog habitat; targeted payments have raised breeding success of curlew and corncrake in some areas; protected areas keep the last strongholds of rare species.
- [ ] K8 Evaluating conservation, limits and arguments against: declines continue (curlew numbers fell further between 2015–17 and 2021); designating a site is not enough without enforcement and management; schemes cost money and can conflict with farmers', turf cutters' and landowners' incomes and traditions; small, isolated populations may still be lost to predators, disease or chance; restoration is slow (bogs take centuries to re-form). Conclusion to model: conservation limits loss when it has funding, enforcement and the support of the local people who manage the land.

- Ash dieback detail belongs to "Emerging diseases and society"; C7 is one bullet.
- Sampling methods belong to "Investigating an Ecosystem"; data evaluation belongs to "Investigation: human activity and species diversity".
- Don't say the grey squirrel "kills" red squirrels: it outcompetes them and spreads squirrelpox.
- Don't say the corncrake or curlew is extinct in Ireland; both still breed in small numbers.
- Don't call every non-native species invasive: invasive means it spreads and causes harm.
- Lough Neagh is in Northern Ireland; say "on the island of Ireland".
- The quagga mussel is a separate species from the zebra mussel.
- "Discuss" needs both sides: K7 and K8 must both appear.
