## 1. Must cover

Biotic factors (one block)
- [ ] B1 (assumed) Biotic factors are the effects living organisms have on each other (defined in "Biosphere, ecosystems, habitats and biodiversity"; use without redefining). In a grassland they include food supply, predation, grazing, competition, parasites and disease, pollination, decomposers, and human activities such as grazing livestock, mowing and fertilising.
- [ ] B2 Grazing by rabbits, cattle or sheep and mowing keep grass short; this lets low rosette plants such as daisy, dandelion and plantain get light, and stops shrubs and trees from invading. If grazing or mowing stops, taller grasses shade out the rosette plants, and in time scrub (e.g. brambles, hawthorn) invades.
- [ ] B3 Decomposers (bacteria and fungi) and detritivores (animals that eat dead organic matter, e.g. earthworms, woodlice, slugs in part) break down dead plants, dung and dead animals, returning minerals to the soil (decomposition in "The carbon cycle" and "The nitrogen cycle"; decomposer nutrition in "Bacteria and fungi"). Earthworms also drag leaves underground, mix and aerate the soil and improve drainage.
- [ ] B4 Pollinators such as bumblebees, hoverflies and butterflies feed on the nectar and pollen of clover, dandelion and buttercup flowers and carry pollen between them.

Feeding relationships in the grassland (one block)
- [ ] F1 Feeding relationships are found from primary data: direct observation of animals feeding; feeding signs (nibbled grass, holes in leaves, broken snail shells at a thrush's stone); droppings and pellets (e.g. undigested fur and bones in the pellets of birds of prey); the habitat where each animal was caught (e.g. aphids on the stems they feed on); then checked against secondary sources (field guides) for what each species eats.
- [ ] F2 Grassland producers: grasses, clover, daisy, dandelion, buttercup and other green plants, which make food by photosynthesis.
- [ ] F3 Primary consumers (herbivores) in the grassland: rabbits and hares graze grass and clover; slugs and snails eat leaves; grasshoppers and caterpillars chew leaves; aphids suck sap from stems; bumblebees take nectar and pollen.
- [ ] F4 Secondary and higher consumers (carnivores and omnivores): ladybirds eat aphids; spiders catch flies, grasshoppers and other insects; ground beetles eat slugs and other invertebrates; song thrushes eat snails (smashing their shells on a stone) and earthworms; blackbirds eat earthworms and insects; hedgehogs eat slugs, beetles and earthworms; badgers eat mainly earthworms; foxes eat rabbits, mice and birds; kestrels eat wood mice, pygmy shrews and large insects; pygmy shrews eat small invertebrates such as beetles, spiders and woodlice.
- [ ] F5 Worked example: grassland food chains from these data: grass → rabbit → fox; clover leaves → slug → song thrush; plant sap → aphid → ladybird → (insect-eating bird); grass seeds and invertebrates → wood mouse → kestrel. Connect the chains into a food web: e.g. the fox eats rabbits, wood mice and birds, so it is on several chains; the earthworm feeds on dead leaves and is eaten by thrushes, blackbirds, badgers and hedgehogs. Diagram: a grassland food web of 8–12 of the species in F2–F4, arrows pointing from the food to the feeder.
- [ ] F6 Explaining the relationships: the web shows interdependence; a change in one population affects others, e.g. if disease kills many rabbits, foxes eat more wood mice and birds, so those populations fall, and the grass grows taller, shading out daisies. An omnivore or a predator with several prey is less affected than one with a single food source. (General prediction from webs is in "Energy and nutrient transfer".)

Predation (one block)
- [ ] P1 Predation is a feeding relationship in which one animal, the predator, kills and eats another, the prey. Examples in grassland: fox (predator) and rabbit (prey); kestrel and wood mouse; ladybird and aphid; song thrush and snail.
- [ ] P2 Predator adaptations: the fox has acute hearing and smell to find prey, forward-facing eyes for judging distance, speed and sharp teeth, and hunts mostly at night, when rabbits feed; the kestrel hovers into the wind while scanning the ground with very keen eyesight, then drops onto prey, gripping it with sharp talons and tearing it with a hooked beak; spiders build webs to trap flying insects.
- [ ] P3 Prey adaptations: rabbits have eyes on the sides of the head giving an almost all-round field of view, large mobile ears, they feed in groups so some can watch for danger, thump the ground with a hind foot and flash the white underside of the tail as a warning, run in zigzags, and shelter in burrows; snails withdraw into their shells; ladybirds have bright warning colours and a bad taste, so predators learn to avoid them; many caterpillars and grasshoppers are green, camouflaged against the grass.
- [ ] P4 Predator–prey population cycle: 1. when prey are plentiful, more predators survive and breed, so predator numbers rise; 2. more predators eat more prey, so prey numbers fall; 3. with less food, predators starve or breed less, so predator numbers fall; 4. with fewer predators, prey numbers recover, and the cycle repeats. Predator numbers peak after prey numbers, and there are usually fewer predators than prey. The classic data are the fur-trading records of the snowshoe hare and the lynx in Canada, which rose and fell in cycles of about ten years.
- [ ] P5 Diagram: a graph of predator and prey numbers against time: two out-of-step waves, the predator curve lower and peaking after the prey curve.
- [ ] P6 Predation controls population size: predators usually take the old, sick, young or weak prey, and their effect increases as prey become more numerous, which helps keep the prey population near the habitat's carrying capacity (carrying capacity in "Carrying capacity and limiting factors"). Removing a predator can let its prey increase until it overgrazes its food.

Competition (one block, Higher Level only)
- [ ] C1 Competition is the struggle between organisms for a resource that is in short supply. Plants compete for light, water, minerals and space; animals compete for food, water, space or territory, shelter and mates. If the resource is plentiful, there is no competition.
- [ ] C2 Intraspecific competition is between members of the same species. It is the most intense kind, because they have exactly the same needs, e.g. daisy rosettes crowding each other for space and light; male blackbirds or robins defending territories that contain food and nest sites. Interspecific competition is between different species that need the same resource, e.g. grasses and daisies competing for light; rabbits and sheep grazing the same grass.
- [ ] C3 Contest competition: there is an active physical contest for the resource and the winner takes it, e.g. birds fighting over territory, or stags fighting over mates. Scramble competition: each competitor gets as much as it can of a shared resource, so when it is scarce all get too little, e.g. seedlings crowded together all grow weak and thin; aphids crowded on a stem.
- [ ] C4 Effects: competition reduces the growth, survival and breeding of the losers; it limits population size, and its effect grows as the population becomes more crowded; between species, one may exclude the other from the habitat, or each may survive by using the resources in a slightly different way (different niches; see "The niche").
- [ ] C5 Grassland example: in short, grazed or mown grass, daisies and other rosette plants get enough light; where grass is left long it outgrows and shades them, and they disappear (interspecific competition for light).
- [ ] C6 Irish example of interspecific competition: grey squirrels, introduced to Ireland at Castle Forbes, Co. Longford, in 1911, compete with the native red squirrel for food (especially seeds and nuts). Greys are larger, can digest acorns, which red squirrels digest poorly, eat hazelnuts before they ripen, and carry squirrelpox virus, which kills red squirrels but rarely harms greys, so reds have disappeared from much of the area greys occupy. Where pine marten numbers have recovered in Ireland, grey squirrels have declined and red squirrels have returned.
- [ ] C7 Adaptations that help organisms compete: fast growth and tall stems or climbing (e.g. ivy, brambles) in plants competing for light; deep roots for water; rapid breeding; aggressive defence of territory in animals.

Symbiosis, mutualism and commensalism (one block, Higher Level only)
- [ ] M1 Symbiosis is a close, long-term relationship in which two organisms of different species live together. There are three types, named by who benefits: mutualism (both benefit, +/+), commensalism (one benefits and the other is unaffected, +/0) and parasitism (one benefits and the other is harmed, +/−).
- [ ] M2 Mutualism, example 1: nitrogen-fixing bacteria (Rhizobium) live in root nodules of clover and other legumes. The bacteria convert nitrogen gas into ammonium, which the plant uses to make amino acids and proteins; the plant gives the bacteria carbohydrates (from photosynthesis) and a protected home. Active nodules are pink inside, from a pigment (leghaemoglobin) that controls oxygen levels for the bacteria. This is why clover grows well in soils poor in nitrogen and enriches grassland (role in "The nitrogen cycle"). To observe: dig up a clover plant, wash the roots and examine the nodules with a hand lens; cut one to see the pink colour.
- [ ] M3 Mutualism, example 2: cellulose-digesting microorganisms (bacteria, protists and fungi) in the rumen (first stomach) of cattle and sheep, and in the caecum of rabbits, digest the cellulose of grass, which the animal has no enzyme to digest; the microorganisms get a warm, food-rich home and the animal gets the products (fatty acids) as food. Rabbits also eat some of their own soft droppings to absorb nutrients released by these microorganisms.
- [ ] M4 Mutualism, example 3: mycorrhizal fungi grow around and into the roots of most grassland plants and trees; the fungal hyphae spread far through the soil, absorbing water and minerals (especially phosphate) and passing them to the plant; the plant supplies the fungus with sugars.
- [ ] M5 Mutualism, example 4: lichens (e.g. on walls, rocks and tree bark around the grassland) are a fungus and an alga (or cyanobacterium) living together: the alga photosynthesises and supplies sugars; the fungus gives shelter, holds water and absorbs minerals. Bacteria in the human gut are another example (see "The microbiome").
- [ ] M6 Pollinators and flowers benefit each other (food for the insect, pollination for the plant), so the relationship is mutualistic, but the two don't live together, so many biologists don't call it symbiosis.
- [ ] M7 Commensalism: one partner benefits, the other is neither helped nor harmed. Examples: mosses and lichens growing on the bark of trees get a place in the light without harming the tree; many bacteria living on human skin feed on dead skin cells and sweat without harming the host (see "The microbiome"); seeds with hooks (e.g. burdock) carried in an animal's fur; robins following a gardener or grazing cattle to catch the invertebrates they disturb.

Parasitism and a summary of relationships (one block, Higher Level only)
- [ ] X1 Parasitism: the parasite lives in or on the host and feeds on it, harming it; a parasite usually weakens its host without killing it quickly, since it depends on it (endoparasites and ectoparasites are defined in "Pathogens"; use the terms without redefining them).
- [ ] X2 Grassland examples: ectoparasites: fleas on rabbits and hedgehogs, ticks on sheep, deer and humans (ticks are also vectors of Lyme disease); the rabbit flea carries the myxoma virus that causes myxomatosis, which has killed very large numbers of rabbits; endoparasites: liver fluke in sheep and cattle on wet pasture, roundworms and tapeworms in the gut of grazing animals (detail of liver fluke and tapeworms in "Pathogens").
- [ ] X3 Plant parasites: rust fungi on grasses and cereals; yellow rattle, a meadow plant that is a partial parasite (it photosynthesises but taps the roots of grasses for water and minerals), weakening the grasses so that other wild flowers can grow; for this reason it is sown when restoring wildflower meadows.
- [ ] X4 Effects on populations: parasites and disease weaken hosts, making them easier prey and less able to breed; they spread fastest when hosts are crowded, so their effect increases with population density, like predation (P6).
- [ ] X5 Summary table (one bullet per relationship): predation (+/−, predator kills and eats prey; fox–rabbit); competition (−/−, both lose some of a scarce resource; grass–daisy for light; grey–red squirrel); mutualism (+/+; Rhizobium in clover); commensalism (+/0; mosses on tree bark); parasitism (+/−, host harmed, usually not killed quickly; flea on rabbit). Predation and parasitism are both +/−; the difference is that a predator kills its prey at once and eats it, while a parasite lives on a living host over a long time.

## 3. Traps

- Predation and parasitism are both +/−; distinguish them by whether the host is killed and eaten at once.
- Symbiosis is the umbrella term (all three types), not a synonym for mutualism.
- Competition is for a resource in short supply; organisms sharing a plentiful resource are not competing.
- Intraspecific (same species) and interspecific (different species) are easily confused.
- Grazing is herbivory: the rabbit is a primary consumer, not a predator of grass; keep "predation" for animals that kill and eat animals.
- In the predator–prey cycle, the predator peak comes after the prey peak.
- Lichens and Rhizobium are mutualism, not commensalism or parasitism. Ivy is not a parasite: it climbs trees but makes its own food and takes nothing from them.
- Food chain, food web, trophic level, energy loss and pyramids are defined and explained in "Energy and nutrient transfer", "Pyramids of numbers" and "Pyramids of biomass"; the niche in "The niche"; limiting factors and carrying capacity in "Carrying capacity and limiting factors". Use them, don't re-teach them.
- The nitrogen-fixation chemistry belongs to "The nitrogen cycle"; M2 gives the relationship only. Pathogen details belong to "Pathogens"; the human microbiome to "The microbiome".
- Keep competition and symbiosis in their own Higher Level blocks; don't use "mutualism" or "commensalism" in the Ordinary-level blocks.
- The diagram cues in F5 and P5 are the only ones wanted. No other diagram cues.
