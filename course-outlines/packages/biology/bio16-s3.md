## 1. Must cover

What DNA replication is (one block)
- [ ] R1 DNA replication is the process by which a DNA molecule makes an exact copy of itself, giving two identical DNA molecules.
- [ ] R2 When and where: in eukaryotic cells it happens in the nucleus during interphase, before the cell divides (the DNA-synthesis part of the cell cycle; see "The cell cycle"). Every chromosome in the nucleus is copied once, and only once, per cycle.
- [ ] R3 Why it is needed: a cell that divides must give each daughter cell a complete and identical set of genetic instructions. Without replication each daughter cell would receive only half the DNA.

How DNA is replicated (one process block, numbered steps, Ordinary level)
- [ ] P1 1. Enzymes unwind the double helix and separate (unzip) the two strands by breaking the weak hydrogen bonds between the base pairs. The strong bonds of each sugar–phosphate backbone are not broken, so each strand stays intact.
- [ ] P2 2. Each separated single strand acts as a template (pattern) for building a new strand.
- [ ] P3 3. Free DNA nucleotides in the nucleus line up against the exposed bases of each template strand by complementary base pairing: A pairs with T, and C pairs with G.
- [ ] P4 4. Enzymes join the new nucleotides to each other to form a new sugar–phosphate backbone, making a complementary strand; hydrogen bonds form between the new and old bases.
- [ ] P5 5. Each new DNA molecule winds back into a double helix. Result: two DNA molecules, each made of one original (parent) strand and one new strand, which is why replication is described as semi-conservative (the evidence is in "Modelling DNA replication and information flow").
- [ ] P6 Because base pairing is fixed, each new strand is an exact complement of its template, so both new molecules have the same base sequence as the original. Worked example: a section of DNA with one strand ATTGCA and the other TAACGT separates; ATTGCA is a template for a new TAACGT, and TAACGT is a template for a new ATTGCA, giving two molecules identical to the original.
- [ ] P7 Enzymes make replication fast and accurate; they also check the new strands and correct most mistakes. A rare uncorrected mistake changes the base sequence (a mutation; detail in "Mutations", Higher Level).
- [ ] P8 Diagram: a short section of DNA (about four base pairs) drawn as a ladder: parent molecule → strands separating, with free nucleotides pairing with the exposed bases → two daughter molecules, each with one old strand (drawn dark) and one new strand (drawn light). Label: parent strand, new strand, hydrogen bonds broken, free nucleotide, complementary base pair, enzyme.

The role of replication and mitosis in the cell cycle (one block)
- [ ] M1 Replication in interphase doubles the DNA: after it, each chromosome consists of two identical copies joined together, so the cell temporarily has twice the normal amount of DNA (a human cell still has 46 chromosomes, but each is double).
- [ ] M2 Mitosis then separates the two copies of every chromosome, sending one copy to each end of the cell, so each daughter nucleus receives one copy of every chromosome: 46 chromosomes in each human daughter cell, the same number and the same genes as the parent.
- [ ] M3 Cytokinesis then divides the cell, so the amount of DNA in each daughter cell is back to the amount in the parent cell before replication. Each daughter cell can then grow and repeat the cycle.
- [ ] M4 Replication and mitosis together therefore keep the genetic information the same in every cell of a multicellular organism, and pass identical copies to offspring in asexual reproduction (roles in "Mitosis and meiosis").
- [ ] M5 Order matters: replication must be complete before mitosis begins (checked by the cell), and it happens only once per cycle; otherwise daughter cells would get missing or extra DNA. When this control fails, cells can be damaged or become cancerous (see "Cancer").
- [ ] M6 Diagram: a graph of the amount of DNA per cell (y-axis, in arbitrary units) against time (x-axis) over two cell cycles: steady at one level in early interphase, rising to double during DNA replication, steady at double until the end of mitosis, then dropping back to the original level at cytokinesis. Label interphase, DNA replication, mitosis and cytokinesis.

## 3. Traps

- Don't name helicase or DNA polymerase, or mention ATP, Meselson and Stahl, or conservative and dispersive models here; they belong to "Modelling DNA replication and information flow" (Higher Level). Say "enzymes".
- Don't teach antiparallel strands, 3′ and 5′ ends, leading and lagging strands, Okazaki fragments, primers or ligase anywhere in this chapter; they are not on the specification.
- Replication happens in interphase, not during mitosis. It produces two DNA molecules, not two cells.
- Replication breaks hydrogen bonds between bases, not the bonds of the sugar–phosphate backbone.
- Base pairing in DNA is A–T and C–G; uracil is never used in replication (U is used when RNA is made, in "Transcription and translation").
- The chromosome number does not double at replication: the number of chromosomes stays at 46; each chromosome becomes double. Chromatids and the centromere are Higher Level terms: use "two identical copies joined together" in this subsection.
- The stages of mitosis belong to "The cell cycle" (Higher Level); M2 gives the outcome only. The roles of mitosis in organisms belong to "Mitosis and meiosis".
- Diagram cues: P8 and M6 only. No other diagram cues.
