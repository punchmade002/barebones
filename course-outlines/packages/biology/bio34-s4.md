## 1. Must cover

The greenhouse effect (one process block)
- [ ] G1 1. Sunlight (mostly visible and short-wave radiation) passes through the atmosphere and warms the Earth's surface; 2. the warm surface gives off long-wave infrared radiation (heat); 3. greenhouse gases in the atmosphere (water vapour, carbon dioxide, methane, nitrous oxide) absorb some of this infrared and re-emit it in all directions, including back to the surface; 4. so the lower atmosphere and surface are warmer than they would otherwise be.
- [ ] G2 The natural greenhouse effect is essential: without it the Earth's average surface temperature would be about −18 °C instead of about 15 °C, too cold for most life. The enhanced greenhouse effect is the extra warming caused by human increases in greenhouse gases.
- [ ] G3 Climate change is the long-term change in the Earth's climate (temperature, rainfall, extreme weather), now driven mainly by the enhanced greenhouse effect. Global warming is the rise in average surface temperature, one part of climate change.
- [ ] G4 Diagram: the greenhouse effect: Sun, incoming sunlight passing through the atmosphere, Earth's surface, outgoing infrared, a layer of greenhouse gases, some infrared escaping to space and some re-emitted back to the surface; labels sunlight, infrared (heat), greenhouse gases (CO₂, CH₄).

The link between CO₂, methane and climate change (one discussion block)
- [ ] L1 Carbon dioxide: atmospheric CO₂ has risen from about 280 parts per million before the Industrial Revolution to over 420 parts per million today, mainly from burning fossil fuels and from deforestation (sources and stores in "The carbon cycle"). It is the largest single cause of human-caused warming because so much is released and it stays in the atmosphere for centuries.
- [ ] L2 Methane (CH₄): its concentration has more than doubled since before the Industrial Revolution. Sources: anaerobic microorganisms (archaea) in the stomachs (rumens) of cattle and sheep, released mainly by belching; manure and slurry stores; rice paddies; landfill sites; natural wetlands and bogs; leaks from natural-gas wells and pipelines. Kilogram for kilogram it traps far more heat than CO₂: over 100 years about 30 times as much, and over 20 years about 80 times. But it lasts only about 12 years in the air before it is broken down to CO₂, so cutting methane slows warming quickly.
- [ ] L3 Nitrous oxide (N₂O) from fertilised and waterlogged soils and from manure (denitrification; see "The nitrogen cycle") is a third biological greenhouse gas, kilogram for kilogram about 270 times as powerful as CO₂ over 100 years. One bullet.
- [ ] L4 Ireland: agriculture is the largest single source of Ireland's greenhouse gas emissions, mainly methane from cattle and nitrous oxide from fertiliser and manure, because Ireland has a large, grass-fed cattle herd. Ireland's law (the Climate Action and Low Carbon Development (Amendment) Act 2021) sets a 51% cut in greenhouse gas emissions by 2030 compared with 2018, and climate neutrality by 2050; agriculture's target is a 25% cut by 2030.
- [ ] L5 Evidence for the link: greenhouse gases absorb infrared, measured in the laboratory since the nineteenth century; direct measurements show CO₂ and methane rising together with global temperature; air bubbles trapped in ancient ice cores show CO₂ and temperature rose and fell together over hundreds of thousands of years; the observed warming pattern (lower atmosphere warming while the upper atmosphere cools) fits greenhouse gases, not a brighter Sun. The Intergovernmental Panel on Climate Change (IPCC) concluded in 2021 that it is unequivocal that human influence has warmed the climate, by about 1.1 °C (2011–2020 compared with 1850–1900). The Paris Agreement (2015) aims to keep warming well below 2 °C and to pursue efforts to limit it to 1.5 °C. (Scientific consensus is in "Evaluating evidence".)
- [ ] L6 Uncertainties and limits of the evidence (one bullet): correlation alone doesn't prove cause (here the physics of infrared absorption supplies the mechanism); exactly how much warming a given rise in CO₂ causes, and how regional rainfall will change, are less certain than the direction of change; feedbacks are hard to model.
- [ ] L7 Feedbacks (one bullet each): warming speeds up decomposition by soil microorganisms, releasing more CO₂ (see "The microbiome" for soil microbiomes and warming); thawing permafrost (frozen soil in the Arctic) lets stored organic matter decompose, releasing CO₂ and methane; warmer oceans dissolve less CO₂; drought and fires turn forests from sinks into sources.
- [ ] L8 Biological effects of climate change (one bullet each, to show why it matters): species' ranges move towards the poles or uphill, and species that can't move or adapt face extinction (see "Biodiversity loss and conservation"); timing of events such as flowering, breeding and migration changes, so species may get out of step with their food; more CO₂ dissolving in the oceans makes them more acidic (ocean acidification), which makes it harder for corals, shellfish and some phytoplankton to build calcium carbonate shells; warmer seas cause coral bleaching; insects that spread disease can spread to new areas (see "Emergence and spread of infectious disease").

Carbon sinks compared (one comparison block; builds on "Photosynthesis as an anabolic process" and "The carbon cycle"; don't redefine carbon sink)
- [ ] S1 Forests and other land plants: trees store carbon in wood for decades or centuries, and in roots and soil. Strong while trees grow; a mature forest takes in roughly what it releases; the store is lost quickly if the forest burns, dies or is felled.
- [ ] S2 Phytoplankton and algae: microscopic algae and cyanobacteria in the oceans carry out about half of all photosynthesis on Earth. When they die or are eaten, some of their carbon sinks to the deep ocean and sea floor (the "biological pump"), where it can stay for centuries or longer. Seaweeds such as kelp, and seagrass meadows and saltmarshes, also store carbon ("blue carbon").
- [ ] S3 Bogs and other peatlands: waterlogged, acidic, low-oxygen conditions stop decomposition (see "The carbon cycle"), so a healthy bog accumulates peat very slowly over thousands of years. Peatlands cover only about 3% of the world's land but hold more carbon than all the world's forests. A bog is a sink only while it stays wet.
- [ ] S4 Soils: soil organic matter (humus) stores more carbon than all plants and the atmosphere together; grassland and undisturbed soils keep it, while ploughing and draining release it.
- [ ] S5 Comparison (one bullet): bogs store the most carbon per hectare and for longest but gain it very slowly; forests take up carbon fastest but store it less securely; oceans are the largest active sink but are becoming more acidic and absorb less as they warm. All natural sinks together absorb only about half of the CO₂ humans release each year, so sinks can slow the rise but not replace cutting emissions.

Biological strategies to reduce CO₂ (one evaluation block; each bullet: how it works → for → against → judgement)
- [ ] B1 Protecting existing forests, bogs and wetlands. Works by keeping carbon in place. For: the quickest and cheapest way to avoid emissions, with large benefits for biodiversity and water. Against: land is valuable for farming or development, and protection needs enforcement and payment to landowners (ethics in "Ethics and sustainability of nutrient cycling"). Judgement: the most effective first step.
- [ ] B2 Afforestation (planting new forests) and reforestation (replanting cleared land). Works by photosynthesis fixing CO₂ into wood. For: takes up carbon for decades; native broadleaved woodland also supports biodiversity, reduces flooding and gives timber that can replace concrete and steel. Against: slow (decades to reach full uptake); fire, storms and disease can release the carbon; needs land that could grow food; conifers planted on deep peat can release more carbon from the drained peat than the trees take up; dense plantations of one non-native species (in Ireland mostly Sitka spruce) support less wildlife. Ireland's forest cover is about 11%, among the lowest in the EU. Judgement: valuable if the right trees are planted in the right places, but slow.
- [ ] B3 Rewetting and restoring bogs. Works by blocking drains so the water table rises, conditions become anaerobic again, decomposition stops and sphagnum moss starts forming peat. For: quickly stops the large CO₂ release from drained peat; restores rare habitat; holds water and reduces flooding; Bord na Móna's cutaway bogs are being rewetted on a large scale. Against: rewetted bogs give off some methane (from anaerobic decomposition), and new peat forms very slowly, so the gain is mainly avoided CO₂ loss rather than fast uptake; land can no longer be used for turf or farming. Judgement: strongly beneficial for Ireland because so much peat is drained.
- [ ] B4 Algae and phytoplankton. Seaweed farming and algae grown in tanks fix CO₂ fast, and algae can be made into food, feed or biofuel. For: algae grow much faster than land plants and need no farmland or fresh water. Against: if the algae are eaten or burned the carbon returns to the air, so it is a lasting sink only if stored; adding iron to the ocean to cause phytoplankton blooms has been tested, but little of the carbon was shown to reach the deep ocean and it risks harming marine ecosystems (for example, low-oxygen zones), so it is not an accepted strategy. Judgement: promising for fuels and food, unproven as large-scale storage.
- [ ] B5 Increasing soil carbon (one bullet): keeping grassland unploughed, adding manure and compost, growing cover crops over winter, reduced tillage, and planting hedgerows add organic matter to soil. For: also improves soil fertility and water-holding. Against: soils fill up (saturate) after some decades, the gain is hard to measure, and it is lost again if the land is ploughed.
- [ ] B6 Bioenergy in place of fossil fuels (one bullet): biofuels (see "Industrial enzymes") and wood or other biomass burned in power stations release CO₂ that the plants recently took in, so in principle they add little; capturing and storing the CO₂ from burning biomass could even remove carbon. Against: fuel crops compete with food for land, clearing land for them releases carbon, and growing, harvesting and transporting them uses fossil fuel.

Biological strategies to reduce methane (one evaluation block)
- [ ] M1 Changes to cattle diet and breeding. Feed additives (e.g. the chemical 3-NOP, and some seaweeds) inhibit the methane-producing microorganisms in the rumen, cutting methane in trials; better-quality feed and earlier finishing of cattle mean less methane per kilogram of meat or milk; breeding cattle that naturally produce less methane is being researched. For: tackles Ireland's largest methane source directly without cutting production. Against: additives are hard to give to cattle that graze outdoors for most of the year, long-term effects and costs are still being studied, some seaweeds contain a compound (bromoform) with health concerns, and breeding is slow.
- [ ] M2 Fewer livestock and dietary change. For: fewer cattle produce less methane and need less land, which could be used for forests or bogs; eating less red meat and dairy reduces demand. Against: farming is central to Ireland's rural economy and exports; cutting the Irish herd may only move production to countries with higher emissions per animal (carbon leakage); meat and milk are good sources of protein, iron and calcium. Judgement: a fair transition needs support for farmers.
- [ ] M3 Anaerobic digestion of slurry, food waste and grass. Microorganisms break down the waste without oxygen in a sealed tank, producing biogas (mainly methane) that is burned for heat or electricity or upgraded to replace natural gas, and a digestate used as fertiliser. For: captures methane that would escape from open slurry stores and landfill; replaces a fossil fuel; recycles nutrients. Against: plants are expensive; leaks release methane; burning the biogas still releases CO₂ (but recent, not fossil, carbon).
- [ ] M4 Other methods (one bullet): capping landfill sites and collecting their gas; composting food waste aerobically instead of sending it to landfill; reducing food waste; covering slurry stores. Rewetted bogs and rice paddies show that wet anaerobic soil makes methane, a trade-off with storing carbon.
- [ ] M5 Nitrous oxide (one bullet): using clover in grassland and applying fertiliser precisely, and using "protected" urea fertiliser that releases nitrogen more slowly, reduce nitrous oxide (and nitrate leaching, see "Ethics and sustainability of nutrient cycling").

The role of biology in a post-fossil-fuel world (one block)
- [ ] P1 A post-fossil-fuel world is one in which energy, fuels, materials and chemicals no longer come from coal, oil and gas. Biology contributes by providing renewable replacements made from recently fixed carbon and by protecting the sinks that absorb CO₂.
- [ ] P2 Biofuels and biogas: bioethanol from fermentation of sugars by yeast (fermentation is in "Respiration as a catabolic process"; enzymes breaking down cellulose for fuel are in "Industrial enzymes"); biodiesel from plant oils and waste cooking oil; biogas from anaerobic digestion (M3); fuels from algae.
- [ ] P3 Bio-based materials: bioplastics made from plant starch or sugars (e.g. polylactic acid, PLA), some of which are biodegradable; wood in construction; plant fibres in place of oil-based fibres.
- [ ] P4 Microorganisms as factories (biomanufacturing): bacteria, yeasts and algae grown in fermenters (bioreactors) can make medicines, enzymes, food proteins and chemicals now made from oil, using sugars or waste as raw materials (see "Microorganisms in industry"; genetic modification in "Genetic engineering").
- [ ] P5 Food and farming: breeding (including by genetic technologies) crops that tolerate drought, heat and new pests, and that need less fertiliser; using legumes and soil microbiomes instead of manufactured fertiliser, which is made with fossil energy ("The nitrogen cycle"); reducing food waste.
- [ ] P6 Biology also monitors and understands change: measuring sinks, tracking species' ranges, and predicting disease spread.
- [ ] P7 Limits (one bullet): biomass can't replace all fossil fuels because land is needed for food and nature; bio-based products are not automatically sustainable (they depend on how the raw material is grown); so biology works alongside other renewables (wind, solar) and using less energy.

## 3. Traps

- The definition of a carbon sink and the bullets on photosynthesis as a carbon sink are in "Photosynthesis as an anabolic process"; the stores and processes of the carbon cycle are in "The carbon cycle". Don't redefine or redraw them; S1–S5 compare sinks and add new facts only.
- Ethical arguments on deforestation, peat cutting and fertiliser use are in "Ethics and sustainability of nutrient cycling". Here evaluate the strategies' biological effectiveness; mention fairness only in a phrase (M2).
- The greenhouse effect is not the same as the hole in the ozone layer; don't link them.
- Greenhouse gases absorb and re-emit infrared (heat) from the Earth, not incoming sunlight; don't say they "trap the Sun's rays".
- Cattle methane comes mainly from belching (microorganisms in the rumen), not mainly from flatulence or manure.
- Methane's warming power is per kilogram (don't say "per molecule"). It is broken down within about 12 years; CO₂ persists for centuries. Students often say methane is "worse" without this qualification.
- Don't claim trees or any one sink can offset all emissions (S5).
- Biofuels are not "carbon-neutral" without qualification (B6).
- Don't name nitrogen-cycle or methane-producing microorganism species.
- Soil microbiomes and warming are taught in "The microbiome"; L7 gives one phrase.
- Biodiversity effects in detail belong to "Biodiversity loss and conservation"; L8 gives one bullet each.
- Diagram cue G4 only. No other diagram cues.
