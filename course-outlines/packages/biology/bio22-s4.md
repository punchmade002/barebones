## 1. Must cover

Why viruses must replicate inside cells (lead of the first block; one bullet)
- [ ] Q1 (assumed) A virus has no ribosomes, almost no enzymes and no energy supply, so it can't make anything by itself; it reproduces by delivering its nucleic acid into a host cell, which then makes new virus particles using its own ribosomes, enzymes, ATP, nucleotides and amino acids (structure in "Viruses").

The general replication cycle (one process block, numbered stages)
- [ ] R1 1. Attachment: proteins on the capsid or envelope bind to specific receptor proteins on the host cell membrane. Examples: HIV binds to the CD4 receptor on helper T cells; SARS-CoV-2's spike protein binds to the ACE2 receptor on cells lining the airways. This decides which cells a virus can infect.
- [ ] R2 2. Entry (penetration): a bacteriophage injects only its nucleic acid through the bacterial wall, leaving the empty coat outside; animal viruses enter whole, either by the host membrane folding in around them (endocytosis) or, for enveloped viruses, by the envelope fusing with the host membrane.
- [ ] R3 3. Uncoating: inside the cell, the capsid breaks down and releases the viral nucleic acid.
- [ ] R4 4. Replication and synthesis: the viral genes take over the cell. Viral mRNA is made and the host's ribosomes translate it into viral proteins (capsid proteins and viral enzymes); the viral nucleic acid is copied many times. Host-cell activities are often shut down.
- [ ] R5 5. Assembly: new nucleic acid and capsid proteins come together to form many new virus particles (often hundreds per cell).
- [ ] R6 6. Release: either the cell bursts (lysis), releasing all the viruses at once and killing the cell, or, for enveloped viruses, each new virus pushes out through the membrane and takes a piece of it as its envelope (budding), so the cell can survive for a time. The new viruses infect neighbouring cells and the cycle repeats.
- [ ] R7 Diagram: the lytic cycle of a bacteriophage in five or six sketches in a circle: phage attached to a bacterium by its tail fibres; DNA injected; phage DNA copied and phage proteins made; new phages assembled; cell bursting and releasing phages. Label phage, bacterial cell wall, phage DNA, bacterial chromosome, new phages.
- [ ] R8 Diagram: an enveloped animal virus (e.g. influenza) entering a cell by endocytosis or fusion and leaving by budding, with the host membrane forming the new envelope.

Lytic and lysogenic cycles (one block)
- [ ] L1 Lytic cycle: the virus replicates immediately and the host cell bursts (R1–R6). Example: bacteriophage T4 infecting the bacterium E. coli.
- [ ] L2 Lysogenic cycle: some viruses insert their DNA into the host cell's chromosome, where it stays inactive and is copied every time the cell divides, so all daughter cells carry it (in bacteria the inserted DNA is called a prophage). Example: bacteriophage lambda in E. coli. Later, a trigger (e.g. stress such as UV light) can cut the viral DNA out and start the lytic cycle.
- [ ] L3 Latency in human viruses: some viruses hide in cells in an inactive state for years and reactivate later. Herpes simplex virus stays in nerve cells after the first infection and reactivates to cause repeated cold sores; varicella-zoster virus causes chickenpox, stays in nerve cells, and can reactivate decades later as shingles. A latent virus is hidden from the immune system and from antiviral drugs.

Modes of action: how viruses harm the host (one block)
- [ ] M1 Killing cells: lysis destroys the infected cells, e.g. influenza destroys cells lining the airways, causing a sore throat and cough; the cold virus damages cells of the nose and throat.
- [ ] M2 Disrupting cell function: the virus diverts the cell's ribosomes, enzymes and energy to making viruses, so the cell stops its normal work.
- [ ] M3 Damaging the immune system: HIV infects and destroys helper T cells. As their numbers fall over years, the immune system can no longer coordinate responses, and the person develops AIDS (acquired immune deficiency syndrome), dying of infections and cancers that a healthy immune system would control (opportunistic infections).
- [ ] M4 Causing cancer: some viruses insert genes that interfere with the control of cell division, e.g. human papillomavirus (HPV) and cervical cancer (detail in "Causes, prevention and treatment of cancer"; one phrase here).
- [ ] M5 Triggering harmful immune responses: much of the fever, aches and tissue damage in viral illness is caused by the body's own immune response (inflammation) to infected cells.

DNA and RNA viruses (one comparison block, paired points)
- [ ] D1 A DNA virus has DNA as its genetic material; an RNA virus has RNA. Examples of DNA viruses: herpes simplex and varicella-zoster (chickenpox) viruses, human papillomavirus (HPV), adenoviruses, the smallpox virus, bacteriophages T4 and lambda. Examples of RNA viruses: influenza, measles, mumps, polio, rhinoviruses (common cold), SARS-CoV-2, HIV, Ebola, tobacco mosaic virus.
- [ ] D2 Where they replicate: most DNA viruses enter the host nucleus and use the host's own enzymes (e.g. DNA polymerase, RNA polymerase) to copy their DNA and make mRNA (poxviruses such as the smallpox virus are an exception, replicating in the cytoplasm with their own enzymes). Most RNA viruses replicate in the cytoplasm and must carry or code for their own enzyme to copy RNA from RNA (an RNA-dependent RNA polymerase), because host cells have no enzyme that does this.
- [ ] D3 Mutation rate: the enzyme that copies RNA has no proofreading, so RNA viruses make many more copying errors and mutate much faster than DNA viruses, whose DNA is copied by host polymerases that proofread. Consequences: RNA viruses such as influenza and HIV change their surface antigens quickly, so new strains escape existing immunity (flu vaccines are updated every year), and drug resistance appears quickly. (Mutation as a factor in disease emergence is in "Emergence and spread of infectious disease".)
- [ ] D4 Some RNA viruses' RNA acts directly as mRNA when it enters the cell and is translated at once (e.g. poliovirus, SARS-CoV-2); in others (e.g. influenza, measles) the RNA must first be copied into a complementary strand that acts as mRNA. One bullet; no further detail of "sense".
- [ ] D5 Stability: DNA is a more stable molecule than RNA. One phrase.

Retroviruses: HIV (one block)
- [ ] H1 A retrovirus is an RNA virus that makes a DNA copy of its RNA inside the host cell, using its own enzyme, reverse transcriptase (it reverses the usual DNA → RNA direction). Example: HIV.
- [ ] H2 HIV replication, numbered: 1. its envelope protein binds the CD4 receptor on a helper T cell; 2. the envelope fuses with the cell membrane and the capsid enters; 3. reverse transcriptase makes double-stranded DNA from the viral RNA; 4. a second viral enzyme, integrase, inserts this DNA into the host's chromosome, where it is called a provirus and can stay hidden for years; 5. when the cell is activated, the provirus is transcribed into viral mRNA and new viral RNA, and viral proteins are made; 6. new viruses assemble and bud from the cell, taking an envelope of host membrane.
- [ ] H3 Reverse transcriptase makes many errors, so HIV mutates very quickly, which is one reason there is still no effective HIV vaccine.
- [ ] H4 Diagram: an HIV particle (envelope with surface proteins, capsid, two RNA strands, reverse transcriptase) and a flow of H2 stages into and out of a helper T cell. Label receptor, viral RNA, viral DNA, host chromosome (provirus), budding virus.

How antivirals act on replication (one block)
- [ ] A1 Antiviral drugs block a step in the cycle that uses a viral protein, so the host cell isn't harmed. Examples: aciclovir blocks the DNA polymerase of herpes viruses (cold sores, shingles); oseltamivir blocks neuraminidase, the influenza enzyme that releases new viruses from the cell surface; HIV drugs include reverse transcriptase inhibitors (zidovudine, AZT, approved in 1987, was the first) and protease inhibitors, which stop new viral proteins being cut into working form.
- [ ] A2 HIV is treated with a combination of several antiretroviral drugs at once (antiretroviral therapy). This keeps the virus at very low levels and lets the immune system recover, but doesn't cure the infection, because the provirus remains in the host DNA. Using several drugs together makes resistance much less likely, since a virus would need several mutations at once.

Modelling viral replication (one Skills block)
- [ ] MD1 To model the cycle: 1. list the stages in order (attachment, entry, uncoating, replication and synthesis, assembly, release); 2. draw each as a labelled sketch or box, showing the host-cell part used at each stage (receptor, membrane, ribosomes, enzymes, nucleotides); 3. join them with arrows into a cycle; 4. mark how a DNA virus, an RNA virus and a retrovirus differ at stage 4; 5. mark where an antiviral drug would act. A physical model can use card or modelling clay for viruses and cells to show attachment and budding.
- [ ] MD2 What the model shows and leaves out (see "Models"): it shows the order of events, the dependence on host machinery, and why one infected cell releases many viruses; it simplifies the timing (a cycle can take minutes to hours), the numbers released, the molecular steps, and the differences between viruses. A worked prediction: if each infected cell releases 100 viruses and each infects a new cell, two cycles from one virus give 100 × 100 = 10,000 viruses.

## 3. Traps

- Don't restate virus structure, size, shapes or the living/non-living debate; they are in "Viruses". Use capsid, envelope and receptor without redefining.
- Don't use SARS-CoV-2 (or other coronaviruses) as an example of an RNA virus without proofreading: coronaviruses have a proofreading enzyme and mutate more slowly than most RNA viruses. Use influenza and HIV in D3.
- Hepatitis B virus is a DNA virus that copies itself through an RNA stage; leave it out of D1 to avoid confusion.
- Viruses don't "grow" or "divide"; they are assembled from parts. A provirus or prophage is viral DNA inside the host chromosome, not a whole virus.
- The lysogenic cycle is for some bacteriophages; for HIV say provirus and latency, not "lysogenic".
- How HIV's damage leads to AIDS is here (M3); helper T cells' normal job belongs to "Innate and adaptive immunity" and "White blood cells". Antibiotics belong to "Acquired immunity, prevention and treatment"; viral mutation as a cause of new strains and pandemics belongs to "Emergence and spread of infectious disease"; viruses as vectors in genetic engineering belong to "Genetic engineering".
- Don't teach positive-sense/negative-sense terminology beyond D4's description, or Baltimore classes.
- Diagram cues: R7, R8, H4 and the MD1 flow chart only. No other diagram cues.
