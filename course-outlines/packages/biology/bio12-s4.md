## 1. Must cover

Evolution as a scientific theory (one block)
- [ ] T1 Applying the definition (from "The nature of scientific knowledge"): evolution by natural selection is a theory because it is a broad explanation of a huge range of observations (the diversity of life, adaptation, shared features, the fossil record, the distribution of species); it is supported by evidence from many independent sources; it makes testable predictions; and it could be shown false by evidence, but no such evidence has been found.
- [ ] T2 The everyday meaning of "theory" (a guess or hunch) is not the scientific meaning. Saying evolution is "only a theory" misunderstands the word: in science a theory is the highest status an explanation reaches. The cell theory and the germ theory of disease are theories in the same sense.
- [ ] T3 Two parts should be distinguished: the fact of evolution (living things have changed over time and share common ancestors, which the evidence shows directly) and the theory of natural selection (the explanation of how that change happens).
- [ ] T4 It is falsifiable: e.g. finding fossils of mammals in rocks older than the first fish, or finding that the DNA of closely related species was no more similar than that of distant species, would contradict it. No such finding has been made.

Why it is so widely accepted and tested (one block)
- [ ] A1 It has been tested for over 160 years, since 1859, by scientists in every field of biology, and also in geology, chemistry and physics (radiometric dating of the Earth).
- [ ] A2 Evidence that was not known to Darwin has confirmed it: genetics (Mendel's work, recognised from 1900), the structure of DNA (1953), molecular comparisons of DNA and proteins, and many transitional fossils (see "Evidence for evolution"). Each new kind of evidence could have contradicted it and did not.
- [ ] A3 Its predictions have been confirmed: e.g. Tiktaalik was found in rocks of the age the theory predicted (F5 in "Evidence for evolution"); trees built from DNA match those built from anatomy; bacteria evolve resistance to new antibiotics; insects evolve resistance to pesticides.
- [ ] A4 It is accepted by almost all biologists and by scientific academies around the world, and is the framework used in all biological research. Acceptance is based on evidence, not on authority or majority opinion.
- [ ] A5 Like all theories, it has been refined: it was combined with genetics into the modern synthesis (detail in "The theory of evolution by natural selection"), and scientists still debate details such as how fast evolution happens and the roles of chance and of epigenetics. Debate about details within the theory is normal science, not doubt about evolution itself.

Why it is the best available explanation (one block, with comparison)
- [ ] B1 Lamarck's theory (Jean-Baptiste Lamarck, 1809): organisms change during their lives through use or disuse of body parts, and pass these acquired characteristics to their offspring, e.g. giraffes stretching to reach leaves would have offspring with longer necks.
- [ ] B2 Why natural selection is the better explanation: acquired characteristics are not passed on through the DNA (a weightlifter's children are not born with large muscles; in the 1880s August Weismann cut off the tails of mice over several generations and their offspring still had normal tails); natural selection works with variation that already exists, explains adaptation without any need for the organism to "try", and is supported by genetics. Darwin's explanation of the giraffe: giraffes with slightly longer necks, by inherited variation, reached more food in times of shortage, survived and reproduced more, and passed on the alleles for longer necks. Epigenetic inheritance (in "Genetic and epigenetic mechanisms") is a small, limited exception and does not restore Lamarck's theory.
- [ ] B3 Special creation, the belief that species were created separately in their present forms, is a matter of religious faith, not a scientific theory: it makes no testable predictions, and it doesn't explain the evidence, such as homologous and vestigial structures, the order of fossils, or the pattern of DNA similarity. Many religious people and organisations accept evolution; science and faith answer different kinds of questions.
- [ ] B4 Evolution by natural selection explains all the evidence together, with one mechanism, and makes predictions; no other explanation does this, so it is the best available explanation.

Life continues to evolve (one block)
- [ ] C1 Evolution is not only in the past: it is observed now, especially in organisms with short generations and large populations. Examples (from "Evidence for evolution" and "The theory of evolution by natural selection"; name each in a phrase, don't re-describe): antibiotic-resistant bacteria such as MRSA; the Galápagos finches' beaks after the 1977 drought; the peppered moth; the new cordgrass species Spartina anglica.
- [ ] C2 Viruses evolve fast: new variants of the virus that causes COVID-19 (SARS-CoV-2), such as Delta and Omicron, spread because their mutations helped them spread or escape immunity, so vaccines were updated; flu vaccines are updated every year for the same reason.
- [ ] C3 Pests and weeds evolve resistance to the pesticides and herbicides used against them, and cancer cells in a tumour can evolve resistance to drugs used to treat them, by the same process of selection.
- [ ] C4 Human activity is now a strong selection pressure: antibiotic and pesticide use, climate change and habitat loss. Highly specialised species with slow reproduction are least able to evolve fast enough (see "The theory of evolution by natural selection").

The importance of the theory in understanding biology (one block; one bullet per area)
- [ ] I1 Unifying idea: the geneticist Theodosius Dobzhansky wrote in 1973 that "nothing in biology makes sense except in the light of evolution". It explains the unity of life (shared cells, DNA, genetic code and ATP, from common ancestry) and its diversity (adaptation and speciation) (see "Explaining biological phenomena").
- [ ] I2 Classification: modern classification is based on evolutionary relationships (phylogeny); new DNA evidence keeps refining it (see "Classification" and "Domains of life").
- [ ] I3 Form and function: it explains why structures suit their functions, since natural selection favoured individuals whose structures did the job better.
- [ ] I4 Medicine and health: it guides the responsible use of antibiotics to slow resistance (antibiotic stewardship), the yearly updating of flu vaccines, the tracking of new virus variants, and the treatment of cancers that evolve drug resistance. Because of shared ancestry, studies of other organisms (e.g. mice, which share many genes with humans) can tell us about human biology and disease.
- [ ] I5 Agriculture and food: selective breeding of crops and animals (artificial selection), keeping varied crop genes to protect against disease (the lesson of the Irish potato, in "Variation"), and managing pesticide use to slow resistance.
- [ ] I6 Conservation and sustainability: it shows why genetic variation in a population matters for its survival when the environment changes, why small, inbred populations of endangered species are at risk, and why specialised species are vulnerable to climate change and habitat loss.
- [ ] I7 Genetics and technology: comparing genomes of different species to find which genes are shared and what they do (see "Bioinformatics: genome databases") depends on evolutionary relationships.
- [ ] I8 Limits of the theory: it explains how life changed and diversified once it existed; how the first living cells arose from non-living chemicals is a separate question, still being researched, and is not part of the theory of natural selection.

## 3. Traps

- Don't redefine "theory", "hypothesis" and "law" in general or repeat their comparison; that is in "The nature of scientific knowledge". T1–T2 apply the definition to evolution.
- Don't say evolution is "proven" or "a fact" without qualification; evidence supports it overwhelmingly (T3 separates the fact of change from the theory of mechanism). Don't say a theory becomes a law.
- Don't re-describe the evidence (fossils, anatomy, embryology, molecular, antibiotic resistance, speciation) or the steps of natural selection; refer to "Evidence for evolution" and "The theory of evolution by natural selection".
- Treat religious belief respectfully: say special creation is not a scientific theory because it isn't testable (B3), not that it is foolish. Don't name religions or organisations.
- Lamarck is taught here only as the comparison for "best available explanation"; don't give him more than B1–B2.
- Origin of life experiments are not on the specification; I8 states the limit in one bullet.
- Cross-cutting themes: name the Health (I4), Sustainability (I5–I6, C4) and Technology (I7, C2) links; don't redefine the themes (in "The cross-cutting themes").
