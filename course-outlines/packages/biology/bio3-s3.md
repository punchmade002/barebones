# Package: bio3-s3 — The cross-cutting themes

- **loId:** `bio3-s3` · **chapterId:** `bio3` · **syllabusRef:** Strands overview
- **Syllabus:** the specification "identifies three cross-cutting themes – Health,
  Sustainability and Technology. These themes, illustrated as surrounding the contextual
  strands, permeate and provide contexts for the study of these strands. They act as lenses
  through which students explore the application of knowledge from biology. Through these
  lenses, students engage with contemporary issues in biology." The written examination
  assesses the "application of Biology to issues relating to the cross-cutting
  themes—sustainability, health, and technology." There is no learning outcome of its own.
  Ordinary and Higher level.
- **Prepared by:** a higher model, 2026-10-22. This subsection is a map: each theme gets a
  definition, what the lens asks, and examples drawn from the course, each pointing to the
  chapter where it is taught. Don't teach the biology of each example here; one sentence each.

## 1. Must cover

What the themes are (one block)
- [ ] O1 The three cross-cutting themes are Health, Sustainability and Technology. They are not separate topics: they run through all three contextual strands (Organisation of Life; Structures and Processes of Life; Interactions of Life), and are shown in the specification's diagram as a ring around them.
- [ ] O2 Each theme is a lens: a way of looking at any piece of biology to ask how it applies to a real issue today. The same topic can be viewed through more than one lens, e.g. genetically engineered insulin is a health issue (treating diabetes) and a technology issue (recombinant DNA).
- [ ] O3 Why they matter: the exam asks students to apply biology to issues linked to the themes, often using unfamiliar stimulus material (a news report, data, a case), so students must be able to bring knowledge from any chapter to the issue.

Health (one block: what the lens asks, then examples)
- [ ] H1 The Health lens asks how biology explains health and disease, how disease is prevented, diagnosed and treated, and how lifestyle and environment affect health. The specification frames health as "promoting health and reducing the risk of disease".
- [ ] H2 Examples: vaccination and immunity, and how vaccination of enough people protects those who are not immune (see "Acquired immunity, prevention and treatment"); the spread of emerging infectious diseases and the reproduction number, R₀ (see "Emergence and spread of infectious disease"); a balanced diet and diet-related conditions such as obesity and vitamin deficiencies (see "Diet" and "Vitamins"); the causes, prevention and treatment of cancer, e.g. avoiding smoking and excess UV light, and vaccination against the human papillomavirus (HPV), which causes most cervical cancers (see "Causes, prevention and treatment of cancer"); the gut microbiome's role in digestion, immunity and metabolic health (see "The microbiome"); dopamine, endorphins, exercise and addiction (see "Dopamine and endorphins"); prenatal and postnatal care (see "Prenatal and postnatal care"); blood groups and safe transfusion (see "Blood"); hormonal treatments such as insulin and the contraceptive pill (see "Hormonal manipulation").

Sustainability (one block)
- [ ] S1 Sustainability means meeting the needs of the present without compromising the ability of future generations to meet their own needs. The Sustainability lens asks how human activity affects ecosystems, biodiversity, resources and climate, and how biology can help people live within the planet's limits.
- [ ] S2 Examples: biodiversity loss through habitat destruction, pollution, invasive species and climate change, and the role of conservation (see "Biodiversity loss and conservation"); the carbon cycle, carbon sinks and climate change, including Ireland's peat bogs, which store large amounts of carbon as peat and release it as CO₂ when drained or burned (see "Climate change and biological strategies"); the nitrogen cycle and excess fertiliser or slurry washing into rivers and lakes, causing eutrophication (algal blooms that use up oxygen when they decay, killing fish) (see "Ethics and sustainability of nutrient cycling"); decomposers and soil microbiomes in recycling nutrients (see "The microbiome"); photosynthesis as a carbon sink (see "Photosynthesis as an anabolic process"); enzymes in biofuels and in detergents that wash at lower temperatures (see "Industrial enzymes"); the role of biology in a post-fossil-fuel world.

Technology (one block)
- [ ] TE1 Technology is the application of scientific knowledge to make tools, techniques and products. The Technology lens asks how technology lets biologists find out more, and how biological knowledge is used to make new technologies (biotechnology: the use of living organisms or their parts, such as enzymes, to make useful products or carry out processes).
- [ ] TE2 Technologies that extend what biologists can study: light and electron microscopes (see "Microscopy"); DNA sequencing and genome databases, which use computers to analyse huge amounts of genetic data (see "DNA sequencing" and "Bioinformatics: genome databases"); data loggers and sensors in investigations; digital tools for modelling and analysing data.
- [ ] TE3 Biotechnologies: genetic engineering, e.g. human insulin made by bacteria (see "Genetic engineering"); PCR and DNA profiling in forensics and paternity testing (see "DNA profiling"); immobilised enzymes and bioreactors (see "Industrial enzymes"); microorganisms in pharmaceutical, agricultural, medical and food industries (see "Microorganisms in industry"); antenatal screening and fertility treatment (see "Prenatal and postnatal care" and "Fertility control and infertility"); stem cells and gene technologies, whose ethics are discussed in "Ethics of genetic technologies".
- [ ] TE4 New technologies raise questions as well as benefits: cost and access, safety, privacy of genetic data, and effects on the environment (the factors for evaluating them are in "Science, scientists and society").

Applying a lens to an issue (one Skills block: numbered method, then two worked examples)
- [ ] A1 Method: 1. Identify the biology behind the issue (the structures, processes or organisms involved). 2. Say which theme or themes it relates to, and why. 3. Explain the issue's causes or mechanism using that biology. 4. Give its benefits or problems, with evidence. 5. Suggest actions or solutions based on the biology. 6. Note the links to the other themes.
- [ ] A2 Worked example, antibiotic resistance (all three themes). Biology: bacteria with mutations that let them survive an antibiotic survive and reproduce, so resistant strains increase by natural selection. Health: infections such as MRSA become hard or impossible to treat. Sustainability: overuse of antibiotics in medicine and in farming speeds this up, and antibiotics are a resource that is being used up faster than new ones are found. Technology: rapid diagnostic tests to show whether an infection is bacterial, genome sequencing to track resistant strains, and the search for new antibiotics. Actions: prescribe antibiotics only for bacterial infections, take them exactly as prescribed, improve hygiene and vaccination to reduce infections, and limit antibiotic use in farm animals.
- [ ] A3 Worked example, Irish peatlands (sustainability with technology). Biology: in waterlogged, acidic, low-oxygen bogs, decomposers work very slowly, so dead plant material (mainly Sphagnum moss) builds up as peat, storing carbon. Sustainability: draining and cutting bogs for fuel lets oxygen in, so the peat decomposes or is burned, releasing CO₂; bogs are also habitats for specialised species. Action: Bord na Móna ended industrial peat harvesting in 2021, and drained bogs are being rewetted to stop carbon loss and restore habitats. Technology: satellite and sensor monitoring of water levels and greenhouse-gas release. Health link: burning peat and other solid fuels pollutes air.

## 3. Traps

- Each example is taught in its own chapter. Give one sentence per example and the chapter subsection title; don't teach the biology (e.g. don't explain the carbon cycle or how PCR works here).
- The four factors (economic, social, sustainability, ethical) for evaluating a scientist's impact are taught in "Science, scientists and society". Sustainability is both a factor there and a theme here; define it once here (S1) and let that subsection cross-refer.
- Health, Sustainability and Technology are the only three themes. Don't add "Ethics" or "Society" as a fourth theme.
- Don't claim HPV vaccination prevents all cervical cancer.
- No diagram cues.

## 4. What exams ask (topics only)

No legacy questions; the themes are new to this specification. Expect:
- Given stimulus material on a contemporary issue (e.g. a disease outbreak, biodiversity loss, a new biotechnology), explain the biology and discuss it through one or more of the themes.
- Name the three cross-cutting themes and give an example of how a named topic relates to each.

## 5. Practice questions to write

1. 6 marks: "Name the three cross-cutting themes in Leaving Certificate Biology and give one biological example of an issue linked to each." Model answer: Health, Sustainability, Technology (O1), each with one example from H2, S2 or TE2–TE3.
2. 12 marks: "Antibiotic resistance is described as a health, sustainability and technology issue. Explain why, using your knowledge of biology." Model answer: A2: the natural-selection mechanism, then one or more points under each theme and at least one action.
3. 9 marks: "Explain why Irish bogs are important carbon sinks and how human activity affects them." Model answer: A3 biology (slow decomposition in waterlogged, acidic, low-oxygen conditions), the effect of draining and burning, and rewetting as a response.
