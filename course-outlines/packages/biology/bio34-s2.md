## 1. Must cover

Why nitrogen must be cycled (one block)
- [ ] N1 Living things need nitrogen to make amino acids and proteins, nucleic acids (DNA and RNA), ATP and chlorophyll (why plants need it, and deficiency signs, are in "Minerals"; one phrase only).
- [ ] N2 About 78% of the air is nitrogen gas (N₂), but plants and animals cannot use it: the two atoms are held by a very strong triple bond that few organisms can break. Plants can absorb nitrogen only as ions dissolved in soil water, mainly nitrate (NO₃⁻), and also ammonium (NH₄⁺). So nitrogen must be converted (fixed) before it enters food chains, and recycled from dead matter.
- [ ] N3 (assumed) The forms to recognise: nitrogen gas N₂; ammonia NH₃ and ammonium NH₄⁺; nitrite NO₂⁻; nitrate NO₃⁻; organic nitrogen in proteins, nucleic acids and urea.

Nitrogen fixation (one block)
- [ ] X1 Nitrogen fixation is the conversion of nitrogen gas (N₂) from the air into nitrogen compounds that living things can use (ammonium, ammonia or nitrate).
- [ ] X2 Biological fixation by free-living nitrogen-fixing bacteria in the soil and in water: they convert N₂ to ammonium, which they use to make their own proteins; when they die, decomposition releases it to the soil.
- [ ] X3 Biological fixation by nitrogen-fixing bacteria in root nodules (small swellings on the roots) of legumes, the pea family, e.g. clover, peas and beans. The bacteria give the plant ammonium, which it uses to make amino acids; the plant gives the bacteria carbohydrates (from photosynthesis) and a protected, low-oxygen home. Both benefit, so this is mutualism (defined in "Biotic factors and relationships"; use the word only).
- [ ] X4 Fixation takes a lot of energy (ATP from the bacteria's respiration) and uses an enzyme that oxygen damages, which is why nodules keep oxygen levels low (they contain a red pigment that binds oxygen, so active nodules are pink inside).
- [ ] X5 Why legumes matter to farmers: legumes grow well in nitrogen-poor soil, and when they die or are ploughed in, the nitrogen in their tissues enriches the soil. Clover grown with grass, and legumes in crop rotation, reduce the need for nitrogen fertiliser.
- [ ] X6 Atmospheric fixation by lightning: the energy of lightning makes nitrogen react with oxygen to form nitrogen oxides, which dissolve in rain and reach the soil as nitrate. A small contribution compared with biological fixation.
- [ ] X7 Industrial fixation: the Haber process combines nitrogen from the air with hydrogen (made mainly from natural gas) at high temperature and pressure to make ammonia, which is turned into nitrogen fertilisers (e.g. ammonium nitrate, urea). Human activity, mainly the Haber process, now fixes at least as much nitrogen each year as all natural processes on land (sustainability issues in "Ethics and sustainability of nutrient cycling").

Assimilation and passing along food chains (one block, assumed)
- [ ] A1 (assumed) Uptake: plant roots absorb nitrate (and ammonium) from soil water; nitrate is taken in by active transport, against its concentration gradient, using ATP (active transport is in "Diffusion, osmosis and active transport"; use the term only). It travels to the leaves in the xylem.
- [ ] A2 (assumed) Assimilation: the plant uses the nitrogen, with sugars from photosynthesis, to make amino acids, then proteins, and also nucleic acids and chlorophyll.
- [ ] A3 (assumed) Feeding: animals get nitrogen only by eating plants or other animals; they digest the proteins into amino acids and build their own proteins (digestion is in "Chemical breakdown").
- [ ] A4 (assumed) Excretion and death: animals excrete excess nitrogen (in mammals, as urea in urine); faeces, urine, dead plants and dead animals return organic nitrogen to the soil.

Decomposition (one block; also called ammonification)
- [ ] D1 Decomposers (bacteria and fungi) feed on dead organisms, faeces and urine. They digest the proteins and other nitrogen compounds and release the nitrogen as ammonia, which dissolves in soil water as ammonium (NH₄⁺). This step is often called ammonification (putrefaction).
- [ ] D2 Without decomposition, the nitrogen in dead matter would be locked up and the soil would run out of usable nitrogen. Decomposition is fastest in warm, moist, aerated soil (same conditions as in "The carbon cycle"; don't repeat).
- [ ] D3 Ammonium is the starting point for nitrification; some is also taken up directly by plants.

Nitrification (one block)
- [ ] F1 Nitrification is the conversion of ammonium into nitrate by nitrifying bacteria in the soil, in two steps: 1. one group of nitrifying bacteria oxidises ammonium (NH₄⁺) to nitrite (NO₂⁻); 2. a second group oxidises nitrite to nitrate (NO₃⁻).
- [ ] F2 Nitrifying bacteria are aerobic: they need oxygen. So nitrification is fastest in well-drained, well-aerated soil; ploughing and draining help it, waterlogging stops it.
- [ ] F3 Nitrifying bacteria are autotrophic: they get energy from these chemical reactions (chemosynthesis) rather than from light or food. (Nutrition types are in "Bacteria and fungi".)
- [ ] F4 Nitrate is the form plants absorb most. It is also very soluble and is not held by soil particles, so it is easily washed out of the soil (leached) by heavy rain into ground water, rivers and lakes (consequences in "Ethics and sustainability of nutrient cycling").

Denitrification (one block)
- [ ] DN1 Denitrification is the conversion of nitrate in the soil back into nitrogen gas (N₂) by denitrifying bacteria, returning it to the atmosphere and completing the cycle.
- [ ] DN2 Denitrifying bacteria work in anaerobic conditions (no oxygen), e.g. waterlogged, compacted or poorly drained soils and the mud of lakes and marshes: they use nitrate in place of oxygen in respiration.
- [ ] DN3 Effects: denitrification removes usable nitrogen from the soil, lowering fertility, so farmers drain wet land; it also gives off some nitrous oxide (N₂O), a greenhouse gas (see "Climate change and biological strategies"). In wetlands and water treatment it is useful, removing excess nitrate before it reaches rivers.

Modelling the nitrogen cycle (one block, diagram and use of the model)
- [ ] M1 Diagram: nitrogen cycle with boxes for nitrogen gas in the air; nitrogen compounds in plants (proteins); in animals (proteins); dead organisms and wastes (faeces, urine); ammonium in the soil; nitrite; nitrate in the soil; root nodules of legumes. Labelled arrows: nitrogen fixation (air → nodules → plant, and air → free-living bacteria → soil ammonium); lightning (air → nitrate); Haber process and fertiliser (air → soil nitrate); absorption (soil nitrate → plants); feeding (plants → animals); death and excretion (→ dead matter); decomposition (dead matter → ammonium); nitrification (ammonium → nitrite → nitrate); denitrification (nitrate → air).
- [ ] M2 Comparison table (one bullet each, paired): fixation, N₂ → ammonium, nitrogen-fixing bacteria (free-living or in nodules), anaerobic inside nodule, adds usable nitrogen; decomposition, organic nitrogen → ammonium, bacteria and fungi, aerobic or anaerobic, recycles nitrogen; nitrification, ammonium → nitrite → nitrate, nitrifying bacteria, needs oxygen, makes the form plants use most; denitrification, nitrate → N₂, denitrifying bacteria, no oxygen, removes usable nitrogen.
- [ ] M3 Using the model to predict, worked examples (one bullet each): (a) a field becomes waterlogged: nitrification stops and denitrification increases, so soil nitrate falls and plants become nitrogen-deficient; (b) a farmer grows clover: more fixation, so more soil nitrogen next season; (c) heavy rain after fertiliser is spread: nitrate is leached into a river (see "Ethics and sustainability of nutrient cycling").
- [ ] M4 Limitations of the model: it shows the processes but not their rates or amounts; it doesn't show where nitrogen is lost (leaching, ammonia gas escaping from slurry) unless they are added; real soils have many more microorganisms and steps than the four named groups. (General model limitations are in "Models".)

## 3. Traps

- No microorganism names: never write Rhizobium, Azotobacter, Nitrosomonas, Nitrobacter, Pseudomonas or cyanobacteria. The specification says names are not required; describe each group by what it does.
- Nitrification and denitrification are opposite in oxygen need: nitrification needs oxygen; denitrification happens without it. Students swap these.
- Nitrogen fixation turns N₂ into ammonium (or nitrate); nitrification turns ammonium into nitrate. Don't call nitrification "fixation".
- Plants can't absorb N₂ from the air, even legumes: the bacteria in their nodules fix it.
- Decomposition produces ammonium, not nitrate directly.
- Why nitrogen is needed and deficiency symptoms belong to "Minerals"; N1 is one phrase.
- Eutrophication, nitrate in drinking water, ammonia from slurry and the ethics of fertiliser use belong to "Ethics and sustainability of nutrient cycling". Nitrous oxide's warming effect belongs to "Climate change and biological strategies". DN3 and F4 mention them in a phrase only.
- Mutualism is defined in "Biotic factors and relationships"; use the word without defining it.
- The structure of bacteria is in "Bacteria and fungi".
- Diagram cue M1 only. No other diagram cues.
