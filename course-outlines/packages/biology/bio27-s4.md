## 1. Must cover

The small intestine as an absorbing surface (one structure-and-function block)
- [ ] A1 Most absorption of digested food, and most of the water, takes place in the small intestine, mainly the ileum.
- [ ] A2 Features that give a huge surface area: it is very long; its inner wall is folded; the folds are covered in millions of villi (singular villus), tiny finger-like projections; and the cells covering each villus have microvilli, microscopic projections of their cell membranes.
- [ ] A3 Features that give a short diffusion distance: the lining of each villus (epithelium) is only one cell thick, and the capillaries lie just beneath it.
- [ ] A4 Features that keep a steep concentration gradient: each villus has a dense network of blood capillaries, and blood flowing through them constantly carries absorbed products away; peristalsis keeps moving fresh digested food past the surface.
- [ ] A5 Each villus has a lacteal, a lymph vessel in its centre, that absorbs fats. The lining cells have many mitochondria to supply ATP for active transport. Goblet cells in the lining secrete mucus.
- [ ] A6 Diagram: a longitudinal section of a villus labelled epithelium (one cell thick), microvilli, goblet cell, blood capillary network, arteriole (bringing blood in) and venule (leading to the hepatic portal vein), lacteal, and an arrow for the direction of absorption.

How each product is absorbed (one process block; one bullet per product)
- [ ] B1 Glucose and amino acids: enter the lining cells of the villus by diffusion and by active transport (using ATP), so they are absorbed even when their concentration in the gut is lower than in the blood. They then pass into the blood capillaries.
- [ ] B2 Fatty acids and glycerol: diffuse into the lining cells (they pass easily through the lipid membrane), where they are recombined into fats. The fats are coated with protein and pass into the lacteal, not the blood capillaries. Lymph carries them through the lymphatic system, which drains into the blood in a large vein near the heart. Fat-soluble vitamins (A, D, E, K) are absorbed with them (see "Vitamins").
- [ ] B3 Water: absorbed by osmosis, following the absorbed solutes. Most water is absorbed in the small intestine; the large intestine absorbs most of what is left (L2).
- [ ] B4 Minerals and water-soluble vitamins: absorbed into the blood capillaries (by diffusion and active transport). Vitamin D is needed to absorb calcium (one phrase; see "Vitamins").

Transport: the hepatic portal vein (one block)
- [ ] H1 The blood capillaries of the villi join to form veins that unite as the hepatic portal vein, which carries the blood from the small intestine (and the rest of the gut) directly to the liver, not to the heart.
- [ ] H2 A portal vein carries blood between two capillary networks: it begins in capillaries (in the villi) and ends in capillaries (in the liver). The hepatic portal vein is the example to give.
- [ ] H3 Its blood is rich in absorbed glucose, amino acids, water-soluble vitamins and minerals after a meal, and the amounts vary greatly with what has been eaten; it is low in oxygen (it has already passed through the gut wall).
- [ ] H4 Why the products go to the liver first: the liver processes and stores them and adjusts what is released to the rest of the body, keeping blood glucose and amino-acid levels steady. Blood leaves the liver in the hepatic vein, which joins the vena cava. The liver also receives oxygen-rich blood in the hepatic artery. One bullet; the full circulation route is in "Circulation".
- [ ] H5 Fats do not travel in the hepatic portal vein at first: they reach the blood through the lymph (B2).

Storage in the liver: glycogen (one process block)
- [ ] G1 After a meal, blood glucose rises. Insulin (released by the pancreas) causes liver cells to take up glucose and convert it into glycogen, an insoluble storage polysaccharide (structure in "Structures of biomolecules"). Muscle cells also store glycogen.
- [ ] G2 Between meals, or during exercise, when blood glucose falls, the liver breaks glycogen back down into glucose and releases it into the blood, so the brain and other cells have a steady supply. One bullet; the feedback loop is in "Homeostasis as a stimulus-response model".
- [ ] G3 The liver can store only a limited amount of glycogen. Glucose beyond this is converted into fat and stored in adipose tissue (link to "Metabolic roles and nutritional sources").
- [ ] G4 The liver also stores iron and vitamins A, D and B12. One bullet.

Deamination of excess amino acids (one process block, numbered)
- [ ] D1 Absorbed amino acids are carried to all body cells and used to make proteins for growth and repair, enzymes, hormones and antibodies (assimilation).
- [ ] D2 Excess amino acids cannot be stored. In the liver: 1. the amino group (–NH₂) is removed from each excess amino acid: this is deamination; 2. the amino group forms ammonia, which is very toxic; 3. liver cells immediately convert the ammonia into urea, which is much less toxic; 4. urea passes into the blood and is carried to the kidneys, which excrete it in urine (see "The urinary system").
- [ ] D3 The rest of the amino acid (the part without nitrogen) is not wasted: it is respired to release energy, or converted into glucose, glycogen or fat.
- [ ] D4 Consequence (one bullet): a very high-protein diet means more deamination and more urea for the kidneys to excrete.
- [ ] D5 Other liver roles (one bullet, names only): making bile, breaking down old red blood cells (forming bile pigments), and breaking down alcohol and many drugs (detoxification).

The large intestine: water absorption and faeces (one block)
- [ ] L1 Material entering the large intestine is watery and contains what could not be digested or absorbed: mainly dietary fibre (cellulose), with dead cells shed from the gut lining, bile pigments, mucus and bacteria.
- [ ] L2 The colon absorbs most of the remaining water (by osmosis) and some mineral ions into the blood, so the contents become semi-solid faeces. This saves water for the body.
- [ ] L3 Bacteria in the colon (the gut microbiota) feed on undigested material, including some fibre, and make vitamin K and some B vitamins, which are absorbed. One bullet; their wider role in health is in "The microbiome".
- [ ] L4 Faeces are moved by peristalsis to the rectum and stored there. When the rectum fills, stretch receptors give the urge to egest; the anal sphincters relax and the faeces are pushed out through the anus (egestion, or elimination).
- [ ] L5 If faeces move too slowly, too much water is absorbed and they become hard and difficult to pass (constipation); fibre and water prevent this (see "Diet"). If they move too fast (e.g. in a gut infection), too little water is absorbed (diarrhoea), which can cause dangerous dehydration, especially in young children.

## 3. Traps

- Don't say most water is absorbed in the large intestine: most is absorbed in the small intestine. The large intestine absorbs most of what remains.
- Fats enter the lacteals (lymph), not the blood capillaries; glucose and amino acids enter the capillaries.
- The hepatic portal vein goes from the gut to the liver; the hepatic vein goes from the liver to the vena cava. Students mix them up.
- Urea is made in the liver, not in the kidneys; ammonia is the toxic intermediate. Deamination happens only to excess amino acids.
- Don't teach glucagon (not on the specification), the urea (ornithine) cycle, transamination, chylomicrons, mesenteric vessels, or co-transport with sodium.
- Glycogen is stored in liver and muscle, not "in the blood".
- Egestion is not excretion (bile pigments are the one excretory product in faeces; one phrase in L1 is enough).
- The microbiome's role in immunity and metabolic health belongs to "The microbiome"; L3 is one bullet.
- Circulation routes beyond H4 belong to "Circulation".
- The diagram cue in A6 is the only one wanted. No other diagram cues.
