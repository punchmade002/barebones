# Package: bio35-s7 — Ethics of genetic technologies

## 1. Must cover

How to discuss an ethical issue (one Skills block)
- [ ] K1 1. Say what the technology does and what is new about it. 2. Identify who is affected (the person tested or treated, relatives, future children, society, other species, the environment). 3. Give arguments for and against, linking each to a principle: autonomy and consent, doing good (beneficence), avoiding harm (non-maleficence), justice and fairness, privacy, and the precautionary principle (see "Science, scientists and society"). 4. Say what rules or safeguards exist or could be added. 5. Reach a justified conclusion. Ethics is decided by reasoning about values informed by evidence; science says what can be done and with what risks, not whether it should be done.

Genetic data: access and use (one block, one bullet per area, each with for and against)
- [ ] D1 What is special about genetic data: it is permanent (it can't be changed like a password), it identifies a person, it predicts some future health risks, and it is shared with relatives, so one person's test reveals information about family members who did not consent. Under the EU's General Data Protection Regulation (GDPR, applying since 2018), genetic data is a special category of personal data with extra protection.
- [ ] D2 Health and research: for: large databases of genomes and health records (biobanks) speed research into causes and treatments of disease and allow faster diagnosis of rare diseases (see "Bioinformatics: genome databases"). Against: risk of data leaks or re-identification of people from "anonymous" data; people must give informed consent and know how their data will be used and shared; who profits from discoveries made with donated data.
- [ ] D3 Insurance and employment: insurers or employers could charge more or refuse people with a high genetic risk, which would be unjust because people don't choose their genes, and fear of this could stop people having useful tests. In Ireland the Disability Act 2005 prohibits the use of genetic test results in insurance, pensions, mortgages and employment; in the USA the Genetic Information Nondiscrimination Act (2008) protects against discrimination in health insurance and employment. Argument on the other side: insurers say that if customers know their risk and insurers don't, pricing becomes unfair.
- [ ] D4 Forensics: for: national DNA databases solve crimes, including old cases, and clear innocent people. Ireland's database was set up under the Criminal Justice (Forensic Evidence and DNA Database System) Act 2014. Against: privacy, especially keeping profiles of people who were arrested but never convicted; in S and Marper v United Kingdom (2008), the European Court of Human Rights ruled that the UK's keeping of DNA of people not convicted of any crime breached their right to privacy; groups that are policed more heavily are over-represented on databases; a match can be given too much weight in court (see "DNA profiling").
- [ ] D5 Ancestry tests and police access: people send DNA to companies for ancestry tests and may upload it to public genealogy websites. In 2018 police in California identified the "Golden State Killer", Joseph James DeAngelo, by matching crime-scene DNA with distant relatives who had uploaded their DNA to such a website. For: serious crimes solved. Against: the relatives never consented to police use of their DNA. Company data can be hacked or sold: in 2023 a data breach at the testing company 23andMe exposed the ancestry information of millions of customers.
- [ ] D6 Industry and patents: for: companies invest in genetic tests and treatments and need patents to recover the cost. Against: patenting human genes lets one company control testing and set high prices. In 2013 the US Supreme Court ruled (in the case against Myriad Genetics, which held patents on the BRCA1 and BRCA2 genes; see "Causes, prevention and treatment of cancer") that naturally occurring human DNA cannot be patented, after which other laboratories began offering BRCA tests. Justice: benefits from genetic resources of poorer countries (e.g. crop varieties, local plants) should be shared with them.
- [ ] D7 Agriculture: genetic data on crops and livestock speeds breeding of higher-yielding, disease-resistant varieties; against: control of seed varieties and their genetic data by a few large companies, and loss of traditional varieties. One bullet.

Genetic modification (one block for GM crops and animals, one for humans)
- [ ] M1 GM crops, for: higher yields and less crop loss; Bt crops need less insecticide spraying, which protects farm workers and other insects (see "Genetic engineering"); crops can be modified for drought or salt tolerance or added nutrition (Golden Rice and vitamin A deficiency); could help feed a growing population on less land. Major scientific bodies have found approved GM foods as safe to eat as their conventional equivalents.
- [ ] M2 GM crops, against: genes can spread in pollen to wild relatives or to non-GM and organic crops nearby (gene flow); weeds have evolved resistance to the herbicide glyphosate where herbicide-tolerant crops are sprayed repeatedly, and some insect pests have evolved resistance to Bt toxins (natural selection; see "The theory of evolution by natural selection"); large areas of a single crop reduce biodiversity; patented seed must be bought each year, making farmers dependent on companies; consumers have a right to choose, so the EU requires food containing more than 0.9% GM material to be labelled; some people object that it is unnatural ("playing God"). Golden Rice: supporters say opposition has delayed a way to save children's sight and lives; opponents say vitamin A deficiency is better tackled by a more varied diet and supplements, and its approval in the Philippines has been challenged in court.
- [ ] M3 GM animals, for: faster-growing food animals such as AquAdvantage salmon (see "Genetic engineering") need less feed per kilogram of food; GM animals model human diseases in medical research. Against: animal welfare (the 3Rs; see "Science, scientists and society"); risk to wild populations if GM fish escape and breed; public unease about eating GM animals.
- [ ] M4 Humans, somatic gene therapy and editing: for: can treat or cure serious genetic diseases (e.g. the CRISPR treatment for sickle-cell disease approved in the UK in 2023; see "Genetic engineering"); changes are in the patient's body cells only, so only a consenting patient is affected. Against: long-term risks are not yet known, including edits at the wrong place in the genome; treatments are extremely expensive, so access is unequal (justice).
- [ ] M5 Humans, germline editing (eggs, sperm or embryos): changes would be passed to all future generations, who cannot consent. In November 2018 the scientist He Jiankui announced in China the birth of twin girls whose embryos he had edited with CRISPR to try to make them resistant to HIV; it was condemned worldwide as unsafe, unnecessary and done without proper consent, and he was sentenced to three years in prison in 2019. Arguments for germline editing in future: it could remove a serious disease allele from a family permanently. Against: unknown risks to future generations; could lead to "designer babies" chosen for traits such as height or intelligence rather than to prevent disease; could increase inequality. It is not allowed in most countries.
- [ ] M6 Mitochondrial donation (the technique is in "Nuclear and non-nuclear inheritance"; the UK permitted it by law in 2015): for: lets a woman with a mitochondrial disease have a genetically related child free of that disease; the donor contributes only the mitochondrial DNA (37 genes), not the nuclear DNA that determines the child's characteristics. Against: it is a germline change, passed on by daughters to their children; long-term safety is still being studied; questions about the donor's relationship to the child ("three-parent baby"); some fear it is a step towards other germline changes.

DNA testing (one block)
- [ ] T1 Diagnostic and carrier testing: for: confirms a diagnosis so treatment can start; carrier testing (e.g. for cystic fibrosis, common in Ireland) informs people's choices before having children (see "Benefits and limitations of Mendelian genetics"). Against: a positive result may reveal that relatives are also carriers, raising questions about whether and how to tell them.
- [ ] T2 Predictive testing for a late-onset disease with no cure, e.g. Huntington's disease (dominant, so a child of an affected parent has a 1 in 2 risk): for: certainty, and the chance to plan life, career and family. Against: a positive result may cause great distress, and many people at risk choose not to be tested; the right not to know (autonomy) must be respected; testing children is usually delayed until they can decide for themselves. Genetic counselling before and after testing is essential.
- [ ] T3 Testing for risk alleles (e.g. BRCA1 and BRCA2; see "Causes, prevention and treatment of cancer"): a positive result gives a raised risk, not certainty, so people face hard decisions about preventive surgery; a negative result may give false reassurance.
- [ ] T4 Testing in reproduction: prenatal tests on the foetus and newborn screening are in "Prenatal and postnatal care". Preimplantation genetic diagnosis (PGD) tests embryos made by IVF (see "Fertility control and infertility") so that only embryos without a serious disease allele are implanted. For: parents avoid passing on a serious disease. Against: unused embryos are destroyed, which some people believe is wrong because an embryo has the moral status of a person; worry about choosing embryos for non-medical traits (sex, appearance); concern that it devalues the lives of people living with the condition.
- [ ] T5 Direct-to-consumer tests bought online: for: cheap, convenient, respect autonomy. Against: results given without counselling may be misunderstood; accuracy varies; may reveal unexpected family relationships (e.g. that a parent is not the biological parent); data privacy (D5).

Stem cells: what they are and their uses (one Structure-and-function block)
- [ ] S1 A stem cell is an unspecialised cell that can divide by mitosis to make more stem cells and can differentiate into specialised cell types (see "Levels of organisation"). Embryonic stem cells, from the inner cell mass of an embryo about five days old (a blastocyst), are pluripotent: they can become any type of body cell. Adult (tissue) stem cells, e.g. in bone marrow, skin and the lining of the gut, are multipotent: they can become only a few related cell types, e.g. bone-marrow stem cells give rise to the blood cells.
- [ ] S2 Induced pluripotent stem (iPS) cells are adult body cells (e.g. skin cells) reprogrammed in the laboratory, by switching on a few genes, to behave like embryonic stem cells. Shinya Yamanaka first made them (in mice) in 2006 and shared the 2012 Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine with John Gurdon. They avoid the use of embryos and, made from a patient's own cells, would not be rejected by the immune system.
- [ ] S3 Therapeutic uses: bone-marrow (stem-cell) transplants already treat leukaemia and some other blood disorders (see "Causes, prevention and treatment of cancer"); skin grown from stem cells treats severe burns; stem-cell treatments are being tested in clinical trials for conditions where cells are lost, e.g. Parkinson's disease, type 1 diabetes, spinal cord injury, heart damage and some forms of blindness; stem cells grown into tissues are used to test new drugs and study diseases.
- [ ] S4 Cloning: a clone is a genetically identical copy. Reproductive cloning makes a whole organism by somatic cell nuclear transfer: the nucleus of a body cell is put into an egg cell whose own nucleus has been removed, the egg is stimulated to divide into an embryo, and the embryo is placed in a surrogate mother. Dolly the sheep, born on 5 July 1996 at the Roslin Institute near Edinburgh, Scotland, was the first mammal cloned from an adult body cell (a cell from the udder); she was one success from 277 attempts, and died in 2003 aged six. Therapeutic cloning uses the same method to make an early embryo only to obtain embryonic stem cells that match the patient; the embryo is not implanted.
- [ ] S5 Diagram: somatic cell nuclear transfer as a flow diagram: body cell from adult A → nucleus removed; egg from female B → its nucleus removed; nucleus of A inserted into the empty egg → embryo; then two arrows: implanted in surrogate mother → clone of A (reproductive cloning), or grown in the laboratory → embryonic stem cells → specialised cells for the patient (therapeutic cloning).

Stem cells and cloning: the ethical issues (one block)
- [ ] E1 Embryonic stem cells, for: the potential to treat many serious diseases and relieve great suffering (beneficence); embryos used are often spare embryos from IVF that would otherwise be discarded. Against: obtaining the cells destroys an embryo, which those who believe life begins at fertilisation regard as killing a human being; concern about creating embryos for research.
- [ ] E2 Adult and iPS cells largely avoid the embryo issue, but have their own concerns: iPS cells may carry a risk of tumours; unproven "stem-cell clinics" sell untested, expensive and sometimes harmful treatments to desperate patients; consent is needed from donors of cells and tissues.
- [ ] E3 Reproductive cloning: in animals, for: copying valuable farm animals or conserving endangered species; against: low success rates, many failed pregnancies and animal suffering, and clones' health problems (animal welfare). In humans, reproductive cloning is almost universally rejected: the risk of serious abnormality, the clone's identity and individuality, and the use of people as means to an end. It is prohibited by the Charter of Fundamental Rights of the European Union. Research fraud has also damaged trust in this field (the Hwang Woo-suk case, see "Science as a global enterprise").
- [ ] E4 Therapeutic cloning, for: stem cells that exactly match the patient, so no rejection. Against: embryos are created to be destroyed; it needs many donated eggs, and egg donation carries health risks and could exploit poor women if they are paid; fear that it makes reproductive cloning easier.

Bringing it together (one bullet)
- [ ] B1 Common threads in all these issues: consent (including that of relatives and future generations who cannot consent); privacy of permanent, shared genetic information; fair access to expensive technologies (justice); the precautionary principle where long-term effects are unknown; and the need for laws and independent ethics committees to keep up with fast-changing technology.

## 3. Traps

- Every discussion needs arguments on both sides and a conclusion; never "weigh benefits against costs" without naming them.
- Somatic changes are not inherited; germline changes are. Mitochondrial donation is a germline change even though it doesn't edit a gene.
- Therapeutic cloning does not produce a baby; reproductive cloning does. Don't confuse cloning with genetic engineering.
- Dolly was cloned from an adult udder cell, not an embryo cell; she was not the first cloned animal of any kind, only the first mammal cloned from an adult cell.
- Pluripotent (embryonic, iPS) vs multipotent (adult): don't swap.
- DNA profiling identifies people; DNA testing looks for disease alleles. Keep the ethics of each in its own bullets (D4 vs T1–T5).
- How each technology works belongs to "Genetic engineering", "DNA profiling", "Generating a DNA profile", "DNA sequencing" and "Bioinformatics: genome databases"; prenatal tests and newborn screening to "Prenatal and postnatal care"; IVF to "Fertility control and infertility"; ethics of other scientific discoveries (penicillin, DDT, thalidomide) to "Science, scientists and society". Name them, don't re-explain.
- Don't state as fact that GM food is dangerous, or that it is proven safe in every case; give M1's statement about approved foods and the arguments.
- Diagram cue: S5 only. No other diagram cues.

## 4. What exams ask (topics only)

Legacy papers asked about ethical issues of genetic engineering and of genetic screening;
both are on this specification. Expect:
- Discuss ethical issues of GM crops, gene therapy or human gene editing, with arguments on each side.
- Discuss issues of access to genetic data (insurance, police databases, ancestry companies).
- Discuss ethical issues of DNA testing (predictive testing, embryo testing).
- Distinguish embryonic and adult stem cells; give a therapeutic use; distinguish reproductive and therapeutic cloning; discuss their ethics.

## 5. Practice questions to write

1. 12 marks: "Discuss the ethical issues arising from the growing of genetically modified crops." Model answer: at least three arguments for from M1 and three against from M2, each explained, and a reasoned conclusion. Must include gene flow or resistance, and a benefit such as reduced insecticide use or improved nutrition.
2. 9 marks: "Distinguish between embryonic and adult stem cells, give one therapeutic use of stem cells, and outline one ethical objection to the use of embryonic stem cells." Model answer: S1 (pluripotent from blastocyst vs multipotent from tissues), S3 use (bone-marrow transplant), E1 objection (destruction of an embryo).
3. 12 marks: "A national DNA database holds the profiles of convicted criminals. Discuss the ethical issues of (i) also keeping the profiles of people who were arrested but not convicted and (ii) police searching ancestry databases for relatives of suspects." Model answer: D4 (crime-solving vs privacy, S and Marper 2008, over-representation); D5 (Golden State Killer solved vs relatives' lack of consent); D1 (shared, permanent data); a conclusion.
