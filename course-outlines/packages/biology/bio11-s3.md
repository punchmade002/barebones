## 1. Must cover

Nuclear inheritance (one process block, Ordinary and Higher level)
- [ ] N1 Nuclear inheritance is the inheritance of genes carried on the chromosomes in the nucleus. Nearly all of an organism's genes (in humans, about 20,000 protein-coding genes) are nuclear.
- [ ] N2 How it is transmitted: (1) the DNA in the nucleus is copied before division; (2) meiosis separates each homologous pair, so each gamete receives one chromosome of every pair (one allele of each gene); (3) at fertilisation the nuclei of a male and a female gamete fuse, so the zygote receives one set of chromosomes from each parent; (4) mitosis passes the full set to every body cell.
- [ ] N3 Both parents contribute equally: the offspring inherits one allele of each autosomal gene from its mother and one from its father, so for nuclear genes it doesn't matter which parent carries an allele (reciprocal crosses, e.g. tall male × short female and short male × tall female, give the same results). Exception: genes on the sex chromosomes (see "Sex-linked traits").
- [ ] N4 Because each gamete gets only one allele of each pair, chosen at random, nuclear genes are inherited in the predictable ratios described by Mendel's laws (see "Monohybrid crosses" and "Mendel's Laws"), and new combinations of alleles arise in every generation (see "Variation").

Non-nuclear inheritance (one block, Higher Level only)
- [ ] M1 Non-nuclear (also called extranuclear or cytoplasmic) inheritance is the inheritance of genes carried in DNA outside the nucleus: in mitochondria (in all eukaryotes) and in chloroplasts (in plants and algae).
- [ ] M2 Form of the DNA: each mitochondrion and chloroplast contains several copies of its own small, circular DNA molecule, not wound around histones (like the DNA of a bacterium), and its own small (70S) ribosomes. A cell contains many such organelles, so it has hundreds to thousands of copies of the organelle DNA.
- [ ] M3 What the genes do: they code for some of the proteins the organelle needs (for mitochondria, some proteins of aerobic respiration; for chloroplasts, some proteins of photosynthesis) and for the organelle's own rRNA and tRNA. Most proteins in these organelles are coded by nuclear genes and imported, so the organelle depends on both.
- [ ] M4 Why the organelles have their own DNA (one bullet): mitochondria and chloroplasts are thought to be descended from bacteria that came to live inside an ancestral cell; evidence includes their circular DNA, 70S ribosomes, double membrane, and division by splitting in two. They are not made by the cell from scratch: new mitochondria and chloroplasts come only from the division of existing ones.
- [ ] M5 How it is transmitted: organelles are in the cytoplasm, and they are shared out between daughter cells at random when the cytoplasm divides, not by the spindle as chromosomes are. Their genes therefore don't segregate in meiosis and don't follow Mendel's laws or Mendelian ratios.
- [ ] M6 Maternal inheritance: in humans and most animals, the egg contributes nearly all the cytoplasm of the zygote, including its mitochondria; the sperm contributes almost only its nucleus (the few sperm mitochondria, in its midpiece, that enter the egg are destroyed). So mitochondrial genes are inherited from the mother only. In most flowering plants, chloroplasts (and mitochondria) are likewise inherited through the egg cell, from the female parent.
- [ ] M7 Consequences: (1) all the offspring of a mother share her mitochondrial DNA, whatever the father; (2) reciprocal crosses give different results (the offspring resemble the female parent for the trait); (3) the mitochondrial DNA doesn't mix between two parents, so it changes only by mutation and passes down the female line almost unchanged.
- [ ] M8 Plant example: in 1909 Carl Correns studied the four-o'clock plant (Mirabilis jalapa), which has branches with green, white or variegated (green-and-white) leaves, the white areas lacking working chloroplasts. Seeds from flowers on green branches gave green seedlings, seeds from flowers on white branches gave white seedlings (which soon died), and seeds from variegated branches gave a mixture, whatever branch the pollen came from. The offspring took the leaf type of the female parent: leaf colour was inherited through the chloroplasts in the egg's cytoplasm. One of the first demonstrations of non-nuclear inheritance.

Mitochondrial inheritance in humans (one block, Higher Level only)
- [ ] H1 Human mitochondrial DNA is about 16,600 base pairs long and carries 37 genes (compare about 20,000 nuclear protein-coding genes on chromosomes of up to hundreds of millions of base pairs each).
- [ ] H2 Mitochondrial diseases: mutations in mitochondrial DNA can cause diseases that mostly affect tissues with high energy demands, such as muscle, nerves, brain and eyes, because they reduce aerobic respiration. Example: Leber's hereditary optic neuropathy (LHON), which causes loss of central vision, usually in young adults. An affected mother can pass a mitochondrial disease to all her children, sons and daughters; an affected father passes it to none.
- [ ] H3 Severity varies: a cell may contain a mixture of normal and mutant mitochondria, and the proportion passed to each egg varies, so children of the same mother can be affected to different degrees.
- [ ] H4 Technology link: mitochondrial donation ("three-parent IVF") replaces a mother's faulty mitochondria with healthy mitochondria from a donor egg, keeping the nuclear DNA of both parents. In 2015 the UK became the first country to allow it by law. The child's nuclear DNA comes from its two parents and its small amount of mitochondrial DNA from the donor. Its ethics are in "Ethics of genetic technologies"; one bullet here.
- [ ] H5 Uses of mitochondrial DNA: because it passes down the female line without mixing and changes only by mutation, it is used to trace maternal ancestry and human migration and evolution, and to identify remains when nuclear DNA is too damaged (there are many copies per cell, so it survives better).

Nuclear and non-nuclear inheritance compared (one comparison block, Higher Level only; paired points or a table)
- [ ] C1 Location: nuclear DNA in chromosomes in the nucleus vs non-nuclear DNA in mitochondria and chloroplasts in the cytoplasm.
- [ ] C2 Form: long, linear DNA molecules wound around histones vs small, circular DNA without histones.
- [ ] C3 Amount: most of the genes (about 20,000 protein-coding genes in humans) vs few genes (37 in human mitochondria), coding mainly for the organelle's own proteins and RNA.
- [ ] C4 Copies: two copies of each autosomal gene in a diploid cell vs hundreds to thousands of copies per cell.
- [ ] C5 Parents contributing: both parents equally vs usually the mother only (maternal inheritance).
- [ ] C6 Distribution to gametes and daughter cells: precise separation of chromosomes by the spindle in meiosis and mitosis vs random sharing of organelles when the cytoplasm divides.
- [ ] C7 Patterns: follows Mendel's laws with predictable ratios, reciprocal crosses give the same results vs no Mendelian ratios, reciprocal crosses give different results (offspring like the mother).
- [ ] C8 Variation between generations: new combinations of alleles from both parents each generation vs no mixing; changes only by mutation.
- [ ] C9 Similarity: both are DNA, using the same genetic code (with minor differences in mitochondria) and the same base pairing; both are copied before cell division; both can mutate and so cause inherited disease.
- [ ] C10 Diagram (Higher Level): a sperm and an egg fusing; the egg drawn large with many mitochondria in its cytoplasm and a nucleus, the sperm with a nucleus in its head and a few mitochondria in its midpiece; arrows show nuclear DNA from both parents and mitochondria from the egg only into the zygote; label nucleus (nuclear DNA, from both parents), mitochondria (mitochondrial DNA, from mother).

## 3. Traps

- Only the "Nuclear inheritance" block is Ordinary level; the other three blocks are Higher Level only.
- "Maternal inheritance" of mitochondria is not the same as an X-linked trait inherited from the mother. Sex-linked genes are nuclear (on the X chromosome) and follow nuclear rules with a sex difference; mitochondrial genes are passed to all children of an affected mother and never by the father. Sex-linked inheritance belongs to "Sex-linked traits".
- Don't say sons don't inherit their mother's mitochondria: sons and daughters both do; only daughters pass them on.
- Don't say non-nuclear DNA is RNA, or that it is found in ribosomes; mitochondria are in all eukaryotic cells, chloroplasts only in plants and algae.
- M4 is explanation only: don't name the endosymbiotic theory's proposer or dates, and don't develop it into a topic.
- Mitochondrial structure and respiration belong to "Respiration"; chloroplasts and photosynthesis to "Photosynthesis"; organelle structure to "Structures and organelles of animal and plant cells". Name, don't describe.
- Crosses and Mendel's laws belong to "Monohybrid crosses" and "Mendel's Laws"; N3–N4 and C7 refer to their results without working a cross.
- Epigenetic inheritance is a third, separate mechanism, in "Genetic and epigenetic mechanisms"; don't include it in the comparison.
- No other diagram cues beyond C10.
