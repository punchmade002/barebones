## 1. Must cover

Variables (one block)
- [ ] V1 A variable is any factor that can change or be changed in an investigation.
- [ ] V2 Independent variable: the one variable the investigator deliberately changes, e.g. the temperature of the water bath in an enzyme investigation; the concentration of sucrose solution in an osmosis investigation.
- [ ] V3 Dependent variable: the variable that is measured, because it is expected to change as a result, e.g. the volume of oxygen released per minute by catalase; the percentage change in mass of potato cylinders. It depends on the independent variable.
- [ ] V4 Controlled variables: all other variables that could affect the dependent variable and so are kept the same, e.g. for enzyme rate: pH (kept constant with a buffer), substrate concentration and volume, the amount of enzyme, the time of measurement; for osmosis: size and surface area of the cylinders, the potato they are cut from, the volume of solution, the time in solution, the temperature.
- [ ] V5 How variables are kept constant: a thermostatically controlled water bath or incubator for temperature; a buffer solution for pH; measuring volumes with a graduated pipette, syringe or graduated cylinder; cutting tissue with the same cork borer and trimming to the same length; timing with a stopwatch.
- [ ] V6 Range and interval of the independent variable: choose at least five values spread across the range in which a change is expected (e.g. 0 °C to 60 °C in 10 °C steps for an enzyme), and add closer intervals around a key point such as an apparent optimum. A preliminary (pilot) trial helps choose the range and check the method works.
- [ ] V7 Writing variables: name each variable precisely with its unit and how it will be measured; "temperature" is an independent variable, "the enzyme" is not.

Control and fair test (one block)
- [ ] C1 Fair test: an investigation in which only the independent variable is changed and all other variables are controlled, so any change in the dependent variable can be put down to the independent variable.
- [ ] C2 Control (control experiment): a set-up identical to the experiment in every way except that the factor being tested is absent or not applied. It is a standard for comparison, showing that the result is due to the factor being investigated and not something else. Example: boiled (denatured) enzyme in place of active enzyme shows that the enzyme, not the substrate breaking down on its own, causes the reaction.
- [ ] C3 Negative control: a set-up expected to give no response (e.g. boiled enzyme; distilled water instead of the test solution). Positive control: a set-up known to give a response, which shows the method can detect one (e.g. a known reducing sugar such as glucose in a food test).
- [ ] C4 Exam distinction: controlled variables are the factors kept the same in every set-up; the control is a separate set-up used for comparison. A question asking for "a control" needs a set-up, not a variable.
- [ ] C5 Validity: an investigation is valid if it actually measures what it sets out to measure and answers the question asked, which requires a fair test and a suitable dependent variable. Example: if a lamp moved closer to pondweed to increase light intensity also warms the water, the investigation changes temperature as well as light, so it can't show the effect of light alone (a heat shield, such as a beaker of water between lamp and plant, restores validity). A conclusion is valid only if it is supported by data from a valid investigation.

Accuracy, precision and reliability (one block, each term with its exam distinction)
- [ ] R1 Error (in measurement): the difference between a measured value and the true value. Every measurement has some error; it is not the same as a mistake. Types of error (random and systematic) are in "Objectivity and data quality".
- [ ] R2 Accuracy: how close a measurement or a mean is to the true value. Improved by calibrated equipment and a method that measures the right thing.
- [ ] R3 Precision: how close repeated measurements are to each other (little spread around the mean). The precision of an instrument is its resolution: the smallest change it can show, e.g. a balance reading to 0.01 g is more precise than one reading to 1 g; a graduated pipette is more precise than a beaker for measuring 5 cm³.
- [ ] R4 Precise results need not be accurate: a balance that reads 0.5 g too high gives readings close to each other (precise) but all wrong (inaccurate). Diagram: four targets showing accurate and precise; precise but not accurate; accurate on average but not precise; neither.
- [ ] R5 Reliability: how far results can be trusted because they are consistent when the investigation is repeated. Reliability is judged by repeating (detail in "Objectivity and data quality") and is improved by replicates, repeats and a large sample size.
- [ ] R6 Replicates: several identical set-ups at each value of the independent variable run at the same time, e.g. three potato cylinders in each sucrose concentration. Repeats: carrying out the whole measurement again. Both allow a mean to be calculated and an anomalous result to be spotted (anomalies in "Producing and analysing data").
- [ ] R7 Sample size: the number of organisms, samples or measurements in each group. Living things vary naturally (two potato cylinders, two leaves or two people are never identical), so a larger sample, chosen at random, reduces the effect of individual variation and gives a more representative and reliable result. Limits on sample size: time, cost, materials and the ethics of using organisms.

Selecting suitable equipment (one block)
- [ ] Q1 Choose equipment whose resolution suits the size of the change: measure small volumes with a syringe, graduated pipette or burette rather than a beaker; small masses with a balance reading to at least 0.01 g; time with a stopwatch; temperature with a thermometer or temperature probe.
- [ ] Q2 Choose equipment that measures objectively where possible: a colorimeter measures colour or cloudiness as a number instead of judging by eye; a gas syringe or an inverted graduated cylinder over water measures the volume of gas instead of counting bubbles, which vary in size; data loggers with sensors (temperature, pH, oxygen, light) record readings automatically at set intervals.
- [ ] Q3 Justify each choice in a plan: say what it measures, why its resolution or range is suitable, and how it reduces error.

Safety (one block)
- [ ] F1 A risk assessment is carried out before practical work: identify each hazard (anything that could cause harm), judge the risk (how likely and how serious the harm is) and state a control measure that reduces it.
- [ ] F2 General control measures: wear safety glasses and a lab coat; tie back long hair; heat with a water bath rather than a direct flame where possible; clean up spills at once; wash hands after practical work; never eat or drink in the laboratory.
- [ ] F3 Examples of hazards and control measures: hot water baths and boiling tubes (use tongs or a test-tube holder); corrosive or irritant reagents such as hydrogen peroxide or Benedict's reagent (glasses, gloves, avoid skin contact); sharp scalpels and cork borers (cut downwards onto a tile, away from the body); microorganisms (aseptic technique, sealed plates, disinfect benches and dispose of cultures by sterilising; detail in "Investigation: growth of microorganisms"); electrical equipment near water.
- [ ] F4 Safety also covers the organisms used: living material should be treated with care and returned to its habitat where possible.

Integrity and ethics (one block)
- [ ] I1 Scientific integrity means honesty in how investigations are carried out and reported: record all results as they were measured, including unexpected and anomalous ones; never invent results (fabrication) or change them to fit the hypothesis (falsification); never present others' work or data as your own (plagiarism), so acknowledge every source.
- [ ] I2 Integrity also means reporting limitations and errors openly, and not leaving out data because they don't support the hypothesis.
- [ ] I3 Ethics with animals: use animals only where there is no alternative, cause the minimum harm or stress, and follow the 3Rs: Replace (use non-animal alternatives such as cell cultures or computer models), Reduce (use the fewest animals that give reliable results), Refine (improve methods to minimise suffering). Return organisms collected from the wild to where they were found.
- [ ] I4 Ethics with human participants: informed consent (participants know what is involved and agree freely), the right to withdraw at any time, confidentiality of personal data, and no physical or psychological harm. Investigations on people, e.g. measuring reaction time after a caffeinated drink, must take account of health conditions and exclude anyone at risk.
- [ ] I5 Ethics also covers the environment (avoid damaging habitats when collecting) and the wider consequences of research for society (wider ethical, social and economic factors are in "Science, scientists and society").

Writing the plan (one skills block)
- [ ] W1 A plan states, in order: the question and hypothesis; the independent, dependent and controlled variables; the control; the equipment and reagents with reasons for choice; a numbered method detailed enough that someone else could repeat it exactly (amounts, concentrations, times, temperatures); the number of replicates or repeats; how data will be recorded; a risk assessment; ethical considerations.
- [ ] W2 Worked outline (do not write the full experiment): for the effect of temperature on catalase, independent variable temperature (five or more values set with water baths), dependent variable volume of oxygen collected in a fixed time, controlled variables pH (buffer), volume and concentration of hydrogen peroxide, mass or volume of enzyme source, control with boiled enzyme, three replicates at each temperature, safety glasses for hydrogen peroxide. The full method is in "Enzymes".

## 3. Traps

- Controlled variable vs control (C4) is the most common error. So is calling the dependent variable the thing that "is controlled".
- Accuracy vs precision vs reliability: students use them interchangeably. Precise ≠ accurate (R4); reliable ≠ accurate.
- Don't define random error, systematic error, repeatability, reproducibility, placebo, blind or double-blind here; they are in "Objectivity and data quality".
- Mean, range and anomalous results are in "Producing and analysing data".
- Don't write any experiment in full (enzyme, osmosis, microorganisms belong to their chapters).
- Peer review and reproducibility as part of how science works globally belong to "Science as a global enterprise".
- Diagram cues: only the accuracy/precision targets in R4. No other diagram cues.

## 5. Practice questions to write

1. 6 marks: "Distinguish between a controlled variable and a control. Give an example of each for an investigation into the effect of temperature on enzyme activity." Model answer: C2, C4, V4 with pH or substrate concentration and boiled enzyme.
2. 9 marks: "A student measures the mass of potato cylinders using a balance that reads to 1 g. Explain why this reduces the precision of the results, and suggest two other ways the student could improve the reliability of the investigation." Model answer: R3 (changes in mass are less than 1 g, so they can't be detected; use a 0.01 g balance), R6 replicates and R7 larger sample or repeats; mustn't say it makes results "inaccurate" as the only point.
3. 12 marks: "Explain how fairness, safety and integrity should be considered when planning an investigation into the effect of a caffeinated drink on reaction time in a class group." Model answer: fair test with controlled variables (same volume of drink, same time before testing, same reaction test, same time of day), C1; a control group given a drink without caffeine, C2; risk assessment and health conditions, F1, I4; informed consent and right to withdraw, I4; honest recording of all data, I1. Mustn't define double-blind in full (one phrase is acceptable).
