# Package: bio3-s1 — Evaluating evidence

- **loId:** `bio3-s1` · **chapterId:** `bio3` · **syllabusRef:** U3.1
- **Syllabus:** "evaluating evidence for relevance, accuracy, bias." Outcome U3.1: "evaluate
  media-based arguments concerning science and technology." Ordinary and Higher level.
  "Evaluate" means examine the evidence to make a judgement: describe how the evidence supports
  or does not support a conclusion, and identify its limitations. The specification's rationale
  adds that "the spread of disinformation is threatening democracies world-wide" and that
  "rationality and scepticism" are principles of the course.
- **Prepared by:** a higher model, 2026-10-22.

## 1. Must cover

Evidence and the three tests (one block)
- [ ] V1 Evidence is the information (observations, measurements, results of studies) used to support or refute a claim. A claim is a statement that something is true, e.g. "vitamin C prevents colds".
- [ ] V2 Relevance: does the evidence actually address this claim? Evidence about a different organism, population, dose or situation may not apply, e.g. a study in mice or on cells in a dish does not show the same effect in humans; a study of elite athletes may not apply to the general population; a study of a pure chemical may not apply to a food that contains a little of it.
- [ ] V3 Accuracy: is the evidence correct and is it reported correctly? Check whether it comes from a reliable method, whether it has been reproduced, whether the numbers in a report match the original study, and whether the conclusion goes further than the data allow.
- [ ] V4 Bias: has the evidence, or the way it is presented, been pushed in one direction? The types of bias in sources (funding, conflict of interest, cherry-picking, publication bias, confirmation bias, sampling bias, political bias) are taught in "Recognising bias and evaluating sources"; here apply them to the evidence behind an argument.

Weighing the strength of evidence (one block; a ranked list from weakest to strongest)
- [ ] W1 Anecdote or personal testimony ("it worked for me"): weakest, because one person's experience can be due to chance, the placebo effect, natural recovery or other causes.
- [ ] W2 Expert opinion without data, or a single case report: weak; useful for raising a question.
- [ ] W3 Laboratory studies on cells or animals: useful to find mechanisms, but may not apply to humans (V2).
- [ ] W4 Observational studies of large groups of people (e.g. following thousands of people and comparing smokers with non-smokers): good for finding links (correlations), but can't on their own show cause and effect, because other factors may differ between the groups.
- [ ] W5 Randomised controlled trials (people randomly placed in a treatment group or a control group, ideally with neither patients nor researchers knowing who gets what): the strongest single study for showing that a treatment causes an effect. (How to design controlled investigations is in "Designing and conducting investigations".)
- [ ] W6 Systematic reviews and meta-analyses, which combine all good-quality studies on a question: strongest overall, because they don't rely on one study.
- [ ] W7 Scientific consensus: the agreed view of most experts in a field, reached after many studies, e.g. that human activity is causing climate change and that vaccines do not cause autism. A single new study rarely overturns a consensus; it needs to be reproduced first.

Common errors in reasoning from evidence (one block, each with a biological example)
- [ ] R1 Correlation is not causation: two things that change together are not necessarily cause and effect. Either may cause the other, or a third factor may cause both, or the link may be chance, e.g. ice-cream sales and sunburn both rise in summer because of sunny weather, not because one causes the other. Strong evidence for cause includes a controlled experiment, a plausible mechanism, a dose–response pattern (more exposure, more effect), and the same link found in many independent studies (as for smoking and lung cancer).
- [ ] R2 Small samples: results from few individuals are more likely due to chance.
- [ ] R3 No control group: without a comparison, an improvement may have happened anyway.
- [ ] R4 Relative vs absolute risk: a headline saying a food "doubles the risk" of a disease may mean the risk rose from 1 in 10,000 to 2 in 10,000 (an illustration of the arithmetic, not real data); the absolute increase is small. Always ask "doubled from what?"
- [ ] R5 Extrapolating beyond the data: assuming a trend continues outside the range tested, or that a high dose's effect applies to a low dose.

How media present science (one block)
- [ ] M1 Media include newspapers, television, radio, websites, podcasts, social media and advertising. Most people learn about science from them, not from journals, so media reports shape public opinion and decisions on health, the environment and technology.
- [ ] M2 How distortion happens: headlines exaggerate to attract readers or clicks (sensationalism); a single preliminary study is reported as a breakthrough or a "cure"; animal or cell results are reported as if they apply to humans; uncertainty and limitations are left out; press releases from universities and companies may themselves exaggerate.
- [ ] M3 False balance: giving equal time to a fringe view and to the scientific consensus (e.g. one climate scientist debating one climate-change denier) makes the evidence seem evenly split when it isn't.
- [ ] M4 Misinformation is false or misleading information spread without intent to deceive; disinformation is false information spread deliberately to deceive, e.g. for profit or political gain. Social media spread both quickly, because posts that provoke emotion are shared more and algorithms show people more of what they already engage with (echo chambers).
- [ ] M5 Persuasion techniques to recognise: appeal to authority (a celebrity or a doctor speaking outside their expertise); appeal to nature ("natural means safe", though many natural substances are poisonous, e.g. deadly nightshade); emotive language and images; testimonials instead of data; "scientists are hiding the truth" conspiracy claims, which can't be tested; scientific-sounding words used to sell a product.
- [ ] M6 Responsible science reporting names the study and where it was published, gives the sample size and the type of study, states the limitations, separates the evidence from opinion, and includes comment from independent experts.

Evaluating a media-based argument (one Skills block: a checklist in numbered steps, then the worked example)
- [ ] K1 1. Identify the claim and who is making it. 2. Find the original evidence: is a study named, and was it peer reviewed? 3. Check relevance: who or what was studied, and does it apply to the claim? 4. Check the strength of the evidence: type of study, sample size, control group, reproduced or not (W1–W7, R2–R3). 5. Check accuracy: does the report match the study? Is correlation presented as causation? Relative or absolute risk? 6. Check bias: who funded the study and the report; what is the purpose; are other views or limitations mentioned? 7. Check what other reliable sources and the scientific consensus say. 8. Make a judgement: how far the evidence supports the claim, and what further evidence is needed.
- [ ] K2 Case study, the MMR vaccine and autism (one block: claim → how it spread → evidence against → effects). In 1998 Andrew Wakefield and colleagues published a paper in The Lancet on 12 children, suggesting a link between the MMR (measles, mumps and rubella) vaccine and autism. Evaluate: the sample was tiny and had no control group (R2, R3); it showed at most a coincidence in timing, not cause (R1); Wakefield had an undisclosed conflict of interest, having been paid by lawyers acting for parents who wanted to sue vaccine manufacturers (V4); the media reported the claim widely, often with false balance (M3). Investigations by the journalist Brian Deer found that the children's medical details had been misrepresented; The Lancet retracted the paper in 2010 and Wakefield was struck off the UK medical register in 2010. Large studies found no link, e.g. a 2019 Danish study of over 650,000 children. Effect: vaccination rates fell in the UK and Ireland and other countries, and measles outbreaks followed. The case shows how one weak study, amplified by media, can harm public health long after it is disproved.
- [ ] K3 Applying the checklist is also required for technology claims (e.g. a claim that a new app "detects cancer" or that a gene test "predicts your ideal diet"): ask what evidence shows it works, in whom, how accurately, and who profits.

## 3. Traps

- Don't repeat the definitions of the types of bias from "Recognising bias and evaluating sources"; V4 lists them by name and applies them.
- Designing controlled trials (randomisation, blinding, placebos, sample size) belongs to "Designing and conducting investigations"; W5 is a one-sentence description of the type of study.
- Analysing graphs, trends and anomalies belongs to "Producing and analysing data".
- Peer review and retraction are taught in "Science as a global enterprise"; K2 uses the terms without redefining them.
- Wakefield's paper was published in The Lancet, which is peer reviewed: the case shows the limits of peer review too. Don't say it was unpublished or never peer reviewed.
- Don't say MMR "caused" or "did not cause" autism in Wakefield's paper: the paper suggested a link; later evidence showed there is none.
- Don't present anecdote as worthless: it can prompt a proper study, but it is not evidence that a treatment works.
- No diagram cues.

## 4. What exams ask (topics only)

No legacy questions; this outcome is new. Expect stimulus-based questions:
- Read a news extract, advert or social media post about a biology topic and evaluate it for relevance, accuracy and bias.
- Explain why correlation does not show causation, with an example.
- Explain why a randomised controlled trial or a systematic review is stronger evidence than an anecdote.
- Explain how misinformation spreads and its effects on public health.

## 5. Practice questions to write

1. 6 marks: "Explain the difference between correlation and causation, using a biological example." Model answer: R1 definition, one example with the third factor, one piece of evidence that would show cause.
2. 12 marks: "A newspaper headline reads 'Chocolate protects against heart disease, say scientists'. The article reports a study of 40 mice. Evaluate this media argument for relevance, accuracy and bias." Model answer: relevance, mice not humans (V2, W3); accuracy, small sample, preliminary, headline overstates (R2, M2); bias, ask who funded it and whether a chocolate company is involved (V4); check other studies and the consensus (K1 steps 7–8); judgement that the claim is not supported for humans.
3. 9 marks: "Using the MMR vaccine case, explain how a weak scientific claim can harm public health." Model answer: K2: the weaknesses of the 1998 study, the media's role, the retraction and later evidence, the fall in vaccination and the measles outbreaks.
