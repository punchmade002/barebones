## 1. Must cover

Respiration as a catabolic process (one block)
- [ ] C1 Respiration is the controlled, enzyme-catalysed breakdown of food (mainly glucose) in every living cell, releasing energy that is transferred to ATP. It is catabolic: a large molecule (glucose) is broken down into smaller ones (CO₂ and H₂O, or lactic acid, or ethanol and CO₂), and energy is released. Photosynthesis is the matching anabolic process.
- [ ] C2 The energy is released in many small enzyme-controlled steps rather than all at once (as in burning), so much of it can be trapped as ATP instead of being lost as heat. The rest is released as heat, which keeps birds and mammals warm.
- [ ] C3 Energy transfer: the ATP made is used for cell activities such as muscle contraction, active transport and building large molecules (uses are listed in "ATP"; name them in one phrase only).
- [ ] C4 Carbon release: the carbon atoms of glucose leave the cell as CO₂, returning to the atmosphere (or water) the carbon that photosynthesis fixed. Respiration by plants, animals and decomposers, together with burning, balances the uptake by photosynthesis in the carbon cycle (detail in "Nutrient Cycling").
- [ ] C5 Respiratory substrates: glucose is the main one; glycogen (animals) and starch (plants) are first broken down to glucose; fats and, when needed, proteins can also be respired. One bullet.

Aerobic respiration: equation, reactants and products (one block)
- [ ] A1 Aerobic respiration uses oxygen and breaks glucose down completely to carbon dioxide and water. Word equation: glucose + oxygen → carbon dioxide + water + energy.
- [ ] A2 Balanced equation: C₆H₁₂O₆ + 6O₂ → 6CO₂ + 6H₂O + energy. It is the reverse of the photosynthesis equation in its chemicals, but it releases energy (as ATP and heat) instead of taking in light energy.
- [ ] A3 Source of glucose: in plants, from photosynthesis (or from stored starch and sucrose transported in the phloem); in animals, from digested food absorbed into the blood, or from glycogen stored in the liver and muscles.
- [ ] A4 Source of oxygen: in humans, from the air through the lungs, carried by red blood cells to the cells; in plants, from the air through stomata and lenticels, from the soil air for roots, and from the plant's own photosynthesis; in aquatic organisms, oxygen dissolved in water.
- [ ] A5 Fate of carbon dioxide: diffuses out of cells; in humans carried in the blood to the lungs and breathed out; in plants it diffuses out through stomata or, in the light, is used in photosynthesis.
- [ ] A6 Fate of water: it joins the cell's water and is used in the cell or excreted (some is breathed out as water vapour).

Anaerobic respiration (one block)
- [ ] N1 Anaerobic respiration releases energy from glucose without oxygen. The glucose is only partly broken down, so much less energy is released per glucose (much of it stays in the products), and much less ATP is made than in aerobic respiration.
- [ ] N2 In animals (e.g. human muscle during intense exercise) and some bacteria, the product is lactic acid. Word equation: glucose → lactic acid + energy. Balanced: C₆H₁₂O₆ → 2C₃H₆O₃ + energy. No CO₂ is produced.
- [ ] N3 In yeast and in plant cells short of oxygen (e.g. roots in waterlogged soil), the products are ethanol and carbon dioxide. Word equation: glucose → ethanol + carbon dioxide + energy. Balanced: C₆H₁₂O₆ → 2C₂H₅OH + 2CO₂ + energy.
- [ ] N4 Anaerobic respiration in microorganisms is called fermentation. It is used in baking (yeast's CO₂ makes dough rise; the ethanol evaporates in the oven), brewing and wine-making (yeast's ethanol), and making yogurt and cheese (bacteria's lactic acid). Industrial uses belong to "Microorganisms"; one bullet here.
- [ ] N5 In human muscle during hard exercise, the lungs and blood can't supply oxygen fast enough, so some respiration is anaerobic. Lactic acid builds up and the muscles tire; afterwards, extra oxygen (the oxygen debt) is needed to remove the lactic acid: it is carried in the blood to the liver, where much of it is converted back to glucose; the rest is respired aerobically. This is why breathing and heart rate stay high after exercise.
- [ ] N6 Some organisms can respire only anaerobically and are poisoned by oxygen (obligate anaerobes, e.g. the bacteria that cause tetanus); yeast can respire either way (a facultative anaerobe). One bullet.

The overall sequence and cellular locations (one block, process)
- [ ] S1 Stage 1 (the first stage), glycolysis, takes place in the cytoplasm (cytosol) of every cell and doesn't need oxygen. 1. Glucose (6 carbon atoms) is split into two molecules of pyruvate (3 carbon atoms each). 2. A small amount of ATP is made.
- [ ] S2 What happens next depends on oxygen. With oxygen: Stage 2 (the second stage) takes place in the mitochondria. 3. Pyruvate enters a mitochondrion and is broken down completely to CO₂ and water. 4. Hydrogen removed from it is passed to oxygen, forming water, and a large amount of ATP is made. Stage 2 needs oxygen.
- [ ] S3 Without oxygen: pyruvate stays in the cytoplasm and is converted to lactic acid (animals, some bacteria) or to ethanol and CO₂ (yeast, plants). No further ATP is made; anaerobic respiration is glycolysis plus this conversion, all in the cytoplasm.
- [ ] S4 Prokaryotes (bacteria) have no mitochondria; their whole of aerobic respiration takes place in the cytoplasm and on the cell membrane (taught in "Prokaryotic and eukaryotic cells"; one phrase).
- [ ] S5 Diagram: a flow diagram of the overall sequence. Glucose in the cytoplasm → (glycolysis, small amount of ATP) → 2 pyruvate; then two branches: "oxygen present" → into a mitochondrion → CO₂ + H₂O + large amount of ATP; "no oxygen" → stays in cytoplasm → lactic acid (animals) or ethanol + CO₂ (yeast, plants). Label cytoplasm, mitochondrion, stage 1 and stage 2.

Comparing aerobic and anaerobic respiration (one comparison block, paired points)
- [ ] K1 Oxygen: needed vs not needed.
- [ ] K2 Location: cytoplasm and mitochondria vs cytoplasm only.
- [ ] K3 Breakdown of glucose: complete, to CO₂ and H₂O vs incomplete, to lactic acid or to ethanol and CO₂.
- [ ] K4 Energy: a large amount of ATP vs a small amount (only the ATP made in glycolysis).
- [ ] K5 Stages: stage 1 and stage 2 vs stage 1 followed by conversion of pyruvate.
- [ ] K6 Comparing respiration with photosynthesis (paired points, one or two bullets): respiration is catabolic, releases energy, uses O₂ and releases CO₂, happens in all living cells all the time, in the cytoplasm and mitochondria; photosynthesis is anabolic, takes in light energy, uses CO₂ and releases O₂, happens only in cells with chloroplasts and only in the light.

## 3. Traps

- Respiration is not breathing (taught in "Characteristics of living things"; don't restate the distinction beyond a word).
- Plants respire all the time, day and night, in every living cell.
- Respiration releases energy; it doesn't "make" or "produce" energy. Energy is transferred to ATP.
- Lactic acid fermentation produces no CO₂; students often add CO₂ to the lactic acid equation.
- Anaerobic respiration releases less energy because glucose is only partly broken down, not because it is "slower".
- Don't claim lactic acid causes the muscle soreness felt a day or two after exercise; N5 says only that the muscles tire.
- Don't name NAD⁺, NADH, acetyl CoA, the citric acid (Krebs) cycle, the electron transport chain or ATP synthase in this subsection's blocks or key terms: they are Higher Level and belong to "First-stage process", "Second-stage process" and "The mitochondrion's internal structure and ATP synthase". S2 says "hydrogen removed from it is passed to oxygen" without naming carriers.
- FADH₂ and ATP totals are not required by the specification; don't include them.
- The fermentation investigation belongs to "Investigation: conditions for fermentation"; don't describe it here.
- Mitochondrion structure at Ordinary Level is in "Structures and organelles of animal and plant cells"; don't add a mitochondrion diagram here.
- Breathing and gas exchange belong to "The Breathing System"; carbon cycle detail to "Nutrient Cycling"; industrial fermentation to "Microorganisms".
- No other diagram cues beyond S5.
