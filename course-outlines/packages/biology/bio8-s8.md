# Package: bio8-s8 — Investigation: reducing sugars, quantitatively

- **loId:** `bio8-s8` · **chapterId:** `bio8` · **syllabusRef:** 1.2.6
- **Syllabus:** Outcome 6: "investigate quantitatively the level of reducing sugars in a range
  of food samples, use primary data to support conclusions." (It shares the "students learn
  about" text "testing for the presence of nutrients in food" with outcome 5.) Ordinary and
  Higher level. No Higher Level only material.
- **Prepared by:** a higher model, 2026-10-22. This outcome is new to the 2025
  specification. The specification names no method. Write the main method as a Benedict's
  test against a colour scale made from standard glucose solutions (the standard school
  method), then one block each for two ways to make it more quantitative: a colorimeter
  calibration curve and glucose test strips. What a reducing sugar is, the Benedict's
  colour sequence and the qualitative method are in "Investigation: food tests"; use them
  without repeating them.

## 1. Must cover

Planning (one block)
- [ ] A1 Aim: to compare the concentration of reducing sugar in a range of food samples, by comparing their Benedict's results with those of glucose solutions of known concentration.
- [ ] A2 Hypothesis (example to write): "Grape juice contains a higher concentration of reducing sugar than milk." Or a prediction ranking the foods.
- [ ] A3 Principle: the more reducing sugar in a sample, the more blue copper(II) ions are reduced to brick-red copper(I) oxide, so the further the colour moves along the scale blue → green → yellow → orange → brick red, and the more precipitate forms. This works only if Benedict's is in excess, so the colour keeps changing with sugar concentration across the range tested.
- [ ] A4 Variables: independent variable is the food sample (and, for the standards, the known glucose concentration); dependent variable is the concentration of reducing sugar, judged from the colour (or measured as absorbance with a colorimeter); controlled variables are the mass of food and volume of water used to make each extract, the volume of sample and of Benedict's solution, the concentration of Benedict's, the water-bath temperature, the heating time, and the time before the colours are compared.
- [ ] A5 Controls: a tube of distilled water with Benedict's (0% glucose: no reducing sugar, stays blue) is the negative control; the standard glucose solutions act as positive controls and the reference scale.
- [ ] A6 Equipment and materials: glucose powder, electronic balance, volumetric flask or graduated cylinder, beakers, distilled water, graduated pipettes or syringes, test tubes and rack, labels, mortar and pestle, filter funnel and paper, hot water bath with thermometer, test-tube holder, timer, Benedict's solution, white tile or card, safety glasses; optional colorimeter with cuvettes, glucose test strips.
- [ ] A7 Example food samples: grape juice, apple juice, orange juice, honey, milk, onion, a sports drink, a table-sugar solution (expected to stay blue: sucrose is non-reducing).

Method: making the standard glucose solutions (one block, numbered)
- [ ] M1 1. Make a stock solution of 1% glucose: dissolve 1 g of glucose in distilled water and make it up to 100 cm³. 2. Dilute the stock with distilled water to make a range of known concentrations, e.g. 1%, 0.5%, 0.25%, 0.1% and 0% (distilled water only). For example, 5 cm³ of 1% stock + 5 cm³ water gives 0.5%; 2.5 cm³ of stock + 7.5 cm³ water gives 0.25%; 1 cm³ of stock + 9 cm³ water gives 0.1%. 3. Label each tube with its concentration. (Any evenly spread range is acceptable; a serial dilution, halving the concentration each time, also works.)

Method: preparing the food samples (one block, numbered)
- [ ] M2 1. Weigh the same mass of each solid food (e.g. 5 g) on an electronic balance. 2. Crush it with a mortar and pestle in the same volume of distilled water (e.g. 50 cm³). 3. Filter to give a clear extract. 4. Dilute liquid foods by the same factor (e.g. 1 cm³ juice + 9 cm³ water) so that very sweet samples fall within the range of the standards; record the dilution so the original concentration can be worked out.

Method: the test (one block, numbered)
- [ ] M3 1. Place 2 cm³ of each standard and 2 cm³ of each food extract in separate labelled test tubes. 2. Add 2 cm³ of Benedict's solution to every tube and mix. 3. Place all tubes in the same hot water bath (near boiling, at least about 80 °C) at the same time, and heat for exactly the same time, e.g. 5 minutes. 4. Remove all tubes at the same time and place them in a rack in front of a white background. 5. Arrange the standards in order to form a colour scale. 6. Compare each food tube with the scale and record the standard it matches most closely (or the two it lies between). 7. Repeat the whole test three times for each food and use the most common or average result.
- [ ] M4 Diagram: a row of test tubes of the standards after heating, labelled 0%, 0.1%, 0.25%, 0.5%, 1%, with their colours running from blue (0%) through green, yellow and orange to brick red (highest), and a food tube being matched against them. Don't tie an exact colour to each concentration: it depends on the Benedict's volume and heating time.

More quantitative method 1: colorimeter (one block)
- [ ] Q1 A colorimeter measures how much light of a chosen colour passes through a solution (transmission) or is absorbed by it (absorbance), giving a number instead of a judgment by eye.
- [ ] Q2 Method: after heating (M3 steps 1–4), let the precipitate settle, or filter or centrifuge each tube, to leave the remaining blue solution. Zero the colorimeter with a cuvette of distilled water. Measure the absorbance of each standard's solution, using a red filter (blue solutions absorb red light).
- [ ] Q3 The more reducing sugar was present, the more copper(II) ions were used up, so the remaining solution is paler blue and its absorbance is lower (transmission higher).
- [ ] Q4 Plot a calibration curve: absorbance (y-axis) against known glucose concentration (x-axis) for the standards, with a line or smooth curve of best fit. Measure the absorbance of each food extract and read its concentration from the curve, then multiply by any dilution factor.

More quantitative method 2: glucose test strips (one block)
- [ ] G1 A glucose test strip has a pad containing the enzymes glucose oxidase and peroxidase and a dye. Dipped in a solution, the pad changes colour according to the glucose concentration, and the colour is matched to a chart on the container giving the concentration. Test the standards first to check the strips.
- [ ] G2 Limitation: the enzyme is specific to glucose, so strips detect glucose only, not other reducing sugars such as fructose, lactose or maltose. Milk (lactose) gives a negative or low strip result but a positive Benedict's result. Strips also have a limited range, so samples may need diluting.
- [ ] G3 Technology and health link: similar strips and electronic glucose meters are used by people with diabetes to monitor glucose (see "The Endocrine System").

Safety (one block)
- [ ] H1 Safety glasses; Benedict's solution is harmful if swallowed or splashed in the eyes; wash splashes off with water.
- [ ] H2 Heat only in a water bath, never directly in a flame (the mixture can boil suddenly and spurt); use a test-tube holder and take care with near-boiling water and hot tubes.
- [ ] H3 Don't eat or taste any food in the lab; note allergies; clean up spills (sugar solutions make benches sticky and slippery floors).

Results and conclusion (one block)
- [ ] D1 Record primary data in a table: food sample; colour after heating; matching standard (estimated % reducing sugar); repeats; and, with a colorimeter, absorbance and concentration read from the curve.
- [ ] D2 Expected pattern: honey and grape juice give orange to brick red (high reducing sugar, mostly glucose and fructose); other fruit juices and sports drinks give yellow to orange; milk gives green to yellow (lactose); table-sugar solution and water stay blue. Actual results depend on the products used; the notes must not give these as exact percentages.
- [ ] D3 Conclusion: rank the foods by reducing-sugar concentration, give each an estimated range (e.g. "between 0.25% and 0.5%"), state whether the results support the hypothesis, and refer to the primary data.
- [ ] D4 This method is semi-quantitative: it estimates a range, not an exact value, because matching colours by eye is a judgment. The colorimeter gives a more precise and objective value.

Sources of error and improvements (one block)
- [ ] E1 Judging colour by eye is subjective; improve with a colorimeter, or by having several people judge independently.
- [ ] E2 Unequal heating (tubes put in or taken out at different times, or the water bath cooling) changes the colour; heat all tubes together for the same time and keep the bath temperature checked with a thermometer.
- [ ] E3 If Benedict's is not in excess, all copper(II) ions are used up and every concentrated sample looks brick red; dilute the samples or use more Benedict's.
- [ ] E4 Coloured or cloudy food extracts affect the colour; filter them and dilute coloured juices.
- [ ] E5 The precipitate settles over time, changing the appearance; compare all tubes at the same time after heating.
- [ ] E6 Inaccurate measuring of volumes and masses; use graduated pipettes or syringes and an electronic balance.
- [ ] E7 Too few standards gives a coarse scale; use more standards spread over the range, and repeat each test at least three times to check repeatability.

## 3. Traps

- Don't repeat what a reducing sugar is or the qualitative colour list; refer to "Investigation: food tests".
- Don't give sugar percentages for named foods or brands; actual products vary.
- Heating must be in a water bath, for a fixed time, all tubes together.
- With a colorimeter, absorbance falls as sugar concentration rises (the remaining solution is less blue). Students often assume it rises.
- Glucose strips don't detect fructose, lactose or maltose; don't call them a reducing-sugar test.
- Don't give the "time to first colour change" or "mass of dried precipitate" methods as the main method; one bullet in D4 or E1 at most if the writer needs an alternative.
- Diabetes detail belongs to "The Endocrine System"; G3 is one bullet.
- The diagram cue in M4 is the only one wanted, plus an optional sketch of a calibration curve (Q4: absorbance against glucose concentration, falling with concentration). No other diagram cues.

## 4. What exams ask (topics only)

This outcome is new to the 2025 specification; the past papers have no questions on it. Expect:
- Describe how to compare the reducing-sugar content of food samples, including standard glucose solutions and controlled variables.
- Explain how to make a range of glucose concentrations by dilution.
- Use a colour scale or calibration curve to estimate the concentration of an unknown.
- Identify sources of error and suggest improvements (colorimeter, repeats).
- Explain why a glucose test strip and Benedict's give different results for the same food.

## 5. Practice questions to write

1. 12 marks: "Describe how you would compare the concentration of reducing sugar in three fruit juices. Include how you would make the comparison quantitative and two variables you would control." Model answer: M1 (standards), M2, M3, two controlled variables from A4; must include the water bath and the colour-scale comparison.
2. 6 marks: "Explain how you would make 10 cm³ each of 1%, 0.5% and 0.1% glucose solutions from a 1% stock solution." Model answer: M1 dilutions (10 cm³ stock; 5 + 5; 1 + 9).
3. 9 marks: "A food gave a brick-red Benedict's result but a negative result with a glucose test strip. Suggest an explanation, and give one advantage of using a colorimeter over judging colour by eye." Model answer: G2 (a reducing sugar other than glucose, e.g. lactose or fructose), Q1 and D4/E1 (objective, numerical, more precise).
