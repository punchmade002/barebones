## 1. Must cover

What it is and how it is weighted (one block)
- [ ] B1 Leaving Certificate Biology has two assessment components: a written examination worth 60%, at Higher and Ordinary level, and an additional assessment component, the Biology in Practice Investigation, worth 40%.
- [ ] B2 The investigation is based on a common brief (the same for all students, not set at two levels). Both components are set and examined by the State Examinations Commission (SEC).
- [ ] B3 Students complete the work during the course and, in Year 2 (sixth year), submit for marking to the SEC evidence of their ability to conduct scientific research on a particular issue and to use appropriate primary data to investigate aspects of that issue.
- [ ] B4 The specification envisages that it will take up to 20 hours to complete; further details are given in the Guidelines.

Purpose (one block)
- [ ] P1 It gives students an opportunity to display evidence of their learning throughout the course, in particular the learning in the Unifying Strand (Nature of Science), i.e. the outcomes covered in this chapter and in Scientific Knowledge, Science in Society and Biological Reasoning.
- [ ] P2 It is designed to be integrated into the flow of teaching and learning, to be motivating and relevant, to draw together the learning outcomes and the cross-cutting themes (Health, Sustainability and Technology), and to show the relevance of biology to students' lives.
- [ ] P3 It lets students pursue their own interests in biology, make their own investigative decisions, gain a depth of conceptual understanding and self-regulate their learning.
- [ ] P4 The specification stresses that research and experimentation are "more about engaging with and learning from the process, rather than seeking a perfect answer". Students should give an authentic account of how their investigative work unfolded, discuss and explain the outcomes of their investigation, and say how they might revise aspects of the process. (How to evaluate and reflect is in "Reflecting on investigations".)

The Investigation Brief (one block)
- [ ] I1 The SEC publishes an Investigation Brief every year, in term two of Year 1 of the course (fifth year).
- [ ] I2 The brief sets out the specific requirements of the investigation for that year.
- [ ] I3 The brief also: allows students to develop their thinking and ideas on areas related to the brief that they would like to pursue; helps teachers and students plan; includes stimulus material to set a context for the investigation; and allows students to develop an investigative log that they can draw on as they complete the investigation (logs in general in "Reflecting on investigations").

The two parts of the work (one block, in order)
- [ ] W1 Part 1, scientific research on an issue related to the brief: students gather, process and evaluate information from secondary sources. What they learn in this phase may help to inform their experimental work. (Evaluating sources and bias is in "Recognising bias and evaluating sources"; conducting research relevant to a scientific issue is outcome U1.2.)
- [ ] W2 Part 2, an experiment related to an issue within the brief: students generate a hypothesis, plan and design their experiment, carry it out and gather primary data, then analyse the data and form conclusions. (The skills are in "Questioning and predicting", "Designing and conducting investigations", "Objectivity and data quality" and "Producing and analysing data".)
- [ ] W3 Students then develop an evidence-based argument in response to the brief, drawing on both their research and their experiment.
- [ ] W4 On completion they submit a report of the investigation in Year 2, in a format prescribed by the SEC. (General report-writing skills are in "Communicating results"; the SEC sets the actual format.)
- [ ] W5 Organisation: schools have a high degree of autonomy in planning and organising the completion of the investigation. A separate document, Guidelines for the Biology in Practice Investigation, gives guidance on its organisation, implementation and oversight.

What the investigation requires students to do (one block)
- [ ] R1 The specification says the investigation requires students to: consider issues related to real-world applications of biology; demonstrate investigative skills; relate their investigative work to the work of scientists in society; and communicate their findings appropriately and effectively.

Descriptors of quality (one block per descriptor, or one comparison block with four rows; give high, moderate and low for each, close to the specification's wording)
- [ ] D1 Knowledge and understanding. High: engage thoroughly with the concepts being investigated; explain clearly and accurately, using appropriate means, the biological phenomena involved; where applicable, pose a testable hypothesis that is underpinned by biological theory and clearly describe the purpose of the investigation. Moderate: good engagement with the concepts; describe the biological phenomena involved; where applicable, pose a testable hypothesis and outline the purpose of the investigation. Low: limited engagement with the concepts; outline the biological phenomena involved; are provided with a testable hypothesis to investigate.
- [ ] D2 Investigative skills (design and method). High: clear, thorough and appropriate investigative design and methods to collect primary data, leading to high quality data, presentation and analysis. Moderate: clear and appropriate design and methods, leading to good quality data, presentation and analysis. Low: unclear design and methods, leading to limited data, presentation and analysis.
- [ ] D3 Investigative skills (analysis and conclusions). High: draw valid conclusions justified by the data and relating to any hypotheses made; thoroughly evaluate the investigation, acknowledging sources of error in the investigative design. Moderate: draw conclusions that relate to any hypotheses made; identify potential sources of error in the investigative design. Low: draw limited conclusions; potential sources of error in the investigative design are not identified.
- [ ] D4 Relating investigative work to the work of scientists in society. High: offer a considered reflection that locates the outcomes of the investigation within broader issues relating to the work of a scientist or science and society. Moderate: reflect on how the outcomes relate to the work of a scientist or science and society. Low: make limited links between the outcomes and the work of a scientist or science and society.

Using the descriptors (one block; this is study advice drawn from the descriptor wording, not SEC rules, and the notes must say so)
- [ ] U1 The differences between levels are in the verbs and qualifiers: "explain clearly and accurately" vs "describe" vs "outline"; "thoroughly evaluate" vs "identify" vs not identifying errors; "considered reflection" within "broader issues" vs "limited links".
- [ ] U2 Posing your own testable hypothesis, grounded in biological theory, is a feature of the high and moderate levels; being given a hypothesis is described at the low level.
- [ ] U3 Acknowledging and evaluating sources of error in the design is expected for high achievement: a perfect result is not required, but an honest evaluation is (links to P4).
- [ ] U4 The fourth descriptor needs explicit links between the student's own findings and the work of scientists or wider issues in science and society, e.g. how scientists would investigate the same issue on a larger scale, or the health, sustainability or technology issue in the brief.

## 3. Traps

- Don't invent SEC rules: no word count, deadline, mark split, number of experiments or sources, or report template. Say these are set out by the SEC in the brief and the Guidelines.
- It is not set at Higher and Ordinary level; it is a common brief. Only the written examination has two levels.
- Don't call it "coursework marked by the teacher": it is submitted to and examined by the SEC.
- Don't describe any particular year's brief or theme.
- It has two parts (research using secondary sources, and an experiment with primary data) plus an evidence-based argument; don't describe it as only an experiment.
- Don't reteach the investigative skills; cross-refer to this chapter's other subsections by title.
- Peer review and the global nature of science are in "Science as a global enterprise"; bias in sources in "Recognising bias and evaluating sources"; models in "Models".
- No diagram cues.

## 4. What exams ask (topics only)

This component is new; there are no past papers. The written examination assesses the Unifying Strand learning, so expect questions on its skills, not on the investigation's rules.

## 5. Practice questions to write

1. 6 marks: "Outline the two parts of the work students carry out for the Biology in Practice Investigation." Model answer: W1 and W2, with W3 (evidence-based argument).
2. 9 marks: "Describe three features of the Investigation Brief published by the SEC." Model answer: three of I1–I3 (published annually in term two of Year 1, sets the specific requirements, includes stimulus material, supports planning, allows an investigative log).
3. 12 marks: "Using the descriptors of quality, explain what distinguishes high from moderate achievement in investigative skills (analysis and conclusions) and in relating investigative work to the work of scientists in society." Model answer: D3 and D4 high vs moderate wording, with U1 and U3; mustn't invent marks or rules.
