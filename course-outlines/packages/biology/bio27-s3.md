## 1. Must cover

Chemical digestion (one block, two bullets)
- [ ] D1 Chemical digestion is the hydrolysis of large food molecules into their small, soluble units by digestive enzymes: each bond is broken by adding a molecule of water. Carbohydrates → simple sugars; proteins → amino acids; fats → fatty acids and glycerol.
- [ ] D2 Each digestive enzyme is specific to one type of substrate and works best at the pH of the part of the gut where it acts. The gut changes pH along its length (neutral mouth, acid stomach, alkaline small intestine) to suit the enzymes there. Most digestive enzymes are released from glands into the gut cavity (extracellular digestion).

Mouth: saliva (one block)
- [ ] M1 Produced by the three pairs of salivary glands; released into the mouth.
- [ ] M2 pH in the mouth: about 7 (neutral to slightly alkaline), the optimum for salivary amylase.
- [ ] M3 Salivary amylase digests starch to maltose. Product of an amylase: maltose. Because food is in the mouth only briefly, only a little starch is digested there; the amylase is denatured by stomach acid soon after it reaches the stomach.
- [ ] M4 Saliva also contains water (dissolves food, so it can be tasted, and moistens it), mucus (lubricates swallowing) and lysozyme, an enzyme that kills some bacteria.

Stomach: gastric juice (one block)
- [ ] G1 Produced by gastric glands in the stomach lining; released into the stomach. Gastric juice contains hydrochloric acid, pepsin (released as inactive pepsinogen) and mucus.
- [ ] G2 pH in the stomach: about 2 (strongly acidic), the optimum for pepsin.
- [ ] G3 Hydrochloric acid: kills most bacteria in food (a defence against infection); provides the acid pH pepsin needs; converts inactive pepsinogen to active pepsin; stops salivary amylase.
- [ ] G4 Pepsin is a protease: it hydrolyses peptide bonds inside protein chains, breaking proteins into shorter chains (polypeptides). Product of a protease: polypeptides (peptides); complete protein digestion gives amino acids, which needs further enzymes in the small intestine.
- [ ] G5 Pepsin is made in the inactive form pepsinogen so that it does not digest the gastric gland cells that make it; it becomes active only in the stomach cavity. A thick layer of mucus protects the stomach lining from the acid and pepsin. If the mucus barrier fails, the lining is damaged (a stomach ulcer; one phrase, no causes).

Duodenum: bile and pancreatic juice (one block for bile, one for pancreatic juice)
- [ ] B1 Bile: produced by the liver, stored and concentrated in the gallbladder, released through the bile duct into the duodenum when chyme (especially fatty chyme) arrives.
- [ ] B2 Bile contains water, bile salts, bile pigments and sodium hydrogencarbonate; it contains no enzymes.
- [ ] B3 Roles of bile: (i) bile salts emulsify fats (detail in "Mechanical breakdown"; one phrase here); (ii) sodium hydrogencarbonate is alkaline and neutralises the acid chyme from the stomach, raising the pH to suit the pancreatic and intestinal enzymes and stopping pepsin's action; (iii) bile pigments are excretory wastes from the breakdown of haemoglobin from old red blood cells in the liver; they give faeces their brown colour.
- [ ] P1 Pancreatic juice: produced by the pancreas; released through the pancreatic duct into the duodenum. It contains sodium hydrogencarbonate (neutralises acid) and three enzymes:
- [ ] P2 Pancreatic amylase: digests the remaining starch to maltose.
- [ ] P3 Trypsin: a protease, released in an inactive form and activated in the duodenum; it digests proteins and polypeptides into shorter peptides.
- [ ] P4 Lipase: digests fats and oils (triglycerides) into fatty acids and glycerol. Product of a lipase: fatty acids and glycerol. It acts on the tiny droplets made by emulsification.
- [ ] P5 pH in the duodenum and small intestine: about 8 (slightly alkaline), because of the sodium hydrogencarbonate in bile and pancreatic juice; this suits pancreatic amylase, trypsin and lipase.

Small intestine wall: completing digestion (one block)
- [ ] W1 The cells lining the villi of the small intestine make enzymes that complete digestion; many of these enzymes are held in the membranes of the lining cells, so the last step of digestion happens right at the absorbing surface.
- [ ] W2 Maltase: maltose → glucose (two molecules). Sucrase: sucrose → glucose + fructose. Lactase: lactose → glucose + galactose.
- [ ] W3 Peptidases: peptides → amino acids.
- [ ] W4 pH: about 8, as in P5.
- [ ] W5 Lactose intolerance (health link, one bullet): people who stop making lactase after childhood can't digest lactose, so bacteria in the large intestine ferment it, causing bloating, wind and diarrhoea. Why lactase persists in some populations is in "Variation"; dietary management is in "Diet".

Summary table (one comparison block; one bullet per enzyme in this exact form)
- [ ] T1 Salivary amylase – made in salivary glands – acts in mouth – pH about 7 – starch → maltose.
- [ ] T2 Pepsin (protease) – made in gastric glands of stomach wall – acts in stomach – pH about 2 – protein → polypeptides.
- [ ] T3 Pancreatic amylase – made in pancreas – acts in duodenum – pH about 8 – starch → maltose.
- [ ] T4 Trypsin (protease) – made in pancreas – acts in duodenum – pH about 8 – proteins and polypeptides → peptides.
- [ ] T5 Lipase – made in pancreas – acts in duodenum – pH about 8 – fats → fatty acids + glycerol.
- [ ] T6 Maltase – made in the wall of the small intestine – acts there – pH about 8 – maltose → glucose.
- [ ] T7 Peptidases – made in the wall of the small intestine – act there – pH about 8 – peptides → amino acids.
- [ ] T8 Bile salts (not an enzyme) – made in liver, stored in gallbladder – act in duodenum – emulsify fats.

Modelling the digestion of each nutrient (one block; three flow-model bullets and one on using them)
- [ ] F1 Carbohydrate model: starch → (salivary amylase, mouth, pH 7) → maltose; remaining starch → (pancreatic amylase, duodenum, pH 8) → maltose; maltose → (maltase, small intestine wall) → glucose. Sucrose and lactose are digested by sucrase and lactase in the small intestine wall. Cellulose is not digested: humans make no enzyme for it, so it passes on as fibre.
- [ ] F2 Protein model: protein → (pepsin, stomach, pH 2) → polypeptides → (trypsin, duodenum, pH 8) → shorter peptides → (peptidases, small intestine wall) → amino acids.
- [ ] F3 Fat model: large fat droplets → (bile salts, duodenum: emulsification, physical) → tiny droplets → (lipase from the pancreas, duodenum, pH 8) → fatty acids and glycerol. Fat is digested only in the small intestine, after emulsification.
- [ ] F4 Using the models to predict (one bullet): e.g. if the bile duct is blocked (e.g. by gallstones), fats are poorly emulsified and digested, and undigested fat appears in the faeces; if the pancreas releases too little juice, as in cystic fibrosis, digestion of all three nutrients is reduced, which is why patients take enzyme capsules (see "Industrial enzymes").
- [ ] F5 Diagram: a flow chart with three rows (carbohydrate, protein, fat), each running from left to right through mouth, stomach, duodenum and small intestine wall, showing the enzyme or bile salts, the pH and the product at each step; or the alimentary canal outline from "The digestive system" with enzymes and pH written at each site.

A physical model: Visking tubing as a model gut (one block)
- [ ] V1 Visking (dialysis) tubing is a selectively permeable membrane: it lets small molecules (water, glucose, maltose) through but not large ones (starch). It models the wall of the small intestine, and the water around it models the blood.
- [ ] V2 Method: 1. Tie one end of two lengths of Visking tubing (soaked in water first). 2. Fill tube A with starch solution plus amylase solution; fill tube B (the control) with the same volume of starch solution plus water (or boiled amylase). 3. Tie the open ends, rinse the outsides, and place each tube in a separate boiling tube of distilled water in a water bath at 37 °C. 4. At the start, and again after about 30 minutes, test samples of the water outside each tube with iodine solution (for starch) and with Benedict's solution in a hot water bath (for reducing sugar), as in "Investigation: food tests".
- [ ] V3 Expected results: the water outside both tubes stays yellow-brown with iodine (no starch passes out). After 30 minutes the water outside tube A turns brick red with Benedict's (reducing sugar present); the water outside tube B stays blue. Inside tube A, a test would show less starch and much reducing sugar.
- [ ] V4 Conclusion: starch molecules are too large to pass through the membrane; amylase digests starch to maltose, which is small enough to pass through. This models why food must be digested before it can be absorbed into the blood.
- [ ] V5 Safety: wear safety glasses; Benedict's solution is an irritant; take care with hot water; heat tubes in a water bath, never directly.
- [ ] V6 Limitations of the model: the tubing has no villi or microvilli, so its surface area is far smaller; no blood flows past to carry products away and keep the concentration gradient; the real gut absorbs some products by active transport, which tubing cannot do; only one enzyme and one nutrient are modelled, at a constant pH, whereas the gut uses a sequence of enzymes and pH values; the tubing does not move food by peristalsis.
- [ ] V7 Diagram: the set-up: a boiling tube of distilled water in a water bath at 37 °C, with a knotted length of Visking tubing containing starch and amylase hanging in it; label Visking tubing, starch + amylase, distilled water, water bath 37 °C; and the control tube beside it.

## 3. Traps

- Bile is made in the liver, not the gallbladder; it contains no enzymes. Bile salts emulsify (physical); bile's chemical role is neutralising acid.
- Amylase produces maltose, not glucose; maltase produces glucose. Lipase produces fatty acids and glycerol (not "fatty acids" alone). Pepsin produces polypeptides, not amino acids.
- Pepsin works in acid (pH 2); the pancreatic enzymes in alkaline conditions (pH 8). Don't give pancreatic lipase an acid optimum.
- Don't teach rennin (not made in significant amounts by humans), gastric or lingual lipase, enterokinase, chymotrypsin, carboxypeptidase, nucleases, or the hormones gastrin, secretin and cholecystokinin. They are not on the specification. Past-paper marking accepted "stomach" for lipase; teach pancreatic lipase in the duodenum only.
- Accept "about": pH values vary along the gut and between textbooks (mouth 6.5–7.5, stomach 1–3, small intestine 7–9). Use the single values in the table; one bullet may say they are approximate.
- How enzymes work, induced fit and denaturation belong to "Enzymes as catalysts" and "Enzyme structure and specificity"; don't re-explain them. Food-test colour changes are given here only as results, not re-taught.
- Industrial uses of digestive enzymes belong to "Industrial enzymes"; F4 is one phrase.
- Absorption of the products belongs to "Absorption, transport and storage"; V4 links to it in one phrase.
- The Visking tubing model is a model, not one of the specification's required investigations; write it as a model block with method and limitations, not as a full investigation with hypotheses and variables.
- The diagram cues listed (F5, V7) are the only ones wanted. No other diagram cues.

## 4. What exams ask (topics only)

- Name an enzyme that digests protein (or fat, or starch); state where it is produced, where it acts, and the approximate pH there; name its products.
- Name the products of complete digestion of a protein.
- What is lipase; give the site of lipase action and its approximate pH.
- State the roles of hydrochloric acid in the stomach and of bile.
- Explain why pepsin is released in an inactive form.
