## 1. Must cover

Genetic mechanisms (one block)
- [ ] G1 Genetic inheritance works by passing on DNA: the sequence of bases in the genes is copied when cells divide and passed to offspring in the gametes (see "Nuclear and non-nuclear inheritance" for how).
- [ ] G2 The information is stored in the base sequence itself. It is copied very accurately, so it is stable over many generations.
- [ ] G3 It changes only by mutation, a change in the base sequence or in the chromosomes (see "Variation"). A mutation is usually permanent: it is copied into every cell descended from the cell where it happened, and, if it is in a gamete, into the offspring.
- [ ] G4 The genetic code is the same in every body cell, so genetic mechanisms alone can't explain why cells with the same DNA become different, or why identical twins grow more different with age. Epigenetic mechanisms explain these.

The concept of epigenetics (one block)
- [ ] E1 Epigenetics is the study of changes in gene expression (which genes are switched on or off, and how strongly) that are not caused by changes in the DNA base sequence. "Epi-" means "on top of": the changes are chemical marks added on top of the DNA. The full set of marks in a cell is called its epigenome.
- [ ] E2 Epigenetic marks are caused by development and by the environment and behaviour: e.g. diet, stress, smoking, exposure to pollutants and other chemicals, temperature, and in insects the food a larva is given.
- [ ] E3 The marks are copied when a cell divides by mitosis, so a change can last for the rest of an organism's life, and they keep specialised cells specialised (a liver cell's daughter cells are liver cells).
- [ ] E4 Some epigenetic changes are reversible: marks can be added and removed by enzymes, e.g. in response to a change in diet or lifestyle. In humans, smokers' blood cells show changed patterns of marks on some genes, and some of these changes partly reverse after a person stops smoking.
- [ ] E5 Passing to the next generation: in mammals most marks are erased and reset in the gametes and the early embryo, so epigenetic changes are usually not passed on; some escape and are inherited (agouti mice, Example 2). In plants, marks are more often passed on through seeds (toadflax, Example 5). This is epigenetic inheritance.
- [ ] E6 Evidence from twins: identical twins have the same DNA, and when young their epigenetic marks are very similar; in older twins, especially those who have lived apart or had different lifestyles, the marks are much more different, matching differences in health. The environment changes the epigenome over a lifetime.

How epigenetic marks work (one process block)
- [ ] M1 DNA methylation: an enzyme adds a small chemical group, a methyl group (CH₃), to cytosine bases in the DNA, especially in the control region at the start of a gene. Methylation usually switches the gene off (silences it), because the cell's machinery can no longer read the gene. Removing the methyl groups can switch it back on. The base sequence is unchanged: a methylated C still pairs with G.
- [ ] M2 Histone modification: chemical groups are added to or removed from the histone proteins that the DNA is wound around (histones are described in "Chromosome structure and genes"). Some changes (e.g. adding acetyl groups) loosen the packing so the genes there can be read (switched on); others tighten it so the genes can't be read (switched off).
- [ ] M3 Result: tightly packed, heavily methylated DNA is inactive; loosely packed, unmethylated DNA is active. So the same DNA can give different phenotypes depending on its marks.
- [ ] M4 Diagram: two versions of the same gene side by side. Left: DNA loosely wound around histones, no methyl groups, arrow "gene read → protein made → gene switched on". Right: DNA tightly packed, methyl groups (drawn as small flags) on the DNA, cross through "gene read" → "gene switched off". Label DNA, histone, methyl group, gene on, gene off.

Genetic and epigenetic mechanisms compared (one comparison block, paired points or a table)
- [ ] C1 What changes: the DNA base sequence vs gene expression, through chemical marks on the DNA and histones; the base sequence is unchanged.
- [ ] C2 Cause: mutation, from random copying errors or mutagens such as radiation vs the environment, behaviour and development (diet, stress, chemicals, temperature).
- [ ] C3 Reversibility: usually permanent vs often reversible.
- [ ] C4 Speed: genetic change in a population is slow, spreading over many generations vs epigenetic change can happen within an organism's lifetime, in response to its surroundings.
- [ ] C5 Inheritance: reliably passed on in every generation through the gametes, following Mendel's laws for nuclear genes vs usually erased in the gametes and early embryo in mammals; sometimes passed on, more often in plants, and often fading over a few generations.
- [ ] C6 Within one organism: the same in all body cells (except for new mutations) vs different in different cell types and tissues.
- [ ] C7 Similarities: both change the phenotype by changing which proteins are made; both are copied when cells divide by mitosis; both can be involved in disease, e.g. some cancers involve both mutations and abnormal methylation that silences genes which normally control cell division (link to "Causes, prevention and treatment of cancer"; one phrase).

Example 1: honeybee queens and workers (one block, Higher Level only)
- [ ] B1 In a honeybee colony, the queen (large, fertile, lays the eggs, lives for years) and the workers (smaller, sterile females that forage and care for the larvae, living for weeks in summer) develop from genetically identical female larvae.
- [ ] B2 Trigger: diet. All young larvae are fed royal jelly, a secretion of worker bees, for the first few days; a larva fed royal jelly throughout its development, in a special large cell, becomes a queen, while the rest are switched to a diet including pollen and honey and become workers.
- [ ] B3 Mechanism: the different diets produce different patterns of DNA methylation in the developing bee, switching on different sets of genes. Evidence: in 2008, researchers in Australia reduced the activity of the enzyme that adds methyl groups to DNA in newly hatched larvae; most of these larvae developed into queens with working ovaries, as if they had been fed royal jelly.
- [ ] B4 What it shows: one genotype gives two very different phenotypes because the environment (food) changes gene expression during development. The marks are passed on through every cell division in the developing bee, but the caste is not passed to the queen's offspring: each larva's caste is decided by its own diet.

Example 2: agouti mice (one block, Higher Level only)
- [ ] A1 The agouti gene in mice normally controls fur colour, giving brown fur. In mice with a variant called agouti viable yellow, an extra stretch of DNA inserted beside the gene can switch it on in every cell all the time.
- [ ] A2 When that region is unmethylated, the gene is always on: the mice have yellow fur and become obese, with a higher risk of diabetes and cancer. When it is heavily methylated, the gene is switched off and the mice are brown and lean; partly methylated mice are mottled. Genetically identical mice in the same litter can be any of these.
- [ ] A3 Effect of the mother's diet: in a study published in 2003 (Robert Waterland and Randy Jirtle), pregnant mothers given extra methyl-donor nutrients (folic acid, vitamin B12, choline and betaine) had more brown, healthy offspring, because more methyl groups were added to the gene in the embryos. In later work, exposing pregnant mothers to the plastics chemical bisphenol A (BPA) reduced methylation and gave more yellow offspring; the effect was cancelled by methyl-donor supplements.
- [ ] A4 Inheritance: the offspring's coat colour tends to resemble its mother's, because the marks on this gene are not fully erased when the eggs are made. So it is a true example of epigenetic inheritance, and it shows the effects can be changed (reversed) by diet.

Example 3: the Dutch Hunger Winter (one block, Higher Level only)
- [ ] D1 In the winter of 1944–1945, during the Second World War, a German blockade cut food supplies to the occupied western Netherlands. Food rations fell well below 1,000 kilocalories a day, and about 20,000 people died before the region was liberated in May 1945.
- [ ] D2 Because the famine had a clear start and end, and records of births were kept, scientists could study people who were in the womb during the famine. Those whose mothers were starved in early pregnancy had higher rates of obesity, heart disease and some other conditions as adults.
- [ ] D3 Epigenetic evidence: a study published in 2008 found that, about 60 years later, people exposed to the famine around conception had less methylation of a growth gene, IGF2 (insulin-like growth factor 2), than their brothers or sisters of the same sex who were not exposed. The environment before birth left a lasting mark on gene expression.
- [ ] D4 Some studies report effects on the next generation (the grandchildren of the starved mothers), but this evidence is weaker and debated. So this is clear evidence of a lasting epigenetic effect of the environment in humans, and possible but uncertain evidence of inheritance across generations.

Example 4: vernalisation in plants (one block, Higher Level only)
- [ ] V1 Vernalisation is the need of some plants to experience a long period of cold (winter) before they can flower. It stops them flowering in autumn and makes them flower in spring, when conditions suit seed production.
- [ ] V2 In the small wild plant thale cress (Arabidopsis thaliana), a gene called FLC (FLOWERING LOCUS C) blocks flowering. During weeks of cold, histone modifications gradually switch FLC off, and the longer the cold lasts, the more cells have FLC switched off.
- [ ] V3 The "memory" of winter: FLC stays switched off after the weather warms, because the marks are copied through all later cell divisions, so the plant flowers in spring. The change is epigenetic: the FLC gene's base sequence is unchanged.
- [ ] V4 It is reset in each generation: FLC is switched back on in the seeds, so each new plant must go through its own winter. So this is an epigenetic memory within one plant's life, not inheritance to offspring.
- [ ] V5 Application: many crops, such as winter wheat, need vernalisation, which is why they are sown in autumn.

Example 5: toadflax flowers (one block, Higher Level only)
- [ ] T1 Common toadflax (Linaria vulgaris), a wild plant, normally has flowers with one plane of symmetry (bilaterally symmetrical) and a single spur. A natural variant, called peloric, has radially symmetrical flowers with five spurs. It was first described by Carl Linnaeus in the 1740s.
- [ ] T2 In 1999, Enrico Coen's group at the John Innes Centre in Norwich showed that the peloric plants have the same base sequence in the key flower-symmetry gene (Lcyc) as normal plants, but the gene is heavily methylated and switched off: an epigenetic change (sometimes called an epimutation), not a mutation.
- [ ] T3 The methylated, switched-off state is passed on through the seeds to the next generation, so the peloric form is inherited in wild populations; occasionally the methylation is lost and branches with normal flowers appear, showing the change is reversible.
- [ ] T4 What it shows: a heritable natural variation, noticed for more than 250 years, caused by an epigenetic mark rather than a change in DNA sequence.

## 3. Traps

- Only the five example blocks and the research skills block are Higher Level. The concept, the mechanisms (M1–M4) and the comparison are Ordinary and Higher level as the outline marks them; don't add the Higher Level label to them.
- Epigenetic change is not a mutation: the base sequence doesn't change. Don't write "the environment mutated the gene".
- Don't say epigenetic changes are always inherited, or always reversible: "some are reversible"; most are erased between generations in mammals.
- Distinguish lasting effects within one life (honeybee caste, vernalisation, Dutch Hunger Winter) from inheritance to offspring (agouti mice, toadflax). Students who name the honeybee as "epigenetic inheritance" must explain that the marks are inherited through cell divisions in the developing bee, not by the queen's offspring.
- Methylation usually switches genes off; histone acetylation switches them on. Don't reverse these.
- Don't teach Lamarck or "inheritance of acquired characteristics" here; the history of ideas belongs to "The importance of the theory".
- Gene expression as a term is defined in "Key concepts of information transfer"; histone structure in "Chromosome structure and genes"; mutation types in "Variation" and "DNA Replication, Protein Synthesis and Mutations"; cancer in "Causes, prevention and treatment of cancer". Refer by title.
- Ethics of epigenetic testing, and any other technology, is not required.
- No other diagram cues beyond M4.
