# Package: bio4-s2 — Explaining biological phenomena

- **loId:** `bio4-s2` · **chapterId:** `bio4` · **syllabusRef:** U4.2
- **Syllabus:** "means by which to explain biological phenomena: systems; interdependence,
  unity and diversity of life; form fits function; transfer of information, matter, and energy,
  etc." Outcome U4.2: "explain biological phenomena using appropriate means." Ordinary and
  Higher level. "Explain" means give a detailed account including reasons or causes. The
  specification's strand overviews use these same ideas (e.g. "all life depends on the transfer
  of energy and matter"; "adaptations of living things suit specific functions"), so each idea
  here is a tool for explaining material from every chapter.
- **Prepared by:** a higher model, 2026-10-22. Each idea gets its own block: a definition, what
  it explains, and two to five examples drawn from the course, each pointing to the chapter
  where the biology is taught. Then a Skills block shows the ideas used together.

## 1. Must cover

Overview (one block)
- [ ] O1 A biological phenomenon is any observable event or feature of living things, e.g. a person sweating on a hot day, bread going mouldy, a population of rabbits crashing. Explaining it means giving the causes and mechanism, not just describing it.
- [ ] O2 Biologists explain phenomena using a small number of big ideas that apply across all of biology: systems; interdependence; the unity and diversity of life; form fits function; and the transfer of information, matter and energy. The specification's "etc." allows others; the most important is evolution by natural selection, which explains both unity and diversity (O3).
- [ ] O3 (assumed) Evolution as the unifying explanation: all living things share common ancestors, so they share features (unity), and natural selection acting on variation in different environments has produced adaptations and many species (diversity) (taught in "The theory of evolution by natural selection").

Systems (one block)
- [ ] S1 A system is a set of interacting parts that work together as a whole. A system has inputs and outputs, and its behaviour depends on how the parts interact, not just on the parts themselves (properties that appear only from the interactions are called emergent properties, e.g. consciousness arises from interacting neurons, not from any single neuron).
- [ ] S2 Biological systems exist at every level of organisation: molecules → organelles → cells → tissues → organs → organ systems → organisms → populations → communities → ecosystems → the biosphere (levels from cell to organism are taught in "Levels of organisation").
- [ ] S3 Examples: the circulatory system (heart, vessels and blood together transport materials; see "Circulation"); the digestive system; the nervous system; an ecosystem (living and non-living parts interacting); a cell, whose organelles work together.
- [ ] S4 Systems are regulated by feedback: negative feedback reverses a change and keeps the system stable (e.g. control of blood glucose and of thyroxine; see "Homeostasis as a stimulus-response model").
- [ ] S5 Systems thinking means looking at how a change in one part affects others, e.g. a fall in red blood cell count (anaemia) reduces oxygen delivery, so cells respire less aerobically, causing tiredness; and organ systems depend on each other (breathing supplies oxygen that the circulatory system transports to respiring cells).

Interdependence (one block)
- [ ] I1 Interdependence means that organisms depend on each other and on their non-living environment, so a change to one affects others.
- [ ] I2 Examples between organisms: feeding relationships in food webs (removing a predator can let its prey increase and overgraze); pollination of flowers by insects, which get nectar (see "Flower structure and pollination"); seed dispersal by animals (see "Fruit and seed dispersal"); decomposers recycling nutrients that plants need (see "The carbon cycle" and "The nitrogen cycle"); nitrogen-fixing bacteria in the root nodules of legumes such as clover; the gut microbiome and its human host (see "The microbiome").
- [ ] I3 Examples between organisms and the environment: plants depend on light, water, CO₂ and minerals; photosynthesis and respiration link organisms through the carbon cycle (producers release the oxygen that consumers use, and consumers release CO₂ that producers use).
- [ ] I4 Interdependence within an organism: organ systems depend on each other (S5); e.g. muscles need the nervous system to stimulate them and the circulatory system to supply glucose and oxygen.

Unity of life (one block)
- [ ] U1 Unity means that all living things share basic features, which is evidence that they share a common ancestor.
- [ ] U2 Shared features: all are made of cells, with a phospholipid membrane, cytoplasm and ribosomes; all use DNA as genetic material; the genetic code is almost universal (the same codons code for the same amino acids in nearly all organisms), which is why a human gene can be expressed in bacteria in genetic engineering; all use ATP to transfer energy; all build proteins from the same set of 20 amino acids; all use enzymes; all show the characteristics of life (see "Characteristics of living things").

Diversity of life (one block)
- [ ] V1 Diversity means the great variety of living things, at the levels of genes (variation within a species), species, and ecosystems (biodiversity; see "Key terms" in Ecology, Ecosystems and Biodiversity).
- [ ] V2 Causes: mutation creates new alleles; sexual reproduction (meiosis and random fertilisation) shuffles them into new combinations (see "Variation"); natural selection in different environments favours different adaptations; populations that are separated can become new species.
- [ ] V3 Biologists organise diversity by classification into three domains (Bacteria, Archaea, Eukaryota) and six kingdoms, based increasingly on genetic evidence (see "Domains of life" and "Classification").
- [ ] V4 Unity and diversity are explained together by evolution (O3): shared ancestry gives unity; adaptation and speciation give diversity. Example: the forelimbs of humans, bats and whales have the same set of bones (unity, from a common ancestor) but different shapes for grasping, flying and swimming (diversity, from adaptation) (see "Evidence for evolution").

Form fits function (one block; each example names the structure, its feature, and how the feature suits the function)
- [ ] FF1 Form fits function means that the structure (form) of a molecule, cell, organ or organism suits the job (function) it does. Natural selection explains why: individuals whose structures did the job better survived and reproduced more.
- [ ] FF2 Molecule: an enzyme's active site has a shape complementary to its substrate, so it catalyses one reaction (see "Enzymes as catalysts"); the DNA double helix with complementary base pairs allows accurate copying.
- [ ] FF3 Cells: red blood cells are biconcave discs with no nucleus, giving a large surface area and more room for haemoglobin to carry oxygen (see "Blood"); neurons have long axons and a myelin sheath that carry impulses quickly over long distances (see "Neurons"); root hair cells have long extensions that increase the surface area for absorbing water and minerals; sperm cells have a tail for swimming and many mitochondria to supply energy; guard cells change shape to open and close stomata.
- [ ] FF4 Tissues and organs: alveoli have walls one cell thick, a moist lining, a large total surface area and a rich blood supply, for fast gas exchange (see "Gas exchange and the breathing mechanism"); villi in the small intestine increase the surface area for absorption (see "Absorption, transport and storage"); arteries have thick, muscular, elastic walls to withstand high pressure, while veins have thinner walls and valves to stop backflow (see "Blood vessels"); xylem vessels are hollow, dead tubes strengthened with lignin to carry water (see "Plant structures for transport"); the left ventricle has a thicker muscular wall than the right because it pumps blood all round the body.
- [ ] FF5 Exam technique: always link a named feature to the function with "so" or "which allows", e.g. "the alveolar wall is one cell thick, so oxygen diffuses only a short distance".

Transfer of information, matter and energy (one block each for information, matter and energy)
- [ ] TI1 Transfer of information: genetic information passes from DNA to RNA to protein, so genes control traits (see "Genes, proteins and traits"); from parent to offspring through gametes (see "Genetic Inheritance" chapter, "Key concepts of information transfer"); and from cell to daughter cells by DNA replication and mitosis. Information is also transferred within the body by nerve impulses (fast) and hormones (slower), and between cells by chemical signals, e.g. neurotransmitters.
- [ ] TM1 Transfer of matter: matter is not created or destroyed but is recycled. Atoms of carbon, nitrogen and other elements cycle between organisms and the environment through feeding, excretion, death and decomposition (see "The carbon cycle" and "The nitrogen cycle"). Within organisms, matter is taken in (digestion and absorption, gas exchange, root uptake), transported (blood, xylem and phloem) and built into new molecules (anabolism) or broken down (catabolism).
- [ ] TE1 Transfer of energy: energy flows through living systems in one direction and is not recycled. Light energy from the Sun is converted to chemical energy in glucose by photosynthesis; respiration releases that energy and stores it in ATP, which transfers it to cell activities (see "ATP"); at each step some energy is lost as heat, so energy decreases at each feeding level of a food chain, which limits food chains to a few links (see "Energy and nutrient transfer").
- [ ] TE2 Contrast to state explicitly: matter cycles; energy flows through and is lost as heat. Diagram: a simple ecosystem diagram with energy as a one-way arrow from the Sun through producer, consumers and decomposers, with heat-loss arrows at each level, and matter (carbon) as a loop returning from decomposers to the air and soil and back to producers.

Using the ideas together (one Skills block: method, then two worked explanations)
- [ ] W1 Method: 1. Describe the phenomenon precisely. 2. Choose the ideas that apply (more than one usually does). 3. Explain the mechanism step by step, naming the structures and processes involved. 4. Link structure to function, and cause to effect. 5. If asked, use evolution to explain why the feature exists.
- [ ] W2 Worked example, why breathing rate and heart rate rise during exercise: transfer of energy (muscles need more ATP, so respire faster); transfer of matter (more oxygen must be supplied and more CO₂ removed); systems and interdependence (the breathing, circulatory and muscular systems work together; the brain detects the rise in CO₂ in the blood and increases breathing rate, a feedback response); form fits function (alveoli and capillaries suit fast exchange).
- [ ] W3 Worked example, why removing a top predator can reduce plant diversity in an ecosystem: interdependence (the predator's prey, e.g. a herbivore, increases); transfer of matter and energy (more energy flows to the herbivores, which eat more of the producers); systems (one change spreads through the food web); diversity (the plants the herbivores prefer decline, so species diversity can fall).

## 3. Traps

- Each example is taught in its own chapter. Give one bullet per example with the subsection title; don't teach whole processes (e.g. don't list the stages of respiration or the nitrogen cycle).
- Models as such are taught in "Models"; don't repeat what a model is.
- Levels of organisation (cell to organism) are taught in "Levels of organisation"; S2 lists the sequence once as context.
- The breathing-control example in W2 is one sentence; the Higher Level detail of CO₂ control is in "CO2 as a controlling factor in breathing".
- Don't say organisms "develop" adaptations because they need them: adaptations arise because variation already exists and natural selection favours the better-adapted individuals.
- Don't say energy is "recycled" or "used up": it is transferred and eventually lost as heat.
- Tropisms and auxin are not on the specification. Don't use them as examples.
- Diagram cue: TE2 only. No other diagram cues.

## 4. What exams ask (topics only)

No legacy questions on this outcome. Expect, often with stimulus material:
- Explain a described phenomenon using one or more of the named ideas.
- Give an example of form fitting function and explain the link.
- Explain what is meant by the unity and diversity of life and how evolution accounts for both.
- Distinguish the transfer of matter from the transfer of energy in an ecosystem.
