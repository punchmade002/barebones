## 1. Must cover

Sources of carbon dioxide (one block)
- [ ] G1 A photosynthesising leaf gets CO₂ from two sources: the air outside, entering through the stomata, and the plant's own respiring cells.
- [ ] G2 The air contains only about 0.04% CO₂, so a leaf must exchange large volumes of air to get enough; this is why it has many stomata and a large internal surface.
- [ ] G3 Respiring cells: every living plant cell respires all the time, day and night, releasing CO₂ from its mitochondria (see "Respiration as a catabolic process"). In the light, the CO₂ released by a mesophyll cell's own mitochondria, and by neighbouring cells, can be used directly by its chloroplasts without ever leaving the leaf.
- [ ] G4 Aquatic plants take in CO₂ dissolved in the water (see "Photosynthesis as an anabolic process"). One phrase.

How CO₂ reaches the chloroplasts (one process block, numbered stages)
- [ ] G5 1. Photosynthesis uses CO₂ in the chloroplasts, so the CO₂ concentration inside the leaf falls below that of the outside air, setting up a concentration gradient. 2. CO₂ diffuses in through the open stomata, from the higher concentration outside to the lower concentration inside. 3. It diffuses through the air spaces of the spongy mesophyll to the mesophyll cells. 4. It dissolves in the thin film of water on the moist cell walls, because gases can only enter cells in solution. 5. It diffuses across the cell wall, cell membrane and cytoplasm into the chloroplasts, where it is used in the light-independent stage. The gradient is kept up because CO₂ is used as fast as it arrives.
- [ ] G6 CO₂ moves through the plant by diffusion only, through the air spaces and across moist cell surfaces; it is not carried in the xylem or phloem and no energy is used to move it. Leaf features that speed up its diffusion: a thin leaf (short distance), a large surface area, many stomata, a network of air spaces, and moist mesophyll cell walls with a large total surface.
- [ ] G7 Stomata control the entry of CO₂: they open in the light, when CO₂ is needed. At Higher Level, the CO₂ concentration inside the leaf itself helps control whether stomata open or close; this is explained in "CO₂ as a controlling factor in plant stomata". One bullet only.
- [ ] G8 Diagram: a transverse section of part of a leaf showing arrows for CO₂ diffusing in through a stoma, through the air spaces, into a palisade cell's chloroplast, and arrows for oxygen and water vapour diffusing out; plus a short arrow from a mitochondrion to a chloroplast in the same cell labelled "CO₂ from respiration".

Gas exchange by day and by night (one block)
- [ ] D1 In bright light, photosynthesis is faster than respiration: the leaf uses more CO₂ than its cells produce, so there is a net uptake of CO₂ through the stomata and a net release of oxygen.
- [ ] D2 In darkness there is no photosynthesis, only respiration: the plant takes in oxygen and gives out CO₂, through stomata that are mostly closed, through the cuticle in small amounts, and through lenticels.
- [ ] D3 At dawn and dusk, in dim light, the rate of photosynthesis equals the rate of respiration (the compensation point; see "Investigation: rate of photosynthesis"): all the CO₂ produced by respiration is used in photosynthesis, so there is no net exchange of CO₂ or oxygen with the air.
- [ ] D4 Non-green parts (roots, woody stems, the inside of fruits and tubers) only respire: they take in oxygen and release CO₂ by diffusion, roots to the air spaces in the soil and woody stems through lenticels (see "Plant structures for transport").
- [ ] D5 The oxygen made in photosynthesis is a photosynthetic product too: it is used in the plant's own respiration, and the excess diffuses out through the stomata (see "Photosynthesis as an anabolic process"). One bullet.

Transport of photosynthetic products in the phloem: translocation (one process block)
- [ ] F1 Translocation is the transport of dissolved food made in photosynthesis, mainly sucrose and also amino acids, through the phloem sieve tubes from one part of the plant to another.
- [ ] F2 Sucrose is the form in which sugar is carried (reasons in "Photosynthesis as an anabolic process"). Amino acids are made in the leaves from sugars and the nitrates brought up in the xylem, and are carried in the phloem to growing regions to make proteins.
- [ ] F3 A source is a part of the plant where sucrose is made or released into the phloem: mainly the photosynthesising leaves, and also storage organs when their stores are being used.
- [ ] F4 A sink is a part where sucrose is used or stored: growing shoot and root tips (respiration and making cellulose for new cell walls), developing flowers, fruits and seeds, the roots, and storage organs, where it is converted to starch (e.g. potato tubers in summer).
- [ ] F5 Because sinks can be above or below the leaves, phloem transport goes both up and down the plant, at different times in different sieve tubes. Example of a part that changes role: a potato tuber is a sink while it fills with starch in summer, and a source the next spring, when its starch is converted back to sucrose and carried to the growing new shoots.
- [ ] F6 Translocation needs energy: at the source, companion cells use ATP to load sucrose actively into the sieve tubes (see "Plant structures for transport"). So it happens only in living phloem and stops if the phloem is killed (e.g. by heat).
- [ ] F7 How the sap flows (the pressure-flow model): 1. Loading sucrose at the source makes the sap in the sieve tubes there very concentrated. 2. Water enters the sieve tubes from the nearby xylem by osmosis, raising the pressure. 3. At the sink, sucrose is removed from the sieve tubes into the cells that use or store it, so water leaves and the pressure there is lower. 4. The sap flows along the sieve tubes, through the sieve plates, from the high-pressure source to the low-pressure sink, carrying the dissolved sucrose and amino acids with it.
- [ ] F8 Diagram: a simple plant outline with a leaf labelled "source" and a root tip, a fruit and a storage organ labelled "sink", and arrows in the phloem showing sucrose moving both up and down from the leaf; beside it, a sketch of a sieve tube between a source cell and a sink cell with arrows for sucrose loaded in, water entering by osmosis, flow along the tube, and sucrose unloaded.

Evidence that phloem carries food (one block)
- [ ] E1 Ringing (girdling): removing a complete ring of bark from a woody stem removes the phloem, which lies just inside the bark, but leaves the xylem (the wood) intact. The bark just above the ring swells as sugars build up there, and the tissues below the ring, including the roots, are starved of food. The leaves above don't wilt at first, because the xylem still carries water up. If the roots run out of food the whole tree dies.
- [ ] E2 Sustainability link: animals that strip a ring of bark from trees, such as deer, rabbits and grey squirrels, can kill them in this way; foresters protect young trees with tree guards. One bullet.
- [ ] E3 Aphids: these small insects feed by pushing their needle-like mouthparts into a single sieve tube. The sap that flows into them is rich in sucrose and amino acids, and if the insect's body is cut away the sap keeps oozing out of the mouthparts, showing it is under pressure in the sieve tube.
- [ ] E4 Radioactive tracers: if a leaf is given CO₂ containing radioactive carbon (¹⁴CO₂), the radioactive carbon soon appears in sucrose, first in the phloem of the leaf and then in the phloem of the stem and in the sinks, not in the xylem.

Water, minerals and food compared (one comparison block, paired points; the processes, not the structures)
- [ ] K1 What moves: water and minerals in the xylem; sucrose and amino acids in the phloem; CO₂ and oxygen by diffusion in air spaces, in neither tissue.
- [ ] K2 Direction: xylem upwards only; phloem up or down, source to sink; CO₂ down its concentration gradient, into the leaf by day and out at night.
- [ ] K3 Driving force: for water, evaporation from the leaves (transpiration pull), helped at Higher Level by root pressure; for sucrose, active loading at the source and the pressure difference between source and sink; for CO₂, diffusion down a concentration gradient.
- [ ] K4 Energy used by the plant: none to lift water (the sun's energy), ATP for loading sucrose and for absorbing minerals, none for CO₂ diffusion.

## 3. Traps

- Don't write any Higher Level CO₂-control content here (how CO₂ concentration opens or closes stomata, the link to breathing). It belongs only in "CO₂ as a controlling factor in plant stomata", which G7 cross-refers to. The breathing comparison is in that subsection too, not here.
- Plants respire all the time, day and night; they don't "respire only at night" or "photosynthesise instead of respiring".
- Sucrose, not glucose, is the sugar carried in the phloem. Starch is never transported; it is stored, and must be converted to sucrose before it can move.
- Phloem transport is active (needs ATP, living cells); xylem transport of water is passive. Students often reverse this.
- CO₂ is not carried in the xylem or phloem; it diffuses through air spaces.
- Don't re-teach the conversion of glucose to sucrose, starch and cellulose, the reasons sucrose suits transport, or the sources of water and light: these are in "Photosynthesis as an anabolic process". Use them in a phrase.
- Phloem and companion cell structure belongs to "Plant structures for transport"; F6 refers to it in a phrase.
- The compensation point and the investigation of photosynthesis rate belong to "Investigation: rate of photosynthesis"; D3 is one bullet.
- Modified storage organs (bulbs, corms, rhizomes) as a classification were on the previous syllabus; F4–F5 name the potato tuber only.
- Don't name Münch or give a date for the pressure-flow model; don't use the terms apoplast, symplast or water potential.
- No other diagram cues beyond G8 and F8.

## 4. What exams ask (topics only)

- Name substances involved in leaf metabolism that pass through the stomata (CO₂, oxygen, water vapour).
- State the role of stomata in photosynthesis (entry of CO₂, exit of oxygen).
- Give the source of the CO₂ used in photosynthesis.
- Name the tissue that carries the products of photosynthesis, and the form in which sugar is carried.
- Explain the terms source and sink, and why phloem transport goes in both directions.

Model answer: G1 (air through stomata; respiring cells); G5 (gradient, diffusion through stomata and air spaces, dissolving in the water film, diffusion into the chloroplast).

Explain this result and predict what would eventually happen to the tree."
