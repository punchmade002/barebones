# Package: bio7-s1 — Prokaryotic and eukaryotic cells

- **loId:** `bio7-s1` · **chapterId:** `bio7` · **syllabusRef:** 1.3.2
- **Syllabus:** "images of cells and their parts from a light microscope and electron
  microscope." Outcome 2: "compare the structure of prokaryotic and eukaryotic cells."
  Ordinary and Higher level. "Compare" needs paired similarities and differences; the "images"
  part needs what each cell type looks like in light and electron micrographs.
- **Prepared by:** a higher model, 2026-10-22.

## 1. Must cover

Definitions (one block)
- [ ] D1 Prokaryotic cell: a cell with no nucleus and no membrane-bound organelles; its DNA lies free in the cytoplasm. ("Pro" = before, "karyon" = kernel or nucleus.) Bacteria and archaea are prokaryotes.
- [ ] D2 Eukaryotic cell: a cell whose DNA is enclosed in a nucleus surrounded by a double membrane (the nuclear envelope), and which has membrane-bound organelles such as mitochondria. ("Eu" = true.) Protists, fungi, plants and animals are eukaryotes.
- [ ] D3 (assumed) Units: 1 mm = 1000 µm (micrometres); 1 µm = 1000 nm (nanometres). Cell sizes are given in µm.

Features they share (one bullet group)
- [ ] S1 Both have a cell membrane that controls what enters and leaves; cytoplasm where many reactions take place; ribosomes for making proteins; DNA as the genetic material, using the same genetic code. Both respire and use ATP. These shared features suggest that all cells evolved from a common ancestor.

Comparison (comparison block: paired points, then a table)
- [ ] C1 Nucleus: prokaryotes have none; their DNA is concentrated in a region of the cytoplasm called the nucleoid. Eukaryotes have a nucleus with a nuclear envelope and nuclear pores.
- [ ] C2 DNA: prokaryotes have one circular chromosome, not wound around histone proteins in bacteria, plus often small extra rings of DNA called plasmids (which can carry genes such as antibiotic resistance). Eukaryotes have several linear chromosomes made of DNA wound around histone proteins.
- [ ] C3 Membrane-bound organelles: prokaryotes have none (no mitochondria, chloroplasts or other membrane-bound organelles); respiration and, in photosynthetic bacteria, photosynthesis take place on the cell membrane or on infoldings of it. Eukaryotes have mitochondria, and plants and algae also have chloroplasts.
- [ ] C4 Ribosomes: both have them, but prokaryotic ribosomes are smaller (70S) than those in eukaryotic cytoplasm (80S). Many antibiotics work by blocking 70S ribosomes, so they harm bacteria and not human cells.
- [ ] C5 Size: prokaryotic cells are small, most about 1–10 µm long; eukaryotic cells are usually about 10–100 µm, so roughly ten times larger in length.
- [ ] C6 Cell wall: almost all prokaryotes have one (made of peptidoglycan in bacteria). Among eukaryotes, plant cells have cellulose walls, fungal cells chitin walls, and animal cells none.
- [ ] C7 Extra outer structures some prokaryotes have: a capsule (slime layer) for protection, and a flagellum for movement; the full bacterial cell is in "Microorganisms".
- [ ] C8 Organisation: prokaryotes are almost all unicellular and divide by binary fission; eukaryotes can be unicellular or multicellular and divide by mitosis (detail in "Cell Division and Cancer").
- [ ] C9 Comparison table with rows: nucleus; DNA form; histones; plasmids; membrane-bound organelles; ribosome size; typical size; cell wall; division; examples.
- [ ] C10 (assumed) Mitochondria and chloroplasts contain their own small circular DNA and 70S ribosomes, like a prokaryote. One bullet; its role in inheritance is in "Genetic Inheritance" (non-nuclear inheritance).

Images from light and electron microscopes (one block)
- [ ] I1 Under a light microscope at ×400, a bacterium appears as a tiny dot, rod or spiral with no visible internal structure; it is too small (about 1–10 µm) for the light microscope to show its parts.
- [ ] I2 Under a light microscope, eukaryotic cells show the nucleus (especially when stained), the cytoplasm, the cell membrane as a line, and in plant cells the cell wall, large vacuole and chloroplasts.
- [ ] I3 Electron micrographs show what the light microscope can't: in prokaryotes, the nucleoid, ribosomes as tiny dots, plasmids and the layers of the wall; in eukaryotes, the double nuclear envelope and its pores, the internal membranes of mitochondria and chloroplasts, and ribosomes.
- [ ] I4 How to tell the cell type from a micrograph: check for a nucleus (present → eukaryotic); check the scale bar or stated magnification to estimate size (about 1–10 µm suggests prokaryotic); look for membrane-bound organelles; then, for a eukaryote, look for a cell wall, large vacuole and chloroplasts (plant) or their absence (animal). Plant and animal cells are compared in "Structures and organelles of animal and plant cells".
- [ ] I5 Diagram: side-by-side labelled diagrams of a generalised prokaryotic cell (cell wall, cell membrane, cytoplasm, nucleoid with circular DNA, plasmid, ribosomes, capsule and flagellum) and a eukaryotic animal cell (cell membrane, cytoplasm, nucleus, nuclear envelope, nuclear pore, mitochondria, ribosomes), drawn to show the eukaryotic cell much larger.

## 3. Traps

- Prokaryotes do have ribosomes, DNA, a cell membrane and cytoplasm; the commonest error is "prokaryotes have no DNA" or "no ribosomes". They lack a nucleus and membrane-bound organelles only.
- Plasmids are found in prokaryotes (and in yeast); don't say all eukaryotes have them.
- Don't call the nucleoid a nucleus.
- Bacterial shapes (cocci, bacilli, spirilla), the full bacterial cell, and Rhizopus belong to "Microorganisms". C7 names capsule and flagellum only.
- Organelle functions belong to "Structures and organelles of animal and plant cells"; here they are only named. ER and Golgi apparatus are Higher Level material there: don't name them in this subsection or in the I5 diagram labels.
- How the microscopes work belongs to "Microscopy".
- Domain-level differences (archaeal lipids, rRNA) belong to "Domains of life".
- Diagram cues: only I5. No other diagram cues.
