## 1. Must cover

The route of water through the plant (one process block, numbered stages)
- [ ] W1 Overview: water moves from the soil, into the root hairs, across the root cortex, into the xylem, up the stem, into the leaves, and out as water vapour through the stomata. This continuous flow is the transpiration stream.
- [ ] W2 1. Into the root hair by osmosis: soil water is a dilute solution (higher water concentration), while the cell sap in the root hair's vacuole is more concentrated (lower water concentration), so water passes in through the selectively permeable cell membrane.
- [ ] W3 2. Across the cortex: water moves from cell to cell by osmosis, each cell inwards being slightly more concentrated than the one before, and also seeps along the cellulose cell walls and the spaces between cells. It passes through the endodermis into the xylem at the centre of the root.
- [ ] W4 3. Up the xylem: water travels up the root and stem xylem as continuous columns, into the veins of the leaves. What moves it is explained at Ordinary Level in W7 and at Higher Level in the root-pressure and cohesion–tension blocks.
- [ ] W5 4. Into the leaf cells: water leaves the xylem in the veins and passes by osmosis into the mesophyll cells, keeping them turgid and supplying a small amount for photosynthesis.
- [ ] W6 5. Out of the leaf: water evaporates from the moist walls of the mesophyll cells into the air spaces, which become saturated with water vapour. The water vapour then diffuses out through the open stomata into the drier air outside, from a higher to a lower concentration of water vapour.
- [ ] W7 Transpiration pull (Ordinary and Higher): as water evaporates from the leaves, more water is drawn out of the xylem to replace it, so the whole column of water in the xylem is pulled up from the roots, like drinking through a straw. The energy comes from the sun's heat, which causes evaporation; the plant uses none of its own ATP to move water up.
- [ ] W8 Diagram: an outline of a whole plant with arrows showing the route: soil water → root hair → cortex → root xylem → stem xylem → leaf vein → mesophyll cell → air space → stoma → air; label osmosis (root hair, cortex), evaporation (mesophyll cell walls) and diffusion (stoma).

Transpiration (one block)
- [ ] T1 Definition: transpiration is the loss of water vapour from the parts of a plant above the ground, mainly through the stomata of the leaves. Small amounts are lost through the cuticle and the lenticels.
- [ ] T2 Most of the water a plant absorbs is lost by transpiration; only a small fraction is used in photosynthesis or kept in the cells (see "Photosynthesis as an anabolic process").
- [ ] T3 Benefits: it pulls water and dissolved minerals up from the roots to the leaves (the transpiration stream); it keeps leaf cells supplied with water, so they stay turgid and the leaves stay firm and spread out to the light; evaporation cools the leaves in strong sunlight (see "Water").
- [ ] T4 Cost: transpiration is unavoidable, because the stomata must be open to let CO₂ in for photosynthesis, and water vapour escapes through the same pores. If water is lost faster than the roots can take it in, cells lose turgor and the plant wilts; prolonged wilting stops photosynthesis and can kill the plant. So the plant balances gas exchange against water loss.
- [ ] T5 Conditions that increase transpiration (one bullet, named only): bright light, high temperature, wind, low humidity, a large leaf area, a thin cuticle. Each is explained and investigated in "Investigation: rate of transpiration".

Stomata: how they open and close (one structure-and-function block)
- [ ] S1 Each stoma is a pore between two guard cells. The wall of a guard cell next to the pore (the inner wall) is thicker and less stretchy than the outer wall.
- [ ] S2 Opening: in the light, guard cells actively take in potassium ions (K⁺) from neighbouring epidermal cells, using ATP. This raises the solute concentration in the guard cells, so water enters them by osmosis from the surrounding cells. The guard cells swell and become turgid; because the thick inner wall stretches less than the thin outer wall, the cells bend outwards and curve apart, and the pore opens.
- [ ] S3 Closing: potassium ions leave the guard cells, water follows by osmosis, the guard cells become flaccid and straighten, and the pore closes.
- [ ] S4 When stomata are open: usually in daylight, when CO₂ is needed for photosynthesis; when the concentration of CO₂ inside the leaf is low (Higher Level detail in "CO₂ as a controlling factor in plant stomata").
- [ ] S5 When stomata close: at night, when there is no photosynthesis and so no need for CO₂; when water is short and the plant begins to wilt, a chemical signal from the roots closes them, saving water even though photosynthesis slows (see "Structures and systems for response"); in very hot, dry conditions; when CO₂ concentration inside the leaf is high.
- [ ] S6 Stomata are mostly on the lower surface of a broad leaf, where it is shaded and cooler, so less water evaporates from them. Leaves floating on water (e.g. water lily) have them on the upper surface instead, because the lower surface touches the water.
- [ ] S7 Diagram: a pair of guard cells drawn twice, open (turgid, curved apart, pore open) and closed (flaccid, straight, pore closed); label guard cell, thick inner wall, thin outer wall, chloroplast, nucleus, stoma (pore); add arrows for K⁺ in and water in for opening.

Reducing water loss (one block)
- [ ] R1 Leaf features: a waxy, waterproof cuticle, thicker on leaves of plants in dry or windy places; most stomata on the lower, shaded surface; stomata that close at night and in drought.
- [ ] R2 Deciduous trees shed their leaves in autumn, so they don't lose water in winter, when cold soil water is hard to absorb. Evergreen conifers have needle-shaped leaves with a small surface area and a thick cuticle.
- [ ] R3 Plants of dry habitats (xerophytes) have extra adaptations, e.g. marram grass of sand dunes rolls its leaves into a tube, with the stomata on the inside surface, trapping humid air; cacti have leaves reduced to spines and store water in a swollen green stem. One bullet.

Minerals: uptake and transport (one block)
- [ ] M1 Plants need minerals such as nitrate, phosphate, potassium, magnesium and calcium (their uses are in "Minerals"). They are taken up from the soil water as dissolved ions (e.g. nitrate NO₃⁻, potassium K⁺, magnesium Mg²⁺).
- [ ] M2 Uptake by active transport: mineral ions are usually more concentrated inside root hair cells than in the soil water, so they must be pumped in against their concentration gradient by carrier proteins in the cell membrane, using ATP from respiration. Where the soil concentration is higher than in the cell, some ions also enter by diffusion.
- [ ] M3 Because active transport needs ATP, roots need oxygen for aerobic respiration. In waterlogged soil the air spaces fill with water, the roots get too little oxygen, mineral uptake falls and the plant grows poorly; this is one reason soil must be well drained and why roots have air spaces in the cortex.
- [ ] M4 The ions move across the cortex to the xylem and are carried up dissolved in the water of the transpiration stream to the leaves, growing tips and other parts, where they are taken into cells. So the faster the transpiration, the faster minerals reach the leaves.
- [ ] M5 Pumping mineral ions into the root also makes the root cells more concentrated than soil water, which keeps water entering by osmosis.

Root pressure [HL] (one block, Higher Level only)
- [ ] P1 [HL] Root pressure is a pressure in the root xylem that pushes water a short distance up the stem from below.
- [ ] P2 [HL] How it arises: living root cells actively pump mineral ions into the xylem, using ATP. This makes the xylem sap more concentrated than the surrounding cells, so water passes into the xylem by osmosis, building up pressure that pushes the water upwards.
- [ ] P3 [HL] Evidence: when a stem is cut off near ground level, sap oozes from the cut stump for some time, pushed out by the roots (e.g. grapevines pruned in spring "bleed" sap). Guttation: on cool, humid nights or early mornings, when transpiration is very slow, drops of water are forced out through special pores at the tips and edges of leaves, e.g. on grass and strawberry leaves. Guttation drops are not dew: they appear at the leaf edges, not all over the surface.
- [ ] P4 [HL] Root pressure needs living, respiring root cells: it stops if the roots are killed, cooled or deprived of oxygen, showing it is an active process.
- [ ] P5 [HL] Importance: it is most significant at night and in spring, before the leaves have opened and when transpiration is low, and it can refill xylem columns that contain air bubbles. It is far too weak to raise water to the top of a tall tree, and some plants show little or none, so it is not the main mechanism.

Cohesion and tension (the cohesion–tension model) [HL] (one process block, Higher Level only)
- [ ] C1 [HL] The cohesion–tension model explains how water rises to the tops of the tallest trees. It was proposed in 1894 by two Irish scientists at Trinity College Dublin, the botanist Henry Dixon and the physicist John Joly. One bullet (Nature of Science link).
- [ ] C2 [HL] 1. Transpiration: water evaporates from the walls of the mesophyll cells and diffuses out through the stomata.
- [ ] C3 [HL] 2. The mesophyll cells replace the lost water by osmosis from the cells deeper in the leaf, and these take water from the xylem in the veins.
- [ ] C4 [HL] 3. Tension: removing water from the top of the xylem puts the column of water in the xylem under tension (a pulling force, or negative pressure), which is passed all the way down the stem to the roots. This is the transpiration pull.
- [ ] C5 [HL] 4. Cohesion: water molecules are attracted to each other by hydrogen bonds (see "Water"), so they stick together and the column does not break; each molecule pulled up drags the one behind it, so the whole unbroken column moves up as one.
- [ ] C6 [HL] 5. Adhesion: water molecules are also attracted to the walls of the narrow xylem vessels, which helps hold the column up against gravity and stops it pulling away from the walls.
- [ ] C7 [HL] 6. At the bottom, water is drawn into the root xylem from the cortex, and into the root hairs from the soil, to replace it.
- [ ] C8 [HL] The process is passive: the energy comes from the sun, which evaporates the water; the plant spends no ATP. The lignified walls of the xylem stop the vessels collapsing inwards under the tension.
- [ ] C9 [HL] Evidence: the trunk of a tree becomes very slightly narrower during the day, when transpiration is fastest, as the tension pulls the xylem walls inwards; when a xylem vessel under tension is cut, air is drawn in rather than water leaking out. If air enters the xylem the column breaks and water flow in that vessel stops, which is why the stems of cut flowers should be cut again under water before being placed in a vase.
- [ ] C10 [HL] Capillarity (water creeping up narrow tubes by adhesion) raises water only a short way and is a minor factor. One phrase.

Root pressure and cohesion–tension compared [HL] (one comparison block, Higher Level only, paired points)
- [ ] K1 [HL] Root pressure pushes water from below; cohesion–tension pulls it from above.
- [ ] K2 [HL] Root pressure is active, using ATP in the root cells; cohesion–tension is passive, powered by the sun's energy.
- [ ] K3 [HL] Root pressure raises water only a short distance; cohesion–tension is the main mechanism and can lift water to the top of the tallest trees.
- [ ] K4 [HL] Root pressure is most important at night and in spring, when transpiration is low; cohesion–tension works when the stomata are open and transpiration is fast, mainly by day.

## 3. Traps

- Root pressure, cohesion and tension, and their comparison are Higher Level only; every heading must say so. Transpiration, stomata, osmosis, diffusion and the transpiration pull in W7 are Ordinary and Higher.
- Water enters root hairs by osmosis; mineral ions enter mainly by active transport. Don't say minerals enter "by osmosis" or water "by active transport".
- Transpiration is the loss of water vapour, not liquid water; it is evaporation followed by diffusion. Guttation (liquid water, root pressure) is not transpiration.
- Cohesion is water sticking to water; adhesion is water sticking to the xylem walls. Students swap them.
- The transpiration pull is passive; the plant uses ATP for active transport of minerals and for root pressure, not for lifting water by cohesion–tension.
- Write "higher to lower water concentration" for osmosis, as "Water" does. Don't introduce water potential, which is not used in this course.
- Open stomata are turgid guard cells; closed stomata are flaccid guard cells. Don't reverse this.
- The guard cell K⁺ mechanism is the accepted explanation. Don't teach the older idea that guard cells open because they make sugar by photosynthesis.
- Don't name abscisic acid (not on the specification); say "a chemical signal from the roots".
- Apoplast and symplast pathways, the Casparian strip and water potential are beyond this specification; W3 stays at the level given.
- Humidity, wind and temperature effects are explained in "Investigation: rate of transpiration"; T5 names them only. The CO₂ mechanism for stomata is in "CO₂ as a controlling factor in plant stomata"; S4–S5 name it only.
- Mineral functions belong to "Minerals"; osmosis and plant wilting in salty or over-fertilised soil belong to "Osmosis in food preservation and plant health". Don't repeat them.
- No other diagram cues beyond W8 and S7.
