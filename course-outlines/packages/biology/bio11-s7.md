## 1. Must cover

Gregor Mendel and his experiments (one block)
- [ ] M1 Gregor Mendel (1822–1884) was an Augustinian friar (later abbot) at the monastery of St Thomas in Brno (then Brünn, in the Austrian Empire; now in the Czech Republic). He bred garden pea plants (Pisum sativum) between 1856 and 1863, presented his results in 1865 and published them in 1866. His work was largely ignored until 1900, when it was rediscovered independently by other botanists, including Hugo de Vries and Carl Correns. He worked before chromosomes, meiosis or DNA were understood, and called the units of inheritance "factors" (now genes, and their versions alleles).
- [ ] M2 Why peas suited the work: many varieties with clear contrasting forms of a trait (e.g. tall or dwarf, with nothing in between); peas normally self-pollinate, so true-breeding lines were available; they can be cross-pollinated by hand (removing the anthers of one flower and dusting its stigma with pollen from another); they grow quickly and produce many seeds, giving large numbers to count.
- [ ] M3 The seven traits he studied (dominant form first): seed shape (round / wrinkled), seed colour (yellow / green), flower colour (purple / white), pod shape (inflated / constricted), pod colour (green / yellow), flower position (axial, along the stem / terminal, at the tip), stem length (tall / dwarf). One bullet listing all seven.
- [ ] M4 His method, the features that made it scientific: he started with true-breeding parents; studied one trait at a time (then two); counted large numbers of offspring; recorded results over several generations (P, F1, F2); used mathematics (ratios) to analyse them; and repeated each cross for several traits.
- [ ] M5 His key results: crossing true-breeding tall with true-breeding dwarf gave an F1 that was all tall; self-pollinating the F1 gave an F2 of tall and dwarf in about 3 : 1. Figures: 787 tall : 277 dwarf (2.84 : 1); 5474 round : 1850 wrinkled seeds (2.96 : 1); 6022 yellow : 2001 green seeds (3.01 : 1). The dwarf trait disappeared in the F1 and reappeared in the F2, so the dwarf "factor" must have been present, hidden, in the F1. He concluded that each plant has two factors for each trait, one from each parent, and that one (dominant) can mask the other (recessive).

The Law of Segregation (one block)
- [ ] S1 Statement: "The two alleles (factors) of a gene separate (segregate) from each other during gamete formation, so that each gamete receives only one allele of each gene." Shorter accepted form: "Inherited traits are controlled by pairs of factors (alleles), which separate during gamete formation."
- [ ] S2 Illustration: a heterozygous tall plant (Tt) forms two types of gamete, T and t, in equal numbers (half the gametes carry T and half carry t). Random fertilisation of these gametes in Tt × Tt gives 1 TT : 2 Tt : 1 tt and 3 tall : 1 dwarf, which is the 3 : 1 Mendel observed (the cross is worked in full in "Monohybrid crosses").
- [ ] S3 Chromosome basis (Mendel didn't know this): the two alleles of a gene lie at the same locus on the two chromosomes of a homologous pair; in meiosis the homologous chromosomes separate into different cells, carrying one allele each into each gamete. Meiosis itself is in "Mitosis and meiosis".
- [ ] S4 Diagram: a diploid cell with one pair of homologous chromosomes, one carrying T and the other t (same position), dividing by meiosis into gametes, half with the T chromosome and half with the t chromosome; labels: homologous pair, allele T, allele t, locus, meiosis, gametes (haploid).

The Law of Independent Assortment (one block)
- [ ] I1 Statement: "When gametes form, the alleles of one gene separate independently of the alleles of another gene, so either allele of one gene can combine at random with either allele of another gene." Equivalent: "Each member of a pair of factors can combine randomly with either member of another pair." Add the condition: this holds for genes on different chromosomes (unlinked genes).
- [ ] I2 Illustration: a plant heterozygous for seed shape and seed colour (RrYy) forms four types of gamete, RY, Ry, rY and ry, in equal numbers (1/4 each), because whether a gamete gets R or r has no effect on whether it gets Y or y.
- [ ] I3 Chromosome basis: when homologous pairs line up in meiosis, the way one pair lines up is independent of the way another pair lines up. With R and Y on different chromosome pairs, half the cells put R with Y (giving RY and ry gametes) and half put R with y (giving Ry and rY gametes), so all four types are made in equal numbers.
- [ ] I4 Diagram: a cell with two pairs of homologous chromosomes (one long pair carrying R and r, one short pair carrying Y and y) shown in the two possible arrangements, each arrangement leading to two kinds of gamete: arrangement 1 → RY and ry; arrangement 2 → Ry and rY. Label: the two homologous pairs, alleles, the two equally likely arrangements, the four gamete types.
- [ ] I5 The evidence Mendel had: a dihybrid cross of true-breeding round yellow × wrinkled green gave an F1 all round yellow; the F1 self-pollinated gave an F2 of 315 round yellow : 108 round green : 101 wrinkled yellow : 32 wrinkled green, close to 9 : 3 : 3 : 1. The new combinations (round green and wrinkled yellow) can only arise if the two genes are inherited independently. The cross is worked in "Dihybrid crosses".

Mendel's legacy (one bullet)
- [ ] L1 Mendel showed that inheritance is particulate: traits are passed on by separate units that stay unchanged from generation to generation, rather than by blending of the parents' characteristics. A blended trait could never reappear unchanged, as dwarf did in the F2. His laws are the basis of the predictions in "Benefits and limitations of Mendelian genetics".

## 3. Traps

- Every block is Higher Level only; mark every heading.
- The specification names two laws. Some books add a "Law of Dominance"; don't present it as one of Mendel's Laws. Dominance is in "Key concepts of information transfer".
- Segregation is about the two alleles of one gene; independent assortment is about two different genes. Students often swap them or state independent assortment as "genes separate independently" without saying it is alleles of different genes.
- Independent assortment applies only to genes on different chromosomes; the exception is "Linkage". Don't explain crossing over (not required).
- Mendel did not know about genes, chromosomes or meiosis; S3 and I3 are the later explanation.
- Mendel did not "discover DNA" or "invent genetics"; he discovered the rules of inheritance.
- The general method for listing gametes from any genotype, and the 16-box Punnett square, belong to "Dihybrid crosses".
- Diagram cues: only S4 and I4. No other diagram cues.

## 4. What exams ask (topics only)

- State Mendel's Law of Segregation and Law of Independent Assortment (asked often; standard wording earns the marks).
- Explain the chromosome basis of the laws in meiosis.
- Explain why Mendel chose pea plants or why his method was successful.
- Relate the laws to cross results (3 : 1, 9 : 3 : 3 : 1).

2. 9 marks, Higher: "Using a labelled diagram, illustrate how Mendel's Law of Independent Assortment applies to the gametes of a plant with the genotype RrYy." Model answer: I2–I4: two homologous pairs, two arrangements, four gamete types in equal numbers.
3. 12 marks, Higher: "Describe how Gregor Mendel's experiments on pea plants led him to the Law of Segregation." Model answer: M2, M4, M5 (true-breeding parents, F1 all tall, F2 about 3 : 1 with one set of figures, recessive reappearing so two factors per plant), then S1–S2.
