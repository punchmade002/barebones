## 1. Must cover

What Mendelian genetics is (one block)
- [ ] M1 Mendelian genetics is the study of how traits controlled by single genes are inherited, using Mendel's principles: each organism has two alleles of each gene, one from each parent; the two alleles separate into different gametes; one allele can be dominant over the other; and alleles of different genes are inherited independently. It predicts offspring ratios with genetic crosses (as in "Monohybrid crosses").
- [ ] M2 A Mendelian (single-gene) trait is controlled by one gene with clear alternative forms (discontinuous variation: an individual is in one category or another, e.g. tall or dwarf pea plants, or having cystic fibrosis or not).

Benefits: the predictive power of Mendelian genetics (one block, one bullet per benefit, each with reason and example)
- [ ] B1 Predicting offspring: knowing the parents' genotypes, a cross predicts the probability of each genotype and phenotype in the offspring, e.g. two carriers of cystic fibrosis have a 1 in 4 chance of an affected child (cross in "Monohybrid crosses").
- [ ] B2 Genetic counselling and screening in medicine: families with a history of a single-gene disorder can be told the risks to future children, and carrier testing tells people whether they carry a recessive allele before they have children. Cystic fibrosis is an important example in Ireland, which has the highest rate of cystic fibrosis in the world; about 1 in 19 Irish people is a carrier. Other single-gene disorders whose inheritance follows Mendel's rules: sickle-cell anaemia (recessive), Huntington's disease (dominant: an affected heterozygous parent has a 1 in 2 chance of passing it to each child), haemophilia and colour blindness (sex-linked; see "Sex-linked traits").
- [ ] B3 Identifying an unknown genotype: a test cross or a pedigree reveals whether an individual carries a hidden recessive allele, e.g. checking whether a breeding animal carries an allele for an inherited disease before it is used for breeding.
- [ ] B4 Plant and animal breeding: breeders use Mendel's principles to produce true-breeding varieties and to combine desirable traits from two parents (e.g. a high yield from one variety with disease resistance from another) into one variety, predicting how many offspring of each kind to expect. New combinations arise from independent assortment ("Dihybrid crosses").
- [ ] B5 Foundation of modern genetics: Mendel's idea that traits are passed on by separate, unchanging units (genes) that segregate in gametes is the basis of later discoveries, such as genes being carried on chromosomes, DNA being the genetic material, and gene mapping; it also explains how hidden recessive alleles keep variation in a population, which natural selection acts on (see "Variation").
- [ ] B6 Genetic databases and testing: modern genetic tests and genome databases identify alleles known to cause single-gene diseases, and Mendel's rules turn a test result into a risk for a person's children (database searches are in "Bioinformatics: genome databases").

Limitations: where Mendel's Laws don't apply (one block, one bullet per limitation, each with reason and example)
- [ ] L1 Complex traits involving multiple gene interactions (polygenic traits): most traits are controlled by many genes, each with a small effect, e.g. human height, skin colour and eye colour. They show continuous variation (a range of values, not a few categories), so they don't give simple ratios such as 3 : 1 and can't be predicted with a Punnett square.
- [ ] L2 Genes interacting with one another: the effect of one gene can depend on other genes, e.g. a gene that blocks all pigment production (as in albinism) hides the effects of the other genes for colour. The offspring ratios no longer match the simple Mendelian ratios.
- [ ] L3 Environmental factors: the phenotype results from the genotype and the environment together. Examples: a person's height depends on diet and health in childhood as well as genes; identical twins, with the same genotype, can differ in height and weight; the flowers of some hydrangea plants are blue in acidic soil and pink in alkaline soil; a plant grown in poor light or poor soil is shorter than its genotype allows. Mendelian ratios can't predict traits strongly affected by the environment.
- [ ] L4 Common diseases are multifactorial: conditions such as heart disease, type 2 diabetes and most cancers depend on many genes plus lifestyle and environment (diet, exercise, smoking), so a person's risk can be estimated but not predicted from a simple cross. Genetic susceptibility to cancer is in "Causes, prevention and treatment of cancer".
- [ ] L5 Epigenetic effects: environment and behaviour can switch genes on or off without changing the DNA sequence, and some such changes can be passed on, which Mendel's laws don't account for (see "Genetic and epigenetic mechanisms").
- [ ] L6 Exceptions to Mendel's own rules (one bullet each, one line): linked genes on the same chromosome don't assort independently ("Linkage"); with incomplete dominance the heterozygote is intermediate, so the F2 gives 1 : 2 : 1 not 3 : 1 ("Incomplete dominance"); sex-linked genes give different results in sons and daughters ("Sex-linked traits"); many genes have more than two alleles in a population, e.g. the ABO blood group gene has three (A, B and O).
- [ ] L7 Non-nuclear inheritance: genes in mitochondria (and chloroplasts) are passed on mainly through the egg's cytoplasm, so they are inherited from the mother only and don't follow Mendel's rules (see "Nuclear and non-nuclear inheritance").
- [ ] L8 Predictions are probabilities: a ratio describes chances, not exact outcomes, and is reliable only for large numbers of offspring. Human families are small, so a couple with a 1 in 4 risk may have no affected children or several. Mendelian genetics also can't say which child will be affected.

Heredity in the modern world: a balanced judgement (one bullet)
- [ ] J1 Mendelian genetics remains accurate and useful for single-gene traits, in genetic counselling, carrier testing and breeding, but most human traits and common diseases are complex; modern genetics adds polygenic risk, gene–environment interaction, epigenetics and whole-genome sequencing to Mendel's principles rather than replacing them.

## 3. Traps

- No Higher Level marking in this subsection; but don't teach the crosses for linkage, incomplete dominance or sex-linkage here (they are Higher Level and belong to their own subsections). L6 gives one line each.
- "Limitations" means traits or situations where Mendel's Laws don't predict the result, not errors Mendel made. Don't accuse Mendel of fraud or discuss his statistics.
- Polygenic is not the same as "many alleles": polygenic means many genes (L1); multiple alleles means more than two versions of one gene (L6).
- Environmental effects on a phenotype are not inherited through the DNA sequence (except as in L5); don't say the environment changes the genotype.
- How the cystic fibrosis and sickle-cell mutations arise belongs to "Mutations"; ethics of genetic testing belongs to "Ethics of genetic technologies". Name, don't explain.
- Eye colour is not a single-gene trait; don't use brown-dominant-to-blue crosses.
- No diagram cues.
