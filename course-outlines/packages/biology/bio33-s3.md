## 1. Must cover

Why microorganisms are used in industry (one block)
- [ ] W1 Biotechnology is the use of living organisms or their parts (cells, enzymes) to make useful products or provide services. Microorganisms are the most used organisms in biotechnology.
- [ ] W2 Reasons: they reproduce very rapidly (some bacteria divide every 20 minutes in ideal conditions), so large amounts of product are made quickly; they can be grown on cheap nutrients, including waste materials from other industries (e.g. whey from cheese-making, molasses from sugar refining); they can be grown in any climate or season in a controlled vessel taking little space; they carry out a huge range of chemical reactions at low temperatures and normal pressure, saving energy compared with chemical manufacture; they are easy to modify genetically, so they can be made to produce human proteins (see "Genetic engineering"); and many secrete their products into the medium, making them easier to collect.
- [ ] W3 The products are either the cells themselves (e.g. yeast for baking, mycoprotein for food), substances the cells make (e.g. ethanol, antibiotics, enzymes, insulin), or the changes they make to a material (e.g. milk to yogurt, slurry to biogas, sewage treatment).

Bioreactors (one block, Structure and function)
- [ ] R1 A bioreactor (fermenter) is a vessel in which microorganisms (or cells or enzymes) are grown or used on a large scale under carefully controlled conditions to make a product (enzyme bioreactors in "Industrial enzymes").
- [ ] R2 Parts and functions: a stainless-steel vessel (strong, doesn't corrode, easy to sterilise with steam); inlets for sterile nutrient medium and for the inoculum (a pure starter culture); a sterile air supply bubbled in at the bottom through a sparger, giving oxygen for aerobic microorganisms (filtered so no contaminating microorganisms enter); a motor-driven stirrer (impeller) that keeps the cells, nutrients and oxygen evenly mixed and spreads heat; a water jacket (or cooling coils) through which cold water flows to remove the heat released by the microorganisms' respiration, keeping the temperature at the optimum; probes that monitor temperature, pH, dissolved oxygen and foam level, linked to a computer; inlets for acid or alkali to correct the pH; a gas outlet for CO₂ and excess air; and an outlet (harvest) tap at the base to remove the culture and product.
- [ ] R3 Before use, the vessel, pipes and medium are sterilised (with steam) so that no contaminating microorganisms compete with the culture, use up nutrients or spoil the product; this is aseptic technique on an industrial scale.
- [ ] R4 Downstream processing: after the product is made, it is separated from the cells and medium (by filtering or centrifuging), then purified, concentrated and packaged. If the product is inside the cells, the cells are first broken open.
- [ ] R5 Diagram: a labelled bioreactor: vessel, nutrient and inoculum inlets, sterile air inlet and sparger, stirrer and motor, water jacket with cold water in and warm water out, temperature, pH and oxygen probes, acid/alkali inlet, gas outlet, harvest outlet.

Pharmaceutical industry (one block)
- [ ] P1 Antibiotics: chemicals made by microorganisms that kill or inhibit bacteria. Penicillin is made by the fungus Penicillium; Alexander Fleming discovered its effect in 1928, and Howard Florey and Ernst Chain purified it and showed it cured infections from 1940 (history in "Science, scientists and society"; one sentence here). Penicillium is now grown in bioreactors, using strains selected and mutated to give far more penicillin than the original. Streptomycin, tetracycline and many other antibiotics are made by soil bacteria of the genus Streptomyces.
- [ ] P2 Human insulin: the gene for human insulin is inserted into bacteria (E. coli) or yeast, which are grown in bioreactors; the insulin is extracted and purified. It was the first genetically engineered medicine approved for use, in 1982. Before that, people with diabetes injected insulin extracted from the pancreases of pigs and cattle, which was in shorter supply and caused allergic reactions in some people (process in "Genetic engineering").
- [ ] P3 Other medicines made by genetically modified microorganisms: human growth hormone (for children who make too little), made by genetically modified bacteria. No further examples needed.
- [ ] P4 Vaccines made using microorganisms: the hepatitis B vaccine is made by genetically modified yeast, which produces a hepatitis B virus surface protein (the antigen) without the virus. Other vaccines contain killed or weakened microorganisms grown in culture (vaccine types in "Acquired immunity, prevention and treatment").

Medical uses (one block)
- [ ] M1 Diagnosis: the enzyme glucose oxidase, made by the fungus Aspergillus niger, is used in the test strips with which people with diabetes measure their blood glucose; Taq polymerase, a heat-stable enzyme from the bacterium Thermus aquaticus, which lives in hot springs, is used in PCR, which detects pathogens (e.g. in COVID-19 tests) and is used in DNA profiling (see "Generating a DNA profile").
- [ ] M2 Treatment: faecal microbiota transplant, in which gut microorganisms from a healthy donor are placed in a patient's gut, is used to treat repeated infections with the bacterium Clostridioides difficile that antibiotics have failed to clear (the gut microbiome in "The microbiome"); probiotics, live microorganisms taken as food or supplements, are used to try to restore gut microorganisms after antibiotics (evidence is mixed; see "The microbiome").
- [ ] M3 Treatment: the toxin of the bacterium Clostridium botulinum, in tiny purified doses (botulinum toxin, "Botox"), relaxes overactive muscles and is used to treat some muscle spasms and other conditions.
- [ ] M4 Medicines and vaccines are made in the pharmaceutical industry (P items); "medical" uses here are those in diagnosis and treatment in hospitals and clinics. The two overlap; either heading is acceptable in an answer.

Agricultural industry (one block)
- [ ] A1 Nitrogen fixation: seeds of legumes such as clover, peas and beans can be coated with nitrogen-fixing bacteria (Rhizobium) before sowing, so that the crop forms root nodules and needs less nitrogen fertiliser (mutualism in "Biotic factors and relationships").
- [ ] A2 Biological pest control: the soil bacterium Bacillus thuringiensis (Bt) makes a protein toxic to the larvae of certain insects (e.g. caterpillars) but harmless to humans and most other animals; it is sprayed on crops as a biopesticide, and the Bt toxin gene has been put into some genetically modified crops (e.g. Bt maize), which then make their own insecticide.
- [ ] A3 Genetic modification of plants: the soil bacterium Agrobacterium tumefaciens naturally transfers part of a plasmid into plant cells; biotechnologists use it as a vector to insert useful genes into crop plants (see "Genetic engineering").
- [ ] A4 Silage: grass stored airtight (in a pit covered with plastic, or in wrapped bales) is fermented by lactic acid bacteria, which convert its sugars to lactic acid; the low pH stops other microorganisms rotting it, so it keeps as winter feed for cattle and sheep. Silage-making is widespread on Irish farms.
- [ ] A5 Anaerobic digestion: animal slurry, food waste and crop waste are broken down by microorganisms in sealed tanks without oxygen; methane-producing microorganisms (archaea) produce biogas (mainly methane), burned for heat or electricity, and the remaining digestate is spread as fertiliser.
- [ ] A6 Composting: aerobic decomposition of plant waste by bacteria and fungi produces compost, which returns humus and minerals to soil.

Food production industry (one block)
- [ ] F1 Bread: baker's yeast (Saccharomyces cerevisiae) ferments sugars in dough, producing CO₂, which makes the dough rise; the ethanol evaporates during baking (fermentation in "Respiration as a catabolic process").
- [ ] F2 Beer and wine: yeast ferments the sugars in malted barley (beer) or grape juice (wine) to ethanol and CO₂, under anaerobic conditions.
- [ ] F3 Yogurt: milk is pasteurised, cooled, and inoculated with lactic acid bacteria (e.g. Lactobacillus and Streptococcus species), then incubated warm (about 40–45 °C) for several hours; the bacteria ferment lactose to lactic acid, which lowers the pH, making the milk proteins thicken and giving the sour taste; the low pH also preserves it.
- [ ] F4 Cheese: lactic acid bacteria sour the milk, rennet (or chymosin, now mostly made by genetically modified microorganisms; see "Industrial enzymes") clots it into curds, and bacteria and moulds ripen the cheese and develop its flavour; the blue veins of blue cheeses are the mould Penicillium roqueforti.
- [ ] F5 Mycoprotein: the fungus Fusarium venenatum is grown in large continuous fermenters on glucose and mineral salts; its hyphae are harvested, heated and pressed to make mycoprotein (sold as Quorn), a high-protein, high-fibre, low-fat meat substitute.
- [ ] F6 Other foods and ingredients: vinegar, made when bacteria (Acetobacter) oxidise the ethanol in wine, cider or beer to acetic (ethanoic) acid; soy sauce, made by fermenting soya beans and wheat with the mould Aspergillus oryzae; citric acid (a flavouring and preservative in soft drinks) made by the mould Aspergillus niger.

Other industries (one block, one bullet each)
- [ ] O1 Sewage (wastewater) treatment: in the secondary stage, aerobic bacteria and protists in aerated tanks (activated sludge) feed on the organic matter in sewage, so the water can be returned to rivers without causing oxygen depletion; the sludge can be anaerobically digested to biogas.
- [ ] O2 Bioremediation: microorganisms that break down pollutants (e.g. bacteria that digest oil) are used to clean up oil spills and contaminated land.
- [ ] O3 Biofuels: yeast ferments sugars from crops (e.g. sugar cane, maize) to ethanol, which is blended with petrol (see "Climate change and biological strategies").

Discussing the importance of microorganisms in industry (one evaluation block, paired points)
- [ ] D1 Health (for): antibiotics have made most bacterial infections curable and made surgery much safer; insulin, vaccines and other medicines from microorganisms are cheap, pure and available in large amounts; human insulin from microorganisms avoids reliance on animal pancreases and the reactions some people had to animal insulin.
- [ ] D2 Health (against or limits): overuse of antibiotics in medicine and in farm animals has selected for resistant bacteria, so some antibiotics no longer work (see "Emergence and spread of infectious disease"); new antibiotics are expensive and slow to develop.
- [ ] D3 Food and agriculture (for): fermentation preserves food and creates many foods; mycoprotein and other microbial protein need far less land and water than livestock; biopesticides and Rhizobium inoculants reduce the use of chemical pesticides and fertilisers; silage gives winter feed.
- [ ] D4 Food and agriculture (against or limits): genetically modified microorganisms and crops raise concerns about safety, the environment and who controls the technology (ethics in "Ethics of genetic technologies"); insects can evolve resistance to Bt toxin; some people object to GM ingredients.
- [ ] D5 Environment and economy (for): microorganisms turn wastes (whey, slurry, sewage, food waste) into useful products, reducing pollution; biogas and bioethanol are renewable energy; biological processes run at low temperatures, using less energy than chemical manufacture; biotechnology and pharmaceutical manufacturing are major employers and exporters in Ireland.
- [ ] D6 Environment and economy (against or limits): bioreactors need sterile conditions, energy for stirring and cooling, and careful disposal of waste culture; contamination of a batch can waste the whole product; growing crops for biofuel uses land that could grow food.
- [ ] D7 Judgement (one bullet): on balance microorganisms are essential to modern medicine, food production and waste treatment, and their importance is growing as biotechnology replaces fossil-fuel-based chemical processes (cross-cutting themes of health, sustainability and technology; see "The cross-cutting themes"), provided antibiotic use and genetic modification are regulated.

The growth curve and its phases (one block, Higher Level only)
- [ ] G1 A growth curve shows how the number of living cells in a population of microorganisms changes over time when they are grown in a closed system: a fixed volume of medium with no nutrients added and no wastes removed (batch culture, G10). The number of cells is usually plotted as a logarithm (log₁₀ of the number of cells), because numbers rise from thousands to billions; on a log scale, the exponential phase is a straight line.
- [ ] G2 Diagram: a growth curve with log number of living cells on the y-axis and time on the x-axis, with the five phases labelled in order: lag, log (exponential), stationary, decline (death), survival.
- [ ] G3 Lag phase: little or no increase in cell number. The cells are adjusting to the new medium: taking in water and nutrients, switching on genes and making the enzymes needed to use the nutrients present, and growing in size before dividing. It is longer if the new conditions differ greatly from those the cells came from.
- [ ] G4 Log (exponential) phase: the cells divide at their maximum rate, as nutrients are plentiful and wastes are low; the number doubles in each generation time (e.g. every 20–30 minutes), so the population grows exponentially. This is when the cells grow fastest and make most of the products linked to growth (primary metabolites, G11).
- [ ] G5 Stationary phase: the rate of cell division equals the rate of cell death, so the number of living cells stays constant. Causes: nutrients are running out, toxic waste products (e.g. acids, ethanol) build up, oxygen may be short and the pH changes. Many microorganisms make secondary metabolites, such as antibiotics, in this phase (G11). Some bacteria form endospores.
- [ ] G6 Decline (death) phase: the death rate is greater than the rate of division, so the number of living cells falls, because nutrients are exhausted and wastes are at toxic levels. Dead cells may break open.
- [ ] G7 Survival phase: a small number of cells survive for a long time, the number falling only slowly or levelling off. They survive by forming endospores (in some bacteria), by becoming inactive, or by using nutrients released from dead cells that have broken open.

Growth-curve calculations (one block, Higher Level only, with worked examples)
- [ ] G8 Number of cells after n generations of the log phase = starting number × 2ⁿ. Worked example: one bacterium dividing every 20 minutes, for 2 hours: n = 120 ÷ 20 = 6 generations; number = 1 × 2⁶ = 64 cells. With 1000 starting cells and a generation time of 30 minutes, after 3 hours: n = 6, number = 1000 × 64 = 64 000.
- [ ] G9 Working out generation time from a graph or table: in the log phase the number rose from 1 × 10⁴ to 8 × 10⁴ cells in 90 minutes. 8 = 2³, so there were 3 generations in 90 minutes; generation time = 90 ÷ 3 = 30 minutes. Reading a log-scale graph: each step of 1 on a log₁₀ axis is a tenfold increase (e.g. from log 4 = 10 000 cells to log 5 = 100 000 cells).

Batch and continuous culture and increasing yields (one block, Higher Level only)
- [ ] G10 Batch culture: the bioreactor is filled with a fixed amount of sterile medium, inoculated, and left to grow (with temperature, pH and oxygen controlled) for a set time; nothing is added except air, acid or alkali, and nothing removed until the end, when the whole contents are harvested. The vessel is then emptied, cleaned and sterilised for the next batch. The population passes through the lag, log and stationary phases. Advantages: simpler to set up and control; if a batch is contaminated, only that batch is lost; best for products made in the stationary phase. Disadvantages: the vessel is idle while being emptied, cleaned and refilled, and for much of each run the cells are not in the log phase, so output per vessel is lower.
- [ ] G11 Primary and secondary metabolites: primary metabolites are substances the cells make as part of normal growth, mostly in the log phase (e.g. ethanol by yeast, amino acids, enzymes, and the cells themselves as mycoprotein); secondary metabolites are not needed for growth and are made mainly in the stationary phase (e.g. the antibiotic penicillin).
- [ ] G12 Continuous culture: fresh sterile nutrient medium is added continuously, and culture (cells, medium and product) is removed at the same rate, so the volume stays constant. Nutrients never run out and wastes don't build up, so the population is held in the log phase for weeks. Advantages: high output from a smaller vessel; no idle time between batches; a steady, uniform product. Disadvantages: harder to control; if contamination enters, a long run is lost; mutations can arise over many generations; suits only products made during growth. Example: mycoprotein (F5).
- [ ] G13 Using growth curves to increase yields (one bullet each): shorten the lag phase by inoculating with a large volume of actively growing cells taken from the log phase of a culture grown in the same medium and at the same temperature, so the cells don't need to adjust; keep the log phase going as long as possible by holding temperature, pH and oxygen at the optimum with the probes and by supplying fresh nutrients and removing wastes (continuous culture for primary metabolites); harvest at the right phase: primary metabolites at the end of the log phase or continuously, secondary metabolites (e.g. penicillin) in batch culture during the stationary phase, before the decline phase reduces yield; delay the stationary and decline phases by removing toxic products (e.g. ethanol) or adding nutrients gradually (fed-batch culture, a batch to which nutrients are added during the run).

## 3. Traps

- The growth curve is for a closed (batch) culture; in continuous culture the population is held in the log phase and the curve doesn't reach the stationary phase.
- In the stationary phase cells are still dividing and dying, at equal rates; the population hasn't simply "stopped".
- The lag phase is not a time of no activity: the cells are making enzymes and growing.
- The survival phase is the fifth phase named in the specification; include it after decline.
- Penicillin is made by a fungus (Penicillium), not a bacterium; streptomycin by bacteria (Streptomyces). Antibiotics don't work on viruses.
- Taq polymerase and glucose oxidase are enzymes from microorganisms; their use as enzymes (immobilisation, enzymes in detergents) belongs to "Industrial enzymes". Don't describe immobilisation here.
- How a gene is inserted into a plasmid belongs to "Genetic engineering"; P2 and A3 name the use only.
- The steps of fermentation (pyruvate, NADH) belong to "First-stage process"; F1–F2 name the products only.
- Factors affecting growth (temperature, pH, etc.) and aseptic technique belong to "Investigation: growth of microorganisms"; R2–R3 apply them only.
- Ethics of GM organisms belongs to "Ethics of genetic technologies"; antibiotic resistance as a spreading problem to "Emergence and spread of infectious disease". D2 and D4 name them only.
- Keep the growth curve, the calculations, batch and continuous culture and primary/secondary metabolites in the Higher Level blocks.
