## 1. Must cover

Overview (one short block, or the lead of the first block)
- [ ] O1 (assumed) Blood flows in a closed circuit: heart → arteries → arterioles → capillaries → venules → veins → heart. Arteries carry blood away from the heart; veins carry blood towards the heart; capillaries link them inside the organs and are where materials are exchanged with the cells.
- [ ] O2 (assumed) The walls of arteries and veins have three layers (one bullet): an inner lining (endothelium), one cell thick and smooth, which reduces friction; a middle layer of smooth muscle and elastic fibres; and an outer layer of tough connective tissue (collagen fibres) that protects the vessel and stops it over-stretching. The space in the centre, through which blood flows, is the lumen. Capillaries have only the endothelium.

Arteries (one structure-and-function block)
- [ ] A1 Role: carry blood away from the heart to the organs, at high pressure. All arteries carry oxygenated blood except the pulmonary artery (see "Circulation").
- [ ] A2 Thick wall with a thick middle layer of muscle and elastic fibres, to withstand the high pressure of blood pumped from the ventricles without bursting.
- [ ] A3 Elastic fibres: the wall stretches as each surge of blood enters and then recoils between beats, which pushes the blood on, keeps the pressure up between beats and smooths the flow. This stretching is felt as the pulse (see "Heartbeat").
- [ ] A4 Narrow lumen relative to the thickness of the wall, which helps keep the pressure high. In section an artery keeps a round shape, because its thick wall holds it open.
- [ ] A5 No valves (except the semilunar valves where the aorta and pulmonary artery leave the heart, taught in "Investigation: the heart"): the high pressure keeps blood moving forwards. Blood flows fast and in spurts; a cut artery spurts bright red blood.
- [ ] A6 Arterioles: the smallest arteries, leading into capillary networks. Their walls have a high proportion of smooth muscle, which contracts to narrow them (vasoconstriction) or relaxes to widen them (vasodilation), so they control how much blood flows to each organ, e.g. more to the muscles in exercise, more to the skin when hot (see "Homeostasis as a stimulus-response model").

Veins (one structure-and-function block)
- [ ] VN1 Role: carry blood from the organs back towards the heart, at low pressure. All veins carry deoxygenated blood except the pulmonary veins.
- [ ] VN2 Thin wall, with a thin middle layer and few elastic fibres, because the pressure is low; the wall is easily squashed and in section a vein often looks flattened or irregular.
- [ ] VN3 Wide lumen, which reduces resistance to the slow-moving blood.
- [ ] VN4 Valves: pocket-shaped (semilunar) valves, found especially in the veins of the limbs, let blood flow only towards the heart; when blood starts to flow backwards it fills the pockets, which close the vein.
- [ ] VN5 How blood is returned to the heart (one bullet each): contracting skeletal muscles squeeze the veins running between them, pushing blood along, with the valves preventing backflow (the skeletal muscle pump); breathing movements lower the pressure in the thorax during inhalation, drawing blood towards the heart; veins next to arteries are squeezed by the arterial pulse. This is why standing still for a long time makes blood pool in the legs.
- [ ] VN6 Venules: small veins that collect blood from capillary networks and join to form veins.
- [ ] VN7 Diagram: a longitudinal section of a vein showing an open valve (blood flowing towards the heart) and a closed valve (pockets filled, blocking backflow), with arrows; label wall, lumen, valve.

Capillaries (one structure-and-function block)
- [ ] CP1 Role: the exchange of materials between the blood and the body cells. Capillaries form dense networks (capillary beds) in every tissue, so that no cell is far from one.
- [ ] CP2 Wall only one cell thick (endothelium only, no muscle or elastic layers), so the diffusion distance is very short.
- [ ] CP3 Very narrow lumen, only wide enough for red blood cells to pass in single file, which brings them close to the wall; capillaries are so many that their total cross-section is large, so blood flows slowly through them, giving time for exchange.
- [ ] CP4 Leaky: there are small gaps between the endothelial cells, so plasma fluid and small dissolved molecules can pass out (and white blood cells can squeeze through), but red blood cells and large plasma proteins can't.
- [ ] CP5 Pressure falls as blood flows through capillaries, so blood leaving them enters the venules at low pressure; that is why veins have no pulse.

Exchange at the capillaries: tissue fluid (one process block)
- [ ] T1 At the arterial end of a capillary bed, the blood pressure is higher, and it forces plasma (water with dissolved glucose, amino acids, salts and oxygen) out through the capillary wall into the spaces between the cells. This fluid is tissue fluid; it bathes every cell (the internal environment; see "Homeostasis as a stimulus-response model"). Red blood cells and plasma proteins stay in the capillary.
- [ ] T2 Substances pass between tissue fluid and cells by diffusion (and active transport): oxygen and nutrients enter the cells; CO₂, urea and other wastes leave them.
- [ ] T3 At the venous end the pressure is lower, and the plasma proteins left in the blood draw water back in by osmosis, so most of the tissue fluid, now carrying CO₂ and wastes, returns to the capillary. The small amount of excess fluid is drained away by lymph vessels and returned to the blood. One phrase only for lymph.
- [ ] T4 Diagram: a capillary bed between an arteriole and a venule, surrounded by body cells, with arrows for fluid leaving at the arterial end and returning at the venous end, and for O₂ and nutrients into the cells and CO₂ and wastes out.

Comparison: arteries, veins and capillaries (one comparison block; one paired bullet per feature)
- [ ] C1 Direction of flow: arteries away from the heart; veins towards the heart; capillaries link arterioles to venules within an organ.
- [ ] C2 Pressure: high and pulsing in arteries; falls through the capillaries; low and steady in veins.
- [ ] C3 Wall: thick, muscular and elastic in arteries; thin, with little muscle and elastic tissue, in veins; one cell thick (endothelium only) in capillaries.
- [ ] C4 Lumen: narrow relative to the wall in arteries; wide in veins; just wide enough for one red blood cell in capillaries.
- [ ] C5 Valves: none in arteries (apart from at the heart); present in veins; none in capillaries.
- [ ] C6 Speed of flow: fast in arteries; slow in capillaries; slow but steady in veins.
- [ ] C7 Oxygen: arteries usually carry oxygenated blood, veins deoxygenated blood, except in the pulmonary circuit; capillaries exchange it. This is not how they are defined: the definition is by direction of flow (C1).
- [ ] C8 Exchange: no exchange with tissues through the thick walls of arteries and veins; all exchange happens in capillaries.
- [ ] C9 Diagram: transverse sections of an artery, a vein and a capillary drawn side by side: artery round, thick wall (endothelium, thick muscle and elastic layer, outer connective tissue), narrow lumen; vein thin wall (same three layers, thinner middle layer), wide lumen, often flattened; capillary a ring of one or two endothelial cells around a lumen holding one red blood cell. Label each layer and the lumen.

## 3. Traps

- Arteries and veins are defined by the direction of blood flow (away from or towards the heart), not by whether the blood is oxygenated.
- Arteries have no valves along their length; veins do. The semilunar valves at the base of the aorta and pulmonary artery belong to the heart ("Investigation: the heart").
- Veins have thinner walls and wider lumens than arteries; don't say veins have "thick walls to stop backflow". Valves, not walls, stop backflow.
- Capillary walls are one cell thick; don't say "one cell wide" or "one cell membrane thick".
- Don't use the Latin names of the wall layers (tunica intima, media, externa); the plain names are enough.
- Varicose veins, aneurysms and atherosclerosis are not required here; coronary artery disease is a health link in "Heartbeat" only.
- The lymphatic system is not on the specification: T3 names lymph vessels in one phrase only. No lymph nodes or lymph composition; lacteals belong to "Absorption, transport and storage".
- The named vessels of each organ belong to "Circulation"; blood pressure values and measurement to "Heartbeat"; tissue fluid's role in homeostasis to "Homeostasis as a stimulus-response model". Use, don't restate.
- The diagram cues VN7, T4 and C9 are the only ones wanted. No other diagram cues.

## 4. What exams ask (topics only)

- Draw labelled transverse sections of an artery and a vein (and a longitudinal section of a vein showing a valve).
- Give structural differences between arteries, veins and capillaries and relate each to its role.
- Explain how blood is moved through veins back to the heart.
- Explain how the structure of capillaries suits exchange; describe how tissue fluid forms.
- Name a vessel with a given property (e.g. one with valves; one with the highest pressure).

## 5. Practice questions to write

1. 6 marks: "Give two structural differences between an artery and a vein and explain the reason for each." Model answer: two of C3–C5 with A2–A5 and VN2–VN4 reasons (thick muscular elastic wall for high pressure vs thin wall at low pressure; valves in veins to prevent backflow at low pressure).
2. 9 marks: "Draw a labelled longitudinal section of a vein to show a valve, and explain how blood is moved through veins towards the heart." Model answer: VN7 diagram; VN4 and VN5 (skeletal muscle pump with valves; breathing movements).
3. 12 marks: "Explain how the structure of capillaries suits them to their role, and describe how materials are exchanged between the blood and the body cells." Model answer: CP2–CP4, T1–T3; must say walls one cell thick and that tissue fluid forms by pressure at the arterial end and returns by osmosis at the venous end.
