## 1. Must cover

What the outcome asks (one short block, or the lead of the first block)
- [ ] O1 Human activities that affect species diversity include trampling, grazing and mowing, ploughing and reseeding, fertiliser and pesticide use, pollution of water and air, drainage, building, forestry, and the introduction of invasive species. Most reduce diversity by removing habitat or favouring a few tolerant species; some (e.g. light grazing, or cutting a meadow once a year late in summer) can keep diversity high by stopping a few vigorous species taking over.
- [ ] O2 Primary data are collected by the student in the field; secondary data come from someone else, e.g. EPA water-quality reports or a published bird survey (see "Recognising bias and evaluating sources"). Evaluate means judging what the data show, how reliable and valid they are, and what conclusions they can and cannot support.
- [ ] O3 Measures of species diversity used here: species richness (the number of species in a sample), the abundance of each species (number of individuals, or percentage cover for plants), and, at Higher Level, the Simpson's Diversity Index, which combines richness and evenness (see "Simpson's Diversity Index").

Investigation A: trampling and grassland plant diversity — planning (one block)
- [ ] A1 Aim: to investigate the effect of trampling on the species diversity of plants in a grassland. Hypothesis: "Grassland on a heavily trampled path has fewer plant species than nearby untrampled grassland." Null hypothesis: there is no difference.
- [ ] A2 Variables: independent variable is the amount of trampling (on the path vs away from the path, or distance from the centre of the path); dependent variables are the number of plant species per quadrat and the percentage cover or number of each species; controlled variables are the quadrat size (e.g. 0.5 m × 0.5 m), the number of quadrats in each area, the same field (so the same soil type, aspect, slope and management), the same day and observer, and the same identification key.
- [ ] A3 Control (comparison): the untrampled grassland beside the path serves as the comparison area, showing the diversity without trampling. The two areas must be alike except for trampling.
- [ ] A4 Equipment: quadrat (a square frame, e.g. 0.5 m × 0.5 m, often divided into 100 small squares to estimate percentage cover), two measuring tapes, random-number table or app, identification key or chart for grassland plants, recording sheet, pencil; optional: soil-compaction measure (e.g. how far a metal rod or pencil pushes into the soil under the same force), soil thermometer.

Investigation A: method (one block, numbered steps)
- [ ] A5 1. Choose a grassland with a well-used path and similar untrampled grass beside it. 2. In each area, lay two tapes at right angles as axes and use random numbers as coordinates to place each quadrat, so that the sample is not biased by choosing interesting spots (random sampling is detailed in "Surveys"). 3. Place at least 10 quadrats in each area. 4. In each quadrat, identify every plant species with the key and record the number of species; estimate the percentage cover of each species (or count individuals, e.g. daisy and plantain rosettes). 5. Optionally measure soil compaction at each quadrat. 6. Calculate the mean number of species per quadrat for each area, and the mean percentage cover of each species. 7. Repeat on another day, or combine class results, to check repeatability. Alternative design: a line or belt transect running at right angles across the path, sampling a quadrat every metre, to show how diversity changes with distance from the path centre (transects in "Surveys").
- [ ] A6 Safety: wash hands after fieldwork; wear suitable clothing and footwear; watch for traffic, livestock, electric fences and stinging nettles; get the landowner's permission; work in pairs. Ethics: don't uproot plants (identify them in place) and leave the site as found.

Investigation A: results and conclusion (one block)
- [ ] D1 Sample data (invented for practice): number of plant species in each of 10 quadrats. Untrampled: 7, 8, 6, 9, 7, 6, 8, 7, 8, 6 (total 72, mean 7.2, range 6–9). Path: 3, 2, 2, 4, 3, 1, 3, 2, 3, 2 (total 25, mean 2.5, range 1–4). Present as a bar chart of mean species per quadrat for the two areas (categoric independent variable; see "Producing and analysing data").
- [ ] D2 Expected pattern: fewer species on the path; the path is dominated by a few trampling-tolerant plants, such as greater plantain and daisy (low, flat rosettes of leaves pressed to the ground, which are not crushed or broken) and perennial ryegrass (tough, flexible leaves and growth from the base); taller, more delicate plants are found only away from the path. Bare soil is more common on the path.
- [ ] D3 Explanation: trampling crushes and breaks shoots and kills seedlings; it compacts the soil, squeezing out air spaces, so roots get less oxygen and water drains poorly or runs off; only species adapted to these conditions survive, so richness and evenness fall.
- [ ] D4 Conclusion: the results support the hypothesis: the mean number of species was much lower on the path (2.5) than in untrampled grassland (7.2), and the ranges do not overlap, so the difference is unlikely to be due to chance. The investigation shows a link between trampling and lower diversity, which is likely to be causal because the areas were otherwise alike.

Investigation B: organic pollution and river invertebrates (one block)
- [ ] B1 Aim: to compare the diversity of freshwater invertebrates upstream and downstream of a source of organic pollution (e.g. a sewage-treatment outfall or a farm drain). Independent variable: site (upstream vs downstream); dependent variables: number of invertebrate groups and the presence and number of indicator species; controlled: same stream, similar depth, flow rate and stony bed at both sites, same sampling time (e.g. 1 minute of kick-sampling) and net, same day.
- [ ] B2 Method: 1. Sample upstream first, so you don't carry disturbed material downstream. 2. At each site, hold a net on the river bed facing upstream and kick the stones for a fixed time so dislodged animals flow into the net (kick-sampling is detailed in "Collection and identification"). 3. Empty the net into a white tray of river water. 4. Identify and count the animals with a key. 5. Measure dissolved oxygen and water temperature at each site. 6. Repeat three times at each site. 7. Return animals to the river. Safety: never work alone; wear waders or wellingtons and stay in shallow water; avoid fast or deep water; cover cuts and wash hands (risk of infections such as Weil's disease, spread in rat urine); no fieldwork in flood.
- [ ] B3 Indicator species are organisms whose presence, absence or abundance shows the condition of the environment. Clean, well-oxygenated water: stonefly nymphs and many mayfly nymphs (pollution-sensitive). Moderately polluted water: freshwater shrimps, caddis fly larvae, water lice (water hoglouse). Badly polluted, low-oxygen water: bloodworms (red midge larvae), sludge worms (Tubifex) and rat-tailed maggots (pollution-tolerant).
- [ ] B4 Expected results and explanation: upstream, many groups including stonefly and mayfly nymphs; downstream, fewer groups, dominated by large numbers of tolerant species such as sludge worms and bloodworms. Decomposing organic matter is broken down by bacteria, whose aerobic respiration uses up dissolved oxygen, so sensitive species die or leave and tolerant ones, with less competition and plenty of food, become very numerous. Diversity falls even though the total number of animals may rise.
- [ ] B5 Link to secondary data: the Environmental Protection Agency (EPA) rates Irish rivers with a biotic index called the Q-value, from Q5 (high quality, unpolluted) to Q1 (bad quality, seriously polluted), based mainly on the proportion of pollution-sensitive to pollution-tolerant invertebrates. Students can compare their primary data with EPA Q-values published for the same river.

Evaluating the data (one block; applies to both investigations and to secondary data)
- [ ] V1 Reliability: was the sample large enough (number of quadrats or kick samples)? Were the results repeatable on repeated sampling? Do class results agree (reproducibility)? A wide range or an anomalous quadrat reduces confidence (see "Objectivity and data quality").
- [ ] V2 Validity: was only the independent variable different between the sites? Confounding variables could explain the difference instead, e.g. the path might also be drier, more shaded or on a slope; the downstream site might also be deeper or slower, or have a different bed. Measuring abiotic factors at each site helps rule these out.
- [ ] V3 Sampling: random placement avoids bias; quadrat size must suit the plants; sampling at one time of year misses seasonal species.
- [ ] V4 Identification errors: similar species (e.g. grasses, or young invertebrates) may be misidentified, so richness may be under- or over-counted; percentage cover estimates are subjective.
- [ ] V5 Correlation and causation: a field comparison shows an association; it supports cause only if the sites are otherwise alike and a mechanism explains it (e.g. soil compaction, oxygen depletion). Cross-ref "Producing and analysing data".
- [ ] V6 Secondary data (e.g. bird surveys, EPA reports, news articles): check who collected it and why, the sample size and methods, whether the same method was used every year (so trends are comparable), whether raw data are available, and whether the source might be biased (see "Recognising bias and evaluating sources").
- [ ] V7 Improvements: more quadrats or samples; several paths or streams; measure abiotic factors at each sample point; sample in different seasons; use the Simpson's Diversity Index to include evenness (Higher Level).

Data-handling skills block (one Skills block)
- [ ] F1 Worked example (sample data, invented for practice): kick samples, mean number per sample. Upstream: stonefly nymphs 6, mayfly nymphs 14, caddis fly larvae 5, freshwater shrimps 9, sludge worms 0 → 4 groups present, 34 animals. Downstream: stonefly nymphs 0, mayfly nymphs 0, caddis fly larvae 1, freshwater shrimps 2, sludge worms 41 → 3 groups present, 44 animals. Interpretation: downstream has more animals but fewer groups, and is dominated by one tolerant group, so diversity is lower; the loss of stonefly and mayfly nymphs indicates low oxygen. Calculate percentages to compare: sludge worms are 41/44 = 93% of the downstream sample.

## 3. Traps

- Quadrat technique, random coordinates, transects, kick-sampling, pooters, traps and keys are taught in "Investigating an Ecosystem"; here name them and give only the steps above. Don't teach capture–recapture here.
- The Simpson's index calculation belongs to "Simpson's Diversity Index" (Higher Level only); don't calculate it here.
- Biodiversity loss case studies belong to "Biodiversity loss and conservation".
- More individuals does not mean more diversity (F1). Diversity depends on the number of species and evenness.
- The eutrophication process and the nitrogen cycle belong to "Nutrient Cycling"; B4 explains oxygen depletion by decomposing bacteria in one bullet.
- Don't claim that a field comparison proves cause without saying what was controlled (V2, V5).
- The sample data must be labelled as invented sample data, not presented as real results.
- No diagram cues beyond, optionally, a bar chart of D1 (cue: "Diagram: bar chart of mean species per quadrat, path vs untrampled, with labelled axes"). No other diagram cues.

## 4. What exams ask (topics only)

No legacy questions on this outcome. Expect:
- Interpret a table or graph of species counts from disturbed and undisturbed sites; calculate means or percentages.

- Evaluate reliability and validity of field data, and suggest improvements.
