## 1. Must cover

Producers, consumers and decomposers (one block)
- [ ] R1 Producers are autotrophs: organisms that make their own food from simple inorganic substances, mostly by photosynthesis, using light energy (green plants, algae, phytoplankton and photosynthetic bacteria). They are the entry point of energy and matter into every food chain (see "Photosynthesis as an anabolic process").
- [ ] R2 Consumers are heterotrophs that obtain energy and nutrients by eating other organisms. Primary consumers (herbivores) eat producers, e.g. rabbits, slugs, caterpillars, limpets. Secondary consumers eat primary consumers, e.g. thrushes, frogs, crabs. Tertiary consumers eat secondary consumers, e.g. sparrowhawks, foxes, herons. A top (apex) consumer is at the end of a chain and is not normally eaten. Carnivores eat animals; omnivores eat plants and animals, e.g. badgers, foxes, humans.
- [ ] R3 Decomposers (mainly bacteria and fungi) feed on dead organisms and waste, breaking them down by secreting enzymes and absorbing the products (saprophytic nutrition), and so releasing minerals and CO₂ for producers to reuse. Detritivores are animals that eat dead organic matter (detritus), breaking it into smaller pieces that decomposers act on faster, e.g. earthworms, woodlice. Detail of bacteria and fungi in "Bacteria and fungi".

Trophic levels and food chains (one block)
- [ ] T1 A trophic (feeding) level is the position an organism occupies in a food chain, according to how it obtains its energy: producers are trophic level 1, primary consumers level 2, secondary consumers level 3, tertiary consumers level 4.
- [ ] T2 A food chain is a sequence of organisms in which each is eaten by the next, showing the transfer of energy and nutrients from one trophic level to the next. Arrows point from the organism eaten to the one that eats it, the direction of energy flow ("is eaten by").
- [ ] T3 Irish examples, one per ecosystem: grassland: grass → rabbit → fox; hedgerow or woodland: oak leaves → caterpillar → blue tit → sparrowhawk; rocky shore: seaweed → periwinkle → crab → herring gull; freshwater: phytoplankton → water flea → minnow → heron (or pike); sea: phytoplankton → zooplankton → herring → seal.
- [ ] T4 Grazing food chains start with living producers; detritus (decomposer) food chains start with dead organic matter, e.g. dead leaves → earthworm → blackbird. Much of the energy in a woodland passes through detritus chains. One bullet.
- [ ] T5 Food chains rarely have more than four or five trophic levels, because so much energy is lost at each level that too little is left to support a further level (see the energy block).

Food webs (one block)
- [ ] W1 A food web is a set of interlinked food chains in an ecosystem. It is more realistic than a food chain because most organisms eat more than one type of food and are eaten by more than one predator, and an organism may feed at more than one trophic level (an omnivore, or a fox eating rabbits and also beetles).
- [ ] W2 Example grassland/hedgerow web (to draw): grass → rabbit, grass → slug, grass → caterpillar; grass seeds → wood mouse; slug → thrush, caterpillar → thrush; rabbit → fox; wood mouse → fox, wood mouse → barn owl; thrush → sparrowhawk. Decomposers (bacteria, fungi, earthworms) feed on dead material from every level.
- [ ] W3 Food webs show interdependence: a change in one population affects many others, directly through feeding and indirectly through competition for the same food (e.g. the fox and the barn owl both eat wood mice).
- [ ] W4 Diagram: the W2 food web, with arrows pointing towards the consumer, organisms placed in rows by trophic level, and decomposers shown at the side receiving arrows from all levels.

Energy flow and loss at each trophic level (one block)
- [ ] E1 Energy enters ecosystems as light. Producers capture only a small fraction of the light that reaches them (around 1–2%) and store it as chemical energy in organic molecules; the rest is reflected, passes through leaves, or is lost as heat.
- [ ] E2 On average only about 10% of the energy in one trophic level is passed on to and stored in the next (the figure varies, roughly 5–20%). About 90% is lost at each transfer.
- [ ] E3 Where the energy goes at each level: (1) respiration releases energy for movement, growth, active transport and keeping warm, and this ends up as heat lost to the surroundings; birds and mammals lose most because they keep a constant body temperature; (2) not all of an organism is eaten (roots, bark, wood, bones, fur); (3) not all that is eaten is digested and absorbed: undigested food is egested as faeces (e.g. cellulose eaten by herbivores); (4) some is lost in excretory products such as urea. The energy in uneaten parts, faeces and dead bodies passes to decomposers, not to the next level.
- [ ] E4 Energy flows in one direction through an ecosystem and is not recycled: it enters as light and leaves as heat, so an ecosystem needs a continuous input of energy from the Sun.
- [ ] E5 Worked example: if the grass in a field stores 10 000 kJ, the rabbits that eat it store about 1 000 kJ, and the foxes that eat the rabbits store about 100 kJ. A fourth level would store about 10 kJ, too little to support a population. So there are fewer top predators, they often need large territories, and they are especially vulnerable to habitat loss.
- [ ] E6 Consequence for food production (sustainability link): eating plants directly (a shorter chain) makes more efficient use of the energy captured by crops than feeding crops to animals and eating the animals, so a given area of land can feed more people on plant-based food.

Transfer of nutrients between trophic levels (one block)
- [ ] N1 Nutrients (matter) pass between trophic levels when one organism eats another: the carbohydrates, lipids, proteins and nucleic acids of the food are digested, absorbed and built into the consumer's own body (assimilated). The elements involved are carbon, hydrogen and oxygen (in all biomolecules), nitrogen (in proteins and nucleic acids), phosphorus (in DNA, ATP and phospholipids) and minerals such as calcium and iron.
- [ ] N2 The amount of matter passed on also decreases at each level: much of the food eaten is respired (its carbon leaves as CO₂ and its hydrogen as water), egested in faeces, or excreted (nitrogen as urea or ammonia). This is why biomass also decreases up a food chain (see "Pyramids of biomass").
- [ ] N3 Unlike energy, matter is not lost from the ecosystem: decomposers break down dead bodies, faeces and urine, releasing CO₂ and minerals such as nitrate, phosphate and ammonium that producers absorb and reuse, so nutrients cycle. Detail in "The carbon cycle" and "The nitrogen cycle".
- [ ] N4 Contrast in one bullet: energy flows (one way, lost as heat); nutrients cycle (reused again and again).
- [ ] N5 Persistent substances that are not broken down or excreted, such as the insecticide DDT and mercury, pass up the food chain with the food and build up in the bodies of organisms at each level, reaching the highest concentrations in top consumers (biomagnification). This is how DDT thinned the eggshells of birds of prey (case in "Science, scientists and society").
- [ ] N6 Diagram: a flow diagram of one food chain with energy arrows getting narrower at each level, heat-loss arrows from every level, and a separate loop of nutrient arrows from all levels to decomposers and back to producers.

Using food webs to explain and predict the impact of change (one block, with a worked prediction)
- [ ] P1 A food chain or web is a model: it allows predictions of what happens when one population changes. Method: 1. find the organisms that eat the changed species (its predators), 2. find what it eats (its food), 3. find its competitors, 4. predict the direction of each change, giving reasons, 5. note other effects (e.g. on plants and habitat).
- [ ] P2 Worked prediction using the W2 web: if disease (e.g. myxomatosis) kills most rabbits: (a) grass is grazed less and grows taller, providing more food and cover for slugs, caterpillars and wood mice; (b) foxes lose their main prey, so they eat more wood mice and the eggs and chicks of ground-nesting birds, and fox numbers may fall; (c) wood mice decline from the extra fox predation, so barn owls, which compete with foxes for wood mice, have less food. Real outcomes depend on how flexible each predator's diet is.
- [ ] P3 Removing a top predator can cause a trophic cascade, a chain of effects down the food web: in Ireland, with no wolves, deer numbers increase, they browse young trees, and woodland can't regenerate, which affects every species that depends on the woodland (see "Carrying capacity and limiting factors").
- [ ] P4 Adding a species also spreads through a web: an invasive filter-feeder such as the zebra mussel removes phytoplankton at the base of a lake food web, reducing food for zooplankton and the fish that eat them (case in "Biodiversity loss and conservation"; one phrase here).
- [ ] P5 Limitations of food chains and webs as models: they simplify real feeding (many links are left out, diets change with season and age), they usually show no numbers or amounts of energy (pyramids do this), and decomposers are often left out. (One bullet; "Models" gives the general idea.)

## 3. Traps

- Arrows point to the eater (direction of energy flow), not from predator to prey.
- Energy is lost as heat, not "used up" or destroyed; energy is not recycled, nutrients are.
- The 10% figure is an approximation; give it as "about 10%".
- Producers are trophic level 1, not 0. Decomposers are not given a trophic level number.
- Pyramids of numbers belong to "Pyramids of numbers"; pyramids of biomass to "Pyramids of biomass" (Higher Level). N2 refers to biomass in one phrase.
- The carbon and nitrogen cycles (photosynthesis, respiration, nitrogen fixation, nitrification, denitrification) belong to "Nutrient Cycling"; N3 states that decomposers release minerals and CO₂, no more.
- Symbiosis, competition and predator–prey adaptations belong to "Biotic factors and relationships".
- Don't call the trophic cascade in P3 proven for Ireland in figures; describe the mechanism only.
- Pyramids of energy are not on the specification; don't teach them here.
- Diagram cues are W4 and N6 only. No other diagram cues.

## 4. What exams ask (topics only)

- Define producer, consumer, decomposer, trophic level, food chain and food web.
- Construct a food chain or web from given information; assign trophic levels.
- Explain why food chains rarely have more than four or five links; explain where energy is lost.
