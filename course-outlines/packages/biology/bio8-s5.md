## 1. Must cover

What vitamins are (one block)
- [ ] V1 Vitamins are organic compounds needed in very small amounts for normal metabolism, growth and health. The body cannot make them, or cannot make enough, so they must come from the diet. They supply no energy and are not used to build tissues in bulk.
- [ ] V2 Role in biological processes: many vitamins act as coenzymes or help enzymes work (e.g. the B vitamins in the release of energy from food in respiration); others are needed to make particular substances (vitamin C for collagen), act as antioxidants protecting cells from damage (vitamins C and E), or control the absorption of minerals (vitamin D and calcium).
- [ ] V3 A lack of a vitamin causes a deficiency disease with characteristic symptoms; the disease is cured or prevented by supplying the vitamin.
- [ ] V4 Vitamins are grouped by what they dissolve in. Water-soluble: vitamin C and the B group. Fat-soluble: vitamins A, D, E and K.

The biological significance of solubility (one comparison block, paired points)
- [ ] S1 Absorption: water-soluble vitamins dissolve in the watery contents of the gut and are absorbed directly into the blood. Fat-soluble vitamins dissolve in dietary fat and are absorbed along with it, so a very low-fat diet, or a condition that prevents fat absorption, can cause a deficiency of A, D, E or K.
- [ ] S2 Transport: water-soluble vitamins travel dissolved in the blood plasma; fat-soluble vitamins are carried with lipids.
- [ ] S3 Storage: excess water-soluble vitamins are excreted by the kidneys in urine and most are not stored, so they must be eaten regularly (ideally daily) and deficiency develops within weeks or months. Fat-soluble vitamins are stored in the liver and fatty tissue, so the body can draw on reserves and they need not be eaten every day.
- [ ] S4 Toxicity: because excess water-soluble vitamins are excreted, overdose is rare. Fat-soluble vitamins can build up in the liver to harmful levels if large supplements are taken (e.g. too much vitamin A is toxic, which is why pregnant women are advised not to eat liver or take vitamin A supplements).
- [ ] S5 Cooking: water-soluble vitamins leach out of food into cooking water, and vitamin C is also destroyed by heat and by long storage, so vegetables lose vitamin C when boiled for a long time; steaming, microwaving or eating them raw keeps more. Fat-soluble vitamins are not lost into cooking water.

Vitamin C, the water-soluble vitamin (one block)
- [ ] C1 Vitamin C (ascorbic acid) is water-soluble.
- [ ] C2 Roles: needed to make collagen, the protein of connective tissue in skin, gums, blood vessel walls, bones and scar tissue; helps wounds heal; is an antioxidant; helps the gut absorb iron from plant foods; supports the immune system.
- [ ] C3 Sources: citrus fruits (oranges, lemons, limes, grapefruit), kiwi fruit, strawberries, blackcurrants, peppers, broccoli, tomatoes, potatoes. Best eaten fresh and raw (S5).
- [ ] C4 Deficiency disease: scurvy. Without vitamin C, collagen is not made properly, so connective tissue weakens. Symptoms: swollen, bleeding gums and loose teeth; easy bruising and bleeding under the skin; wounds that heal slowly or reopen; tiredness and aching joints. Untreated, it can be fatal. It is cured quickly by vitamin C.
- [ ] C5 History (one bullet, Health and Nature of Science link): scurvy killed many sailors on long voyages without fresh food. In 1747 James Lind, a Scottish naval surgeon, gave different treatments to groups of sailors with scurvy and found that those given oranges and lemons recovered: an early controlled trial.

Vitamin D, the fat-soluble vitamin (one block)
- [ ] D1 Vitamin D (calciferol) is fat-soluble.
- [ ] D2 Role: it increases the absorption of calcium (and phosphate) from food in the small intestine, so it is needed for the formation and hardening of bones and teeth. It also helps muscle function and the immune system.
- [ ] D3 Sources: made in the skin when it is exposed to ultraviolet light in sunlight; oily fish (e.g. salmon, mackerel, sardines), fish-liver oils such as cod-liver oil, egg yolk, liver, and fortified foods such as some milks, spreads and breakfast cereals.
- [ ] D4 Deficiency disease: rickets in children. Bones don't harden (they stay soft and weak), so the leg bones bend under the body's weight, giving bowed legs; growth is poor and bones are painful. In adults the equivalent condition is osteomalacia (softening of the bones). Low vitamin D also raises the risk of osteoporosis in later life.
- [ ] D5 People at risk: those who get little sunlight (living at northern latitudes such as Ireland, where winter sunlight is too weak for the skin to make vitamin D; staying indoors; covering the skin), people with darker skin living at high latitudes, older people, and babies. Irish health advice recommends a vitamin D supplement for babies in their first year.

Other vitamins students may choose (one block, one bullet each)
- [ ] O1 Vitamin A (retinol), fat-soluble: needed for vision in dim light and for healthy skin and linings. Sources: liver, dairy produce, eggs, oily fish; orange and dark green vegetables (e.g. carrots) contain beta-carotene, which the body converts to vitamin A. Deficiency: night blindness.
- [ ] O2 Vitamin K, fat-soluble: needed for blood clotting. Sources: green leafy vegetables; also made by bacteria in the large intestine. Newborn babies have little, so they are given vitamin K soon after birth to prevent bleeding.
- [ ] O3 Vitamin E, fat-soluble: an antioxidant that protects cell membranes. Sources: vegetable oils, nuts, seeds. No named deficiency disease is needed.
- [ ] O4 B-group vitamins, water-soluble: help enzymes release energy from food. Vitamin B1 (thiamine) deficiency causes beriberi; vitamin B3 (niacin) deficiency causes pellagra. Sources: wholegrain cereals, meat, eggs, dairy produce, beans.
- [ ] O5 Folic acid (folate, a B vitamin) and vitamin B12 are needed to make red blood cells; a lack of either causes a form of anaemia. B12 is found naturally only in animal foods, so vegans need fortified foods or supplements. Women are advised to take folic acid before and in early pregnancy, as it reduces the risk of neural tube defects such as spina bifida in the baby.

Health and technology link (one bullet)
- [ ] T1 Food fortification (adding vitamins to foods such as breakfast cereals and milk) and supplements are used to prevent deficiency diseases in groups at risk.

## 3. Traps

- Rickets is a vitamin D deficiency disease (also linked to low calcium); scurvy is vitamin C. Don't swap them.
- Vitamin D is the one vitamin the body makes in quantity (in skin, with sunlight); it is still classed as a vitamin.
- Don't say vitamins provide energy; the B vitamins help release energy from food but contain none usable themselves.
- Don't say all water-soluble vitamins are never stored: write "most are not stored" (B12 is stored in the liver).
- Don't say carrots contain vitamin A itself; they contain beta-carotene, which is converted to it.
- Minerals are inorganic (see "Minerals"); vitamins are organic.
- Absorption of digested food belongs to "The Digestive System"; S1 is about solubility only.
- No diagram cues: vitamins need none.

## 4. What exams ask (topics only)

- Name a water-soluble and a fat-soluble vitamin; give a source, a role and the deficiency disease of each.
- Explain how a named vitamin-deficiency disease may be recognised (symptoms).
- Explain the significance of solubility: storage, excretion, toxicity, loss in cooking (new emphasis on this specification).
