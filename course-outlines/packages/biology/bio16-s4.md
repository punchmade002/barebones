## 1. Must cover

What cancer is (one block)
- [ ] D1 Cancer is a group of diseases in which some body cells lose control of mitosis: they divide too often (the rate of division is abnormal) and too many times (the number of divisions is not limited), and they can invade nearby tissues and spread to other parts of the body.
- [ ] D2 There are over 200 kinds of cancer, named after the organ or tissue where they start (e.g. lung, breast, skin, bowel, blood). Main groups: carcinomas start in epithelial tissues that line or cover organs (e.g. most lung, breast and bowel cancers); sarcomas in bone, muscle or connective tissue; leukaemias in the blood-forming cells of the bone marrow; lymphomas in the lymphatic system.
- [ ] D3 Cancer starts from a single cell whose DNA has changed (mutated); all the cancer cells in a tumour are descended from it by mitosis.

How normal cell division is regulated (one block)
- [ ] N1 Normal cells divide only when they receive signals to divide (growth factors from other cells) and stop when signals tell them to stop. Checkpoints in the cell cycle halt the cycle if the DNA is damaged or not copied correctly.
- [ ] N2 Two groups of genes control this. Proto-oncogenes code for proteins that stimulate the cell cycle (the "accelerator"), e.g. growth factor receptors. Tumour suppressor genes code for proteins that stop the cycle at checkpoints (the "brakes"), repair damaged DNA, or make a badly damaged cell destroy itself.
- [ ] N3 Apoptosis is programmed cell death: a damaged or unwanted cell breaks itself down in a controlled way, without harming neighbouring cells. It removes cells with damaged DNA before they can divide.
- [ ] N4 Example of a tumour suppressor gene: TP53, which codes for the protein p53. When DNA is damaged, p53 stops the cell cycle so that the DNA can be repaired, or triggers apoptosis if the damage is too great; it is called the "guardian of the genome". TP53 is mutated in about half of all human cancers.
- [ ] N5 Contact inhibition: normal cells in a tissue (and in a culture dish) stop dividing when they touch neighbouring cells, so they form a single layer.
- [ ] N6 Most normal human cells can divide only a limited number of times before they stop; the protective caps at the ends of their chromosomes (telomeres) shorten with each division.

How control is lost (one process block)
- [ ] L1 A mutation in a proto-oncogene can turn it into an oncogene, which is permanently "switched on" and keeps telling the cell to divide even with no signal, like an accelerator stuck down.
- [ ] L2 A mutation that inactivates a tumour suppressor gene removes the brakes: damaged cells keep dividing instead of stopping, being repaired or dying by apoptosis. Usually both copies (alleles) of a tumour suppressor gene must be inactivated before control is lost.
- [ ] L3 One mutation is usually not enough: several mutations in different control genes must build up in the same cell, often over many years. This is why the risk of most cancers rises with age, and why anything that increases the rate of mutation raises the risk.
- [ ] L4 Once one cell has lost control, every daughter cell inherits the same mutations; as they divide faster and faster, further mutations arise, and cells that divide fastest or survive best take over (the cancer cells become more abnormal over time).
- [ ] L5 The immune system destroys many abnormal cells before they form a tumour; people whose immune system is weakened have a higher risk of some cancers. One bullet (immunity is in "Immunity").

Characteristics of cancer cells (one comparison block: normal cell vs cancer cell, paired points)
- [ ] K1 Division: normal cells divide only when signalled and at a controlled rate; cancer cells divide rapidly and continuously without signals and ignore signals to stop.
- [ ] K2 Lifespan: normal cells divide a limited number of times and die by apoptosis when damaged; cancer cells avoid apoptosis and many keep their telomeres long (using the enzyme telomerase), so they can divide indefinitely ("immortal" cells).
- [ ] K3 Contact inhibition: normal cells stop at contact; cancer cells lack it and pile up into a mass.
- [ ] K4 Specialisation: normal cells are specialised (differentiated) for a job; cancer cells are unspecialised (poorly differentiated) and don't carry out the tissue's function.
- [ ] K5 Appearance under the microscope: cancer cells vary in size and shape and have large, irregular, darkly staining nuclei, often with abnormal numbers of chromosomes; many are seen dividing. This is how cancer is diagnosed from a biopsy or smear (see "Microscopy").
- [ ] K6 Blood supply: cancer cells can release signals that make new blood vessels grow into the tumour (angiogenesis), supplying the oxygen and nutrients needed for further growth.
- [ ] K7 Attachment: normal cells stick to their neighbours and stay in place; cancer cells can lose this adhesion, break away and move.
- [ ] K8 A famous example of immortal cancer cells: HeLa cells, taken in 1951 from a cervical cancer of Henrietta Lacks at Johns Hopkins Hospital in Baltimore, USA, without her knowledge or consent. They were the first human cells to keep dividing indefinitely in the laboratory and are still used in research worldwide, including in developing the polio vaccine. Her case is a well-known example of the ethics of consent in research.

Tumours: benign and malignant (one comparison block)
- [ ] T1 A tumour is an abnormal mass of cells produced by uncontrolled division.
- [ ] T2 Benign tumour: grows slowly; usually enclosed in a capsule; does not invade surrounding tissue or spread; cells look fairly normal; usually removable by surgery and not life-threatening, though it can be dangerous if it presses on a vital organ such as the brain. Examples: most moles; warts. A benign tumour is not cancer.
- [ ] T3 Malignant tumour (cancer): grows quickly; has no capsule; invades and destroys surrounding tissue; can spread around the body; cells are abnormal. Malignant tumours are cancers.
- [ ] T4 Comparison table with rows: rate of growth; capsule; invasion; spread; appearance of cells; danger.

Spread: metastasis (one process block, numbered)
- [ ] S1 1. A cell mutates and divides without control, producing a clone of abnormal cells. 2. Further mutations and division form a small tumour in the tissue of origin (the primary tumour). 3. Angiogenesis supplies the tumour with blood, so it grows larger. 4. Cancer cells invade the surrounding tissue. 5. Some cells break away, enter blood vessels or lymph vessels, and are carried around the body. 6. They leave the vessels in another organ and divide there to form secondary tumours (metastases).
- [ ] S2 Metastasis is the spread of cancer from the primary tumour to form secondary tumours elsewhere. Secondary tumours contain the same type of cells as the primary tumour: e.g. breast cancer that spreads to the bones is still breast cancer cells, not bone cancer. Metastasis makes cancer much harder to treat, which is why early detection matters (see "Causes, prevention and treatment of cancer").
- [ ] S3 How tumours harm the body: they press on and damage organs; block tubes such as the airways, gut or blood vessels; destroy normal tissue and stop it working (e.g. leukaemia cells crowd out normal blood-forming cells in the bone marrow, causing anaemia and infections); and use up the body's nutrients, causing weight loss.
- [ ] S4 Diagram: a sequence from normal tissue (a single neat layer of cells) → one mutated cell → a mass of abnormal cells (primary tumour) with a blood vessel growing into it → cells invading and entering a blood vessel → a secondary tumour in another organ. Label: normal cells, cancer cell with large nucleus, primary tumour, blood vessel, invasion, metastasis, secondary tumour.

## 3. Traps

- Cancer is not caused by "cells dividing too fast" alone: the key is loss of control over both the rate and the number of divisions, plus invasion. A benign tumour is uncontrolled growth but is not cancer.
- Proto-oncogenes are normal, necessary genes; only the mutated form is an oncogene. Don't say "people with oncogenes" for everyone with proto-oncogenes.
- A mutated oncogene acts like a stuck accelerator (gain of function); a mutated tumour suppressor gene is like a failed brake (loss of function). Don't swap them.
- Secondary tumours are made of cells of the primary cancer type.
- Cancer is not contagious; some infections raise the risk of some cancers (see "Causes, prevention and treatment of cancer").
- Don't name carcinogens, risk factors, screening, treatments, vaccines or BRCA genes here; they belong to "Causes, prevention and treatment of cancer". K8 is the only named case here.
- Checkpoints and the stages of the cycle are introduced in "The cell cycle"; N1 refers to them without repeating them. How mutations happen belongs to "Mutations" (Higher Level).
