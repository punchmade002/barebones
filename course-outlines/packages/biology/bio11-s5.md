## 1. Must cover

Background the cross rests on (one block)
- [ ] B1 (assumed) Each body cell carries two alleles of each gene, one on each chromosome of a homologous pair, and each gamete carries only one, because meiosis separates the two chromosomes of each pair; at fertilisation the zygote again has two alleles, one from each parent. One bullet: homologous chromosomes are in "Chromosome structure and genes", meiosis in "Mitosis and meiosis" (Cell Division and Cancer), and allele, homozygous, heterozygous, pure-breeding and carrier in "Key concepts of information transfer".
- [ ] B2 What the terms mean for gametes (the step every cross depends on): a homozygous individual (TT or tt) makes only one type of gamete for that gene (T, or t); a heterozygous individual (Tt) makes two types, T and t, in equal numbers (half of its gametes each). True-breeding is another name for pure-breeding (homozygous): crossed with one like itself, it always gives offspring with the same trait.
- [ ] B3 A monohybrid cross is a cross that follows the inheritance of one trait (one gene with two alleles). "Unlinked" means the gene is considered on its own, without other genes on the same chromosome affecting the result (linkage is in "Linkage").
- [ ] B4 Generations: P (parental generation) are the parents crossed; F1 (first filial generation) are their offspring; F2 (second filial generation) are the offspring of crossing F1 individuals with each other. "First generation" in the outcome means the offspring of the cross being modelled.

Symbols and the standard layout (one Skills block, the core of the subsection)
- [ ] L1 Choosing symbols: use the first letter of the dominant trait, capital for the dominant allele and lower case for the recessive allele (T = tall, t = dwarf). Use the letters a question gives. Avoid letters whose capital and lower case look alike when handwritten (C/c, O/o, S/s, W/w, P/p, K/k, V/v, X/x, Z/z). Always state a key first: "T = allele for tall, t = allele for dwarf".
- [ ] L2 The layout, one line each, in this order: 1. Key. 2. Parents' phenotypes (e.g. tall × dwarf). 3. Parents' genotypes (e.g. TT × tt). 4. Gametes: each different allele each parent can pass on, written once and usually circled (T from TT; t from tt; T and t from Tt). 5. Punnett square (or fertilisation lines joining every gamete of one parent to every gamete of the other). 6. Offspring genotypes, with their ratio. 7. Offspring phenotypes, with their ratio. Marks are given for the correct parental genotypes, gametes, offspring genotypes and offspring phenotypes, so show every line.
- [ ] L3 The Punnett square: a grid with one parent's gametes across the top and the other parent's gametes down the side; each box gets the allele from its column and the allele from its row, the dominant allele written first (Tt, not tT). Each box is one equally likely outcome of fertilisation, so with two gametes from each parent each box is a 1 in 4 (25%) chance.
- [ ] L4 Diagram: a 2 × 2 Punnett square for Tt × Tt: gametes T and t across the top (labelled "gametes of parent 1"), T and t down the side ("gametes of parent 2"); boxes TT, Tt, Tt, tt; each box labelled with its phenotype (tall, tall, tall, dwarf).
- [ ] L5 Ratios and probability: a ratio is a prediction of the proportions expected, not an exact count. Each fertilisation is independent, so in a family where the first child is affected, the chance for the next child is unchanged. Ratios can be given as a ratio (3 : 1), a fraction (3/4), a percentage (75%) or a probability (0.75, or "a 3 in 4 chance"). Observed results come close to the predicted ratio only with large numbers of offspring (e.g. hundreds of pea plants), not in a small human family.

Worked example 1: heterozygous × heterozygous (one block, every line of L2 shown)
- [ ] W1 Pea plants: tall (T) is dominant to dwarf (t). Cross two heterozygous tall plants. Key: T = tall, t = dwarf. Parents' phenotypes: tall × tall. Parents' genotypes: Tt × Tt. Gametes: T, t × T, t. Punnett square: TT, Tt / Tt, tt. Offspring genotypes: 1 TT : 2 Tt : 1 tt. Offspring phenotypes: 3 tall : 1 dwarf (75% tall, 25% dwarf). Of the tall offspring, 1/3 are homozygous (TT) and 2/3 heterozygous (Tt).
- [ ] W2 The genotype ratio (1 : 2 : 1) differs from the phenotype ratio (3 : 1) because TT and Tt look the same: the dominant allele masks the recessive one. A recessive trait reappears in offspring of parents who both show the dominant trait only if both parents are heterozygous.

Worked example 2: homozygous × homozygous (one block)
- [ ] W3 True-breeding tall × true-breeding dwarf. Parents' genotypes: TT × tt. Gametes: T × t. Every offspring is Tt (heterozygous). Offspring phenotype: all (100%) tall. This is Mendel's P → F1 cross; crossing these F1 plants together (Tt × Tt) gives the F2 result of W1, in which the dwarf trait reappears. (Mendel's own figures are in "Mendel's Laws".)

Worked example 3: heterozygous × homozygous recessive (one block)
- [ ] W4 Tall heterozygous × dwarf. Parents' genotypes: Tt × tt. Gametes: T, t × t. Punnett square (2 × 1, or 2 × 2 with t written twice): Tt, tt. Offspring genotypes: 1 Tt : 1 tt. Offspring phenotypes: 1 tall : 1 dwarf (50% each).

All six possible monohybrid crosses (one bullet each, as a summary table in bullet form)
- [ ] S1 TT × TT → all TT → all tall. TT × Tt → 1 TT : 1 Tt → all tall. TT × tt → all Tt → all tall. Tt × Tt → 1 TT : 2 Tt : 1 tt → 3 tall : 1 dwarf. Tt × tt → 1 Tt : 1 tt → 1 tall : 1 dwarf. tt × tt → all tt → all dwarf. Write this as six bullets, one per cross.
- [ ] S2 Reading backwards from results: offspring in a 3 : 1 ratio means both parents are heterozygous; a 1 : 1 ratio means one parent is heterozygous and the other homozygous recessive; any offspring showing the recessive trait means each parent carries at least one recessive allele.

The test cross (one block)
- [ ] T1 (assumed) A test cross finds the unknown genotype of an organism showing the dominant trait (TT or Tt?) by crossing it with a homozygous recessive (tt) organism. The tt parent can give only t, so each offspring's phenotype shows directly which allele came from the unknown parent.
- [ ] T2 Results: if all offspring show the dominant trait (all tall), the unknown parent is almost certainly TT (TT × tt → all Tt). If any offspring show the recessive trait (about half dwarf), the unknown parent is Tt (Tt × tt → 1 : 1). Many offspring are needed to be confident that a TT result is not chance.

Worked example 4: a human recessive disorder (one block)
- [ ] H1 Cystic fibrosis is caused by a recessive allele of a gene on an autosome (a chromosome that is not a sex chromosome). Key: F = normal allele, f = cystic fibrosis allele. Parents: two unaffected carriers, Ff × Ff. Gametes: F, f × F, f. Offspring genotypes: 1 FF : 2 Ff : 1 ff. Offspring phenotypes: 3 unaffected : 1 with cystic fibrosis. So each child has a 1 in 4 (25%) chance of having cystic fibrosis, a 1 in 2 (50%) chance of being a carrier, and a 1 in 4 chance of being neither. Why the mutation causes the disease belongs to "Mutations"; its use in genetic counselling is in "Benefits and limitations of Mendelian genetics".

## 3. Traps

- Don't redefine allele, dominant, recessive, genotype, phenotype, homozygous, heterozygous or carrier (all in "Key concepts of information transfer"); B1 and B2 use them.
- Gametes carry one allele of each gene, never two: the gametes of Tt are T and t, not "Tt". Don't write a gamete twice for a homozygous parent's line (TT gives T).
- Genotype is the alleles (Tt); phenotype is the trait (tall). Students often give a genotype when the phenotype is asked, or a genotype ratio for a phenotype ratio.
- Dominant does not mean common, better or more likely to be inherited.
- A 3 : 1 ratio is a probability, not a guarantee that a family of four has exactly one affected child.
- Mendel's Laws, Mendel's own experiments and his figures belong to "Mendel's Laws"; mention Mendel only as the source of the pea example.
- Incomplete dominance (where the heterozygote is intermediate) belongs to "Incomplete dominance"; this subsection assumes complete dominance throughout.
- Codominance and multiple alleles (e.g. ABO blood groups) are not named on the specification; don't teach crosses with them. Blood groups are mentioned as a limitation only in "Benefits and limitations of Mendelian genetics".
- Two-gene crosses belong to "Dihybrid crosses"; sex-chromosome crosses belong to "Sex determination" and "Sex-linked traits".
- Pedigree charts are taught in "Sex-linked traits"; not here.
- Diagram cues: only L4. No other diagram cues; write the other Punnett squares as text in the order of L3 (row by row).

## 4. What exams ask (topics only)

- Use homozygous, heterozygous, genotype and phenotype correctly in a cross (definitions in "Key concepts of information transfer").
- Using a genetic cross, show the genotypes and phenotypes of the offspring of a named monohybrid cross and give the ratio or percentage.
- Explain how a test cross shows whether an organism with the dominant phenotype is homozygous or heterozygous.
- Give the probability that a child of two carriers of a recessive disorder will be affected.

## 5. Practice questions to write

1. 6 marks: "Give the gametes produced by pea plants with the genotypes RR, Rr and rr, and explain why a heterozygous plant produces two types of gamete in equal numbers." Model answer: B2 (RR → R; rr → r; Rr → R and r) with B1 (meiosis separates the homologous chromosomes, one allele per gamete).
2. 12 marks: "In pea plants, round seed (R) is dominant to wrinkled seed (r). Two plants heterozygous for seed shape are crossed. Using a genetic cross, give the genotypes and phenotypes of the offspring and the phenotype ratio." Model answer: every line of L2: parents round × round, Rr × Rr, gametes R, r × R, r, Punnett square RR, Rr, Rr, rr, genotypes 1 RR : 2 Rr : 1 rr, phenotypes 3 round : 1 wrinkled. Must not give gametes as "Rr".
3. 9 marks: "A tall pea plant may be TT or Tt. Describe a cross that would identify its genotype and explain how the results would be interpreted." Model answer: T1–T2, with both crosses (TT × tt → all tall; Tt × tt → 1 tall : 1 dwarf).
