# Package: bio6-s1 — Viruses

- **loId:** `bio6-s1` · **chapterId:** `bio6` · **syllabusRef:** 1.1.2
- **Syllabus:** "structure and functions of a virus including constituents (DNA or RNA,
  protein coat)." Outcome 2: "discuss the difficulty of defining viruses, their economic and
  medical importance." Ordinary and Higher level. "Discuss" needs the evidence on both sides
  (living vs non-living) and a conclusion; importance needs harmful and useful examples.
- **Prepared by:** a higher model, 2026-10-22.

## 1. Must cover

Structure and functions of the parts (structure-and-function block)
- [ ] V1 A virus is a tiny, non-cellular particle made of nucleic acid inside a protein coat. It can reproduce only inside a living host cell, so it is an obligate intracellular parasite.
- [ ] V2 Nucleic acid core: either DNA or RNA, never both. It carries the genes (instructions) for making new virus particles. It may be single- or double-stranded.
- [ ] V3 Protein coat (capsid): made of many repeating protein subunits (capsomeres). It protects the nucleic acid, and its proteins attach to specific receptor molecules on the surface of the host cell. This is why each virus infects only particular species and particular cell types (host specificity).
- [ ] V4 Envelope (some viruses only, e.g. influenza virus, HIV): an outer lipid membrane taken from the host cell's membrane as the virus leaves, studded with viral proteins (spikes) that help the virus attach to and enter new cells.
- [ ] V5 Viruses have no cytoplasm, no ribosomes, no cell membrane of their own and almost no enzymes, so they must use the host cell's ribosomes, enzymes, ATP and raw materials to make copies of themselves. How they do it is in "Viruses: replication" (Immunity); one sentence here.
- [ ] V6 Size: most viruses are about 20–300 nm across, much smaller than a bacterium (about 1–10 µm). They pass through filters that trap bacteria and can be seen only with an electron microscope.
- [ ] V7 Shapes: rod-shaped or helical (e.g. tobacco mosaic virus); many-sided or polyhedral (e.g. adenovirus); spherical and enveloped (e.g. influenza virus); complex, with a head and tail (bacteriophages, which infect bacteria).
- [ ] V8 Diagram: a bacteriophage labelled head (protein coat), nucleic acid (DNA) inside the head, tail and tail fibres; and an enveloped virus labelled envelope, spike proteins, protein coat and RNA.

The difficulty of defining viruses (comparison block: living vs non-living, then a conclusion)
- [ ] D1 Features of living things that viruses show: they contain genetic material (DNA or RNA) using the same genetic code as cells; they reproduce, making many copies of themselves (but only inside a host cell); they show heredity, passing genes to new viruses; they mutate and evolve by natural selection, e.g. new strains of influenza appear every year, so the flu vaccine is updated; their structure is highly organised.
- [ ] D2 Features of living things that viruses lack: they are not made of cells (acellular), so they don't fit the cell theory; they have no metabolism of their own (no respiration, no nutrition, no excretion); they don't grow; they don't respond to stimuli; they can't reproduce outside a host cell; outside a host they are inert particles.
- [ ] D3 Some viruses can be crystallised like a chemical and remain infectious afterwards: Wendell Stanley crystallised tobacco mosaic virus in 1935.
- [ ] D4 Conclusion: viruses are usually described as on the border between living and non-living. Most biologists don't count them as living organisms, so they are not placed in any of the three domains or six kingdoms (see "Classification"). Others argue that a virus is "alive" only while it is inside a host cell, using the cell's machinery.

Medical importance (one block)
- [ ] M1 Viruses cause many human diseases: common cold (rhinoviruses), influenza (flu), measles, mumps, chickenpox, COVID-19 (caused by SARS-CoV-2), AIDS (caused by HIV, which destroys helper T cells of the immune system), hepatitis B.
- [ ] M2 Some viruses cause cancer: human papillomavirus (HPV) causes most cervical cancers; a vaccine against HPV protects against them (detail in "Cell Division and Cancer").
- [ ] M3 Antibiotics do not work against viruses, because antibiotics target structures and processes viruses don't have (e.g. bacterial cell walls and ribosomes). Viral infections are treated with antiviral drugs, which are fewer and more specific, and prevented by vaccination and hygiene (detail in "Immunity"). Misusing antibiotics for viral infections contributes to antibiotic resistance in bacteria.
- [ ] M4 Useful medical roles: vaccines are made from weakened or inactivated viruses or from viral proteins; harmless modified viruses are used as vectors to carry genes into human cells in gene therapy and in some vaccines (some COVID-19 vaccines used a harmless adenovirus to carry the gene for a coronavirus spike protein); bacteriophages can be used to kill bacteria that are resistant to antibiotics (phage therapy).

Economic importance (one block)
- [ ] E1 Costs to farming: plant viruses reduce crop yields, e.g. tobacco mosaic virus (tobacco, tomatoes), potato viruses, and barley yellow dwarf virus in cereals, which is spread by aphids. Animal viruses such as foot-and-mouth disease (cattle, sheep and pigs) and avian influenza (bird flu, poultry) lead to culling of infected herds and flocks, bans on exports and movement restrictions.
- [ ] E2 Costs to society: health-care costs, lost working days, and in a pandemic the closure of businesses, schools and travel (e.g. COVID-19, from 2020).
- [ ] E3 Economic benefits: viruses used as tools in genetic engineering (as vectors that carry genes into cells); in biological control of pests, e.g. myxoma virus was released in Australia in 1950 to control rabbits; vaccine production is a major pharmaceutical industry.

## 3. Traps

- How viruses replicate (attachment, entry, copying, assembly, release; lytic cycle) and the differences between DNA and RNA viruses belong to "Viruses: replication" in Immunity. Here only V5's one sentence.
- Prions are not viruses (they have no nucleic acid); they are covered under "Pathogens" in Immunity. Don't discuss them here.
- Viruses are not bacteria and are not "very small cells"; never give them organelles or a membrane of their own (the envelope comes from the host).
- A virus contains DNA or RNA, never both.
- Vaccination, immunity and antivirals in detail belong to Immunity; HPV and cancer to "Cell Division and Cancer"; genetic-engineering vectors to "Genetic Engineering". One bullet each here.
- The previous syllabus placed viruses in the Microorganisms chapter. Here they sit with classification.
- Diagram cues: only V8. No other diagram cues.
