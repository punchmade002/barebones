## 1. Must cover

The heartbeat and the pacemaker (one process block)
- [ ] P1 A heartbeat is one contraction and relaxation of the heart muscle, pumping blood out of the ventricles into the arteries. The heart beats about 70 times a minute in an adult at rest (a normal resting range is about 60 to 100), every minute of life.
- [ ] P2 Cardiac muscle is myogenic: the beat starts within the heart muscle itself, without a nerve impulse. A heart removed for transplant keeps beating for a time if supplied with oxygenated fluid. (Cardiac muscle features are in "Investigation: the heart".)
- [ ] P3 The pacemaker is the sinoatrial node (SA node), a small patch of specialised cardiac muscle in the wall of the right atrium, near where the superior vena cava enters. It produces electrical impulses at regular intervals by itself, and each impulse starts one heartbeat, so the SA node sets the rate of the heartbeat.
- [ ] P4 How the impulse spreads, step by step: 1. the impulse from the SA node spreads across the walls of both atria, so the atria contract together, pushing blood into the ventricles; 2. a layer of non-conducting tissue between the atria and the ventricles stops the impulse passing straight to the ventricles; 3. the impulse reaches the atrioventricular node (AV node), at the base of the right atrium near the septum, which delays it for a fraction of a second, giving the atria time to empty into the ventricles; 4. the AV node passes the impulse down conducting fibres in the septum (the bundle of His) to the apex and then up through the ventricle walls (Purkinje fibres); 5. the ventricles contract from the apex upwards, squeezing blood up into the aorta and pulmonary artery. This ensures the atria contract before the ventricles and the two sides beat together.
- [ ] P5 Diagram: a front-view outline of the heart showing the SA node in the wall of the right atrium, the AV node near the septum, the conducting fibres down the septum and up the ventricle walls, with arrows for the spread of the impulse.

Control of heart rate (one block)
- [ ] R1 The SA node sets the basic rhythm, but the rate is changed to match the body's needs: e.g. it rises during exercise, when muscles need more oxygen and glucose and produce more CO₂.
- [ ] R2 Nervous control: the cardiac centre in the medulla oblongata sends impulses along two sets of nerves to the SA node: one set (sympathetic nerves) speeds up the heart rate; the other (the vagus nerve, a parasympathetic nerve) slows it down. The medulla responds to information from receptors, e.g. a rise in CO₂ in the blood increases heart rate (see "CO₂ as a controlling factor in breathing") and a rise in blood pressure slows it. (The medulla is described in "CNS and PNS".)
- [ ] R3 Hormonal control: adrenaline, released by the adrenal glands in stress, danger or excitement, acts on the SA node and increases the heart rate and the force of contraction (see "Glands, locations and hormones").
- [ ] R4 Other factors that change heart rate (one bullet): exercise and fitness, age (higher in babies and children), body temperature (fever raises it), emotion, caffeine and nicotine (raise it), and some medicines.
- [ ] R5 Artificial pacemaker (technology link): if the SA node fails or the impulse does not reach the ventricles properly (heart block), the heart beats too slowly or irregularly. A small battery-powered device is implanted under the skin below the collarbone, with a wire (lead) running through a vein into the heart; it detects the heart's own rhythm and sends an electrical impulse to start a beat when one is missed or the rate is too slow. Batteries last several years and are replaced in a minor operation.

Pulse (one block; includes a short skills method)
- [ ] U1 The pulse is a wave of expansion of an artery wall caused by the surge of blood forced into the arteries each time the ventricles contract. The stretched elastic wall then recoils. So the pulse rate equals the heart rate.
- [ ] U2 It can be felt where an artery runs close to the skin over a bone or firm tissue: the radial artery at the wrist (on the thumb side) and the carotid artery in the neck (beside the windpipe) are the usual sites. Veins have no pulse, because the pressure wave has been lost in the capillaries.
- [ ] U3 Method: 1. sit quietly for a few minutes; 2. press lightly with the index and middle fingers (not the thumb, which has its own pulse) on the radial artery; 3. count the beats for 30 seconds and double it (or count for a full 60 seconds) to give beats per minute; 4. repeat three times and take the mean.
- [ ] U4 Exercise and pulse (a standard class activity using primary data): measure the resting pulse; exercise (e.g. step-ups for two minutes); measure the pulse at once and then every minute until it returns to the resting value. Result: the pulse rises during exercise and falls gradually afterwards. The time taken to return to the resting rate (the recovery time) is shorter in fitter people, who also tend to have a lower resting pulse because each beat pumps more blood. Variables: independent: activity level (rest, after exercise); dependent: pulse rate in beats per minute; controlled: type, duration and intensity of exercise, the same person, the same counting method, posture.

Blood pressure (one block)
- [ ] B1 Blood pressure is the force that the blood exerts on the walls of the blood vessels. It is produced by the contraction of the ventricles. It is highest in the aorta and large arteries, falls as blood passes through the arterioles and capillaries, and is lowest in the veins and venae cavae (details of vessels in "Blood vessels").
- [ ] B2 Measurement: with a sphygmomanometer (a blood pressure monitor), using an inflatable cuff on the upper arm over the brachial artery; given in millimetres of mercury (mmHg) as two numbers: the higher pressure when the ventricles contract and the lower pressure when the heart relaxes between beats, e.g. about 120/80 mmHg for a healthy young adult at rest. Don't use the words systolic or diastolic.
- [ ] B3 Blood pressure rises naturally during exercise or stress and falls during sleep. High blood pressure (hypertension) means a resting value consistently at or above about 140/90 mmHg. It usually has no symptoms but strains the heart, damages artery walls, and raises the risk of heart attack, stroke and kidney damage.
- [ ] B4 Factors that raise blood pressure: too much salt in the diet (see "Minerals"), being overweight, lack of exercise, smoking, too much alcohol, stress, older age and a family history. Controlled by lifestyle changes (less salt, exercise, weight loss, not smoking) and medication.

The heart's own blood supply (one structure-and-function block)
- [ ] C1 The heart muscle can't take oxygen and nutrients from the blood passing through its chambers: the wall is too thick for diffusion to reach most cells, and the left side's blood passes too quickly. So the heart has its own circulation, the coronary circulation.
- [ ] C2 The two coronary arteries (left and right) are the first branches of the aorta, arising from its base just above the aortic semilunar valve. They run over the surface of the heart, branching into smaller arteries that enter the muscle and supply a dense network of capillaries, giving the cardiac muscle oxygen and glucose for the aerobic respiration that keeps it working continuously.
- [ ] C3 Deoxygenated blood from the heart muscle is collected by the cardiac (coronary) veins, which run over the surface and join a large vein at the back of the heart (the coronary sinus) that empties into the right atrium.
- [ ] C4 Diagram: front view of the heart showing the aorta, the left and right coronary arteries branching from its base and running over the surface, and the cardiac veins; label each.

Coronary heart disease (health link; one block, kept short)
- [ ] D1 Fatty deposits (atheroma, plaque containing cholesterol) can build up in the walls of the coronary arteries, narrowing them (atherosclerosis), so less blood reaches the heart muscle. This can cause chest pain during exercise (angina).
- [ ] D2 If a blood clot forms on a damaged plaque and blocks a coronary artery, the part of the heart muscle it supplies is starved of oxygen and dies: a heart attack (myocardial infarction). Clot-dissolving enzymes given soon after are in "Industrial enzymes"; clotting is in "Blood".
- [ ] D3 Risk factors: smoking, a diet high in saturated fat (raising blood cholesterol; see "Metabolic roles and nutritional sources"), high blood pressure, obesity, lack of exercise, diabetes, older age and family history. Prevention: not smoking, a balanced diet, regular exercise, healthy weight.
- [ ] D4 Technology (one bullet): a narrowed coronary artery can be widened by angioplasty, in which a balloon on a catheter is inflated inside the artery and a small mesh tube (a stent) is left in place to hold it open; or bypassed by a coronary artery bypass graft, using a piece of vein (often from the leg) or artery to carry blood around the blockage.

## 3. Traps

- Cardiac cycle not required: don't use the words systole or diastole (including "systolic" and "diastolic" pressure), don't describe the phases of the cycle or their timing, the heart sounds ("lub-dub"), or graphs of pressure in the chambers. P4 describes how the impulse spreads, which is the pacemaker's role; it stops at "the ventricles contract".
- The pacemaker is in the right atrium, not the left, and not in the brain. The brain only changes the rate set by the SA node; the heart would beat without it.
- The vagus nerve slows the heart; sympathetic nerves and adrenaline speed it up.
- The pulse is a wave in the artery wall, not blood "being felt flowing"; pulse rate equals heart rate. Veins have no pulse.
- The coronary arteries branch from the aorta, not the pulmonary artery, and they run on the outside of the heart; the heart muscle is not fed from the blood inside its chambers.
- Electrocardiograms (ECGs) and defibrillators are not on the specification; leave them out.
- The breathing response to CO₂ belongs to "CO₂ as a controlling factor in breathing"; R2 mentions the heart-rate effect in one phrase.
- Coronary heart disease is a health link only: no disease statistics, no named drugs (statins etc.), no detail of how cholesterol is carried.
- The diagram cues P5 and C4 are the only ones wanted. No other diagram cues.
