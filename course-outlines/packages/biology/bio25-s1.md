## 1. Must cover

The membrane as a barrier (assumed; one block)
- [ ] M1 Every cell is surrounded by a cell membrane: a phospholipid bilayer with proteins scattered through it (fluid mosaic model). Materials constantly cross it: cells take in oxygen, glucose, water and ions, and release carbon dioxide and wastes such as urea. This is the transport and transfer of materials across membranes; the same applies to the membranes around organelles.
- [ ] M2 The membrane is selectively permeable: it lets some substances through and not others. Small, non-polar molecules (oxygen, carbon dioxide) pass straight through the bilayer; water passes through slowly between the phospholipids and quickly through protein water channels (aquaporins); ions and large polar molecules such as glucose cross only through specific channel or carrier proteins; large molecules such as proteins and starch can't cross at all.
- [ ] M3 Terms (one bullet): fully permeable (lets water and all solutes through, e.g. the plant cell wall); semi-permeable or partially permeable (lets water and small molecules through but not larger ones, by the size of its pores, e.g. Visking (dialysis) tubing); selectively permeable (a living membrane, which also controls what crosses using its proteins and can move substances actively).
- [ ] M4 Solution terms (assumed, one bullet): a solution is a solute (e.g. sugar or salt) dissolved in a solvent (water). A concentrated solution has much solute and so a lower concentration of water; a dilute solution has little solute and a higher concentration of water.

Diffusion (one block)
- [ ] D1 Diffusion is the net movement of molecules or ions from a region of their higher concentration to a region of their lower concentration, until they are evenly spread. The difference in concentration between two regions is the concentration gradient; diffusion is movement down the gradient.
- [ ] D2 It is passive: it needs no energy from the cell (no ATP), because it results from the random movement of particles, which have kinetic energy. Individual particles move in all directions; more move from the crowded region than into it, giving the net movement. It happens in gases and liquids, in living and non-living systems, with or without a membrane.
- [ ] D3 Examples: oxygen diffuses from the air in the alveoli into the blood and carbon dioxide diffuses the other way (detail in "Gas exchange and the breathing mechanism"); carbon dioxide diffuses into a leaf through the stomata and oxygen out; digested food molecules diffuse from the small intestine into the blood; the scent of perfume spreads through a room (non-living example).
- [ ] D4 Facilitated diffusion (assumed, one bullet): ions and polar molecules such as glucose diffuse down their concentration gradient through specific channel or carrier proteins; it is still passive, needing no ATP; e.g. glucose entering red blood cells.
- [ ] D5 Factors that increase the rate of diffusion (one bullet): a steeper concentration gradient, higher temperature (particles move faster), a larger surface area, a shorter distance (thinner barrier), and smaller particles. Exchange surfaces such as alveoli and villi have a large surface area, a thin wall and a steep gradient kept up by blood flow. Osmosis depends on the same factors (see "Investigation: rate of osmosis").

Osmosis (one block)
- [ ] O1 Osmosis is the net movement of water molecules from a region of higher water concentration (a dilute solution) to a region of lower water concentration (a more concentrated solution) across a semi-permeable (selectively permeable) membrane.
- [ ] O2 Osmosis is a special case of diffusion: it is the diffusion of water only, and it happens only across a membrane that lets water through but holds back the solute. It is passive (no ATP).
- [ ] O3 Why water moves: solute molecules attract water molecules around them, so fewer water molecules are free to move in a concentrated solution. More free water molecules cross the membrane from the dilute side than from the concentrated side, so there is a net flow of water into the concentrated solution, until the concentrations are equal or a pressure (e.g. a cell wall) stops it.
- [ ] O4 Diagram: a U-tube (or beaker) divided by a semi-permeable membrane, with dilute solution on one side and concentrated sucrose solution on the other; water molecules shown passing in both directions with a larger arrow towards the concentrated side, sucrose molecules too large to cross, and the liquid level rising on the concentrated side. Label: semi-permeable membrane, water molecule, sucrose molecule, net movement of water.

Osmosis and cells (one block; assumed terms)
- [ ] C1 Terms used for the solution outside a cell, compared with the cell's contents (assumed): hypotonic (more dilute than the cell: water enters the cell), hypertonic (more concentrated than the cell: water leaves), isotonic (the same concentration: no net movement).
- [ ] C2 Animal cells in a hypotonic solution (e.g. pure water) gain water, swell and may burst because they have no cell wall; red blood cells bursting is haemolysis. In a hypertonic solution they lose water and shrink, and red blood cells become shrivelled and spiky (crenated). This is why body fluids must be kept isotonic with cells (osmoregulation; see "Water").
- [ ] C3 Plant cells in a hypotonic solution take in water into the vacuole; the cytoplasm is pushed against the cell wall, which resists further swelling, so the cell becomes turgid (firm) and doesn't burst. The pressure of the contents against the wall is turgor pressure.
- [ ] C4 Plant cells in a hypertonic solution lose water from the vacuole; the cell becomes flaccid (soft), then the cytoplasm and cell membrane shrink away from the cell wall: plasmolysis. The space between wall and membrane fills with the outside solution, because the cell wall is fully permeable. Plasmolysis can be reversed (deplasmolysis) if the cell is put back into water before it is damaged.
- [ ] C5 Diagram: a plant cell in water (turgid: large vacuole, cytoplasm pressed against wall), in an isotonic solution (flaccid) and in a concentrated solution (plasmolysed: cell membrane and cytoplasm pulled away from the wall), labelled cell wall, cell membrane, vacuole, cytoplasm; and red blood cells in water (swollen, bursting), isotonic solution (normal disc) and concentrated solution (crenated).

Active transport (one block)
- [ ] A1 Active transport is the movement of molecules or ions across a membrane from a region of lower concentration to a region of higher concentration (against the concentration gradient), using energy from ATP.
- [ ] A2 How it works: 1. The substance binds to a specific carrier protein (a pump) in the membrane. 2. ATP made in respiration releases energy, which changes the shape of the carrier protein. 3. The change in shape moves the substance across the membrane and releases it on the other side. 4. The carrier returns to its original shape, ready to pick up another molecule.
- [ ] A3 Because it needs ATP, active transport happens only in living cells and depends on respiration. It stops or slows if respiration is stopped, e.g. by lack of oxygen, low temperature or a respiratory poison such as cyanide. Cells that carry out much active transport have many mitochondria (e.g. root hair cells, cells lining the kidney tubules and the small intestine).
- [ ] A4 The sodium–potassium pump: a carrier protein in the membranes of animal cells, including nerve cells, which uses one ATP to pump three sodium ions (Na⁺) out of the cell and two potassium ions (K⁺) into it, both against their concentration gradients. It keeps Na⁺ high outside the cell and K⁺ high inside, which nerve cells need in order to carry impulses, and it helps control cell volume. No detail of the nerve impulse.
- [ ] A5 Other examples: root hair cells take up mineral ions (e.g. nitrate) from soil water in which they are more dilute than in the cell (detail in "Transport in Plants"); glucose and amino acids are absorbed from the small intestine and reabsorbed from the kidney tubules into the blood (detail in "Absorption, transport and storage" and "The nephron").
- [ ] A6 Diagram: a carrier protein in a phospholipid bilayer with a molecule binding on the low-concentration side, ATP → ADP + Pi releasing energy, the carrier changing shape and the molecule released on the high-concentration side; label carrier protein, phospholipid bilayer, ATP, direction of movement against the gradient.

Distinguishing the three processes (one comparison block, paired points)
- [ ] X1 Energy: diffusion and osmosis are passive (no ATP); active transport needs ATP from respiration.
- [ ] X2 Direction: diffusion and osmosis go down a concentration gradient (high to low concentration of the substance moving, for osmosis of water); active transport goes against it (low to high).
- [ ] X3 What moves: diffusion moves any particle (gases, solutes); osmosis moves only water; active transport moves particular solutes, mainly ions and small molecules such as glucose, not water.
- [ ] X4 Membrane and proteins: diffusion can happen with or without a membrane and needs no proteins for small non-polar molecules; osmosis needs a semi-permeable membrane; active transport needs specific carrier proteins in a living membrane.
- [ ] X5 Where: diffusion and osmosis happen in living and non-living systems (e.g. Visking tubing); active transport happens only in living cells.

## 3. Traps

- Osmosis moves water, not solute. Always write "higher water concentration to lower water concentration" (or "dilute to concentrated solution"); never "high concentration to low concentration" unqualified, which reads as the solute moving the wrong way.
- Water does not move "only one way" in osmosis: it crosses in both directions, with a net flow towards the concentrated solution.
- Plant cells don't burst in water (the cell wall); plasmolysis happens only in plant (walled) cells, not animal cells. Haemolysis and crenation are for red blood cells.
- Active transport does not move water. Water always moves by osmosis.
- Hypotonic and hypertonic describe the outside solution relative to the cell; don't call a cell "hypertonic" without saying compared with what.
- Water potential (and its negative values) is not needed; don't introduce it.
- Endocytosis and exocytosis are not named in this outcome; don't add a block on them (exocytosis is mentioned in "Structures and organelles of animal and plant cells").
- No resting potential, action potential or millivolt values with the sodium–potassium pump (A4 gives only what the pump does).
- Membrane structure is assumed (M1–M3 recap only); the history of membrane models is in "Models". Don't repeat the phospholipid structure or the Davson–Danielli model.
- Food preservation, wilting and salt damage belong to "Osmosis in food preservation and plant health"; root hair uptake and xylem to "Transport in Plants"; kidney detail to "The nephron".
- The diagram cues O4, C5 and A6 are all that are wanted. No other diagram cues.

## 4. What exams ask (topics only)

- Define diffusion, osmosis and active transport; give an example of each.
- Explain the difference between osmosis and diffusion, or between active transport and diffusion.
- Explain what happens to animal and plant cells placed in water and in concentrated solutions (turgor, plasmolysis, bursting).
- Explain what is meant by a selectively permeable or semi-permeable membrane.
- Explain why active transport needs ATP or stops when respiration stops.
