## 1. Must cover

Chromosomes and chromosome number (one block; Ordinary and Higher)
- [ ] K1 (assumed) A chromosome is one long DNA molecule wound around proteins, carrying many genes (detail in "Chromosome structure and genes"; one sentence here). Between divisions the chromosomes are long, thin and spread out as chromatin; before division they coil up (condense) and become short and thick, so they can be seen with the light microscope when stained.
- [ ] K2 (assumed) Homologous chromosomes: a pair of chromosomes of the same size and shape, with the same genes at the same positions, one inherited from each parent (the alleles on them may differ). Body cells of humans contain 23 such pairs.
- [ ] K3 Diploid (2n): a cell or organism with two sets of chromosomes, i.e. its chromosomes in homologous pairs. Human body (somatic) cells are diploid, 2n = 46.
- [ ] K4 Haploid (n): a cell with one set of chromosomes, i.e. one chromosome from each homologous pair. Human gametes (sperm and egg cells) are haploid, n = 23.
- [ ] K5 Why it matters: when two haploid gametes fuse at fertilisation, the zygote is diploid again (23 + 23 = 46), so the chromosome number stays the same from generation to generation. Haploid cells are made by meiosis; mitosis keeps the number the same (detail in "Mitosis and meiosis").
- [ ] K6 Each species has its own chromosome number, e.g. human 2n = 46; the fruit fly Drosophila 2n = 8; the garden pea 2n = 14. The number does not reflect how complex an organism is.

The cell cycle: an outline (one process block; Ordinary and Higher)
- [ ] C1 The cell cycle is the sequence of events in the life of a cell, from the time it is formed by division until it divides itself into two daughter cells.
- [ ] C2 It has three main activities, in this order: growth (the cell makes new proteins and organelles and gets bigger); DNA synthesis (every chromosome is copied); division (the nucleus divides so that each new nucleus gets one copy of every chromosome, then the cytoplasm divides to give two cells).
- [ ] C3 The phase between divisions, when growth and DNA synthesis happen, is interphase. A cell spends most of its life in interphase (about 90% of the cycle); division is short. In many human cells grown in the laboratory one cycle takes about 24 hours. Interphase is not a "resting" stage: the cell is very active, carrying out its normal functions as well as preparing to divide.
- [ ] C4 The result of one cycle (with mitosis) is two daughter cells, each with the same number and type of chromosomes as the parent cell and genetically identical to it.
- [ ] C5 Not all cells keep cycling: some leave the cycle once they are specialised and stop dividing, e.g. most mature nerve cells; mature red blood cells have no nucleus and can't divide. Others divide often throughout life, e.g. cells of the skin, the lining of the gut and the bone marrow, and in plants the cells at root and shoot tips (meristems).
- [ ] C6 The cycle is controlled: proteins inside the cell check at several points (checkpoints) that the cell is large enough, that the DNA has been copied correctly and is undamaged, and that the chromosomes are attached correctly before the cell moves on; signals from other cells (growth factors) tell it whether to divide. One bullet; how loss of this control leads to cancer is in "Cancer".
- [ ] C7 Diagram: the cell cycle as a circle divided into a large sector for interphase (labelled cell growth and DNA replication) and a small sector for division (labelled mitosis, then cytokinesis), with an arrow showing the direction. Higher Level students label the interphase sub-stages and prophase, metaphase, anaphase, telophase within the mitosis sector.

Interphase in detail (Higher Level only; one block)
- [ ] I1 [HL] Interphase has three parts. G1 (first growth phase): the cell grows, makes proteins (including enzymes needed to copy DNA) and organelles, and carries out its normal job. S (synthesis) phase: DNA replication, so each chromosome now consists of two identical copies called sister chromatids, joined at the centromere. G2 (second growth phase): further growth, more organelles (e.g. mitochondria) and the proteins of the spindle are made; ATP is stored for division.
- [ ] I2 [HL] A cell that stops dividing leaves the cycle from G1 into a non-dividing state (sometimes called G0), e.g. mature nerve cells.
- [ ] I3 [HL] Throughout interphase the nuclear membrane (envelope) is intact, the nucleolus is visible and the chromosomes are uncoiled as chromatin, so individual chromosomes can't be seen.

The stages of mitosis (Higher Level only; one block, stage by stage)
- [ ] M1 [HL] Nuclear division (mitosis) is a continuous process divided for convenience into four stages: prophase, metaphase, anaphase, telophase (order: I-PMAT including interphase). It is followed by cytokinesis.
- [ ] M2 [HL] Prophase: the chromosomes condense (shorten and thicken) and become visible, each as two sister chromatids joined at a centromere; the nucleolus disappears; the nuclear membrane breaks down; spindle fibres (made of protein tubes, microtubules) begin to form. In animal cells two centrioles move to opposite ends (poles) of the cell and the spindle forms between them; plant cells have no centrioles but still form a spindle.
- [ ] M3 [HL] Metaphase: the chromosomes line up singly on the equator (middle) of the spindle; each is attached by its centromere to spindle fibres from both poles.
- [ ] M4 [HL] Anaphase: the centromeres split; the spindle fibres shorten, pulling the sister chromatids apart to opposite poles, centromere first (giving a V shape). Once separated, each chromatid is called a chromosome. Each pole now has a full set of identical chromosomes. Energy for the movement comes from ATP (see "ATP").
- [ ] M5 [HL] Telophase: the chromosomes reach the poles and uncoil back into chromatin; a nuclear membrane forms around each set; the nucleoli reappear; the spindle breaks down. There are now two nuclei, each genetically identical to the original.
- [ ] M6 [HL] Diagram: the four stages of mitosis in an animal cell with 2n = 4 (two homologous pairs, one pair long and one short, shaded to show the two parents' copies), labelled: chromosome, sister chromatids, centromere, spindle fibres, centriole, pole, equator, nuclear membrane (breaking down, and re-forming), cell membrane, cleavage furrow at the end of telophase.
- [ ] M7 [HL] Identifying stages: in stained cells from a growing root tip (e.g. onion or garlic) viewed at ×400, most cells are in interphase (a nucleus with no visible chromosomes), because it is the longest part of the cycle; dividing cells are identified by the arrangement of their chromosomes (condensed and scattered: prophase; in a line across the middle: metaphase; two groups moving apart: anaphase; two groups at opposite ends, new nuclei forming: telophase). The mitotic index is the percentage of cells in a sample that are dividing; worked example: 24 of 200 cells dividing gives (24 ÷ 200) × 100 = 12%. A high mitotic index shows a tissue with rapid cell division (such as a root tip or a tumour).

Cytokinesis (Higher Level only; one block)
- [ ] Y1 [HL] Cytokinesis is the division of the cytoplasm to form two separate daughter cells. It usually starts during late telophase. Organelles such as mitochondria are shared between the two cells.
- [ ] Y2 [HL] In animal cells: the cell membrane is pulled inwards around the middle of the cell, forming a groove (cleavage furrow) that deepens until the cell pinches into two.
- [ ] Y3 [HL] In plant cells: the rigid cell wall can't pinch in, so vesicles (from the Golgi apparatus) collect across the middle of the cell and fuse to form a cell plate, which grows outwards to the sides and becomes the new cell membranes and cell wall between the two cells.
- [ ] Y4 [HL] Diagram: an animal cell with a cleavage furrow beside a plant cell with a cell plate forming, each labelled.

## 3. Traps

- Interphase is not a resting phase; the cell is active and the DNA is copied then. Don't put DNA replication in prophase.
- Don't teach prometaphase, or the stages of meiosis (meiosis I and II and their sub-stages are not on the specification). The specification names prophase, metaphase, anaphase and telophase for mitosis only.
- Chromatids and the centromere are Higher Level terms (they are also in "Chromosome structure and genes"). Use them only in the Higher Level blocks; the Ordinary blocks say "two identical copies of each chromosome".
- Sister chromatids are identical copies of one chromosome; homologous chromosomes are a pair, one from each parent, which carry the same genes but may carry different alleles. Students confuse the two. In mitosis chromatids separate; homologous chromosomes don't pair up.
- Mitosis is nuclear division; cytokinesis is division of the cytoplasm. Don't call cytokinesis a stage of mitosis.
- Centrioles are in animal cells only; plant cells form a cell plate, not a cleavage furrow. Don't say plant cells "pinch in".
- Haploid is not "half a chromosome": it is one complete set. The diploid number counts chromosomes, not pairs (2n = 46, not 23).
- The specification gives no cell-cycle investigation; M7 is a skill for interpreting images and data, not a required practical. Don't write it as an Investigation block.
- Boundaries: how DNA is copied (enzymes, templates) belongs to "DNA replication and mitosis in the cell cycle" and, at Higher Level, "Modelling DNA replication and information flow"; I1 says "DNA replication" only. What mitosis and meiosis achieve in different organisms belongs to "Mitosis and meiosis". Checkpoints failing, oncogenes and tumours belong to "Cancer". Gamete production belongs to "Gametes and fertilisation" (Human Reproduction).
- Diagram cues: C7, M6 and Y4 only. No other diagram cues.

## 5. Practice questions to write

1. 6 marks: "Explain the terms haploid and diploid, giving one example of each type of cell in humans." Model answer: K3, K4 definitions with 2n = 46 body cell and n = 23 gamete, and K5 (fertilisation restores diploid).
2. 12 marks, Higher: "Describe the events of interphase and of each stage of mitosis in an animal cell." Model answer: I1 (G1, S with DNA replication giving sister chromatids, G2), then M2–M5, one point on chromosomes and one on the spindle or nuclear membrane for each stage; with a labelled M6 diagram of one stage.
3. 9 marks, Higher: "A student counted 300 cells in a stained root tip and found 36 dividing. Calculate the mitotic index, explain why most cells were in interphase, and state how cytokinesis in these cells differs from that in an animal cell." Model answer: 36 ÷ 300 × 100 = 12% (M7); interphase is the longest part of the cycle (C3); cell plate from vesicles vs cleavage furrow (Y2, Y3).
