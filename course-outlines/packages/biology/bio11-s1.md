## 1. Must cover

What a chromosome is (one structure block)
- [ ] S1 A chromosome is a single, very long molecule of DNA together with proteins, found in the nucleus of a eukaryotic cell. It carries many genes in a fixed linear order along its length. Ordinary level: "DNA and protein"; the protein is named in H1.
- [ ] S2 Chromatin is the name for the DNA–protein material of the chromosomes when the cell is not dividing. Then the chromosomes are long, thin and uncoiled, spread through the nucleus, and can't be seen as separate threads. Before a cell divides, the chromatin coils and folds up tightly (condenses), so each chromosome becomes short and thick and visible under the light microscope when stained. Chromatin and chromosomes are the same material in two states. (bio7's "Structures and organelles" note already introduced the word; here give the full contrast.)
- [ ] S3 (assumed) Number and pairs: human body cells have 46 chromosomes in 23 pairs: 22 pairs of autosomes and one pair of sex chromosomes (XX in females, XY in males; how these determine sex is in "Sex determination"). The two chromosomes of a pair are homologous chromosomes: they are the same length, have the centromere in the same position and carry the same genes at the same positions, one chromosome of each pair coming from each parent. The number is fixed for a species (e.g. 46 in humans); a different number does not mean a more complex organism. Haploid and diploid are defined in "The cell cycle"; use them without redefining.
- [ ] S4 (assumed) Size: the human genome (one set of 23 chromosomes) is about 3 billion base pairs; the DNA in one human body cell, stretched out, would be about 2 m long, yet it fits in a nucleus a few micrometres across. This is why packing (H1–H3) is needed.
- [ ] S5 Prokaryotes are different: one circular chromosome, in the cytoplasm, not wound around histones (bacteria), plus plasmids. One bullet, citing "Prokaryotic and eukaryotic cells"; do not restate the comparison.

Histones and how DNA is packed (one block, Higher Level only)
- [ ] H1 Histones are small proteins around which the DNA of a eukaryotic chromosome is wound. They are positively charged, and the phosphate groups of the DNA backbone are negatively charged, so DNA binds tightly to them.
- [ ] H2 The DNA wraps nearly twice around a core of eight histone proteins, forming a bead called a nucleosome; the nucleosomes are joined by short stretches of linker DNA, like beads on a string. The string of nucleosomes then coils and loops further, and before division it condenses into the visible chromosome.
- [ ] H3 Functions of histones: (1) pack a very long DNA molecule into a small nucleus; (2) protect the DNA and keep it untangled during division; (3) control which genes can be read: DNA wound tightly around histones can't be reached by the enzymes that read genes, so those genes are switched off, while loosely packed regions are active. Chemical changes to histones are one of the epigenetic mechanisms in "Genetic and epigenetic mechanisms"; one phrase here.
- [ ] H4 Diagram (Higher Level): DNA double helix wound around histone cores to form nucleosomes, then coiled chromatin fibre, then a condensed chromosome; label DNA, histone, nucleosome, chromatin, chromosome.

Chromatids and the centromere (one block, Higher Level only)
- [ ] C1 Before a cell divides, each chromosome's DNA is copied (replication, in "DNA replication and mitosis in the cell cycle"). The two identical copies stay joined together; each copy is called a chromatid. So a replicated chromosome consists of two identical sister chromatids, each containing one complete DNA molecule with the same genes and the same alleles.
- [ ] C2 The centromere is the constricted (narrowed) region of a chromosome where the two sister chromatids are held together. During division the spindle fibres attach at the centromere, and the chromatids are pulled apart to opposite poles (detail in "Mitosis and meiosis" and "The cell cycle"). Once separated, each chromatid is a chromosome in its own right.
- [ ] C3 The centromere is in a fixed position for each chromosome, dividing it into two arms that may be of equal or different lengths. Its position, with length and banding pattern, is used to identify each chromosome.
- [ ] C4 Distinguish: a chromosome before replication is one DNA molecule (one chromatid); after replication it is still one chromosome but has two chromatids (the X shape usually drawn). Homologous chromosomes (S3) are a pair from different parents and may carry different alleles; sister chromatids are copies of each other and carry identical alleles.
- [ ] C5 Diagram (Higher Level): an unreplicated chromosome (single rod) beside a replicated chromosome (X shape); label chromatid (sister chromatids), centromere, arm, and a gene locus marked as a band at the same position on both chromatids. Optional second diagram: a homologous pair of replicated chromosomes, with the same locus marked on each, labelled "homologous chromosomes".

The role of a gene (one block)
- [ ] G1 A gene is a section of DNA on a chromosome that codes for a protein (definition in "Genes, proteins and traits"; use it). Each gene has a fixed position on a particular chromosome, called its locus (plural loci). The locus of a gene is the same in every member of a species.
- [ ] G2 Role: a gene is the unit of heredity. It (1) carries the information to make one protein (some genes code instead for RNA molecules that are used directly, e.g. ribosomal RNA and transfer RNA); (2) through that protein controls a trait; (3) is copied and passed to daughter cells in division and to offspring in gametes.
- [ ] G3 Humans have about 20,000 protein-coding genes spread across the 23 kinds of chromosome; each chromosome carries hundreds to thousands of genes. Because homologous chromosomes carry the same genes, a body cell has two copies of each autosomal gene, which may be the same or different versions (alleles, defined in "Key concepts of information transfer").

Coding and non-coding DNA (one block)
- [ ] N1 Coding DNA is DNA whose base sequence is used to make a protein: the protein-coding parts of genes. In humans, only about 1–2% of the DNA is coding DNA.
- [ ] N2 Non-coding DNA is all DNA that does not code for protein, about 98% of the human genome. It includes: (1) regulatory sequences beside genes that act as switches, controlling when, where and how much a gene is expressed; (2) genes for functional RNA (rRNA, tRNA); (3) non-coding sections within genes (introns), which are copied but removed from the mRNA before it is used to make protein; (4) repeated sequences, such as the DNA at centromeres and at the ends of chromosomes (telomeres), which protect the chromosome and help it divide correctly; (5) DNA whose function is unknown.
- [ ] N3 Non-coding DNA was once called "junk DNA", but much of it is now known to have functions, especially in regulating genes. Changes in regulatory DNA can change a trait without changing any protein.
- [ ] N4 Repeated non-coding sequences vary greatly in length between people, which is why they are used in DNA profiling (detail in "DNA profiling"). One phrase.
- [ ] N5 Diagram: a stretch of a chromosome's DNA drawn as a line, with two genes shown as boxes (each with a regulatory region at one end, and coding sections separated by an intron) and the DNA between genes labelled non-coding; label gene, coding DNA, non-coding DNA, regulatory sequence, intron.

## 3. Traps

- Histones, chromatids and centromere are Higher Level only; keep them in the two marked blocks. Key terms for them are Higher Level material.
- Don't say a chromosome "is made of genes": it is DNA plus protein, and genes are sections of the DNA; most of the DNA is non-coding.
- Chromatid vs chromosome vs chromatin: chromatin is the uncoiled material; a chromatid is one of the two copies of a replicated chromosome; don't call each arm of the X a chromosome. Don't confuse sister chromatids (identical copies) with homologous chromosomes (one from each parent).
- The centromere is not "the middle" of the chromosome; its position varies.
- Non-coding DNA is not all "junk" (N3). Don't say all genes code for proteins (G2).
- Don't give figures for the number of genes on particular chromosomes, or other chromosome numbers (dog, potato, etc.), beyond the table.
- Mitosis, meiosis and the stages of division belong to "Mitosis and meiosis" and "The cell cycle"; DNA replication to "DNA replication and mitosis in the cell cycle"; transcription, translation and the removal of introns as a process to "DNA Replication, Protein Synthesis and Mutations". Name them only.
- Allele, homozygous, heterozygous, genotype and phenotype are defined in "Key concepts of information transfer"; G3 mentions alleles in a phrase only. Linkage (genes on the same chromosome) is in "Linkage"; don't explain it here.
- Sex chromosomes are named in S3 only; X and Y in sex determination belong to "Sex determination".
- Chromosome mutations belong to "Variation" and "DNA Replication, Protein Synthesis and Mutations".
- Crossing over is not on the specification (the specification says "knowledge of crossing over not required"); don't mention it.
- No other diagram cues beyond H4, C5 and N5.
