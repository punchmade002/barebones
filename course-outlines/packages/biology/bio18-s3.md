## 1. Must cover

The appendicular skeleton (lead or one bullet)
- [ ] A1 The appendicular skeleton is the skeleton of the limbs (appendages) and the two girdles that attach them to the axial skeleton: the pectoral (shoulder) girdle for the arms and the pelvic (hip) girdle for the legs. Its main job is movement; the legs and pelvic girdle also bear the body's weight.

Structure of a long bone (assumed; one structure-and-function block)
- [ ] B1 (assumed) The limb bones named in the specification (except the patella) are long bones: a long shaft with a wider end at each joint. The shaft is a hollow tube of dense compact bone; a tube is nearly as strong as a solid rod of the same width but much lighter, so the limb is strong but light enough to move quickly.
- [ ] B2 (assumed) The wider ends are made of spongy bone, a lattice of thin bony struts that is light but strong along the lines of force, and contains red bone marrow; the central cavity of the shaft contains yellow marrow. The ends are covered with smooth articular cartilage where they form joints (its function is in "Muscles and joints"), and the whole bone, apart from these joint surfaces, is covered by a tough membrane (the periosteum) to which tendons and ligaments attach.
- [ ] B3 (assumed) Diagram: a long bone (the femur) in longitudinal section, labelling compact bone, spongy bone, marrow cavity with yellow marrow, red marrow in the spongy bone, articular cartilage, periosteum and shaft.

Pectoral girdle (one structure-and-function block)
- [ ] P1 The pectoral girdle is formed on each side by a clavicle and a scapula. It attaches the arm to the axial skeleton and is built for a wide range of movement rather than for bearing weight.
- [ ] P2 Clavicle (collarbone): a slender, S-shaped bone running across the top of the chest from the sternum to the scapula. It acts as a strut, holding the shoulder out to the side so the arm can swing freely, and passes forces from the arm to the axial skeleton. Its joint with the sternum is the only bony connection between the arm and the axial skeleton.
- [ ] P3 Scapula (shoulder blade): a large, flat, triangular bone lying on the back of the rib cage. It is not joined to the ribs or vertebrae but held in place by muscles, so it can slide and rotate, increasing the arm's range of movement. Its large flat surface and ridges give a large area for the attachment of shoulder and arm muscles (including the biceps and triceps; see "Muscles and joints").
- [ ] P4 The scapula has a shallow socket into which the rounded head of the humerus fits, forming the shoulder joint, a ball-and-socket joint. The shallowness of the socket allows the arm to move in almost every direction, including rotation, but makes the joint less stable than the hip.

Pelvic girdle (one structure-and-function block)
- [ ] G1 The pelvic girdle (pelvis) is a strong, bowl-shaped ring formed by the two hip bones, joined firmly to the sacrum at the back and to each other at the front. Each hip bone is made of several bones fused together; the names of these bones are not required.
- [ ] G2 It is joined firmly to the vertebral column through the sacrum, so it is rigid: it carries the weight of the upper body and passes it to the legs. This makes it much stronger and less movable than the pectoral girdle.
- [ ] G3 It has a deep, cup-shaped socket on each side into which the head of the femur fits, forming the hip joint, a ball-and-socket joint. The deep socket makes the hip very stable (suited to bearing weight) but gives a smaller range of movement than the shoulder.
- [ ] G4 It protects the organs of the lower abdomen (bladder, lower intestine and the reproductive organs, including the uterus), and its broad surfaces anchor the large muscles of the legs and trunk.
- [ ] G5 The female pelvis is wider and shallower, with a larger, rounder central opening than the male pelvis, so a baby's head can pass through during birth.
- [ ] G6 Comparison of the two girdles (paired bullets): pectoral, loosely attached by muscles, shallow socket, mobility; pelvic, fused to the sacrum, deep socket, stability and weight-bearing.

Upper limb (one structure-and-function block)
- [ ] L1 Humerus: the long bone of the upper arm. Its rounded head fits into the socket of the scapula at the shoulder (ball-and-socket joint); its lower end meets the radius and ulna at the elbow (a hinge joint). Its long shaft gives attachment to the muscles that move the shoulder and elbow.
- [ ] L2 Ulna: the longer forearm bone, on the little-finger side. Its upper end hooks around the end of the humerus, forming the main part of the elbow hinge and the point of the elbow, which stops the arm bending backwards beyond straight. The triceps is attached to it (see "Muscles and joints").
- [ ] L3 Radius: the shorter forearm bone, on the thumb side. It forms most of the joint with the wrist. At the elbow, its rounded head turns against the ulna (a pivot joint), so the radius can rotate around the ulna to turn the palm up or down. The biceps is attached to it.
- [ ] L4 Two bones in the forearm make the hand both strong and highly mobile: the ulna gives a stable hinge at the elbow, and the radius allows rotation of the hand.

Lower limb (one structure-and-function block)
- [ ] L5 Femur (thigh bone): the longest and strongest bone in the body. Its rounded head fits into the deep socket of the pelvic girdle at the hip; its lower end forms the knee joint (a hinge joint) with the tibia. Its thick shaft of compact bone carries the body's weight, and it gives attachment to the large thigh muscles used in walking, running and jumping.
- [ ] L6 Patella (kneecap): a small, flat, roughly triangular bone at the front of the knee, embedded in the tendon of the quadriceps (the large muscle at the front of the thigh). It protects the front of the knee joint and holds the tendon away from the joint, improving the leverage of the quadriceps when it straightens the leg.
- [ ] L7 Tibia (shinbone): the larger, inner bone of the lower leg (on the big-toe side). It forms the knee joint with the femur and the ankle joint below, and carries almost all the weight passed down from the femur.
- [ ] L8 Fibula: the thin outer bone of the lower leg. It is not part of the knee joint and carries little weight; it provides attachment for muscles of the lower leg and helps form and stabilise the outer side of the ankle.
- [ ] L9 Hands and feet: the wrist, hand, ankle and foot bones are not named in the specification. Label them on diagrams as "wrist bones", "hand bones", "ankle bones" and "foot bones"; many small bones give the hands their fine, flexible movements and the feet a flexible, weight-spreading base.

Limb pattern (one bullet)
- [ ] M1 The arm and the leg are built to the same plan: one bone in the upper part (humerus; femur), two in the lower part (radius and ulna; tibia and fibula), then many small bones in the wrist or ankle and five digits. The same plan in the forelimbs of other vertebrates is evidence of common ancestry (see "Explaining biological phenomena").

Whole skeleton diagram (one bullet; may close the subsection)
- [ ] W1 Diagram: front view of the whole human skeleton, labelling the axial parts (skull, vertebral column, ribs, sternum) and the appendicular parts named in the specification: clavicle, scapula, pelvic girdle, humerus, radius, ulna, femur, patella, tibia and fibula; with wrist, hand, ankle and foot bones labelled as groups.

## 3. Traps

- The pelvic girdle's bone names are explicitly not required; don't list them.
- The radius is on the thumb side, the ulna on the little-finger side. The tibia is the larger, inner (big-toe side) bone; the fibula is the thinner, outer one and is not part of the knee joint.
- The patella is part of the appendicular skeleton (a limb bone), not part of the femur or tibia.
- The clavicle and scapula are appendicular, though they lie on the chest; the sternum and ribs are axial.
- The shoulder is more mobile and less stable than the hip; don't reverse this.
- Joint structure (articular cartilage function, synovial fluid, ligaments) and antagonistic muscle action belong to "Muscles and joints"; B2 and L1–L3 name only where each joint is and its type.
- Bone growth (growth plates, ossification) and disorders (fractures, osteoporosis, arthritis, dislocation) are not on the specification. Don't teach them.
- Homologous structures and evolution belong to "Explaining biological phenomena" and "Evolution: The Origins of Life"; M1 is one bullet.
- The diagram cues in B3 and W1 are the only ones wanted. No other diagram cues.

## 4. What exams ask (topics only)

- Name and label the bones of the arm, leg, and pectoral girdle on a diagram of the skeleton.
- State the function of a named bone (e.g. patella, scapula, femur).
- Compare the pectoral and pelvic girdles in structure and function.
- Explain why long bones are hollow tubes; label a section of a long bone.

## 5. Practice questions to write

1. 6 marks: "Name the two bones of the pectoral girdle and give one function of each." Model answer: P2 (clavicle: strut holding the shoulder out; links arm to sternum) and P3 (scapula: muscle attachment; socket for humerus; mobility).
2. 9 marks: "Compare the structure of the pelvic girdle and the pectoral girdle and relate each to its function." Model answer: G6, with P3–P4 and G2–G3 (loose muscle attachment and shallow socket for mobility; fused to sacrum and deep socket for weight-bearing and stability).
3. 9 marks: "Name the bones of the human leg and relate the structure of any two to their functions." Model answer: femur, patella, tibia, fibula; two of L5–L8 with structure-to-function links; B1 may be used for the femur.
