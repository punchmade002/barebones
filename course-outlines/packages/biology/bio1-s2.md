# Package: bio1-s2 — Science as a global enterprise

- **loId:** `bio1-s2` · **chapterId:** `bio1` · **syllabusRef:** U1.1
- **Syllabus:** "science as a global enterprise that relies on clear communication,
  international conventions, peer review and reproducibility." Outcome U1.1: "appreciate how
  scientists work and how scientific ideas are modified over time." Ordinary and Higher level.
  Each of the four named features gets its own block: what it is, how it works, why science
  needs it, and a real example.
- **Prepared by:** a higher model, 2026-10-22.

## 1. Must cover

Science as a global enterprise (one block)
- [ ] G1 Science is global: scientists in many countries work on the same problems, share data and build on each other's published results, so knowledge is not owned by one country or person and claims are tested by people with different backgrounds and assumptions.
- [ ] G2 Large problems need international teams, shared funding and shared equipment. Example: the Human Genome Project (1990–2003) sequenced the human genome through an international consortium of 20 research groups in six countries (the USA, the UK, Japan, France, Germany and China). Under the Bermuda Principles (1996), its sequence data were released publicly within a day, free for anyone to use.
- [ ] G3 Example of speed through sharing: in January 2020 Chinese scientists published the genome sequence of the new coronavirus (SARS-CoV-2) online, and research groups around the world used it within days to design tests and begin developing vaccines.
- [ ] G4 Funding is international too: e.g. Irish researchers are funded by the state agency Research Ireland (formed in 2024 from Science Foundation Ireland and the Irish Research Council) and by the EU's research programme, Horizon Europe (2021–2027), which funds teams that include several countries. (Funding as a source of bias is in "Recognising bias and evaluating sources".)
- [ ] G5 Science needs four things to work across borders: clear communication, international conventions, peer review and reproducibility (blocks below).

Clear communication (one block)
- [ ] CC1 Scientists communicate mainly through papers in scientific journals, and also at conferences, in review articles and through shared online databases (e.g. genome databases, taught in "Bioinformatics: genome databases").
- [ ] CC2 (assumed) A research paper has a standard structure: title and authors; abstract (short summary); introduction (background and aim or hypothesis); methods (exactly what was done, with materials and conditions); results (data in tables and graphs); discussion (what the results mean, limitations, comparison with other work); references to other work. The methods section must be detailed enough for another scientist to repeat the work, which makes reproducibility possible.
- [ ] CC3 Scientific language must be precise and unambiguous: terms have one agreed meaning (e.g. "theory" in science is not "a guess"), quantities are given with units, and uncertainty is stated rather than hidden. English is the main shared language of international science.
- [ ] CC4 Open access journals make papers free to read for everyone, not only subscribers. Preprints are papers posted online before peer review (e.g. on bioRxiv, launched 2013, and medRxiv, launched 2019, for medical research); they speed up sharing, as in the COVID-19 pandemic, but readers must remember they have not yet been checked.
- [ ] CC5 Poor communication slows science. Example: Gregor Mendel published his results on inheritance in pea plants in 1866, in the journal of a local natural history society in Brünn (now Brno, Czech Republic); they were little read, and their importance was recognised only when other scientists reached the same conclusions independently in 1900.
- [ ] CC6 Communication with the public (news, policy, outreach) is also part of a scientist's work; evaluating media reports is in "Evaluating evidence". How students present their own findings is in "Communicating results".

International conventions (one block)
- [ ] IC1 An international convention is a set of rules or standards agreed by scientists or governments across countries, so that results mean the same everywhere and can be compared and repeated.
- [ ] IC2 SI units (Système International): the agreed units of measurement, e.g. metre (length), kilogram (mass), second (time), mole (amount of substance), kelvin (temperature, with degrees Celsius also widely used); prefixes such as milli- and micro- (e.g. mm, µm, the unit for cell sizes; nm for organelles and molecules).
- [ ] IC3 Binomial nomenclature: each species has a two-part Latin name, genus then species, e.g. Homo sapiens; the genus starts with a capital letter, the species name is lower case, and the name is printed in italics or underlined when handwritten. Carl Linnaeus established the system; the starting point for plant names is his Species Plantarum (1753). One scientific name avoids confusion between common names, which differ between languages and regions. (Classification itself is in "Classification".)
- [ ] IC4 Other naming conventions: human genes have agreed symbols, written in italics (e.g. the CFTR gene, whose faulty alleles cause cystic fibrosis), with the protein written without italics; chemical names and formulas follow agreed rules (e.g. CO₂, C₆H₁₂O₆).
- [ ] IC5 Ethical conventions: international agreements set rules for research. The Declaration of Helsinki (World Medical Association, 1964) sets out ethical principles for research on people, including informed consent and independent ethical review; it followed the Nuremberg Code (1947), written after the trials of Nazi doctors. The Convention on Biological Diversity (1992, agreed at the Rio Earth Summit) commits countries to conserving biodiversity, using it sustainably and sharing fairly the benefits of genetic resources. CITES (1973) controls international trade in endangered species.
- [ ] IC6 Conventions on safety and data, named without detail: agreed standards for laboratory safety signs (hazard symbols) and for depositing data in public databases.

Peer review (one block for the process, one for its strengths and limits)
- [ ] PR1 Peer review is the evaluation of a scientific paper by independent experts in the same field (the author's "peers") before it is published.
- [ ] PR2 The steps: 1. the scientists submit the paper to a journal; 2. the editor checks that it suits the journal and is of reasonable quality, and may reject it straight away; 3. the editor sends it to usually two or three independent experts (reviewers or referees); 4. the reviewers check whether the methods are suitable, the data support the conclusions, the analysis is sound, the work is original and important, ethical rules were followed and other work is properly referenced; 5. they recommend accept, accept with minor revisions, revise and resubmit (major revisions), or reject; 6. the authors revise the paper and reply to each comment; 7. the editor makes the final decision and the paper is published. Diagram: a flowchart of the seven steps, with a loop from "revise" back to "reviewers".
- [ ] PR3 Forms: in single-blind review the reviewers know who the authors are but the authors don't know the reviewers; in double-blind review neither knows the other, which reduces bias from the authors' reputation, gender or country; in open review names (and sometimes reports) are published.
- [ ] PR4 Why it matters: it filters out poor-quality or unsupported work, improves papers before publication, and gives readers some confidence in published work; a peer-reviewed paper is a more reliable source than one that isn't (see "Recognising bias and evaluating sources").
- [ ] PR5 Limitations: reviewers don't normally repeat the experiments, so they can't detect well-hidden fraud or errors in raw data; reviewers can be biased (e.g. against ideas that challenge their own, or towards famous labs); it is slow; reviewers are usually unpaid volunteers; and some low-quality "predatory" journals charge authors to publish while doing little or no real review.
- [ ] PR6 Checking continues after publication: other scientists read, cite and try to repeat the work; errors lead to published corrections, and serious errors or misconduct lead to retraction (the journal withdraws the paper). Example: in 2004 and 2005 Hwang Woo-suk's team published papers in the journal Science claiming to have made stem cells from cloned human embryos; the data were found to be fabricated, and the papers were retracted in 2006. Peer review had not caught the fraud; scrutiny after publication did.

Reproducibility (one block)
- [ ] R1 A result is reproducible if other scientists, working independently in other laboratories, get the same result when they repeat the work, using the same or different methods. (The difference between repeatability and reproducibility, and evaluating data with them, is in "Objectivity and data quality".)
- [ ] R2 Why science relies on it: a single result may be due to chance, error, an unusual sample, bias or fraud; agreement between independent groups shows the result is a real feature of nature; only reproduced findings become accepted knowledge and can support theories (e.g. H. pylori as the cause of ulcers was accepted once others reproduced the findings; see "The nature of scientific knowledge").
- [ ] R3 Reproducibility depends on clear communication: full methods, raw data and materials must be shared so others can repeat the work.
- [ ] R4 Not all published results reproduce: scientists at the company Amgen reported in 2012 that they could confirm the findings of only 6 of 53 "landmark" preclinical cancer studies. Causes include small samples, selective reporting of positive results, pressure to publish, poorly described methods and, rarely, fraud. Responses include sharing data openly, registering study plans in advance and publishing replication studies.

## 3. Traps

- Repeatability vs reproducibility as properties of data, and random and systematic error, belong to "Objectivity and data quality". R1 gives the definition of reproducibility in one sentence and cross-refers.
- Writing a report, graphs and tables for one's own investigation belong to "Communicating results"; CC2 describes a journal paper's structure only.
- How hypotheses, theories and laws develop belongs to "The nature of scientific knowledge"; don't redefine them.
- Media reports and misinformation belong to "Evaluating evidence"; the Wakefield MMR case is taught there, not here.
- Evaluating sources and bias types belong to "Recognising bias and evaluating sources"; PR4 cross-refers.
- Don't say peer review guarantees a paper is correct (PR5).
- Don't call a preprint "published research" without saying it is not peer reviewed.
- The only diagram cue is the peer-review flowchart (PR2). No other diagram cues.
