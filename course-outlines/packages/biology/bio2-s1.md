# Package: bio2-s1 — Questioning and predicting

- **loId:** `bio2-s1` · **chapterId:** `bio2` · **syllabusRef:** U2.1–U2.2
- **Syllabus:** students learn about "questioning and predicting". Outcome 1: "recognise
  questions that are appropriate for scientific investigation". Outcome 2: "pose testable
  hypotheses developed using scientific theories and explanations, and evaluate and compare
  strategies for investigating hypotheses." Ordinary and Higher level (Unifying Strand, no bold
  outcomes; nothing in this subsection is Higher Level only).
- **Prepared by:** a higher model, 2026-10-22. The spec glossary defines "recognise" as
  identifying what is critical to understanding a situation, "pose" as "put forward for
  consideration" and "evaluate" as making judgements about ideas, solutions or methods, so the
  strategies block must give real advantages and limitations of each strategy, not a list.

## 1. Must cover

Questions that science can investigate (one block)
- [ ] Q1 A question is appropriate for scientific investigation when it can be answered by collecting evidence through observation or measurement of the natural world. It must refer to things that can be observed, measured or counted, and the answer must be capable of being tested against data.
- [ ] Q2 Questions science can't answer by investigation: questions of value, taste, morality or belief, e.g. "Is it wrong to keep animals in zoos?", "Which flower is the most beautiful?" Science can supply evidence that informs such a question (e.g. how captivity affects an animal's stress hormones), but evidence alone can't settle it. The ethical side of scientific work is covered in "Designing and conducting investigations".
- [ ] Q3 Broad questions must be narrowed into an investigable one by naming the factor to be changed and the effect to be measured. Example: "Does temperature affect enzymes?" becomes "How does temperature affect the rate at which catalase breaks down hydrogen peroxide?"; "Does salt affect plants?" becomes "How does the concentration of a salt solution affect the change in mass of potato cylinders?"
- [ ] Q4 A good investigable question is specific, names one independent and one dependent variable (terms defined in "Designing and conducting investigations"), can be carried out with available equipment in the time available, and is safe and ethical to carry out.

From observation to prediction (one block, in sequence)
- [ ] P1 (assumed) Scientific investigation usually begins with an observation or with existing knowledge that raises a question; the question leads to a hypothesis, the hypothesis leads to a prediction, and the prediction is tested by experiment or further observation. The general account of how scientists work and of hypothesis, theory and law is in "The nature of scientific knowledge"; give only the sequence here.
- [ ] P2 Hypothesis: a proposed, testable explanation for an observation, or a proposed relationship between two variables, based on existing scientific knowledge. It explains why something might happen. Example: "Increasing the temperature increases the rate of catalase activity, because molecules move faster and collide more often with the active site, up to the temperature at which the enzyme denatures."
- [ ] P3 Prediction: a specific statement of the result that will be observed if the hypothesis is correct, often written "If … then …". It says what will happen in this particular experiment, not why. Example: "If potato cylinders are placed in sucrose solutions of increasing concentration, then their percentage change in mass will decrease, becoming negative in the more concentrated solutions."
- [ ] P4 The exam distinction: the hypothesis is the explanation (general, gives a reason); the prediction is the expected observation (specific, measurable outcome of the planned test). One hypothesis can generate several predictions.
- [ ] P5 Null hypothesis: a statement that the independent variable has no effect on the dependent variable, e.g. "The concentration of sucrose has no effect on the change in mass of potato cylinders." An experiment tests whether the data give reason to reject it.

What makes a hypothesis testable (one block)
- [ ] T1 A testable hypothesis is falsifiable: it is possible to imagine a result that would show it to be wrong. "Plants grow better when people talk to them because they enjoy it" is not testable as stated (enjoyment can't be measured); "Plants exposed to sound grow taller than plants kept in silence" is testable.
- [ ] T2 It names variables that can be measured or controlled, and it states the direction of the expected effect (increase or decrease), not just "will have an effect".
- [ ] T3 It is developed from scientific theories and explanations, not a guess: the reasoning comes from known biology. Examples of the theory a hypothesis can draw on: collision theory and denaturation for enzyme rate (detail in "Enzymes"); movement of water from a dilute solution to a more concentrated one across a partially permeable membrane for osmosis (detail in "Transport Across Membranes"); light as the energy source for photosynthesis for a light-intensity investigation.
- [ ] T4 A hypothesis is supported or not supported by data; a single investigation never proves it. Whether data support it is judged at the conclusion stage (see "Producing and analysing data").

Strategies for investigating hypotheses: evaluate and compare (one comparison block, paired points)
- [ ] S1 Controlled laboratory experiment: the investigator changes one variable and controls the others. Strength: the strongest evidence that a change in one variable causes a change in another. Limitations: artificial conditions may not match the real world (an enzyme in a test tube vs in a living cell); some variables can't be changed in a lab for ethical or practical reasons.
- [ ] S2 Field study or survey (observational study): the investigator observes and measures organisms where they live without changing anything, e.g. comparing the number of a plant species in sun and shade. Strength: real conditions; can study large areas and many organisms. Limitations: many variables can't be controlled, so they must be measured and recorded; can show a correlation but not prove a cause. Sampling methods belong to "Surveys" (Investigating an Ecosystem chapter); don't teach them here.
- [ ] S3 Analysis of secondary data (data collected by somebody else), e.g. published datasets, databases of genetic information, government health statistics. Strength: large data sets, long time periods, or data the student could never collect; cheap and quick. Limitations: the user didn't control how the data were collected and may not know their quality; the source may lack detail or show bias (detail in "Recognising bias and evaluating sources").
- [ ] S4 Modelling and simulation: using a physical model or computer simulation to test how a system behaves, e.g. a simulation of population growth. Strength: fast, safe, can test conditions impossible in reality. Limitation: only as good as its assumptions; predictions must be checked against real data (detail in "Models").
- [ ] S5 Trials with human participants (e.g. testing a treatment): needed for questions about human health. Strength: direct evidence about people. Limitations: ethical restrictions, need for informed consent, high cost, large natural variation between people, so large samples and blinding are needed (blinding in "Objectivity and data quality"; consent in "Designing and conducting investigations").
- [ ] S6 Criteria for comparing strategies: whether it can show cause and effect or only correlation; how many variables can be controlled; how realistic the conditions are; sample size achievable; time, cost and equipment; safety; ethics. Often the best answer uses more than one strategy, e.g. a field survey suggests a hypothesis and a lab experiment tests it.

## 3. Traps

- Hypothesis vs prediction is the key distinction; students often write a prediction ("the mass will decrease") when asked for a hypothesis, or a hypothesis with no reason.
- Hypothesis vs theory vs law, and how scientific ideas change over time, belong to "The nature of scientific knowledge". Don't define theory or law here.
- Don't say a hypothesis is "an educated guess" without adding that it is a testable explanation based on scientific knowledge.
- Don't say a hypothesis is "proved" or "proven true".
- Variables, control and fair test are defined in "Designing and conducting investigations"; use the terms here without defining them.
- Correlation vs causation is taught in "Producing and analysing data"; S2 states only that a survey shows correlation, not cause.
- Don't write the catalase or osmosis experiments in full; they belong to their own chapters.
- Diagram: one flow diagram, observation → question → hypothesis → prediction → test → data → conclusion (hypothesis supported or rejected, leading to a new question). No other diagram cues.

## 4. What exams ask (topics only)

Past papers are on the previous syllabus; these topics remain on the specification:
- What is a hypothesis? (A proposed or possible explanation for an observation that can be tested.)
- Distinguish a hypothesis from a prediction, or write one for a described experiment.
- New to this specification, expect: decide whether a given question can be investigated scientifically and justify; write a testable hypothesis from given background theory; compare a laboratory and a field approach to the same question.

## 5. Practice questions to write

1. 6 marks: "Explain the difference between a hypothesis and a prediction, using an example from an investigation into the effect of sucrose concentration on potato cylinders." Model answer: P2, P3 and P4 with the osmosis example; must give the reason (osmosis) in the hypothesis and the measurable outcome in the prediction.
2. 9 marks: "A student asks 'Does pollution harm plants?' (a) Explain why this is not yet a good question for investigation. (b) Rewrite it as a testable question. (c) Write a hypothesis for it." Model answer: Q3–Q4 (too broad, no named variables or measurement); a question naming one pollutant, its concentration and a measurable plant response; a hypothesis with direction and a biological reason (T1–T3). Mustn't claim the hypothesis will be proved.
3. 12 marks: "Evaluate the use of a laboratory experiment and a field survey to investigate whether light intensity affects the growth of a plant species." Model answer: S1 and S2 paired (control vs realism, cause vs correlation), S6 criteria, and a judgement that the two together give the strongest evidence.
