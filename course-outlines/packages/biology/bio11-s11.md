## 1. Must cover

What sex-linkage is (one block)
- [ ] L1 Building on the definition in "Key concepts of information transfer" (don't restate it in full): sex-linked genes are almost all on the X chromosome (X-linked), because the Y carries very few genes. A sex-linked trait is a trait controlled by such a gene. The Y has no matching allele for most X-linked genes.
- [ ] L2 Females (XX) have two copies of each X-linked gene, so they can be homozygous or heterozygous; a heterozygous female with one recessive allele is a carrier and does not show the trait. Males (XY) have only one copy, so whatever allele their single X carries is expressed: a male cannot be a carrier, and one recessive allele is enough for him to show the trait.
- [ ] L3 Consequences: X-linked recessive conditions are much more common in males than in females; a female shows the condition only if she inherits the recessive allele from both parents (an affected father and a carrier or affected mother).
- [ ] L4 A father passes his X to all his daughters and his Y to all his sons. So an affected father never passes an X-linked condition to his sons, and all his daughters are at least carriers. A son's X-linked allele always comes from his mother.
- [ ] L5 Symbols: write the allele as a superscript on the X, and the Y with no allele. Colour blindness: Xᴮ = normal colour vision, Xᵇ = colour blind. Haemophilia: Xᴴ = normal clotting, Xʰ = haemophilia. Genotypes: female XᴮXᴮ (normal), XᴮXᵇ (carrier), XᵇXᵇ (colour blind); male XᴮY (normal), XᵇY (colour blind). Use the letters a question gives.

The two named examples (one block)
- [ ] E1 Red-green colour blindness: an X-linked recessive condition in which a person has difficulty telling red and green apart. The genes for the red- and green-sensitive light-absorbing pigments of the cone cells of the retina are on the X chromosome; the recessive allele gives a pigment that is missing or works abnormally. It affects about 1 in 12 men (8%) and about 1 in 200 women (0.5%) of northern European ancestry. It is detected with coloured-dot test charts (Ishihara plates). It is not treatable, but most people adapt to it.
- [ ] E2 Haemophilia: an X-linked recessive condition in which blood does not clot properly because a clotting protein is missing or faulty; haemophilia A lacks clotting factor VIII, haemophilia B lacks factor IX. Bleeding after injury lasts much longer and bleeding can occur into joints and muscles. Haemophilia A affects about 1 in 5,000 male births. It is treated by injections of the missing clotting factor, now mostly produced by genetically engineered cells (detail in "Genetic engineering").
- [ ] E3 A historical example: Queen Victoria (1819–1901) was a carrier of haemophilia. Her son Leopold had haemophilia, and through two of her daughters, who were carriers, it passed into the royal families of Russia (Tsarevich Alexei, son of Tsar Nicholas II) and Spain. Use only these facts.
- [ ] E4 Duchenne muscular dystrophy is another X-linked recessive condition (lack of the muscle protein dystrophin, causing progressive muscle weakness), almost always in boys. One bullet; for questions asking for a sex-linked condition "other than haemophilia".

Worked crosses (one block per cross, every line of the standard layout; use colour blindness for C1, C3 and haemophilia for C2, C4)
- [ ] C1 Carrier female × normal male. Parents' phenotypes: normal-vision (carrier) female × normal-vision male. Genotypes: XᴮXᵇ × XᴮY. Gametes: Xᴮ, Xᵇ × Xᴮ, Y. Punnett square: XᴮXᴮ, XᴮY / XᴮXᵇ, XᵇY. Offspring: 1 normal female (XᴮXᴮ) : 1 carrier female (XᴮXᵇ) : 1 normal male (XᴮY) : 1 colour-blind male (XᵇY). So half the sons are expected to be colour blind and half the daughters carriers; no daughters are colour blind; 25% of all children are colour blind.
- [ ] C2 Normal female × affected male. Genotypes: XᴴXᴴ × XʰY. Gametes: Xᴴ × Xʰ, Y. Offspring: XᴴXʰ (carrier daughters) and XᴴY (normal sons), 1 : 1. All daughters are carriers; no children have haemophilia.
- [ ] C3 Carrier female × colour-blind male. Genotypes: XᴮXᵇ × XᵇY. Gametes: Xᴮ, Xᵇ × Xᵇ, Y. Offspring: XᴮXᵇ (carrier female), XᵇXᵇ (colour-blind female), XᴮY (normal male), XᵇY (colour-blind male), 1 : 1 : 1 : 1. Half the daughters and half the sons are colour blind. This is how a colour-blind daughter can arise.
- [ ] C4 Affected female × normal male. Genotypes: XʰXʰ × XᴴY. Gametes: Xʰ × Xᴴ, Y. Offspring: XᴴXʰ (carrier daughters) and XʰY (sons with haemophilia). All sons are affected; all daughters are carriers. (Females with haemophilia are very rare; this cross is used to show the pattern.)
- [ ] C5 Diagram: the Punnett square for C1, with the mother's gametes Xᴮ and Xᵇ on one side and the father's Xᴮ and Y on the other, each box labelled with genotype and phenotype (normal female, carrier female, normal male, colour-blind male).

Pedigrees (one Skills block)
- [ ] P1 (assumed) A pedigree is a family-tree diagram showing which members of a family have a trait. Symbols: square = male; circle = female; shaded = affected; half-shaded (or a dot in the centre) = carrier; a horizontal line joining a male and a female = parents; a vertical line down to a horizontal line = their children, in birth order from left to right. Generations are numbered I, II, III and individuals 1, 2, 3 within each.
- [ ] P2 Signs of an X-linked recessive trait in a pedigree: many more affected males than females; affected males usually have unaffected parents (the mother is a carrier); the trait can skip a generation, passing from an affected man through his carrier daughters to his grandsons; never passed from father to son.
- [ ] P3 Method for solving a pedigree: 1. write XY for every male and XX for every female; 2. fill in the alleles of affected males (XʰY) and unaffected males (XᴴY), which follow directly from their phenotype; 3. any daughter of an affected male is at least a carrier; any mother of an affected son is a carrier or affected; 4. use these to fill in the remaining females; 5. then work any further cross with a Punnett square.
- [ ] P4 Diagram: a three-generation pedigree of an X-linked recessive trait: generation I, a normal man and a carrier woman; generation II, an affected son, a normal son, a carrier daughter and a normal daughter; generation III, the carrier daughter's children with a normal man, including an affected grandson. Label the symbols and the generations.

Y-linked traits (one bullet)
- [ ] Y1 Genes on the Y chromosome (e.g. SRY, in "Sex determination") are passed only from father to son and are found only in males. No other Y-linked example.

## 3. Traps

- Every block is Higher Level only; mark every heading.
- Write the Y with no allele (XᵇY, not XᵇYᵇ). Males are never "carriers" of an X-linked trait.
- Gametes carry one sex chromosome with its allele: the gametes of XᴮXᵇ are Xᴮ and Xᵇ; of XᴮY, Xᴮ and Y.
- Give ratios for the right group: "half the sons" is not "half the children". State whether a percentage is of sons, daughters or all children.
- Sex-linkage is not sex determination, and sex-linked does not mean the trait affects only one sex.
- Linkage between autosomal genes belongs to "Linkage".
- Don't teach X-inactivation, dominant X-linked conditions or the genetics of other types of colour blindness.
- Diagram cues: only C5 and P4. No other diagram cues.

## 4. What exams ask (topics only)

- Explain the term sex-linkage; name a sex-linked condition (and one other than haemophilia).
- Explain why sex-linked recessive conditions are more common in males.
- Using a genetic cross, give the genotypes and phenotypes of children of a carrier mother and a normal or affected father, with probabilities.
- From a pedigree of haemophilia or colour blindness, give the genotypes of named individuals and the chance of a child of a given couple being affected or a carrier.

## 5. Practice questions to write

1. 6 marks, Higher: "Explain why red-green colour blindness is more common in males than in females." Model answer: L1–L3 (gene on the X; males one X, no matching allele on Y; one recessive allele expressed; females need two).
2. 12 marks, Higher: "A woman who is a carrier of haemophilia has children with a man with normal blood clotting. Using a genetic cross, give the genotypes and phenotypes of the possible children, and the probability that a son will have haemophilia." Model answer: C1's layout with the haemophilia symbols (XᴴXʰ × XᴴY); four offspring types; 50% of sons, 25% of all children.
3. 9 marks, Higher: "A colour-blind man and a woman with normal vision whose father was colour blind have a daughter. Give the parents' genotypes and the probability that the daughter is colour blind." Model answer: P3 reasoning: the woman must be XᴮXᵇ (from her father's Xᵇ), the man XᵇY; C3: half the daughters XᵇXᵇ, so the probability is 1/2.
