## 1. Must cover

Rate and how it is measured (one block)
- [ ] R1 The rate of an enzyme-catalysed reaction is the amount of substrate converted into product per unit time, e.g. cm³ of oxygen produced per minute. It can be measured either by how fast a product appears or by how fast the substrate disappears.
- [ ] R2 Two standard school reactions: catalase breaks down hydrogen peroxide (2H₂O₂ → 2H₂O + O₂), and the rate is measured by the oxygen released, as foam or as a volume of gas; amylase digests starch to maltose, and the rate is measured by the time taken for the starch to disappear, tested with iodine.
- [ ] R3 Calculating rate: rate = amount of product ÷ time (e.g. 30 cm³ of foam in 2 minutes = 15 cm³ per minute). Where the time taken to reach an end-point is measured, rate = 1 ÷ time (e.g. starch gone in 120 s gives a rate of 1/120 = 0.0083 s⁻¹; often written 1000/time to give whole numbers). A shorter time means a faster rate.
- [ ] R4 Measure the rate at the start of the reaction (the initial rate, e.g. in the first one or two minutes), because the reaction slows as substrate is used up.
- [ ] R5 Presenting primary data: a results table with the independent variable in the first column, the repeat readings, the mean and the rate, each column headed with its unit; a graph with the independent variable on the x-axis and the rate on the y-axis, points plotted accurately and joined by a smooth curve or line of best fit.

Enzymes, equipment and safety common to all the investigations (one block)
- [ ] E1 Catalase is found in almost all cells that use oxygen, where it breaks down hydrogen peroxide, a toxic by-product of metabolism. Good sources: celery, potato, liver and yeast. A catalase extract is made by blending a fixed mass of chopped celery (e.g. 20 g) with distilled water (e.g. 100 cm³) and filtering it through muslin or filter paper; one batch is made and used for every test.
- [ ] E2 Amylase is used as a commercial (e.g. fungal) amylase solution, made up at a fixed concentration; starch solution (e.g. 1%) is its substrate. (Saliva contains amylase but is avoided for hygiene reasons.)
- [ ] E3 Equipment: 100 cm³ graduated cylinders, syringes or graduated pipettes for measuring volumes, test tubes and boiling tubes, water baths (thermostatically controlled electric baths, or beakers of water warmed with kettle water or cooled with ice, checked with a thermometer), thermometer, stopwatch, blender or mortar and pestle, knife and chopping board, electronic balance, filter funnel, spotting tile and droppers, conical flask with bung and delivery tube, trough, labels.
- [ ] E4 Reagents: hydrogen peroxide solution (one stock of fixed concentration), buffer solutions or buffer tablets of known pH, washing-up liquid, distilled water, starch solution, amylase solution, iodine solution.
- [ ] E5 A buffer is a solution that keeps the pH constant even when acids or alkalis are produced or added. A buffer at pH 7 (or at the enzyme's optimum) is added in every investigation except the pH investigation, so that pH is a controlled variable.
- [ ] E6 A drop of washing-up liquid traps the oxygen released by catalase as a foam, so its volume can be read in a graduated cylinder; the same amount is added every time.
- [ ] E7 Reaching temperature (equilibration): the enzyme and the substrate are placed in the same water bath in separate containers for about 5 minutes before they are mixed, so the reaction starts at the chosen temperature.
- [ ] E8 A control in each investigation uses enzyme that has been boiled (denatured), or distilled water in place of the enzyme, with everything else the same. It shows that the oxygen (or the loss of starch) is caused by the enzyme and not by the substrate breaking down on its own.
- [ ] H1 Safety: hydrogen peroxide is an oxidising agent that irritates or burns the eyes and skin: wear safety glasses (and gloves), measure it with a syringe, and wash any splash off with plenty of water.
- [ ] H2 Buffers of very low or very high pH are irritants; wear safety glasses.
- [ ] H3 Use tongs or a test-tube holder and take care with boiling water and hot water baths; keep electrical water baths and blenders away from spilt water.
- [ ] H4 Take care with knives and blender blades. If liver is used, treat it as raw meat: wear gloves, keep it off benches used for other work and wash hands afterwards.
- [ ] H5 Iodine solution stains skin and clothes. Never eat or drink in the laboratory.

Investigation 1: substrate concentration (block 1, plan and method)
- [ ] SC1 Aim: to investigate the effect of substrate (hydrogen peroxide) concentration on the rate of catalase activity. Hypothesis: "Increasing the concentration of hydrogen peroxide increases the rate of oxygen production, because more substrate molecules collide with active sites per second, until all the active sites are occupied and the rate levels off."
- [ ] SC2 Independent variable: hydrogen peroxide concentration, made by diluting the stock with distilled water to 0%, 20%, 40%, 60%, 80% and 100% of the stock (e.g. 2 cm³ stock + 8 cm³ water = 20%), always to the same total volume (e.g. 10 cm³). Dependent variable: volume of oxygen collected in the first minute (cm³), giving the rate in cm³ per minute. Controlled variables: volume of the same celery extract (enzyme concentration), temperature (water bath at e.g. 25 °C), pH (same volume of pH 7 buffer), total volume of solution, timing, apparatus.
- [ ] SC3 Control: the 100% hydrogen peroxide with boiled extract (or with water in place of extract), which gives little or no oxygen; the 0% tube also shows that no oxygen forms without substrate.
- [ ] SC4 Method: 1. Fill a graduated cylinder with water and turn it upside down in a trough of water, with no air at the top. 2. Put 10 cm³ of the first hydrogen peroxide dilution and 2 cm³ of pH 7 buffer in a conical flask (or boiling tube) in the water bath, with 2 cm³ of celery extract in a separate tube beside it, for about 5 minutes. 3. Place the end of the delivery tube under the mouth of the upturned cylinder. 4. Add the extract to the flask, immediately put in the bung with the delivery tube and start the stopwatch. 5. Read the volume of oxygen collected in the cylinder after 1 minute (or every 30 seconds for 3 minutes). 6. Rinse the flask and repeat for each concentration. 7. Do each concentration three times and calculate the mean rate.
- [ ] SC5 Alternatives (one bullet): a gas syringe connected to the flask measures the oxygen volume more accurately than collection over water; or the foam method (P4) can be used; or filter-paper discs soaked in catalase extract are dropped into each concentration and the time for each disc to rise to the surface (buoyed by oxygen bubbles) is timed, with rate = 1 ÷ time.
- [ ] SC6 Diagram: a conical flask in a water bath with a bung and delivery tube leading under an upturned graduated cylinder full of water in a trough, labelled hydrogen peroxide + buffer + catalase extract, water bath, thermometer, delivery tube, oxygen collecting, trough of water.

Investigation 1: substrate concentration (block 2, results and explanation)
- [ ] SC7 Expected results: no oxygen at 0%; at low concentrations the rate rises steeply, roughly in proportion to concentration; at high concentrations it rises less and levels off (a plateau). The boiled-enzyme control gives little or no oxygen.
- [ ] SC8 Explanation of the rise: more substrate molecules in the same volume means more collisions with active sites per second, so more enzyme–substrate complexes form and more product is made per minute.
- [ ] SC9 Explanation of the plateau: at high substrate concentration every active site is occupied at any moment (the enzyme is saturated), so the enzyme is working at its maximum rate; adding more substrate cannot increase the rate. The amount of enzyme is now the limiting factor; adding more enzyme would raise the rate again. Within the range used in a school investigation the plateau may not be reached if the enzyme is in excess.
- [ ] SC10 Diagram (graph): rate on the y-axis, substrate concentration on the x-axis; a curve rising from the origin, steep at first then levelling off, with the plateau labelled "all active sites occupied (saturated)".
- [ ] SC11 Conclusion: state the trend shown by the mean rates, whether it supports the hypothesis, and identify the concentration above which the rate stopped increasing, if one was found.

Investigation 2: enzyme concentration (block 1, plan and method)
- [ ] EC1 Aim: to investigate the effect of enzyme (amylase) concentration on the rate at which starch is digested. Hypothesis: "Increasing the concentration of amylase increases the rate of starch digestion, because more active sites are available to bind starch molecules, as long as starch is in excess."
- [ ] EC2 Independent variable: amylase concentration, made by diluting the stock amylase solution with distilled water to 0%, 25%, 50%, 75% and 100% of the stock, always to the same volume. Dependent variable: time taken for the starch to disappear (the iodine test no longer turns blue-black), converted to rate = 1 ÷ time. Controlled variables: volume and concentration of starch solution, volume of amylase solution, temperature (water bath at e.g. 37 °C), pH (buffer at pH 7), volume of iodine in each well, sampling interval.
- [ ] EC3 Control: starch solution with distilled water (0% amylase) or boiled amylase in place of the amylase; it stays blue-black with iodine for the whole investigation, showing the starch does not disappear without active enzyme.
- [ ] EC4 Method: 1. Put a drop of iodine solution into each well of a spotting tile. 2. Put 5 cm³ of starch solution and 1 cm³ of pH 7 buffer into a test tube, and 2 cm³ of the first amylase dilution into another, and stand both in the water bath for 5 minutes. 3. Pour the amylase into the starch, mix and start the stopwatch. 4. Every 30 seconds, take one drop of the mixture with a clean dropper and add it to the next well of iodine. 5. Note the time at which a drop first leaves the iodine yellow-brown (no blue-black): this is the end-point, when all the starch has been digested. 6. Repeat for each amylase concentration, three times each, and calculate the mean time and the rate.
- [ ] EC5 Alternative (one bullet): with catalase, vary the volume of celery extract (e.g. 0.5, 1, 1.5, 2, 2.5 cm³, each made up to the same total volume with distilled water) or the number of equal-sized celery discs, and measure the foam (P4) or oxygen volume (SC4) in a fixed time.
- [ ] EC6 Diagram: a spotting tile with a row of wells, the first wells blue-black and the later wells yellow-brown, labelled with sampling times; and the starch–amylase tube in a water bath with a thermometer.

Investigation 2: enzyme concentration (block 2, results and explanation)
- [ ] EC7 Expected results: the higher the amylase concentration, the shorter the time for the starch to disappear, so the faster the rate; the 0% and boiled controls never reach the end-point. A graph of rate against enzyme concentration is a straight line through the origin: doubling the enzyme roughly doubles the rate.
- [ ] EC8 Explanation: more enzyme molecules means more active sites, so more substrate molecules can be bound and converted at the same time, and more product is made per unit time.
- [ ] EC9 The straight line continues only while substrate is in excess. If the enzyme concentration becomes very high, substrate becomes the limiting factor and the line levels off.
- [ ] EC10 Diagram (graph): rate on the y-axis, enzyme concentration on the x-axis; a straight line rising from the origin, labelled "substrate in excess".

Investigation 3: pH (block 1, plan and method)
- [ ] P1 Aim: to investigate the effect of pH on the rate of catalase activity. Hypothesis: "Catalase has an optimum pH at which its rate is highest, and the rate decreases at pH values above and below it, because the shape of the active site is changed."
- [ ] P2 Independent variable: pH, set with buffer solutions (e.g. pH 4, 5, 6, 7, 8, 9 and 10). Dependent variable: volume of foam produced in 2 minutes (cm³), giving rate in cm³ per minute. Controlled variables: temperature (water bath at e.g. 25 °C), volume and concentration of hydrogen peroxide, volume of the same celery extract (or mass of celery), volume of buffer, one drop of washing-up liquid, time, size of cylinder.
- [ ] P3 Control: at each pH (or at pH 7), boiled celery extract (or water) in place of the extract, giving no foam.
- [ ] P4 Method: 1. Into a 100 cm³ graduated cylinder put 10 cm³ of pH 4 buffer, 2 cm³ of celery extract (or a fixed mass of chopped celery) and one drop of washing-up liquid. 2. Stand the cylinder in the 25 °C water bath, with a boiling tube of hydrogen peroxide beside it, for 5 minutes. 3. Note the starting volume of liquid in the cylinder. 4. Add 2 cm³ of hydrogen peroxide with a syringe and start the stopwatch. 5. After 2 minutes, read the volume at the top of the foam, at eye level; the foam volume is this reading minus the starting volume. 6. Wash the cylinder and repeat with each buffer, three times each, and calculate the mean rate.
- [ ] P5 Diagram: a graduated cylinder standing in a water bath with a thermometer, containing buffer, celery extract and washing-up liquid at the bottom and a column of foam above, with a syringe adding hydrogen peroxide; label each part and the foam height being read.

Investigation 3: pH (block 2, results and explanation)
- [ ] P6 Expected results: the most foam forms at around pH 7 (the optimum for celery and liver catalase), with less in more acidic and more alkaline buffers and very little at the extremes. The control gives no foam.
- [ ] P7 Explanation: the optimum pH is the pH at which the active site has the shape and charges that best fit the substrate. Hydrogen ions in more acidic or alkaline conditions change the charges in the active site and the bonds holding the enzyme's shape, so the substrate fits less well and fewer enzyme–substrate complexes form. Small changes from the optimum are reversible; extreme pH denatures the enzyme permanently.
- [ ] P8 Each enzyme's optimum suits where it works (secondary data, see X2): pepsin in the acid stomach has an optimum of about pH 2; salivary amylase in the mouth about pH 7; trypsin in the alkaline small intestine about pH 8.
- [ ] P9 Diagram (graph): rate on the y-axis, pH on the x-axis; a peaked (bell-shaped) curve with its peak labelled "optimum pH" at about 7 for catalase; optionally a second curve peaking at about 2, labelled pepsin.

Investigation 4: temperature (block 1, plan and method)
- [ ] T1 Aim: to investigate the effect of temperature on the rate of catalase activity. Hypothesis: "As temperature increases, the rate of catalase activity increases up to an optimum temperature, because molecules move faster and collide more often; above the optimum the rate falls sharply because the enzyme is denatured."
- [ ] T2 Independent variable: temperature, set with water baths at e.g. 0 °C (ice water), 10, 20, 30, 40, 50, 60 and 70 °C, each checked with a thermometer. Dependent variable: volume of foam in 2 minutes (cm³), giving rate in cm³ per minute. Controlled variables: pH (same volume of pH 7 buffer), volume and concentration of hydrogen peroxide, volume of the same celery extract, one drop of washing-up liquid, time, size of cylinder.
- [ ] T3 Control: boiled celery extract (or water) in place of the extract at each temperature, giving no foam.
- [ ] T4 Method: as in P4, but with pH 7 buffer in every cylinder, and with each cylinder (buffer, extract and washing-up liquid) and its tube of hydrogen peroxide left in a water bath at the chosen temperature for 5 minutes before the peroxide is added; check the bath temperature with the thermometer during the 2 minutes. Repeat three times at each temperature and calculate the mean rate. The same method works with amylase and starch (EC4), timing the starch's disappearance at each temperature.
- [ ] T5 Diagram: the P5 set-up, with a row of water baths at different labelled temperatures.

Investigation 4: temperature (block 2, results and explanation)
- [ ] T6 Expected results: little foam at 0 °C; the rate increases as the temperature rises, to a maximum at the optimum temperature; above the optimum the rate falls steeply, with little or no activity at the highest temperatures tested. The graph is not symmetrical: a gradual rise and a steep fall. For human enzymes the optimum is about 37 °C, body temperature.
- [ ] T7 Explanation of the rise: as temperature increases, enzyme and substrate molecules have more kinetic energy, move faster and collide more often, and more collisions have enough energy to react, so more enzyme–substrate complexes form per second. Over the lower part of the range, the rate roughly doubles for each 10 °C rise.
- [ ] T8 Explanation of the fall: above the optimum, the enzyme molecule vibrates so much that the bonds holding its shape break, the active site changes shape, and the substrate no longer fits: the enzyme is denatured. As temperature rises further, more enzyme molecules are denatured, so the rate falls even though collisions are more frequent.
- [ ] T9 At low temperature the enzyme is inactive, not denatured: the molecules move too slowly for many collisions, but the active site keeps its shape, so activity returns when the enzyme is warmed (shown in D7).
- [ ] T10 Diagram (graph): rate on the y-axis, temperature (°C) on the x-axis; a curve rising gradually to a peak labelled "optimum temperature" and then falling steeply to zero, with the falling part labelled "enzyme denatured".

Investigation 5: heat denaturation (one or two blocks)
- [ ] D1 Aim: to investigate the effect of heat denaturation on the activity of catalase. Denaturation is the permanent loss of an enzyme's shape, and so of its active site and its activity, caused by high temperature or extreme pH. Hypothesis: "Catalase that has been boiled will not break down hydrogen peroxide, even when cooled, because its active site has been permanently changed."
- [ ] D2 Independent variable: whether the extract has been boiled (heat-treated) or not. Dependent variable: volume of foam in 2 minutes (cm³). Controlled variables: both extracts tested at the same temperature (e.g. 25 °C) and pH (pH 7 buffer), with the same volumes of extract, buffer and hydrogen peroxide, the same drop of washing-up liquid and the same time.
- [ ] D3 Control: the unboiled extract, which shows how much foam active catalase produces under the same conditions.
- [ ] D4 Method: 1. Divide the celery extract between two labelled boiling tubes, A and B. 2. Place tube A in a boiling water bath for 10 minutes; leave tube B at room temperature. 3. Cool tube A, then stand both tubes in the 25 °C water bath for 5 minutes, so the boiled extract is tested at the same temperature as the unboiled. 4. Set up two graduated cylinders, each with 10 cm³ of pH 7 buffer, a drop of washing-up liquid and 2 cm³ of extract A or B. 5. Add 2 cm³ of hydrogen peroxide to each and read the foam after 2 minutes. 6. Repeat three times.
- [ ] D5 Expected results: the boiled extract gives little or no foam; the unboiled extract gives a large column of foam. (Any small amount of foam from the boiled extract comes from hydrogen peroxide breaking down slowly on its own, or from catalase that was not fully denatured.)
- [ ] D6 Conclusion: boiling denatured the catalase; its activity did not return on cooling, so the change is permanent. This supports the hypothesis and explains why boiled enzyme is used as the control in the other investigations.
- [ ] D7 Extension, low temperature compared (one bullet): extract kept in ice water for 10 minutes and then warmed to 25 °C gives as much foam as unchilled extract, showing that cooling makes an enzyme inactive without denaturing it, whereas heating denatures it.
- [ ] D8 The same investigation works with amylase: boiled amylase added to starch leaves the iodine test blue-black at every sampling time; unboiled amylase digests the starch so the iodine stays yellow-brown.

Secondary data (one block)
- [ ] X1 Secondary data are data collected by someone else: results pooled from other groups in the class, published tables, textbooks or research papers, or online databases. The specification requires conclusions supported by both primary and secondary data.
- [ ] X2 Uses: compare your optimum with published values (e.g. P8: pepsin about pH 2, salivary amylase about pH 7, trypsin about pH 8; human enzymes about 37 °C); check whether your trend agrees with other groups' (reproducibility); extend a conclusion beyond the range you could test.
- [ ] X3 Secondary data on enzymes from extreme environments support the link between structure and conditions: Taq polymerase, from the hot-spring bacterium Thermus aquaticus, works best at about 72 °C and is not denatured at temperatures that denature human enzymes.
- [ ] X4 When using secondary data, record its source, check it was measured under comparable conditions (same enzyme source, substrate, pH or temperature) and judge whether the source is reliable (see "Recognising bias and evaluating sources").

Sources of error and reliability (one block)
- [ ] F1 Foam volume is imprecise: bubbles burst, the top of the foam is uneven, and foam may stick to the cylinder walls. Read at eye level at exactly the same time each run; collecting the oxygen in a gas syringe is more accurate.
- [ ] F2 Gas is lost in the time between adding the enzyme and inserting the bung, or through leaks; some oxygen dissolves in the water when collected over water.
- [ ] F3 Enzyme activity varies between celery samples and falls as an extract stands, so make one batch, keep it cool, and use it quickly for all the tests; a fresh batch for a repeat can give different values.
- [ ] F4 Water-bath temperatures drift, especially in beakers of hot water or ice; check with a thermometer throughout or use a thermostatically controlled bath. The reaction mixture may not reach the bath temperature if equilibration is too short.
- [ ] F5 Hydrogen peroxide slowly breaks down on standing, especially in light, so its concentration may fall during the session: use one fresh stock kept in a dark bottle.
- [ ] F6 The iodine end-point is a judgement of colour, and sampling every 30 seconds means the time is only known to within 30 seconds; sample more often near the end-point, compare against a control well, or use a colorimeter.
- [ ] F7 Measure volumes with syringes or graduated pipettes rather than by eye, and use a clean syringe or dropper for each solution to avoid contamination.
- [ ] F8 Repeat each value three times, calculate the mean, and identify and investigate any anomalous result; pool class data to check reproducibility. Use at least five values of the independent variable so the shape of the graph (peak or plateau) can be seen.

## 3. Traps

- Rate is product formed (or substrate used) per unit time, not the total amount of product. A longer time to the end-point means a slower rate.
- The temperature graph is not a symmetrical bell: it rises gradually and falls steeply. The pH graph is a peaked curve on both sides.
- Low temperature does not denature an enzyme; it only slows it. Don't say enzymes are "killed": they are not alive.
- At the substrate-concentration plateau the enzyme is not denatured or "used up"; its active sites are all occupied.
- In the pH and temperature investigations, the buffer (pH) and water bath (temperature) are both used: one is the independent variable and the other is controlled. Students often forget to control the other one.
- In the heat-denaturation investigation, the boiled extract must be cooled to the same temperature as the unboiled before testing, or temperature becomes a second variable.
- The control is boiled enzyme or water in place of enzyme, not "no hydrogen peroxide".
- Don't teach the bond-level reasons for denaturation (hydrogen, ionic and disulfide bonds; R-group charges) beyond P7 and T8; that detail is Higher Level and in "Enzyme structure and specificity".
- The general meaning of variables, controls, repeatability, reproducibility, and random and systematic error belongs to "Investigating in Science"; use the terms here only as applied to these investigations.
- Immobilised enzymes and the alginate-bead method belong to "Industrial enzymes". The digestive enzymes as part of digestion belong to "The Digestive System"; P8 gives their optimum pH as secondary data only.
- Enzyme inhibitors as a "factor" are not on the specification. Don't add an inhibitor investigation.
- Germinating-seed enzyme plates (starch agar, skimmed-milk agar) were on the previous syllabus and have been removed. Don't use them.
- The diagram cues in SC6, SC10, EC6, EC10, P5, P9, T5 and T10 are the only ones wanted. No other diagram cues.

## 4. What exams ask (topics only)

- Name an enzyme used in an investigation of the effect of pH or temperature, its source and its substrate.
- Describe how the rate of enzyme activity was measured (foam or oxygen volume per minute; time for starch to disappear with iodine).
- Describe how pH was varied and kept constant (buffers), and how temperature was varied and kept constant (water baths, thermometer).
- Name variables kept constant and explain the purpose of a control (boiled enzyme).
- Sketch and label graphs of rate against pH, temperature and substrate concentration; identify an optimum.
- Explain why pH or temperature changes enzyme activity; explain the term optimum activity; explain denaturation.
- Describe how to show that heat denatures an enzyme, and how you would show that the change is permanent.

## 5. Practice questions to write

1. 6 marks: "In an investigation of the effect of temperature on catalase activity, name the independent and dependent variables and two variables that must be controlled." Model answer: T2 (temperature; foam or oxygen volume per unit time; two of pH, hydrogen peroxide volume and concentration, volume of extract, time).
2. 12 marks: "Describe how you would investigate the effect of pH on the rate of catalase activity. Include how you would vary the pH, how you would measure the rate, a control and a safety precaution." Model answer: E1 (extract), P2, P3, P4 (buffers, water bath at a fixed temperature, foam in 2 minutes) and H1. Must keep temperature constant.
3. 9 marks: "Sketch a graph of the effect of substrate concentration on the rate of an enzyme-catalysed reaction and explain its shape." Model answer: SC10 graph, SC8 (more collisions, more enzyme–substrate complexes) and SC9 (active sites saturated; enzyme concentration limiting).
4. 9 marks: "Describe how you would show that heat denatures amylase, and state the results you would expect." Model answer: D4 method applied to amylase (boil, cool, add to starch, sample into iodine), D8 results, and D6 conclusion that activity does not return on cooling.
