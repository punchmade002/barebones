## 1. Must cover

What scientific knowledge is (one block)
- [ ] K1 Science is both a body of knowledge and a process for building it: it explains the natural world using evidence gathered by observation and experiment. A scientific claim must be testable against evidence from the natural world; questions of value or morals (e.g. "should we clone animals?") can be informed by science but not answered by it.
- [ ] K2 Scientific knowledge is evidence-based (empirical): ideas are accepted because they agree with observations and experimental results, not because of who proposed them.
- [ ] K3 Scientific knowledge is tentative but durable: any idea can be revised if new evidence contradicts it, yet well-tested ideas such as the cell theory have survived more than 150 years of testing. "Tentative" does not mean "weak" or "a guess".
- [ ] K4 Ideas must be falsifiable: it must be possible, in principle, to imagine an observation that would show the idea is wrong. The philosopher Karl Popper made falsifiability his test of whether an idea is scientific. Evidence can support an idea or refute it, but can never prove it true for all time; one reliable contrary result can disprove it.
- [ ] K5 Observation vs inference: an observation is what is directly detected with the senses or instruments (e.g. "the broth went cloudy"); an inference is an explanation drawn from it (e.g. "microorganisms grew in the broth"). Scientists keep the two separate.

How scientists work (one block)
- [ ] W1 There is no single fixed "scientific method": the process is iterative (it loops back), and scientists follow many different paths, but it usually involves the stages in W2. The specification says the process of science is "often complex and iterative, following many different paths".
- [ ] W2 The usual cycle: observation → question → hypothesis → prediction → experiment or further observation → collect and analyse data → conclusion (hypothesis supported or rejected) → repeat and check → compare with existing knowledge → publish in a scientific journal after peer review → other scientists test the findings → over time, well-supported findings contribute to a theory or law. A rejected hypothesis leads to a new or modified hypothesis, so the cycle starts again. Diagram: the cycle drawn as a loop with arrows, with an arrow from "hypothesis rejected" back to "new hypothesis", and "publish and peer review" leading out to "theory or law".
- [ ] W3 Not all science is experimental: some fields test ideas by observation and comparison, e.g. fossil records and comparative anatomy as evidence for evolution, or field surveys of ecosystems.
- [ ] W4 Science is creative: imagining hypotheses and designing ways to test them takes imagination, and chance observations can start new lines of work (the discovery of penicillin is the standard example; detail in "Science, scientists and society").
- [ ] W5 Science is collaborative and sceptical: scientists work in teams, build on others' published work, and expect their claims to be checked and challenged; scepticism means doubting a claim until the evidence for it is good, which is a norm, not a personal attack (how this works internationally is in "Science as a global enterprise").
- [ ] W6 Science is done by people, so it is influenced by the society, culture, funding and technology of its time, and individual scientists can be biased (types of bias are in "Recognising bias and evaluating sources").

Limitations of the scientific method (one block; asked on past papers)
- [ ] L1 Limited by current knowledge and technology: some questions can't yet be investigated, e.g. cells could not be seen until microscopes were invented, and the internal structure of organelles was not seen until electron microscopes.
- [ ] L2 The basis of the investigation may be wrong: if the theory or assumption a hypothesis rests on is false, the conclusions may be false too.
- [ ] L3 Human error and the interpretation of results: data can be misread, and scientists can interpret the same results in different ways.
- [ ] L4 Experimental design: a poorly designed experiment can give results that seem to support a hypothesis when they don't (design is taught in "Designing and conducting investigations").
- [ ] L5 Applying results to nature: conditions in a laboratory are simpler and more controlled than in a living organism or ecosystem, so results may not apply outside the lab (e.g. a drug that works on cells in a dish may not work in a person).
- [ ] L6 Science can't answer questions of value, ethics or the supernatural, and can't prove a hypothesis absolutely (K4).

Hypothesis, theory and law (one block each for H, T and La; then the comparison block)
- [ ] H1 A hypothesis is a proposed, testable explanation for an observation. It makes predictions that can be checked by experiment or observation. It may be supported or rejected; it is never proved. Example: in the early 1980s Robin Warren and Barry Marshall proposed that stomach ulcers are caused by infection with the bacterium Helicobacter pylori, not by stress and excess acid as was then believed. (How to write a testable hypothesis and a prediction is in "Questioning and predicting".)
- [ ] T1 A theory is a broad, well-tested explanation of a wide range of observations, supported by many confirmed hypotheses and by evidence from many independent sources. It explains why something happens and makes predictions. A theory is the highest status an explanation reaches in science; it is not a guess, so the everyday phrase "just a theory" misuses the word.
- [ ] T2 Example, the cell theory: all living things are made of one or more cells; the cell is the basic unit of structure and function of life; all cells come from pre-existing cells. Matthias Schleiden proposed in 1838 that plants are made of cells, Theodor Schwann extended this to animals in 1839, and Rudolf Virchow added in 1855 that all cells arise from existing cells ("omnis cellula e cellula").
- [ ] T3 Other biological theories: the theory of evolution by natural selection (Darwin and Wallace; detail in "The theory of evolution by natural selection" and "The importance of the theory"); the germ theory of disease (many diseases are caused by microorganisms).
- [ ] La1 A law (or principle) is a statement, often mathematical, that describes a pattern that always occurs under stated conditions. It describes what happens but does not explain why.
- [ ] La2 Biology has few laws because living systems are variable. Examples: Mendel's laws of segregation and independent assortment describe how alleles pass to offspring (taught in "Mendel's Laws"); they have known exceptions, e.g. linked genes; the laws of thermodynamics apply to living things, e.g. energy is not created or destroyed, only converted, as when light energy is converted to chemical energy in photosynthesis.
- [ ] C1 Comparison block (hypothesis vs theory vs law, as paired points): scope (one observation vs a wide range vs one pattern); purpose (proposes an explanation vs gives a tested explanation vs describes a pattern); evidence (little or none yet vs a large body from many sources vs repeated observation without exception under stated conditions); all three can be revised or rejected if evidence demands.
- [ ] C2 A theory does not "grow up" into a law: they are different kinds of knowledge. A law describes a pattern, a theory explains it, and a theory can explain a law (e.g. Mendel's laws are explained by the behaviour of chromosomes in meiosis).
- [ ] C3 (assumed) A scientific fact is a repeatable observation, e.g. "DNA is found in the nucleus of eukaryotic cells". A model is a simplified representation used to explain or predict (taught in "Models").

How scientific ideas are modified over time (one block per case, each: old idea → new evidence → new idea → why it was accepted)
- [ ] M1 Why ideas change: new evidence that the old idea can't explain; new technology that allows new observations (e.g. microscopes, DNA sequencing); reinterpretation of existing evidence; and replication by other scientists. Change is usually gradual refinement; occasionally a whole framework is replaced, which the historian Thomas Kuhn called a paradigm shift (1962).
- [ ] M2 Case: spontaneous generation → biogenesis. Old idea: living things such as maggots and microorganisms arise from non-living matter. In 1668 Francesco Redi put meat in jars that were open, sealed, or covered with gauze; maggots appeared only on meat that flies could reach (on the open jars; flies laid eggs on the gauze, not the meat). In the early 1860s Louis Pasteur boiled broth in swan-neck (S-shaped neck) flasks: air could enter, but dust and microorganisms were trapped in the bend, and the broth stayed clear; when the neck was broken off, or the broth was tipped into the bend, microorganisms grew. New idea: living things come only from other living things (biogenesis), which supported the germ theory.
- [ ] M3 Case: the cell theory was built up by several scientists over decades as microscopes improved (T2), and is still being extended, e.g. the discovery that mitochondria and chloroplasts contain their own DNA.
- [ ] M4 Case: stomach ulcers. Old idea: ulcers are caused by stress, spicy food and excess acid, and no bacterium could survive stomach acid. Warren observed curved bacteria in biopsies of inflamed stomach linings; Marshall and Warren cultured Helicobacter pylori in 1982; in 1984 Marshall drank a culture of the bacteria, developed gastritis, and treated it with antibiotics. Other scientists repeated the findings and trials showed antibiotics cured most ulcers. New idea: most stomach ulcers are caused by H. pylori infection and are treated with antibiotics. Marshall and Warren received the Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine in 2005. The case also shows that a new idea is often resisted at first and is accepted when the evidence is reproduced.
- [ ] M5 Case: childbed fever. In 1847 Ignaz Semmelweis, at Vienna General Hospital, noticed far more mothers died of childbed (puerperal) fever on the ward run by doctors, who came from dissecting corpses, than on the ward run by midwives. He made staff wash their hands in chlorinated lime solution and deaths fell sharply. Many doctors rejected his idea because it lacked an explanation; it was accepted only after the germ theory of disease explained it. Shows that evidence without an explanatory theory can be ignored.
- [ ] M6 Ideas in this course that changed in the same way, named for cross-reference only: the five-kingdom system replaced by three domains and six kingdoms (see "Domains of life" and "Classification"); the lock-and-key model of enzyme action refined to the induced-fit model, and the membrane "sandwich" model replaced by the fluid mosaic model (see "Models").

## 3. Traps

- Don't say a hypothesis or theory is "proven". Evidence supports or refutes.
- Don't say a theory becomes a law with more evidence (C2), or that "theory" means a guess.
- Principles of good experimental design (control, replicates, fair test, sample size, random selection, double-blind testing, safety) belong to "Designing and conducting investigations". W2 names "experiment" only. The 2024 legacy question on "principles of good experimentation" is that chapter's topic.
- Writing testable hypotheses and predictions belongs to "Questioning and predicting"; H1 defines the term as a kind of knowledge only.
- Peer review, journals and reproducibility are taught in "Science as a global enterprise"; W2 names peer review in the cycle only.
- Bias is taught in "Recognising bias and evaluating sources"; W6 is one sentence.
- Natural selection, its evidence and why evolution is a theory belong to the Evolution chapter; T3 names it only.
- Don't add Mendel's laws content beyond La2's one sentence; they are taught in "Mendel's Laws" (Higher Level only there). Here it is an Ordinary-level example of what a law is, so don't mark it Higher Level.
- The Hardy–Weinberg principle is not on the specification. Don't use it as an example.
- No other diagram cues than the W2 cycle.
