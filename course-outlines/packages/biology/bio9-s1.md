## 1. Must cover

Transfer molecules (one block)
- [ ] T1 A transfer molecule carries energy, or electrons and hydrogen, from the reaction that releases them to the reaction that uses them. It is not used up: after handing on its load it returns to be "recharged", so a cell needs only a small amount of each transfer molecule, recycled continuously.
- [ ] T2 ATP is the main energy-transfer molecule in all living cells, from bacteria to humans, which is why it is called the "energy currency" of the cell. (One sentence only: the electron carriers are Higher Level and belong to "NAD⁺ and NADP⁺".)
- [ ] T3 (assumed) Metabolism is all the chemical reactions in a cell. Catabolic reactions break large molecules into smaller ones and release energy (e.g. respiration); anabolic reactions build large molecules from smaller ones and need energy (e.g. protein synthesis, photosynthesis). ATP links the two: catabolic reactions make ATP and anabolic reactions and other cell work use it. Define the terms in one bullet; their detail is in "Biomolecules and metabolism".

Structure of ATP (one block, structure and function)
- [ ] S1 ATP stands for adenosine triphosphate. It is made of three parts: the nitrogenous base adenine, the five-carbon sugar ribose, and a chain of three phosphate groups attached to the ribose.
- [ ] S2 Adenine joined to ribose is called adenosine; "tri" means three phosphates. ATP is a modified nucleotide, the same building plan of base, sugar and phosphate as an RNA nucleotide (see "Nucleotides, DNA and RNA").
- [ ] S3 ADP (adenosine diphosphate) has only two phosphate groups. Pi (inorganic phosphate) is a single free phosphate group.
- [ ] S4 Diagram: ATP drawn as a box labelled adenine, joined to a pentagon labelled ribose, joined to a chain of three circles labelled phosphate; mark the bond to the third (end) phosphate as the one broken to release energy. A second sketch shows ADP + Pi.

Releasing energy: hydrolysis of ATP (one block, process)
- [ ] R1 Energy is released when the end phosphate is removed: ATP + H₂O → ADP + Pi + energy. The reaction uses water, so it is a hydrolysis reaction, and is catalysed by an enzyme (an ATPase).
- [ ] R2 The bond to the end phosphate is often called a high-energy bond: it is easily broken and the reaction releases a useful amount of energy, about 30 kJ per mole of ATP under standard conditions.
- [ ] R3 The energy is released in one quick step, in a small packet that suits a single reaction, and right where the cell needs it.
- [ ] R4 Often the released phosphate is attached to another molecule (e.g. a carrier protein or a reactant), making it more reactive or changing its shape so it can do work.

Remaking ATP: phosphorylation (one block, process)
- [ ] F1 ATP is remade by adding a phosphate group back on to ADP: ADP + Pi + energy → ATP + H₂O. Adding phosphate is called phosphorylation. It is catalysed by enzymes, mainly ATP synthase.
- [ ] F2 The energy to make ATP comes from respiration (breaking down food such as glucose) in all living cells: some in the cytoplasm and most in the mitochondria. Name the process only; the stages belong to "Respiration".
- [ ] F3 In plants and algae, ATP is also made using light energy in the light-dependent stage of photosynthesis in the chloroplasts; this ATP is used inside the chloroplast to make glucose. Name the process only; the stages belong to "Photosynthesis".

The ATP/ADP cycle (one block)
- [ ] C1 ATP is continuously broken down to ADP + Pi to release energy for cell work, and ADP + Pi are continuously rebuilt into ATP using energy from respiration (or light). This loop is the ATP/ADP cycle.
- [ ] C2 Cells hold only a small store of ATP at any time; each molecule is recycled many times, so ATP must be made constantly while a cell is alive. When respiration stops (e.g. lack of oxygen for a long time, or a poison that blocks respiration), ATP runs out within minutes and cell processes fail.
- [ ] C3 Diagram: the ATP/ADP cycle as a circle, with ATP at the top and ADP + Pi at the bottom; one arrow down labelled "energy released for cell work: muscle contraction, active transport, synthesis" and one arrow up labelled "energy in from respiration (and light in photosynthesis)".

Roles of ATP in the cell (one block, at least these)
- [ ] U1 Muscle contraction: ATP provides the energy for protein filaments in muscle fibres to slide past each other, shortening the muscle. Very active muscle cells therefore contain many mitochondria. (Muscle structure belongs to "The Musculoskeletal System".)
- [ ] U2 Active transport: carrier proteins in the cell membrane use energy from ATP to pump substances against their concentration gradient, e.g. root hair cells taking in mineral ions from dilute soil water, and the sodium–potassium pump in nerve cells restoring the ion balance after an impulse. (Detail in "Transport Across Membranes".)
- [ ] U3 Synthesis (anabolic reactions): building large molecules from small ones needs ATP, e.g. joining amino acids into proteins at ribosomes, making DNA when it is copied before cell division, making glycogen and starch from glucose, and the light-independent stage of photosynthesis, which uses ATP to build glucose from carbon dioxide.
- [ ] U4 Other uses: cell division (moving chromosomes), movement of cilia and flagella, and the light produced by fireflies (bioluminescence), where the enzyme luciferase uses ATP.

Why cells use ATP instead of using glucose directly (one block, explanation)
- [ ] W1 Breaking down one glucose molecule releases far more energy than any single reaction needs, and does it over many steps; ATP releases a small, manageable amount, matched to one reaction, in a single step, so less energy is wasted as heat.
- [ ] W2 ATP is available instantly where it is needed and is easily recycled; one type of molecule can supply energy to every kind of reaction in the cell.
- [ ] W3 ATP is a short-term energy carrier, not a store: long-term energy is stored in starch (plants), glycogen (animals and fungi) and fats and oils, which respiration uses to make ATP when needed.

Technology link (one bullet)
- [ ] K1 Because every living cell contains ATP, hygiene tests in food factories and hospitals swab a surface and add luciferase: light given off shows that ATP, and therefore living or recently living material such as bacteria or food residue, is present.

## 3. Traps

- NAD⁺, NADP⁺, NADH and NADPH are Higher Level only and belong to "NAD⁺ and NADP⁺". Don't name them in this subsection's blocks or key terms (T2 may say "other transfer molecules carry electrons" without naming them).
- The sugar in ATP is ribose, not deoxyribose; the base is adenine, not adenosine. Adenosine is adenine plus ribose.
- ATP is not a long-term energy store; don't call it "stored energy" in the sense of starch or fat.
- Avoid saying "energy is stored in the bond and breaking it releases energy" as the whole story; R2's wording ("the reaction releases energy when the end phosphate is removed") is the safe one.
- Glycolysis, the Krebs (citric acid) cycle, the electron transport chain, ATP yields and FADH₂ belong to "Respiration"; the light-dependent and light-independent stages belong to "Photosynthesis". Here they are named only, as in F2, F3 and U3.
- Muscle structure (actin and myosin detail) belongs to "The Musculoskeletal System"; U1 stays generic.
- How active transport works belongs to "Transport Across Membranes"; U2 gives examples only.
- Nucleotide structure in full belongs to "Nucleotides, DNA and RNA"; S2 makes the link in one sentence.
- No other diagram cues beyond S4 and C3.

## 4. What exams ask (topics only)

- Name the base and the sugar in ATP; draw and label its structure.
- Give the advantage to a cell of using ATP rather than breaking down a sugar each time energy is needed (small, manageable packets; immediate; easily recycled).
- State the products of ATP breakdown and how ATP is reformed; name the processes that make ATP.
- Give uses of ATP in cells.
