# Package: bio15-s5 — The mitochondrion's internal structure and ATP synthase

- **loId:** `bio15-s5` · **chapterId:** `bio15` · **syllabusRef:** 2.2.5
- **Syllabus (Higher Level only):** "cellular structures facilitating photosynthesis and
  respiration: electron transport chains involving a number of protein complexes and mobile
  electron carriers embedded in the mitochondrial membrane (respiration) and thylakoid
  membrane (photosynthesis); transfer of electrons to protein complexes causes a charge
  difference which attracts protons to the complex, given by donor molecules; these protons
  pass through the complex outside the mitochondrial inner membrane, creating a high
  concentration of protons outside the membrane; protons flow back down the gradient, across
  the inner membrane, through the enzyme ATP synthase, which harnesses the flow of protons to
  make ATP." Outcome 5: "recognise the significance of the internal structures of mitochondria
  and chloroplasts in facilitating the processes of photosynthesis and respiration." This
  subsection takes the mitochondrion, the proton mechanism, and the comparison with the
  thylakoid membrane. The chloroplast's structure is in "The chloroplast's internal
  structure".
- **Level:** every block in this subsection ends its heading with " (Higher Level only)".
- **Prepared by:** a higher model, 2026-10-26. The basic mitochondrion (double membrane,
  cristae, matrix; site of most of aerobic respiration; many in active cells) is taught at
  Ordinary Level in "Structures and organelles of animal and plant cells" and is assumed: add
  the internal detail here. The stages (link step, citric acid cycle, electron transport chain
  passing electrons to oxygen) are in "Second-stage process"; here, teach how the chain's

  own wording, explained in plain terms (see Traps for how it differs from textbooks).

## 1. Must cover

Internal structure of the mitochondrion (one block, structure and function)
- [ ] S1 Outer membrane: smooth; surrounds the mitochondrion and is permeable to small molecules such as pyruvate, ADP and ATP.
- [ ] S2 Inner membrane: folded into many cristae (singular crista), greatly increasing its surface area. It holds the electron transport chains and ATP synthase. It is impermeable to protons except through ATP synthase.
- [ ] S3 Intermembrane space: the narrow space between the outer and inner membranes, into which protons are moved (the "outside the inner membrane" of the specification).
- [ ] S4 Matrix: the fluid inside the inner membrane, containing the enzymes of the link step and the citric acid cycle, and the mitochondrion's own small loop of DNA and ribosomes, so it makes some of its own proteins. (That this DNA is inherited from the mother belongs to "Nuclear and non-nuclear inheritance"; don't add it.)
- [ ] S5 Diagram: an electron-microscope-level drawing of a mitochondrion in section, labelled outer membrane, inner membrane, cristae, intermembrane space, matrix, DNA, ribosomes, and ATP synthase shown as small knobs on the matrix side of the cristae. This extends the simpler diagram in "Structures and organelles of animal and plant cells".

The electron transport chain in the inner membrane (one block, structure)
- [ ] E1 The chain is made of a number of large protein complexes embedded in (spanning) the inner membrane, linked by smaller mobile electron carriers that move within or along the membrane and carry electrons from one complex to the next.
- [ ] E2 NADH delivers electrons to the first complex; the electrons move from complex to complex, each at a lower energy level, and at the last complex they are passed to oxygen, forming water (the stages are in "Second-stage process"; refer to it, don't repeat it).

How the chain makes ATP: the proton gradient and ATP synthase (one block, process; numbered stages in the specification's order)
- [ ] P1 1. Electrons arrive: high-energy electrons from NADH are transferred to a protein complex in the inner membrane. Electrons are negatively charged, so their arrival causes a charge difference: the complex becomes negatively charged.
- [ ] P2 2. Protons are attracted: the charge difference attracts positively charged protons (H⁺) to the complex. The protons are given by donor molecules: NADH releases a proton when it gives up its electrons, and more protons come from the matrix solution.
- [ ] P3 3. Protons are moved out: the protons pass through the complex from the matrix side to the outside of the inner membrane, into the intermembrane space. Energy from the electrons drives this. This happens at several complexes along the chain, as the electrons pass from one to the next. Textbooks call this proton pumping.
- [ ] P4 4. A gradient builds: protons accumulate in the narrow intermembrane space, creating a high concentration of protons outside the inner membrane and a low concentration in the matrix. This difference is a proton (concentration) gradient, a store of energy, like water held behind a dam. It also makes the intermembrane space more positive (and more acidic) than the matrix.
- [ ] P5 5. Protons flow back: the inner membrane doesn't let protons through except through the enzyme ATP synthase. Protons flow back down their gradient, from the intermembrane space across the inner membrane into the matrix, through a channel in ATP synthase.
- [ ] P6 6. ATP is made: ATP synthase harnesses the flow of protons, which turns part of the enzyme like a water wheel or turbine, and uses the energy to join ADP and Pi into ATP on the matrix side.
- [ ] P7 7. Oxygen keeps the system running: oxygen accepts the electrons and protons at the end of the chain (forming water), so electrons keep flowing and protons keep being moved out. Without oxygen the gradient isn't maintained and ATP synthase stops.
- [ ] P8 Making ATP using a proton gradient across a membrane is called chemiosmosis. Nature of Science link (one bullet, optional): the idea was proposed by the British biochemist Peter Mitchell in 1961 and was at first widely doubted; he was awarded the Nobel Prize in Chemistry in 1978 once experiments had supported it.
- [ ] P9 Diagram: a section of the inner membrane with the matrix below and the intermembrane space above. Show NADH → NAD⁺ + H⁺ + 2e⁻ at the first complex; electrons moving along the protein complexes and mobile carriers (arrow); H⁺ passing up through the complexes into the intermembrane space and building up there (many H⁺ drawn); ½O₂ + 2H⁺ + 2e⁻ → H₂O at the last complex; and ATP synthase with H⁺ flowing down through it into the matrix and ADP + Pi → ATP. Label protein complex, mobile electron carrier, inner membrane, intermembrane space, matrix, ATP synthase, proton gradient.

How the internal structure facilitates respiration (one block, one structure–function point per bullet)
- [ ] F1 Cristae: folding gives a large area of inner membrane, holding very many electron transport chains and ATP synthase molecules, so more ATP can be made. Mitochondria in very active cells (e.g. heart and flight muscle) have more densely packed cristae.
- [ ] F2 Narrow intermembrane space: a small volume, so the protons moved into it quickly build up a high concentration and a steep gradient.
- [ ] F3 Inner membrane impermeable to protons: the gradient is kept and protons can return only through ATP synthase, so their energy isn't wasted.
- [ ] F4 Complexes embedded in the membrane in a fixed order: electrons pass quickly and in sequence, and protons are moved in one direction only, across the membrane.
- [ ] F5 Matrix next to the inner membrane: the enzymes of the link step and the citric acid cycle are right beside the chains, so NADH is delivered quickly, and ATP is made into the matrix, from where it is exported to the cytoplasm.
- [ ] F6 Double membrane: separates the matrix's enzymes and conditions from the cytoplasm; outer and inner membranes together create the intermembrane space.
- [ ] F7 Own DNA and ribosomes: the mitochondrion makes some of its own proteins, including some of the chain's, and can divide to make more mitochondria when a cell needs more energy (e.g. muscles with training). One bullet.

Comparing the mitochondrion with the thylakoid membrane (one comparison block, paired points)
- [ ] C1 Same: both have electron transport chains of protein complexes and mobile electron carriers embedded in a membrane; both use the energy of electrons passing along the chain to move protons across the membrane, building a proton gradient; both make ATP as protons flow back through ATP synthase (chemiosmosis); both have a large membrane area (cristae; stacked thylakoids); both organelles have a double outer membrane and their own DNA and ribosomes.
- [ ] C2 Source of energy: the mitochondrion uses chemical energy from food (glucose), delivered as electrons by NADH; the chloroplast uses light energy, absorbed by chlorophyll.
- [ ] C3 Source of electrons: NADH (from glucose) in the mitochondrion; water, via the reaction-centre chlorophyll, in the chloroplast.
- [ ] C4 Final electron acceptor: oxygen, forming water, in the mitochondrion; NADP⁺, forming NADPH, in the chloroplast (non-cyclic pathway).
- [ ] C5 Oxygen: used at the end of the chain in mitochondria; released by photolysis in chloroplasts.
- [ ] C6 Which membrane: the inner membrane of the envelope (cristae) in the mitochondrion; the thylakoid membrane, a separate internal membrane system, in the chloroplast (not its envelope).
- [ ] C7 Where protons build up: in the intermembrane space (between inner and outer membranes) in the mitochondrion; inside the thylakoid space in the chloroplast.
- [ ] C8 Where ATP is made and used: into the matrix, then exported for use all over the cell, in the mitochondrion; into the stroma, used there in the light-independent reactions, in the chloroplast.
- [ ] C9 Diagram: side-by-side sketches of a section of the inner mitochondrial membrane and a section of the thylakoid membrane, each with its chain, the direction of proton movement, the high-proton compartment (intermembrane space; thylakoid space) and ATP synthase; label the electron source and final acceptor on each.

## 3. Traps

- Every block is Higher Level only; mark every heading. Key terms (cristae, intermembrane space, matrix, protein complex, mobile electron carrier, proton gradient, ATP synthase, chemiosmosis) are Higher Level.
- Specification wording, followed and explained: it says electron transfer "causes a charge difference which attracts protons to the complex, given by donor molecules", and that the protons pass "outside the mitochondrial inner membrane". This is a simplified version of proton pumping: textbooks say the energy released by electrons is used to pump protons across the membrane. Teach P1–P3 in the specification's order, explain "donor molecules" as NADH (and the matrix), and give "proton pumping" as the textbook name. "Outside the inner membrane" means the intermembrane space, not outside the mitochondrion or in the cytoplasm; say so explicitly.
- Protons flow back through ATP synthase into the matrix; they don't pass back through the electron transport complexes.
- The proton gradient, not the electrons directly, powers ATP synthase. The electrons go to oxygen; the protons go through ATP synthase.
- In mitochondria the high-proton compartment is the intermembrane space; in chloroplasts it is the thylakoid space. Students often reverse these or say "the stroma".
- ATP synthase is an enzyme (a protein), not a carrier; ATP is made on the matrix side.
- FAD/FADH₂ and ATP totals are not on the specification.
- The stages of the second stage belong to "Second-stage process"; the chloroplast's structure and its two proton bullets belong to "The chloroplast's internal structure". Here, C1–C8 compare without re-teaching the photosynthesis stages.
- Mitochondrial inheritance and endosymbiosis are not to be added.
- No other diagram cues beyond S5, P9 and C9.
