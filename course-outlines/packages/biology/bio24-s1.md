## 1. Must cover

Reproductive structures of a flowering plant (one block)
- [ ] R1 Flowering plants reproduce sexually by means of flowers. The flower is a short shoot whose parts are modified leaves arranged in rings (whorls) on the receptacle. Its job is to produce gametes, bring about pollination and fertilisation, and then produce seeds and fruits.
- [ ] R2 The three reproductive structures and their functions: the flower makes the male and female gametes and brings about pollination; the seed (a fertilised, developed ovule) contains the embryo, a new plant, with a food store and a protective coat; the fruit (a developed ovary) protects the seeds and helps to disperse them. Seed detail is in "Seeds"; dispersal in "Fruit and seed dispersal".
- [ ] R3 Many flowers have both male and female parts (they are bisexual, or hermaphrodite), e.g. buttercup. Some plants have separate male and female flowers on the same plant, e.g. hazel (male flowers in hanging catkins; female flowers small with red stigmas), and in some species male and female flowers are on different plants, e.g. holly. One bullet.

Flower structures and their functions (one Structure-and-function block, outside to inside)
- [ ] F1 Receptacle: the swollen tip of the flower stalk, to which all the other flower parts are attached. In strawberry it swells to form the fleshy part of the fruit.
- [ ] F2 Sepal: a small, usually green, leaf-like part in the outer ring; together the sepals form the calyx. Sepals enclose and protect the flower while it is a bud; some photosynthesise.
- [ ] F3 Petal: inside the sepals; together the petals form the corolla. In insect-pollinated flowers the petals are large, coloured and often scented, attracting insects; many have lines or patches (nectar guides) that lead insects to the nectar. In wind-pollinated flowers petals are small, dull or absent.
- [ ] F4 Nectary (assumed): a gland, usually at the base of the petals, that secretes nectar, a sugary liquid that rewards visiting insects. Pollen is also eaten by some insects, e.g. bees collect it to feed their larvae.
- [ ] F5 Stamen: the male part, made of an anther on a stalk, the filament. The stamens together are the androecium (name only, optional).
- [ ] F6 Anther: produces pollen grains by meiosis, so they are haploid. Each pollen grain contains the male gamete nuclei. When ripe the anther splits open to release the pollen.
- [ ] F7 Filament: the stalk that holds the anther in position (where insects brush against it, or out in the wind) and carries water and nutrients to the anther through a small vascular strand.
- [ ] F8 Carpel: the female part, made of the stigma, style and ovary. A flower may have one carpel or many; the carpels together are the gynoecium (name only, optional). Several fused carpels are sometimes called a pistil; the term isn't needed.
- [ ] F9 Stigma: the tip of the carpel that receives (catches) pollen. It is sticky in insect-pollinated flowers and large and feathery in wind-pollinated grasses.
- [ ] F10 Style: the stalk joining the stigma to the ovary. It holds the stigma in a position to receive pollen, and the pollen tube grows down through it to the ovary.
- [ ] F11 Ovary: the swollen base of the carpel. It contains one or more ovules and protects them. After fertilisation the ovary develops into the fruit.
- [ ] F12 Ovule (assumed): a structure inside the ovary that contains the egg cell (female gamete), made by meiosis. After fertilisation the ovule develops into the seed. The micropyle is a tiny pore at one end of the ovule through which the pollen tube usually enters.
- [ ] F13 Diagram: a half-flower (vertical section) of an insect-pollinated flower such as buttercup or wallflower, labelled receptacle, sepal, petal, nectary, stamen (anther, filament), carpel (stigma, style, ovary), ovule. A second diagram of the stamen and of the carpel, each labelled with its parts.

Pollination (one block)
- [ ] P1 Pollination is the transfer of pollen from an anther to a stigma of a flower of the same species. It must happen before fertilisation can take place.
- [ ] P2 Self-pollination: pollen is transferred to a stigma of the same flower or of another flower on the same plant. Cross-pollination: pollen is transferred to a stigma of a flower on a different plant of the same species.
- [ ] P3 Cross-pollination combines genes from two different parents, so the offspring show more genetic variation; the species is better able to adapt to change (e.g. new diseases or climate) and avoids the harmful effects of inbreeding. Self-pollination needs no second plant or pollinator and is more certain, but gives less variation; it suits isolated plants and plants in places with few pollinators.
- [ ] P4 Ways flowers favour cross-pollination: the stamens and stigmas of a flower ripen at different times; self-incompatibility, where pollen landing on a stigma of the same plant fails to grow a pollen tube; separate male and female flowers (hazel) or separate male and female plants (holly); in primrose, two flower forms exist (pin flowers with a long style and low anthers, thrum flowers with a short style and high anthers), so insects carry pollen between the two forms.
- [ ] P5 Agents of pollination: insects (most showy flowers), wind (grasses and many trees, e.g. hazel, oak, birch), and less often other animals (birds and bats in other parts of the world) or water. The detailed comparison of insect- and wind-pollinated flowers is in "Investigation: insect- and wind-pollinated plants".

The role of flowers in pollination (one block)
- [ ] W1 In insect-pollinated plants the flower attracts the pollinator (colour, scent, nectar guides), rewards it (nectar and pollen), and places its anthers and stigma where the insect's body touches them. Pollen sticks to the insect at one flower and is rubbed onto the sticky stigma of the next flower of the same species it visits.
- [ ] W2 Many insects visit only one species on a foraging trip (they are faithful to the flower they have learned), so pollen is carried to the right species. Plant and pollinator both benefit: a mutualism (detail in "Biotic factors and relationships").
- [ ] W3 In wind-pollinated plants the flower's job is to release very large amounts of pollen into moving air and to catch pollen on a large stigma; there is nothing to attract animals, so no showy petals, scent or nectar.
- [ ] W4 Sustainability link: many crops (e.g. apples, strawberries, oilseed rape) and wild plants depend on insect pollinators, especially bees. Bee numbers are falling through habitat loss, pesticides and disease. Ireland's response is the All-Ireland Pollinator Plan, first published in 2015 and coordinated by the National Biodiversity Data Centre, which encourages councils, farmers, schools and gardeners to provide food and nesting places for pollinators (e.g. leaving wildflowers and mowing less often).
- [ ] W5 Health link (one bullet): wind-pollinated plants, especially grasses, release huge numbers of small, light pollen grains that cause hay fever (allergic rhinitis) in early summer.

From pollination to seed and fruit (assumed; one block)
- [ ] D1 1. A pollen grain of the same species lands on the stigma. 2. It grows a pollen tube down through the style to the ovary, digesting a path with enzymes. 3. The tube enters an ovule, usually through the micropyle, and carries the male gamete nuclei to it. 4. Fertilisation: a male gamete nucleus fuses with the egg cell nucleus to form a diploid zygote. 5. The zygote divides by mitosis to form the embryo.
- [ ] D2 Flowering plants have double fertilisation (name it; one bullet): a second male nucleus fuses with two other nuclei in the ovule (the polar nuclei) to form the endosperm nucleus, which divides to form the endosperm food store. No embryo-sac development or further detail.
- [ ] D3 After fertilisation: the ovule becomes the seed and its outer layers (integuments) become the testa; the ovary becomes the fruit and its wall becomes the fruit wall (pericarp); the petals, stamens and usually the sepals wither and fall off.
- [ ] D4 Diagram: a carpel in section with a pollen grain on the stigma, the pollen tube growing down the style and entering an ovule through the micropyle, labelled pollen grain, pollen tube, style, ovary, ovule, egg cell, micropyle.

## 3. Traps

- Pollination is not fertilisation. Pollination is the transfer of pollen; fertilisation is the fusion of gamete nuclei in the ovule. Students often confuse them.
- Pollen grains are not the male gametes; they contain the male gamete nuclei.
- The ovary becomes the fruit; the ovule becomes the seed. Don't swap them.
- Stamen = anther + filament; carpel = stigma + style + ovary. Don't list the ovule as part of the carpel's three named parts, though it lies inside the ovary.
- Development of the embryo sac (megaspore mother cell, eight nuclei, antipodals, synergids) and the pollen grain's tube and generative nuclei were Higher Level on the previous syllabus and are not on this specification. D2 is one bullet only.
- The insect-versus-wind comparison table, the practical and pollen-grain features belong to "Investigation: insect- and wind-pollinated plants". Here, F3, F9 and W3 mention the difference in one phrase each.
- Seed structure (testa, plumule, radicle, embryo, endosperm functions) and growth regulators belong to "Seeds"; dispersal belongs to "Fruit and seed dispersal".
- Germination, seed dormancy and vegetative propagation (asexual reproduction, runners, tubers, cuttings) are not on this specification. Don't mention them.
- Gametes and fertilisation in humans are in "Gametes and fertilisation"; don't compare.
- The diagram cues F13 and D4 are all that are wanted. No other diagram cues.

## 4. What exams ask (topics only)

- Name labelled parts of a flower diagram and give the function of named parts (anther, stigma, sepal, petal, ovary).
- Define pollination; distinguish self- from cross-pollination; give an advantage of cross-pollination or a disadvantage of self-pollination.
- State which part of the flower becomes the fruit and which becomes the seed.
- Outline the events from pollination to the formation of a zygote.
