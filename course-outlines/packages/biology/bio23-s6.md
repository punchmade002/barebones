## 1. Must cover

Overview (one short block)
- [ ] O1 Reproduction is controlled by hormones from three places: the pituitary gland at the base of the brain (oxytocin and prolactin, and, at Higher Level, FSH and LH), the testes (testosterone) and the ovaries (oestrogen and progesterone); in pregnancy the placenta also makes oestrogen and progesterone. Locations as in "Glands, locations and hormones".
- [ ] O2 Prolactin (not covered in The Endocrine System): made by the pituitary gland; its main role is to stimulate milk production in the breasts (P3). One bullet giving its source.

Puberty (one block)
- [ ] U1 Puberty is the stage, usually in the early teens, when the reproductive organs mature and become able to produce gametes, and the secondary sexual characteristics develop. It typically begins between about 8 and 13 in girls and between about 9 and 14 in boys.
- [ ] U2 It begins when the brain signals the pituitary to increase its release of the hormones that stimulate the gonads; as a result the testes begin to make much more testosterone and the ovaries much more oestrogen. (Higher Level detail in F1.)
- [ ] U3 Testosterone at puberty: starts sperm production; causes growth of the penis and testes and the male secondary sexual characteristics (see "Glands, locations and hormones"); increases sex drive. Oestrogen at puberty: causes growth of the uterus and vagina and the female secondary sexual characteristics (see "Glands, locations and hormones"), and with progesterone starts the menstrual cycles; the first period is called menarche. One bullet each; don't re-list the characteristics.

Sperm and egg production (one block)
- [ ] S1 Testosterone is needed throughout adult life for sperm production in the seminiferous tubules and to maintain the male reproductive organs and secondary sexual characteristics. Its level stays fairly steady from day to day, so sperm are made continuously (see "Fertility control and infertility" for continuous fertility). Control by FSH and LH is in F2–F3 (Higher Level).
- [ ] S2 Egg production: each month hormones cause one follicle to mature and release its egg, and prepare the endometrium; the levels and feedback are described in "The menstrual cycle". One bullet only.

Implantation and pregnancy (one block)
- [ ] I1 Progesterone prepares the endometrium for implantation: it keeps it thick, spongy and well supplied with blood vessels, and makes its glands secrete nutrients for the early embryo before the placenta forms. Without high progesterone, implantation can't succeed.
- [ ] I2 (assumed) hCG (human chorionic gonadotrophin) is a hormone made by the outer cells of the embryo from implantation. It keeps the corpus luteum alive and secreting progesterone, so the endometrium is not shed and menstruation stops. Home pregnancy tests detect hCG in urine (a monoclonal-antibody test; one phrase only). hCG is not in the specification's list; teach it in this block only, in two bullets at most, because it explains why there is no period in pregnancy.
- [ ] I3 From about the end of the third month the placenta takes over from the corpus luteum, making large amounts of progesterone and oestrogen for the rest of pregnancy; the corpus luteum then breaks down.
- [ ] I4 Progesterone during pregnancy: maintains the endometrium and so the placenta; keeps the muscle of the uterus relaxed, so it doesn't contract and expel the foetus early; keeps the cervical mucus plug thick; prepares the milk-producing glands of the breasts; and (with oestrogen) prevents further ovulation and menstruation.
- [ ] I5 Oestrogen during pregnancy (growth of the foetus): stimulates growth of the muscle of the uterus so it enlarges with the growing foetus, increases blood flow to the uterus and placenta, and develops the milk ducts of the breasts. Late in pregnancy it makes the uterus muscle more sensitive to oxytocin, preparing it for labour.
- [ ] I6 Level summary in pregnancy: progesterone and oestrogen rise steadily to very high levels by late pregnancy, far above their levels in the menstrual cycle, and fall sharply after the placenta is delivered.

Birth (one process block)
- [ ] B1 Near the end of pregnancy the balance of hormones changes: oestrogen's effects increase relative to progesterone's, so the uterus muscle becomes more responsive to oxytocin and begins to contract.
- [ ] B2 Oxytocin is made in the hypothalamus and released by the pituitary into the blood. It causes the muscle of the uterus to contract strongly in labour.
- [ ] B3 Positive feedback: 1. the baby's head presses on and stretches the cervix; 2. stretch receptors in the cervix send nerve impulses to the brain; 3. the pituitary releases more oxytocin; 4. stronger contractions push the head harder against the cervix, stretching it more; 5. the cycle repeats, building up until the baby is born, which removes the stimulus. A nervous and a hormonal pathway work together here (see "Nervous vs hormonal coordination"; positive feedback is defined in "Homeostasis as a stimulus-response model").
- [ ] B4 After the birth, oxytocin keeps the uterus contracting, which expels the placenta and closes the blood vessels at its attachment site, reducing bleeding; it also helps the uterus shrink back towards its normal size. Synthetic oxytocin is given by drip in hospital to start (induce) or speed up labour and to reduce bleeding after birth.
- [ ] B5 Diagram: the positive feedback loop of B3 as a cycle: baby's head stretches cervix → nerve impulses to brain → pituitary releases oxytocin → uterus contracts more strongly → (back to start) → birth ends the loop.

Colostrum and milk (one process block)
- [ ] P1 During pregnancy, oestrogen and progesterone develop the breasts' ducts and milk glands, and prolactin rises, but the high levels of oestrogen and progesterone stop milk being produced.
- [ ] P2 When the placenta is delivered, oestrogen and progesterone fall sharply, so prolactin can act and milk production begins.
- [ ] P3 Prolactin stimulates the milk glands to produce milk. Suckling by the baby stimulates nerve endings in the nipple, causing the pituitary to release more prolactin, so the more the baby feeds, the more milk is made (supply matches demand).
- [ ] P4 Oxytocin causes the milk to be released ("let-down" or milk ejection): suckling triggers oxytocin release, which makes muscle cells around the milk glands contract and squeeze milk into the ducts and out of the nipple. Distinguish: prolactin makes milk; oxytocin releases it.
- [ ] P5 Colostrum is the first milk, produced for the first few days after birth: a small volume of thick, yellowish fluid, richer in protein and antibodies than later milk and lower in fat and sugar. It gives the newborn passive immunity (see "Acquired immunity, prevention and treatment"). Over the following days and weeks it changes to mature milk, with more fat and lactose for energy and growth.
- [ ] P6 While a mother breastfeeds frequently, high prolactin suppresses ovulation, so periods often don't return for some months; this is not reliable contraception (see "Fertility control and infertility"). Higher Level detail: prolactin suppresses the release of FSH and LH (F5).

Menopause (one block)
- [ ] M1 Menopause is the end of a woman's menstrual cycles and of her fertility, confirmed when she has had no period for 12 months. It usually happens between about 45 and 55 years of age.
- [ ] M2 Cause: the ovaries run out of follicles that can respond, so ovulation stops and the ovaries make much less oestrogen and progesterone. Without these hormones the endometrium is no longer built up, and periods stop.
- [ ] M3 Effects of falling oestrogen: hot flushes and night sweats, mood changes and poorer sleep, vaginal dryness, and, longer term, loss of bone density, raising the risk of osteoporosis (brittle bones), and a rise in the risk of heart disease. Hormone replacement therapy (HRT) is in "Hormonal manipulation".
- [ ] M4 Men have no equivalent of the menopause: testosterone falls only slowly with age, and many men can produce sperm into old age, although sperm numbers and quality decline.

Roles and levels of each hormone (one summary block; one bullet per hormone)
- [ ] R1 Testosterone (testes; small amounts also in females): male development before birth (see "Sex determination"), puberty, sperm production, sex drive. Level: low in childhood, rises sharply at puberty, high and fairly steady in adult men, declines slowly in later life.
- [ ] R2 Oestrogen (ovaries; placenta in pregnancy): female puberty, repair and thickening of the endometrium each cycle, growth of the uterus and breast ducts in pregnancy, preparing the uterus for oxytocin, keeping bones strong. Level: low in childhood, rises at puberty, rises and falls each cycle, very high in pregnancy, falls at menopause.
- [ ] R3 Progesterone (corpus luteum; placenta in pregnancy): maintains the endometrium for implantation and pregnancy, relaxes the uterus muscle, prepares milk glands, prevents ovulation in pregnancy. Level: high only in the second half of each cycle; very high in pregnancy; falls sharply at birth; very low after menopause.
- [ ] R4 Oxytocin (made in the hypothalamus, released by the pituitary): contractions of the uterus in labour and after birth; milk let-down. Level: rises sharply during labour and with each feed.
- [ ] R5 Prolactin (pituitary): milk production. Level: rises during pregnancy, stays high while breastfeeding, rising further with each feed; falls when breastfeeding stops.
- [ ] R6 FSH and LH: see the Higher Level block (F6). One bullet pointing to it.

FSH and LH in males and females (Higher Level only; one block)
- [ ] F1 [HL] At puberty the hypothalamus (part of the brain above the pituitary) signals the pituitary to increase its release of FSH and LH. In males these stimulate the testes to make sperm and testosterone; in females they stimulate follicles to develop and the ovaries to make oestrogen, starting the cycles.
- [ ] F2 [HL] In males, FSH stimulates sperm production in the seminiferous tubules (working with testosterone); LH (sometimes called interstitial-cell-stimulating hormone in males) stimulates the interstitial cells of the testes to secrete testosterone.
- [ ] F3 [HL] Male negative feedback: when testosterone in the blood rises, it inhibits the release of LH (and FSH) from the pituitary, so less testosterone is made; when testosterone falls, LH rises again. This keeps testosterone and sperm production steady and continuous, unlike the female cycle. Example: men who take anabolic steroids (synthetic testosterone; see "Hormonal manipulation") have high testosterone-like levels, which switch off their own LH and FSH, so their testes shrink and sperm production falls.
- [ ] F4 [HL] In females, FSH stimulates follicle development and oestrogen production, and the LH surge causes ovulation and forms the corpus luteum; the levels and feedback through the month are in "The menstrual cycle". One bullet only.
- [ ] F5 [HL] In pregnancy, high progesterone and oestrogen keep FSH and LH low, so no follicles develop and no ovulation happens; while breastfeeding, high prolactin also suppresses FSH and LH. After the menopause, FSH and LH rise to high levels, because the ovaries no longer make enough oestrogen and progesterone to inhibit them; a high FSH level in the blood is a sign of the menopause.
- [ ] F6 [HL] Levels summary: FSH and LH are low in childhood, rise at puberty; in men they are fairly steady; in women they vary through each cycle with an LH surge at ovulation, are low in pregnancy and while breastfeeding, and are high after the menopause.

Modelling the control system (Higher Level only; one skills block)
- [ ] F7 [HL] A model of hormonal control shows, in order: the control centre (hypothalamus and pituitary), the hormone released, the target organ, the hormone or effect the target produces, and the feedback (− for inhibition, + for stimulation). Diagram: two flow charts side by side. Male: hypothalamus → pituitary → FSH and LH → testes → sperm and testosterone, with a "−" arrow from testosterone back to the pituitary. Female: hypothalamus → pituitary → FSH and LH → ovary → follicle, oestrogen, corpus luteum, progesterone → endometrium, with "−" arrows from progesterone (and oestrogen) to the pituitary and a "+" arrow from high oestrogen to the LH surge. Use the model to predict: e.g. blocking testosterone's feedback raises LH; giving steady oestrogen and progesterone (as in the pill) keeps FSH and LH low.

## 3. Traps

- Mark F1–F7 Higher Level. Ordinary blocks must not mention FSH or LH; where they need the idea, say "pituitary hormones that stimulate the gonads" (U2) or point to the Higher Level block.
- Don't repeat the menstrual-cycle hormone changes; they are in "The menstrual cycle" (S2 and F4 are one-bullet pointers). Don't repeat the secondary sexual characteristics lists from "Glands, locations and hormones" (U3 points to them).
- Prolactin produces milk; oxytocin releases it. Don't swap them.
- Oxytocin's positive feedback in labour is not homeostasis; don't call it negative feedback.
- hCG is the only hormone taught here that the specification doesn't list (I2). Teach no others: no GnRH by name (F1 says "the hypothalamus signals the pituitary"), no relaxin, no inhibin, no human placental lactogen.
- Testosterone is made by the interstitial cells, not by the seminiferous tubules, which make sperm.
- Progesterone, not oestrogen, prevents contractions in pregnancy.
- Don't say menopause happens because "the eggs are used up by ovulation": most follicles degenerate without ever ovulating (see "Gametes and fertilisation"); say the ovaries run out of follicles that can respond.
- Uses of hormones as drugs (pill, HRT, fertility drugs, steroids) belong to "Hormonal manipulation" and "Fertility control and infertility"; name them in one phrase only.
- Diagram cues B5 and F7 only. No other diagram cues.

## 5. Practice questions to write

1. 6 marks: "Distinguish between the roles of prolactin and oxytocin after birth, and state what colostrum is." Model answer: P3 (prolactin, milk production, stimulated by suckling), P4 (oxytocin, let-down), P5 (first milk, rich in antibodies and protein).
2. 9 marks: "Explain the roles of progesterone in the maintenance of pregnancy, and why menstruation does not happen during pregnancy." Model answer: I1, I4 (endometrium maintained, uterus relaxed, mucus plug), I2–I3 (hCG keeps the corpus luteum making progesterone, then the placenta takes over).
3. 12 marks, Higher: "Draw a model of the hormonal control of the male reproductive system and use it to explain why the use of anabolic steroids can reduce sperm production." Model answer: F7 male flow chart with the negative-feedback arrow; F2 (FSH and LH roles); F3 (steroids inhibit LH and FSH, so the testes make less sperm and shrink).
