## 1. Must cover

Key terms (one block)
- [ ] K1 An emerging infectious disease is one that has newly appeared in a population, or that was known but is rapidly increasing in incidence (number of new cases) or in geographic range. A re-emerging disease is one that had declined and is increasing again (e.g. measles where vaccination rates fall; TB with drug-resistant strains).
- [ ] K2 Endemic: always present at a fairly steady level in a region. Epidemic (outbreak): a sudden increase in cases above the expected level in a region. Pandemic: an epidemic that spreads across several continents or worldwide (e.g. COVID-19, declared a pandemic by the World Health Organization on 11 March 2020).
- [ ] K3 A zoonosis is an infectious disease that passes from animals to humans (e.g. avian influenza, rabies). Most emerging human diseases are zoonoses, often from wild animals (examples in "Emerging diseases and society").
- [ ] K4 A reservoir is a host population in which a pathogen persists and from which it infects others (e.g. badgers for bovine TB in Ireland; wild birds for avian influenza). A carrier is an infected individual who shows no symptoms but can pass on the pathogen.

How infectious diseases are transmitted (one block, one bullet per route with an example)
- [ ] T1 Airborne droplets and aerosols from coughs, sneezes and breath: influenza, TB, measles, COVID-19. Wind-borne spores in plants: ash dieback, cereal rusts, potato blight.
- [ ] T2 Direct contact (touch, skin to skin): ringworm, athlete's foot. Indirect contact via contaminated objects and surfaces (fomites), hands, tools and clothing; in plants, contact with hands and tools spreads tobacco mosaic virus.
- [ ] T3 Contaminated food and water (faecal–oral route): cholera, Salmonella food poisoning, amoebic dysentery.
- [ ] T4 Body fluids: sexual contact, blood (shared needles, unscreened transfusions) and mother to baby: HIV, hepatitis B.
- [ ] T5 Vectors: animals that carry pathogens between hosts: mosquitoes (malaria), ticks (Lyme disease), tsetse flies (sleeping sickness), elm bark beetles (Dutch elm disease), aphids (many plant viruses, e.g. barley yellow dwarf virus), biting midges (bluetongue virus of sheep and cattle).
- [ ] T6 In plants, also: rain splash, soil, infected seed, tubers and cuttings (vegetative propagation passes viruses and blight to the next crop), and grafting.

Movement of organisms: local, regional and global (one block)
- [ ] MV1 Local: spread within a household, school, hospital or farm, between neighbouring fields by wind and rain splash, and between neighbouring farms by animals straying, shared equipment, vehicles and people's boots.
- [ ] MV2 Regional: movement of farm animals through marts and between farms; trade in plants and animals within a country or the EU; seasonal movement of wild animals; commuting and holidays. Example: in the 2001 foot-and-mouth disease epidemic in Britain, infected sheep were moved through markets across the country before the disease was detected, spreading it to many farms; control needed a ban on animal movement and the culling of millions of animals.
- [ ] MV3 Global: air travel can carry an infected person to any continent within a day, faster than many diseases take to show symptoms (e.g. COVID-19 reached every continent within months of its first identification in Wuhan, China, in December 2019). International trade moves pathogens and vectors in plants, wood, soil, live animals and goods: ash dieback spread through Europe partly in imported young ash plants and was found in Ireland in 2012; the second, more aggressive epidemic of Dutch elm disease reached Britain from the late 1960s in imported elm logs; the Asian tiger mosquito (Aedes albopictus), a vector of several viruses, has spread to many countries in used tyres and plants. Migrating wild birds carry avian influenza between continents.
- [ ] MV4 The pet and wildlife trade: chytrid fungus (Batrachochytrium dendrobatidis) has been spread around the world in the trade in amphibians and is linked to the decline of hundreds of amphibian species and the extinction of some.

Factor 1: persistence of the pathogen in a host (one block)
- [ ] F1a The longer a host is infectious, the more others it can infect. Persistence includes long infectious periods, carriers without symptoms, latent infections that reactivate, and reservoir hosts.
- [ ] F1b Human examples: HIV may cause no symptoms for years while the person can pass it on; herpes viruses stay latent in nerve cells for life (see "Viruses: replication"); TB bacteria can stay dormant in the lungs for years and become active later. Mary Mallon ("Typhoid Mary"), an Irish-born cook in New York, born in Co. Tyrone in 1869, carried the typhoid bacterium (Salmonella Typhi) without symptoms and infected many of the families she cooked for; she was isolated by the health authorities for most of the rest of her life.
- [ ] F1c Animal and plant examples: bovine TB persists in Irish cattle partly because badgers act as a wildlife reservoir that reinfects herds. Plant viruses persist in perennial plants, weeds and tubers, so infected seed potatoes carry viruses into the next year's crop; Phytophthora infestans survives the winter in infected tubers left in the ground or in dumps.

Factor 2: mutations (one block)
- [ ] F2a Pathogens with short generation times and large populations produce many mutants. RNA viruses mutate fastest because their copying enzyme doesn't proofread (see "Viruses: replication").
- [ ] F2b Antigenic drift: small mutations gradually change a virus's surface antigens, so memory cells and antibodies from earlier infections or vaccines no longer fit well. This is why influenza returns every winter and flu vaccines are updated each year, and why new SARS-CoV-2 variants (e.g. Delta, Omicron) caused new waves of COVID-19.
- [ ] F2c Antigenic shift (influenza): if two different flu viruses infect the same cell (e.g. in a pig), their RNA segments can mix to form a new strain with surface antigens no human population has met; such shifts have caused flu pandemics, e.g. the 2009 H1N1 ("swine flu") pandemic.
- [ ] F2d Mutations can let a pathogen infect a new host species (spillover, e.g. a bird virus gaining the ability to infect mammals), become more transmissible, or overcome resistance bred into crops: e.g. the Ug99 strain of wheat stem rust, identified in Uganda in 1999, overcame resistance genes used in most of the world's wheat varieties.

Factor 3: antibiotic or chemical resistance (one block)
- [ ] F3a How resistance arises, by natural selection (see "The theory of evolution by natural selection"): 1. random mutations (or genes passed between bacteria on plasmids) make a few individuals resistant; 2. the antibiotic or chemical kills the susceptible ones; 3. the resistant survivors reproduce with less competition; 4. the next generation is mostly resistant. The chemical doesn't cause the mutation; it selects it.
- [ ] F3b What speeds it up: overuse and misuse of antibiotics (for viral infections, not finishing courses, use in farm animals), poor infection control in hospitals, and heavy repeated use of one pesticide.
- [ ] F3c Examples: MRSA (methicillin-resistant Staphylococcus aureus), resistant to many antibiotics, spreads in hospitals; multidrug-resistant TB; malaria parasites resistant to some antimalarial drugs; mosquitoes resistant to insecticides, which weakens malaria control; fungi that cause crop diseases resistant to some fungicides; parasitic worms of sheep and cattle resistant to anthelmintics. Resistant pathogens persist longer in hosts and spread further, linking factors 1 and 3.

Factor 4: immunity level in the population (one block)
- [ ] F4a A pathogen spreads only if it can reach susceptible (non-immune) hosts. The higher the proportion of immune individuals, the fewer susceptible hosts, and the slower the spread (herd immunity; see "Acquired immunity, prevention and treatment").
- [ ] F4b A pathogen new to a population meets no immunity at all, so it spreads fast and can be very severe: e.g. COVID-19 in 2020, before vaccines; smallpox and measles brought by Europeans to the Americas from the late 1400s killed very large numbers of Indigenous people, who had never been exposed.
- [ ] F4c Immunity falls when vaccination rates drop (measles outbreaks return), when immunity from vaccination or infection wanes over time, and where many people have weakened immune systems (e.g. untreated HIV, malnutrition, the very young and very old).
- [ ] F4d In plants, the equivalent is genetic uniformity: a crop of one genetically identical variety (a monoculture) has no resistant individuals, so a pathogen that infects one plant can infect them all. Example: in Ireland in the 1840s, most of the poor depended on one potato variety, the Lumper, grown from tubers (genetically identical clones) and susceptible to blight. When Phytophthora infestans arrived in 1845, crops failed across the country in successive years, and about one million people died and over a million emigrated in the Great Famine.

Factor 5: mobility in affected populations (one block)
- [ ] F5a The more infected hosts move and mix, the more contacts each has and the further the pathogen spreads (MV1–MV3 give the routes): crowding in cities, refugee camps, festivals and mass gatherings; travel and migration; movement of livestock; migration of wild birds.
- [ ] F5b Mobility of vectors: climate warming lets vectors survive further north and longer each year, carrying disease into new regions: e.g. bluetongue, a midge-borne virus of sheep and cattle, spread into northern Europe from 2006.
- [ ] F5c Reducing mobility reduces spread: animal movement bans in foot-and-mouth outbreaks, travel restrictions and quarantine in human epidemics, and import controls on plants (detail in "Emerging diseases and society").

Factor 6: the reproduction number, R₀ (one block, with worked examples)
- [ ] R1 The basic reproduction number, R₀ ("R nought"), is the average number of new (secondary) infections caused by one infected individual in a population where everyone is susceptible (no immunity and no control measures).
- [ ] R2 R₀ greater than 1: each case causes more than one new case on average, so numbers grow and an epidemic can occur. R₀ = 1: numbers stay steady (endemic). R₀ less than 1: each case causes fewer than one new case, so the outbreak dies out.
- [ ] R3 Worked example of growth: with R₀ = 2, one case leads to 2, then 4, 8, 16 … new cases per generation of infection; after 10 generations, one case has led to 2¹⁰ = 1,024 new cases in that generation. With R₀ = 0.5, 100 cases give 50, then 25, then about 12, and the outbreak fades. Diagram: a branching tree from one case, giving 2, then 4, then 8 cases (R₀ = 2), beside a single chain of one case per generation (R₀ = 1); label each generation.
- [ ] R4 R₀ depends on the pathogen, the host and the environment: how long a host is infectious (persistence, factor 1); how many contacts an infected host has per day (mobility, crowding, factor 5); and the chance that a contact passes on the infection (the transmission route, and mutations that make a pathogen more transmissible, factor 2). So R₀ is not fixed for a disease: it differs between populations and settings.
- [ ] R5 The effective reproduction number, R (sometimes Rt), is the average number of new infections per case at a given time, in a population where some people are immune or control measures are in place. Approximately R = R₀ × the proportion still susceptible. Example: R₀ = 3 and half the population immune gives R = 3 × 0.5 = 1.5. Control aims to bring R below 1.
- [ ] R6 Herd-immunity threshold: to stop sustained spread, the proportion immune must be at least 1 − 1/R₀. Examples: R₀ = 2 needs 1 − ½ = 50% immune; R₀ = 4 needs 1 − ¼ = 75%; R₀ = 10 needs 90%. Measles is one of the most contagious diseases (R₀ usually quoted as 12–18), so about 95% of people must be immune, which is why the World Health Organization recommends at least 95% coverage with two doses of measles vaccine.
- [ ] R7 The model's assumptions (see "Models"): everyone is equally likely to meet everyone else and R₀ stays constant. Real populations are clustered, behaviour changes during outbreaks, and new variants change R₀, so estimates are updated as data come in.

How the factors interact (one block)
- [ ] I1 The factors act together, not one at a time: e.g. a mutation that makes a virus more transmissible raises R₀; a population with low immunity and high mobility then lets it spread widely; long persistence in carriers lets it reach new areas undetected; and resistance to drugs lets infections last longer, raising R₀ further. Plant example: a genetically uniform crop (low "immunity"), a pathogen spread by wind and trade (mobility), and surviving in tubers (persistence) together explain the speed of the potato famine.
- [ ] I2 Other drivers that increase contact between pathogens and new hosts: clearing forests and expanding farms into wild areas (more contact with wild animals), intensive farming of large numbers of genetically similar animals close together, climate change (vectors' ranges), poverty, poor sanitation and conflict (weak health systems). One bullet.

## 3. Traps

- Define R₀ exactly as in R1: secondary infections caused by one case in a fully susceptible population. It is not the number of people infected in total, not the death rate, and not the effective R (R5). Don't say R₀ changes when people get vaccinated; R does.
- R₀ = 1 is the threshold; R₀ greater than 1 means growth, not "1 or more".
- Antibiotics don't cause the mutations for resistance; they select resistant individuals. Bacteria, not people, become resistant.
- Potato blight is caused by Phytophthora infestans, a water mould, not a fungus or a virus. Don't give the famine dates as a single year or claim exact death figures beyond "about one million".
- Don't name the origin of SARS-CoV-2 (bat, market or laboratory); it is still debated. Say "first identified in Wuhan".
- Ways to control spread (vaccination campaigns, biosecurity, surveillance, One Health) belong to "Emerging diseases and society"; herd immunity's meaning to "Acquired immunity, prevention and treatment"; natural selection to "The theory of evolution by natural selection"; how RNA viruses mutate to "Viruses: replication"; the pathogens themselves to "Pathogens". Here only one phrase each.
- Disease-spread models as a kind of model are in "Models"; R7 refers there.
- Diagram cues: R3 only. No other diagram cues.
