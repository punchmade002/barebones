## 1. Must cover

Composition of blood (one block)
- [ ] K1 Blood is a liquid tissue made of cells and cell fragments suspended in a liquid, the plasma. An adult has about 5 litres of blood.
- [ ] K2 If blood (with a chemical added to stop clotting) is spun in a centrifuge, it separates into about 55% plasma (a straw-coloured liquid on top) and about 45% cells: a thin pale layer of white blood cells and platelets above a dark red layer of red blood cells at the bottom. Red blood cells make up nearly all of the cell volume.
- [ ] K3 Numbers in a healthy adult (one bullet): red blood cells about 5 million per mm³ (cubic millimetre); white blood cells about 4,000 to 11,000 per mm³; platelets about 150,000 to 400,000 per mm³. So red cells greatly outnumber white cells (several hundred to one).
- [ ] K4 Blood cells and platelets are all made in the red bone marrow (in spongy bone, e.g. of the sternum, ribs, vertebrae, pelvis and the ends of long bones), from stem cells that divide by mitosis (see "The musculoskeletal system as a system for response").
- [ ] K5 Functions of blood summarised (one bullet; each is taught in the blocks below): transport (O₂, CO₂, nutrients, wastes, hormones, heat), defence against infection, clotting to seal wounds, and keeping the internal environment steady (pH, temperature, water balance).
- [ ] K6 Diagram: a stained blood smear as seen under a light microscope: many red blood cells (pale centres, no nucleus), one or two white blood cells (larger, with a stained nucleus: e.g. a phagocyte with a lobed nucleus and a lymphocyte with a large round nucleus), and small platelets; label each. And a centrifuged tube showing the plasma, the white-cell-and-platelet layer and the red-cell layer, with percentages.

Plasma (one block)
- [ ] L1 Plasma is about 90% water, with substances dissolved or suspended in it; water is a good solvent and carries heat (see "Water").
- [ ] L2 Dissolved substances transported (one bullet): nutrients (glucose, amino acids, fatty acids and glycerol, vitamins, minerals); wastes (urea from the liver to the kidneys, and most of the carbon dioxide); hormones; salts (ions such as sodium, chloride, calcium and hydrogencarbonate).
- [ ] L3 Carbon dioxide is carried mostly in the plasma as hydrogencarbonate ions (HCO₃⁻), formed in red blood cells by the enzyme carbonic anhydrase (CO₂ + H₂O → carbonic acid → H⁺ + HCO₃⁻); a small amount is dissolved and some is carried by haemoglobin. One bullet (its role in breathing control is in "CO₂ as a controlling factor in breathing").
- [ ] L4 Plasma proteins: fibrinogen and other clotting factors (for clotting); antibodies (for defence; see "Innate and adaptive immunity"); albumin, which holds water in the blood by osmosis and carries some substances. Serum is plasma with the clotting proteins removed.
- [ ] L5 Plasma also distributes heat from active organs around the body, and its hydrogencarbonate and protein buffers keep the blood pH near 7.4.

Red blood cells (one structure-and-function block)
- [ ] RB1 Red blood cells (erythrocytes) are biconcave discs (thinner in the centre than at the edge), about 7 to 8 µm across. They carry oxygen from the lungs to all body cells.
- [ ] RB2 Structure related to function (one bullet each): (a) biconcave shape gives a large surface area for their volume, so oxygen diffuses in and out quickly, and every part of the cell is close to the surface; (b) no nucleus (and no mitochondria) in the mature cell, leaving more room for haemoglobin; (c) packed with haemoglobin; (d) small and flexible, so they squeeze through capillaries in single file, close to the capillary wall. Because they have no mitochondria they respire anaerobically and don't use up the oxygen they carry.
- [ ] RB3 Haemoglobin is a red protein made of four polypeptide chains, each with a haem group containing iron; each haemoglobin molecule can carry four oxygen molecules. In the lungs, where oxygen is plentiful, haemoglobin combines with oxygen to form oxyhaemoglobin (bright red); in respiring tissues, where oxygen is low, oxyhaemoglobin releases its oxygen. Haemoglobin + oxygen ⇌ oxyhaemoglobin.
- [ ] RB4 Life cycle: red blood cells are made in the red bone marrow and live about 120 days (with no nucleus they can't repair themselves). Old cells are broken down in the liver and spleen; the iron is recycled to make new haemoglobin, and the rest of the haem is converted into bile pigments (excreted in bile). The kidney hormone EPO stimulates their production (see "Hormonal manipulation").
- [ ] RB5 Health links (one bullet each): anaemia is a shortage of red blood cells or haemoglobin, so less oxygen is carried, causing tiredness and pale skin; a common cause is iron deficiency (see "Minerals"). Carbon monoxide (in cigarette smoke and from faulty gas appliances) combines with haemoglobin far more tightly than oxygen does, so less oxygen is carried; it can kill. Sickle-cell anaemia is in "Mutations" (name only).

White blood cells (one block, kept short; the types and their immune roles belong to "White blood cells")
- [ ] W1 White blood cells (leucocytes) defend the body against infection. Unlike red blood cells they have a nucleus, are larger, are colourless (no haemoglobin), and are far fewer (K3). They are made in the red bone marrow, and many can squeeze out through capillary walls into the tissues.
- [ ] W2 Two broad groups (one bullet): phagocytes, which engulf and digest pathogens (phagocytosis), and lymphocytes, which produce antibodies or kill infected cells. How they act is in "Innate and adaptive immunity"; the named types (monocytes, natural killer cells, B and T lymphocytes) are in "White blood cells" (Higher Level). Name the groups only; don't describe the types.
- [ ] W3 Their number rises during an infection, so a white cell count is used in diagnosing infections; leukaemia is a cancer of the white-cell-forming cells (see "Cancer"; one phrase).

Platelets and clotting (one structure-and-process block)
- [ ] PL1 Platelets (thrombocytes) are small fragments of cytoplasm enclosed in membrane, with no nucleus, broken off from large cells in the red bone marrow. They live about 7 to 10 days. They start the clotting of blood.
- [ ] PL2 Why clotting matters: it stops blood loss from a damaged vessel and seals the wound so pathogens can't enter (part of the first line of defence; see "Innate and adaptive immunity").
- [ ] PL3 Clotting, step by step: 1. a blood vessel is damaged; 2. platelets stick to the exposed damaged wall and to each other, forming a temporary plug; 3. the platelets and damaged tissue release clotting factors; 4. a series of reactions, needing calcium ions and clotting proteins made in the liver using vitamin K, converts the inactive plasma protein prothrombin into the active enzyme thrombin; 5. thrombin converts the soluble plasma protein fibrinogen into insoluble threads of fibrin; 6. the fibrin threads form a mesh that traps red blood cells and platelets, forming a clot; 7. at the surface the clot dries and hardens into a scab, under which the tissue repairs.
- [ ] PL4 Links (one bullet): calcium and vitamin K are needed for clotting (see "Minerals" and "Vitamins"); haemophilia is an inherited disorder in which a clotting factor is missing (see "Sex-linked traits"); an unwanted clot inside a vessel (a thrombosis) can block an artery, causing a heart attack or stroke (see "Heartbeat").
- [ ] PL5 Diagram: a flow chart of clotting (damage → platelets → clotting factors (with Ca²⁺, vitamin K) → prothrombin → thrombin → fibrinogen → fibrin mesh → clot), and a sketch of fibrin threads trapping red blood cells.

ABO blood groups (one block)
- [ ] G1 A blood group is set by the antigens on the surface membrane of a person's red blood cells. An antigen is a molecule that can trigger an immune response (see "Innate and adaptive immunity"). In the ABO system there are two antigens, A and B.
- [ ] G2 The plasma naturally contains antibodies against the ABO antigen(s) that the person's own red cells lack. One bullet per group: group A: antigen A on the red cells, anti-B antibodies in the plasma; group B: antigen B, anti-A; group AB: both antigens, no antibodies; group O: no antigens, both anti-A and anti-B.
- [ ] G3 Agglutination: if red cells carrying an antigen meet the matching antibody (e.g. A cells meet anti-A), the antibodies bind to the antigens and stick the cells together in clumps. In a transfusion the clumps can block small blood vessels, and the donor cells are destroyed (burst), which can cause kidney failure and death.
- [ ] G4 Transfusion rule: what matters is the donor's red-cell antigens and the recipient's antibodies. A recipient can safely receive red cells that carry no antigen their own plasma has antibodies against: group O red cells (no antigens) can be given to anyone, so group O is called the universal donor; group AB people (no antibodies) can receive red cells of any ABO group, so group AB is the universal recipient. In practice hospitals give the same group whenever possible.
- [ ] G5 Compatibility table (one bullet or a table in words): O can receive O only; A can receive A or O; B can receive B or O; AB can receive A, B, AB or O.
- [ ] G6 Blood-group testing: a drop of blood is mixed separately with anti-A serum and anti-B serum. Clumping with anti-A only = group A; with anti-B only = group B; with both = AB; with neither = O. A third test with anti-D tests the Rhesus group (R1).
- [ ] G7 Diagram: four red blood cells, one per group, showing the antigens on the membrane (A, B, both, none), with the antibodies in the plasma written beside each; and a sketch of agglutination (antibodies linking red cells into a clump).

Rhesus factor (one block)
- [ ] R1 The Rhesus (Rh) factor is another red-cell antigen, the D antigen. People whose red cells carry it are Rhesus positive (Rh+); those without it are Rhesus negative (Rh−). A person's full blood group combines both systems, e.g. O negative, A positive.
- [ ] R2 Unlike anti-A and anti-B, anti-D antibodies are not naturally present: a Rh− person makes them only after Rh+ red cells enter their blood (e.g. through a transfusion of Rh+ blood or during pregnancy). A first exposure therefore does little harm, but a second exposure triggers a rapid, strong attack on Rh+ cells. So Rh− patients must receive Rh− blood.
- [ ] R3 Pregnancy (haemolytic disease of the newborn), step by step: 1. a Rh− mother carries a Rh+ baby (inherited from a Rh+ father); 2. normally the blood of mother and fetus does not mix, but at birth (or after a miscarriage, abortion or injury) some fetal red cells enter the mother's blood; 3. her immune system makes anti-D antibodies and memory cells (she is now sensitised); the first baby is usually unaffected, because it is born before many antibodies are made; 4. in a later pregnancy with a Rh+ baby, her anti-D antibodies cross the placenta and destroy the fetus's red blood cells; 5. the baby can suffer severe anaemia and jaundice, brain damage, or death.
- [ ] R4 Prevention: a Rh− mother is given an injection of anti-D antibodies (anti-D immunoglobulin) during pregnancy and within 72 hours after the birth of a Rh+ baby (or after a miscarriage or injury). These antibodies destroy any fetal Rh+ cells in her blood before her own immune system can respond, so she does not make her own anti-D or memory cells; the injected antibodies are broken down over a few weeks. This is artificial passive immunity (see "Acquired immunity, prevention and treatment"). Every pregnant woman's blood group is tested early in pregnancy for this reason (see "Prenatal and postnatal care").

The value of knowing blood groups for health (one evaluation block; one bullet per use)
- [ ] V1 Safe blood transfusion: before a transfusion, the patient's ABO and Rhesus groups are tested and the donor blood is cross-matched (mixed with the patient's plasma to check that no clumping occurs). This prevents fatal transfusion reactions and lets transfusions be used in surgery, after accidents and childbirth, for anaemia and for cancer treatment.
- [ ] V2 Emergencies: group O Rh− red cells are given when there is no time to test the patient, because they carry no A, B or D antigens. Only a minority of people are O negative, so their donations are especially valuable.
- [ ] V3 Pregnancy: identifying Rh− mothers and giving anti-D (R4) has made haemolytic disease of the newborn rare.
- [ ] V4 Blood donation: knowing which groups are needed lets the blood service plan donor sessions and stocks; donors of all groups are needed, as blood can be stored only for a limited time. In Ireland, donations are collected and tested by the Irish Blood Transfusion Service. Donated blood is screened for infections (see "Acquired immunity, prevention and treatment").
- [ ] V5 Organ and bone-marrow transplants: donors and recipients are matched for ABO group (and tissue type) to reduce the chance of rejection.
- [ ] V6 Nature of Science (one bullet): before blood groups were known, transfusions often killed patients. Karl Landsteiner discovered the ABO blood groups in 1901 (Nobel Prize 1930), which made safe transfusion possible; in 1940 Landsteiner and Alexander Wiener described the Rhesus factor, named after rhesus monkeys whose blood was used in the experiments.

## 3. Traps

- The antigens are on the red blood cells; the antibodies are in the plasma. Don't put antibodies on the cells.
- Group O has no antigens but both antibodies; group AB has both antigens but no antibodies. Students often swap these.
- "Universal donor" refers to giving red cells; group O plasma contains anti-A and anti-B, so it is not a universal plasma donor. One phrase only.
- Agglutination (clumping by antibodies) is not clotting (fibrin formation). Keep them apart.
- Anti-D antibodies are not naturally present, unlike anti-A and anti-B; a Rh− person must first be exposed. The danger in pregnancy is to a Rh+ baby of a Rh− mother, not the reverse, and usually to a second or later baby.
- Mature red blood cells have no nucleus and can't divide (stated in "The cell cycle"; use without restating).
- White blood cell types, phagocytosis stages, antibody production, and T and B cell roles belong to "White blood cells" and "Innate and adaptive immunity"; W1–W3 give composition, structure and a one-line role only.
- Blood-group inheritance (alleles A, B and O, codominance, crosses) is not on the specification and is used elsewhere only as an example of multiple alleles; don't teach it here.
- The lymphatic system and lymph are not on this specification; don't add them.
- How the blood carries CO₂ is here in one bullet (L3); its role in controlling breathing is in "CO₂ as a controlling factor in breathing".
- Haemophilia's inheritance belongs to "Sex-linked traits"; clot-dissolving enzymes to "Industrial enzymes"; sickle-cell anaemia to "Mutations". Name them only.
- The diagram cues K6, PL5 and G7 are the only ones wanted. No other diagram cues.

## 4. What exams ask (topics only)

- Name the components of blood and give a function of each; state what plasma carries.
- Describe the structure of a red blood cell and relate it to its function; explain the role of haemoglobin; give one difference between red and white blood cells.
- State where blood cells are made and how long red cells live; explain anaemia.
- Describe the role of platelets in clotting.
- Explain the ABO blood group system: antigens, antibodies, agglutination, universal donor and recipient; explain why compatibility matters in transfusion.
- Explain the Rhesus factor and the risk to a Rhesus positive baby of a Rhesus negative mother, and how it is prevented.

## 5. Practice questions to write

1. 6 marks: "Give two features of red blood cells and explain how each suits them to carrying oxygen." Model answer: two of RB2 (a)–(d), each linked to the function (surface area, more room for haemoglobin, close to capillary wall), with RB3 for haemoglobin.
2. 12 marks: "Explain the ABO blood group system and why a person of blood group A cannot safely receive blood from a person of group B. Explain why knowledge of blood groups is important for health." Model answer: G1, G2 (group A: antigen A, anti-B), G3 (anti-B binds the B antigens on donor cells, agglutination, blocked vessels, cells destroyed), and two of V1–V5.
3. 9 marks: "Explain why a Rhesus negative mother who has had a Rhesus positive baby may be given an injection of anti-D after the birth." Model answer: R1, R3 (fetal cells enter her blood at birth; she would make anti-D and memory cells that could cross the placenta in a later Rh+ pregnancy and destroy the baby's red cells), R4 (injected anti-D destroys fetal cells first; passive immunity). Must say the risk is to a later baby.
