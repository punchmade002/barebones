# Package: bio15-s2 — Investigation: conditions for fermentation

- **loId:** `bio15-s2` · **chapterId:** `bio15` · **syllabusRef:** 2.2.3
- **Syllabus:** "conditions necessary for fermentation to occur: presence of sugars and
  microorganisms (yeast or bacteria); anaerobic environment; suitable temperature." Outcome 3:
  "investigate the conditions necessary for fermentation, use primary and secondary data to
  support conclusions." Ordinary and Higher level. No Higher Level only material.
- **Prepared by:** a higher model, 2026-10-26. Write as one full investigation in the
  NOTES_STANDARD "Investigation" block type, using the standard school method: yeast in a
  glucose solution under anaerobic conditions, with CO₂ shown by limewater and measured by
  counting bubbles or collecting gas, and ethanol shown by the iodoform test. Blocks:
  background; planning; equipment; the basic method; one set-up per condition (sugar,
  microorganism, anaerobic conditions, temperature) run as a set; the ethanol test; a short
  bacterial alternative; safety; results and conclusion; secondary data; sources of error.
  The fermentation equations are taught in "Respiration as a catabolic process" and the
  general vocabulary of investigations in "Investigating in Science"; use them without
  restating them.

## 1. Must cover

Background (one block)
- [ ] B1 Fermentation is anaerobic respiration by microorganisms. Yeast (a single-celled fungus) ferments sugar to ethanol and carbon dioxide; lactic acid bacteria ferment sugar to lactic acid. The two products of yeast fermentation are the evidence looked for in this investigation: CO₂ (bubbles; limewater turns milky) and ethanol (iodoform test).
- [ ] B2 The four conditions, and why each is needed: sugar is the respiratory substrate (the source of energy); living microorganisms contain the enzymes that carry out fermentation; an anaerobic environment (no oxygen) makes the cells respire anaerobically, since yeast with oxygen respires mainly aerobically, producing CO₂ and water instead of ethanol; a suitable temperature lets the enzymes work at a good rate, as they are slow when cold and denatured when too hot.
- [ ] B3 Yeast can respire with or without oxygen (a facultative anaerobe), so the conditions decide which kind of respiration it carries out (taught in "Respiration as a catabolic process"; one phrase).

Planning (one block)
- [ ] A1 Aim: to investigate the conditions necessary for fermentation by yeast: the presence of sugar, the presence of living yeast, the absence of oxygen and a suitable temperature.
- [ ] A2 Hypothesis (example to write): "Yeast will ferment glucose, producing carbon dioxide and ethanol, only when sugar and living yeast are present, oxygen is absent and the temperature is warm; fermentation will be fastest at about 30–40 °C and will stop at high temperature (e.g. 60 °C)."
- [ ] A3 Design: set up a full fermentation flask that meets all four conditions, then a series of flasks each identical except that one condition is missing or changed. The independent variable is the condition changed (sugar present or absent; living, dead or no yeast; oxygen present or absent; temperature). The dependent variable is the amount or rate of fermentation: rate of CO₂ production (bubbles per minute or cm³ of gas in a set time), whether limewater turns milky, and whether ethanol is present at the end. Controlled variables are the volume and concentration of glucose solution, the mass of yeast, the volume of the flask, the time allowed, and the temperature (except in the temperature series).
- [ ] A4 Control: a flask with glucose solution and yeast that has been boiled (killed) for a few minutes and cooled, set up identically. It shows that the CO₂ and ethanol come from living yeast, not from the glucose solution itself.
- [ ] A5 Equipment and materials: conical flasks (or boiling tubes), fermentation locks or rubber bungs fitted with delivery tubes, test tubes of limewater (calcium hydroxide solution), dried or fresh baker's yeast, glucose (or sucrose), distilled water, a balance and graduated cylinders, a hot plate or kettle to boil water, liquid paraffin or vegetable oil, a water bath or incubator, thermometer, stopwatch, labels, and for the ethanol test: filter funnel and filter paper, test tubes, potassium iodide solution, sodium hypochlorite solution (dilute bleach) and a water bath. Optionally a gas syringe or inverted measuring cylinder in a trough of water to collect the gas.

Setting up the full fermentation flask (one block, numbered method)
- [ ] M1 1. Boil distilled water for a few minutes and allow it to cool to about 30 °C. Boiling drives out dissolved oxygen (and kills unwanted microorganisms); cooling stops the hot water killing the yeast.
- [ ] M2 2. Dissolve glucose in the cooled boiled water to make a glucose solution (e.g. 10 g in 100 cm³), place it in a conical flask and add a set mass of yeast (e.g. 2 g), swirling to mix.
- [ ] M3 3. Gently pour a layer of liquid paraffin or vegetable oil on top of the solution, about 1 cm deep, to stop oxygen from the air dissolving in it (anaerobic conditions).
- [ ] M4 4. Fit a fermentation lock containing water, or a bung with a delivery tube leading into a test tube of limewater, so that gas can escape but air can't get in.
- [ ] M5 5. Place the flask in a water bath or incubator at about 30 °C. Set up the control flask (A4) beside it.
- [ ] M6 6. Measure the rate: after the yeast has had time to start (about 10–15 minutes), count the bubbles passing through the fermentation lock or into the limewater in one minute, three times, or collect the gas over a set time in a gas syringe or inverted measuring cylinder; note when the limewater turns milky. Leave the flasks until bubbling stops (a day or more) and then test for ethanol (E1).

Set-ups for each condition (one block; one bullet per condition, each compared with the full flask)
- [ ] V1 Sugar: a flask with yeast in boiled, cooled water only, no glucose, under oil, at 30 °C. Expected: very few or no bubbles, limewater stays clear, no ethanol. Conclusion: sugar is needed.
- [ ] V2 Microorganisms: a flask with glucose solution and boiled (dead) yeast (the control, A4), and if wished a flask with glucose solution only. Expected: no bubbles, limewater stays clear, no ethanol. Conclusion: living microorganisms are needed.
- [ ] V3 Anaerobic environment: a flask with glucose solution and yeast but made with unboiled water, no oil layer, open to the air or with air bubbled through it (with an aquarium pump), or shaken often. Expected: CO₂ is still produced (from aerobic respiration, often faster), but little or no ethanol is detected. Conclusion: ethanol is made when oxygen is absent.
- [ ] V4 Suitable temperature: identical full flasks placed at a range of temperatures, e.g. about 5 °C (fridge), 20 °C (room), 30 °C, 40 °C and 60 °C (water baths), each checked with a thermometer. Expected: very slow bubbling at 5 °C (enzymes slow but not denatured; it speeds up if warmed), faster at 20 °C, fastest at about 30–40 °C, none at 60 °C (enzymes denatured, yeast killed; it doesn't restart on cooling). Conclusion: a suitable warm temperature is needed.

Testing for ethanol: the iodoform test (one block, method and result)
- [ ] E1 1. When bubbling has stopped, filter a sample of the liquid from each flask into a clean test tube (about 3 cm³). 2. Add about 3 cm³ of potassium iodide solution. 3. Add about 5 cm³ of sodium hypochlorite solution and mix. 4. Warm the tube gently in a water bath (about 60 °C) for a few minutes. 5. Allow to cool and observe. Test water as a negative control and a little dilute ethanol as a positive control.
- [ ] E2 Result: pale yellow crystals (iodoform, triiodomethane) form, with an antiseptic ("hospital") smell, if ethanol is present; no crystals form without ethanol.
- [ ] E3 Alternative test (one bullet): acidified potassium dichromate solution turns from orange to green when warmed with ethanol. Many schools avoid it because potassium dichromate is toxic.

Using bacteria instead of yeast (one block, short)
- [ ] Y1 The specification allows bacteria: lactic acid bacteria ferment the sugar lactose in milk to lactic acid, as in making yogurt. Set up warm milk with a spoonful of live natural yogurt (the source of the bacteria) in clean, closed containers kept at a warm temperature in an incubator or water bath; compare with milk without yogurt, and boiled yogurt.
- [ ] Y2 Measure the fall in pH (with a pH meter, pH probe or universal indicator) over several hours; the milk also thickens as the acid makes its protein clump. No CO₂ is produced in lactic acid fermentation, so limewater is not used. The same four conditions can be tested.

Safety (one block)
- [ ] H1 Wear safety glasses. Sodium hypochlorite (bleach) is corrosive and irritant to skin and eyes; wash splashes off with plenty of water. Potassium dichromate, if used, is toxic and must be handled with gloves.
- [ ] H2 Take care with boiling water, the hot plate and water baths; let water cool before adding yeast.
- [ ] H3 Never seal a fermenting flask with a solid bung and no outlet: the CO₂ builds up pressure and can force out the bung or crack the glass.
- [ ] H4 Ethanol is flammable: keep it away from flames. Never taste the products: laboratory glassware and the solutions are not food-safe.
- [ ] H5 Treat microorganisms with care: wash hands after the practical, and dispose of yeast and bacterial cultures as the teacher directs. Never eat the yogurt made in the laboratory.

Results and conclusion (one block)
- [ ] D1 Record primary data in a table: one row per flask, with the condition, mean bubbles per minute (or volume of gas), limewater (milky or clear) and the ethanol test (crystals or none). For the temperature series, plot the mean rate (y-axis) against temperature (x-axis).
- [ ] D2 Expected results: the full flask bubbles steadily, limewater turns milky and the iodoform test is positive; the flasks without sugar or without living yeast show no CO₂ and no ethanol; the aerated flask gives CO₂ but little or no ethanol; the rate rises with temperature to a peak at about 30–40 °C and stops at 60 °C. The control shows no activity.
- [ ] D3 Conclusion: fermentation (production of ethanol and CO₂) takes place only when sugar and living yeast are present, oxygen is absent and the temperature is suitable. Say whether the results support the hypothesis.
- [ ] D4 Diagram: the fermentation set-up: a conical flask in a water bath with thermometer, containing glucose solution and yeast under a layer of oil, fitted with a fermentation lock (or a delivery tube into a test tube of limewater); label each part. Optionally beside it the control flask with boiled yeast.

Secondary data (one block)
- [ ] S1 Pool class results, or compare with published data, to check reproducibility, e.g. CO₂ production rates measured with a CO₂ sensor and data logger, or the mass lost by a fermenting flask on a balance as CO₂ escapes.
- [ ] S2 Applications of the conditions (one bullet; industry detail in "Microorganisms"): brewers and wine-makers exclude air so that yeast makes ethanol, and control the temperature; bakers keep dough warm so yeast makes CO₂ quickly to raise it; yogurt makers incubate milk warm with bacteria.

Sources of error and limitations (one block)
- [ ] R1 Oxygen can enter if the water was not boiled, the oil layer is broken, or the fermentation lock leaks, so some respiration is aerobic and less ethanol is made.
- [ ] R2 Yeast can still ferment some sugar when oxygen is present if plenty of sugar is available, so the aerated flask may give a weak positive ethanol test; compare the amount of crystals rather than presence alone.
- [ ] R3 Bubbles vary in size and some CO₂ dissolves in the solution, so bubble counts are imprecise and underestimate the gas produced; collecting the gas, or weighing the mass lost, is more accurate.
- [ ] R4 Limewater turns milky slowly in air, and very large amounts of CO₂ make the milkiness clear again; set up a limewater tube open to the air for comparison and observe at set times.
- [ ] R5 Temperature can drift; check the water bath regularly. Yeast takes time to start, so begin timing after the same period in every flask.
- [ ] R6 Differences between samples of yeast; use yeast from the same batch, mixed well, for every flask, and repeat each set-up at least three times.

## 3. Traps

- Boil the water to remove oxygen and then cool it before adding yeast; adding yeast to hot water kills it. Students often give only one of the two reasons.
- The oil layer excludes oxygen; it doesn't "keep the heat in" or "stop evaporation" as its purpose.
- The control is boiled (dead) yeast with glucose, not "no glucose"; the no-sugar flask tests a different condition.
- Limewater shows CO₂, not ethanol, and CO₂ is produced in aerobic respiration too. Only the ethanol test shows that fermentation (anaerobic respiration) has happened.
- At low temperature enzymes work slowly but are not denatured; only high temperature denatures them.
- Lactic acid fermentation gives no CO₂: don't use limewater with the bacterial version.
- Don't call the iodoform test product "iodine crystals"; they are iodoform crystals.
- The industrial uses of fermentation, bioreactors and immobilised yeast belong to "Microorganisms" and "Industrial enzymes"; S2 gives one bullet only. The fermentation pathways (pyruvate, NADH) belong to "First-stage process".
- No other diagram cues beyond D4.

## 4. What exams ask (topics only)

- Name the microorganism used and the sugar it was given.
- Explain why the glucose solution was boiled and then cooled before the yeast was added.
- State how anaerobic conditions were achieved and how the temperature was kept constant.
- Describe a control and explain its purpose.
- Name a product of fermentation other than ethanol and give a test for it (CO₂, limewater); describe the test for ethanol and the positive result.
- Explain the result of a set-up that lacks one of the conditions.

## 5. Practice questions to write

1. 6 marks: "Explain why, in an investigation of fermentation, the glucose solution is boiled and cooled before yeast is added, and why a layer of oil is placed on top." Model answer: M1 (removes oxygen and microorganisms; cooling so the yeast isn't killed); M3 (keeps air out, anaerobic).
2. 12 marks: "Describe how you would show that yeast needs sugar and anaerobic conditions to produce ethanol. Include a control and the test for ethanol." Model answer: M1–M6; V1 and V3 set-ups and expected results; A4 control; E1–E2 iodoform test (pale yellow crystals).
3. 9 marks: "A student set up identical flasks of yeast in glucose solution at 5 °C, 30 °C and 60 °C. Predict and explain the rate of bubbling in each flask." Model answer: V4 with B2 reasons (slow, enzymes slowed not denatured; fastest; none, enzymes denatured and yeast killed).
