## 1. Must cover

Microorganisms in nutrient cycling (one block)
- [ ] N1 Microorganisms are organisms too small to be seen without a microscope, e.g. bacteria, many fungi (such as yeasts and moulds), protists and viruses. Most are harmless or useful; only some are pathogens (see "Pathogens").
- [ ] N2 Bacteria and fungi are the main decomposers: they feed on dead organisms, dead leaves, dung and other wastes, breaking down the complex organic compounds and releasing carbon dioxide (by respiration) and minerals such as ammonium and phosphate into the soil, where plants reuse them. Without them, nutrients would stay locked in dead matter (cycles in "The carbon cycle" and "The nitrogen cycle").
- [ ] N3 Other bacteria carry out particular steps of the nitrogen cycle: nitrogen-fixing bacteria (free-living in soil, or in the root nodules of legumes), nitrifying bacteria (which convert ammonium to nitrate) and denitrifying bacteria (which convert nitrate to nitrogen gas). Their names are not required; the steps are in "The nitrogen cycle".

The bacterial cell (one block, Structure and function)
- [ ] B1 (assumed) Bacteria are single-celled prokaryotes: no nucleus and no membrane-bound organelles; most are about 1–10 µm long, so they are seen as tiny dots or rods under the light microscope and their parts need the electron microscope ("Prokaryotic and eukaryotic cells"; one sentence).
- [ ] B2 Cell wall: a rigid layer outside the membrane, made of peptidoglycan (a polymer of sugars and amino acids). It gives the cell its shape and stops it bursting when water enters by osmosis. Penicillin works by stopping bacteria building it (see "Acquired immunity, prevention and treatment").
- [ ] B3 Capsule (slime layer): a sticky layer of polysaccharide outside the wall, present in some bacteria. It protects the cell from drying out and from being engulfed by white blood cells, and helps it stick to surfaces (e.g. teeth). Bacteria with capsules are often more harmful.
- [ ] B4 Cell membrane: controls what enters and leaves the cell; the enzymes of respiration are on it (bacteria have no mitochondria).
- [ ] B5 Cytoplasm: the jelly-like contents where most reactions take place; contains food stores (e.g. granules of glycogen or lipid).
- [ ] B6 Nucleoid (chromosome): one circular chromosome of DNA, lying free in the cytoplasm in a region called the nucleoid, not enclosed by a membrane and not wound around histones. It carries the genes for all the cell's essential functions.
- [ ] B7 Plasmids: small extra rings of DNA, separate from the chromosome, which copy themselves independently. They carry a few genes that are useful in some conditions (e.g. antibiotic resistance) and can be passed from one bacterium to another, even of a different species. Plasmids are used as vectors in genetic engineering (see "Genetic engineering").
- [ ] B8 Ribosomes: small (70S) ribosomes free in the cytoplasm, where proteins are made; smaller than the 80S ribosomes in eukaryotic cytoplasm, which is why some antibiotics can block them without harming human cells.
- [ ] B9 Flagellum (plural flagella): a long, whip-like thread, present in some bacteria, that rotates like a propeller to move the cell, e.g. towards food.
- [ ] B10 Pili (fimbriae): short, hair-like threads on the surface of some bacteria that attach them to surfaces and host cells; a longer sex pilus links two bacteria so that a copy of a plasmid can pass between them.
- [ ] B11 Diagram: a large labelled bacterial cell (a rod): capsule, cell wall, cell membrane, cytoplasm, nucleoid with circular chromosome, plasmids, ribosomes, flagellum, pili, and a food-storage granule; with a function written beside each label.

Bacterial shapes, reproduction and endospores (one block)
- [ ] S1 Bacteria are classified partly by shape: cocci (singular coccus) are spherical, e.g. Streptococcus (in chains) and Staphylococcus (in clusters); bacilli (singular bacillus) are rod-shaped, e.g. Escherichia coli; spirilla are spiral-shaped; vibrios are comma-shaped, e.g. Vibrio cholerae, which causes cholera.
- [ ] S2 Diagram: the four shapes: a chain of cocci, a cluster of cocci, a rod (bacillus), a spiral (spirillum) and a comma (vibrio), each labelled.
- [ ] S3 (assumed) Reproduction: bacteria reproduce asexually by binary fission: the circular chromosome is copied, the cell grows and the two copies move apart, then the cell divides in two by a new wall forming across the middle, giving two genetically identical cells. In ideal conditions this can happen as often as every 20 minutes, so populations grow very fast (growth curves in "Microorganisms in industry"). Variation comes only from mutations and from genes passed on plasmids.
- [ ] S4 (assumed) Endospores: some bacteria (e.g. Clostridium and Bacillus) form an endospore when conditions become unfavourable (e.g. lack of nutrients or water): the chromosome and a little cytoplasm are enclosed in a tough, thick wall inside the cell, and the rest of the cell breaks down. Endospores survive boiling, drying, freezing and many disinfectants for years; when conditions improve, each grows into one new active bacterium. Endospores are why equipment is sterilised in an autoclave at 121 °C rather than by boiling, and why tetanus can be caught from soil (see "Pathogens"). An endospore is a survival structure, not a means of reproduction (one cell gives one spore gives one cell).

Nutrition of bacteria (one block)
- [ ] T1 Autotrophic bacteria make their own food from simple inorganic substances such as carbon dioxide and water ("Characteristics of living things" defines autotroph; use the term).
  - Photosynthetic bacteria use light energy, e.g. cyanobacteria, which contain chlorophyll and release oxygen; some others (purple and green sulfur bacteria) use hydrogen sulfide in place of water and release no oxygen.
  - Chemosynthetic bacteria get energy by oxidising inorganic chemicals instead of from light, e.g. nitrifying bacteria in soil, which oxidise ammonium to nitrite and nitrite to nitrate, and use the energy released to make food from carbon dioxide.
- [ ] T2 Heterotrophic bacteria take in food made by other organisms. Most bacteria are heterotrophic, in one of these ways:
  - Saprophytic: they feed on dead organic matter. A saprophyte (saprotroph) secretes digestive enzymes out of its cell onto the food (extracellular digestion), then absorbs the small soluble products (e.g. sugars and amino acids) through its wall and membrane. Saprophytic bacteria are decomposers (N2); they also cause food to rot.
  - Parasitic: they feed on a living host and harm it, causing disease, e.g. Mycobacterium tuberculosis (tuberculosis) (pathogenic bacteria in "Pathogens").
  - Mutualistic: some live with a host to the benefit of both, e.g. nitrogen-fixing bacteria in clover root nodules and bacteria in the gut (relationships in "Biotic factors and relationships"; the gut in "The microbiome").
- [ ] T3 Respiration (one bullet): obligate aerobes need oxygen; obligate anaerobes are killed by oxygen (e.g. Clostridium, which is why tetanus grows in deep wounds); facultative anaerobes respire aerobically when oxygen is present and anaerobically when it isn't (e.g. E. coli).

Fungi in general (one block)
- [ ] G1 (assumed) Fungi are eukaryotes: each cell, or each section of hypha, has nuclei with a nuclear membrane, mitochondria and 80S ribosomes. Their cell walls are made of chitin (not cellulose). They have no chlorophyll, so they can't photosynthesise; they store carbohydrate as glycogen and lipid, not starch (kingdom features in "Classification"; use without restating).
- [ ] G2 Most fungi (moulds and mushrooms) are made of hyphae (singular hypha): long, branching, thread-like tubes with a wall around cytoplasm containing many nuclei. A mass of hyphae is a mycelium. Some fungi have cross-walls (septa) dividing the hyphae into cells; Rhizopus has none. Mushrooms are the reproductive structures of a mycelium that grows mostly hidden in soil or wood.
- [ ] G3 Yeasts are single-celled fungi, e.g. Saccharomyces cerevisiae (baker's and brewer's yeast). They are oval cells that reproduce asexually by budding: a small outgrowth grows from the parent cell, the nucleus divides by mitosis, and the bud separates as a new cell. Yeast can respire aerobically or ferment sugar to ethanol and CO₂ (see "Respiration as a catabolic process").
- [ ] G4 Fungi reproduce by spores, tiny reproductive cells released in great numbers and carried by air, water or animals; each can grow into a new mycelium on a suitable food. They also reproduce sexually, but that is not required.

Rhizopus (one block, Structure and function)
- [ ] R1 Rhizopus stolonifer (black bread mould) is a common mould that grows on bread, fruit and other moist, starchy or sugary food. Its spores are everywhere in the air, which is why bread left moist and warm goes mouldy within days.
- [ ] R2 Hyphae: the mycelium is made of branched hyphae with no cross-walls, so each hypha is a continuous tube of cytoplasm with many nuclei. The walls contain chitin.
- [ ] R3 Stolons: hyphae that grow horizontally over the surface of the food, spreading the mould to new areas of food (the name stolonifer means "bearing stolons").
- [ ] R4 Rhizoids: short, branched, root-like hyphae that grow down into the food where a stolon touches it. They anchor the mould and secrete digestive enzymes into the food and absorb the products.
- [ ] R5 Sporangiophores: upright hyphae that grow up from the stolons, above the rhizoids, each carrying a sporangium at its tip.
- [ ] R6 Sporangium (plural sporangia): a round spore case at the tip of a sporangiophore, white at first and black when ripe (giving the mould its black, fuzzy look). Inside, many spores form around a dome-shaped swelling at the top of the sporangiophore, the columella, which supports the sporangium.
- [ ] R7 Spores: when the sporangium is ripe, its wall breaks and the spores are released and carried by air currents; each spore that lands on moist food grows into a new hypha and mycelium. This is asexual reproduction, so the offspring are genetically identical.
- [ ] R8 Nutrition: Rhizopus is a saprophyte (block below). Being heterotrophic, it can't make food, so it must grow on its food.
- [ ] R9 Diagram: a large labelled drawing of Rhizopus growing on bread: mycelium, hyphae, stolon, rhizoids penetrating the bread, sporangiophores, sporangia (one cut open to show spores and the columella), spores being released.

Nutrition of fungi (one block)
- [ ] F1 All fungi are heterotrophic; none is autotrophic, because fungi have no chlorophyll.
- [ ] F2 Saprophytic fungi: most fungi are saprophytes, feeding on dead organic matter by extracellular digestion (T2). Example: Rhizopus secretes enzymes such as amylase (starch → maltose and glucose) and proteases (proteins → amino acids) from its rhizoids and hyphae into bread; the soluble products diffuse back and are absorbed and used for respiration and growth. Mushrooms and other wood- and leaf-rotting fungi are the main decomposers of the cellulose and woody material of dead plants, which most bacteria can't break down.
- [ ] F3 Parasitic fungi feed on living hosts and harm them, e.g. athlete's foot in humans and ash dieback in ash trees (examples in "Pathogens").
- [ ] F4 Mutualistic fungi live in partnership: mycorrhizal fungi with plant roots, and the fungus in a lichen (see "Biotic factors and relationships").

Distinguishing bacteria and fungi (one comparison block, paired points)
- [ ] D1 Cellular nature: bacteria are prokaryotic, with no nucleus and no membrane-bound organelles; fungi are eukaryotic, with nuclei, mitochondria and other membrane-bound organelles.
- [ ] D2 DNA: bacteria have one circular chromosome, not wound around histones, plus plasmids; fungi have several linear chromosomes of DNA wound around histones, inside a nucleus.
- [ ] D3 Ribosomes: bacteria 70S; fungi 80S in the cytoplasm.
- [ ] D4 Cell wall: bacteria peptidoglycan; fungi chitin.
- [ ] D5 Organisation and size: bacteria are single cells about 1–10 µm long (they grow in colonies, but each cell lives independently); most fungi are multicellular, with hyphae that form a mycelium visible to the naked eye, although yeasts are single cells (larger than most bacteria).
- [ ] D6 Nutrition: bacteria can be autotrophic (photosynthetic or chemosynthetic) or heterotrophic (saprophytic, parasitic or mutualistic); fungi are always heterotrophic (saprophytic, parasitic or mutualistic).
- [ ] D7 Food stores: bacteria store glycogen or lipid granules; fungi store glycogen and lipid (neither stores starch).
- [ ] D8 Reproduction: bacteria by binary fission (with endospores in some for survival); fungi by spores (moulds) or budding (yeasts), and also sexually.
- [ ] D9 Treatment of infections: antibiotics act on bacteria (targeting the peptidoglycan wall or 70S ribosomes), not on fungi; fungal infections need antifungal drugs.
- [ ] D10 Similarities (one bullet): both can be decomposers that digest food outside their cells and absorb it; both have cell walls, a cell membrane, cytoplasm, ribosomes and DNA; both include pathogens and many useful species (see "Microorganisms in industry").

## 3. Traps

- Bacteria have no nucleus, but they do have DNA (in the nucleoid). Don't write "bacteria have no DNA" or "the nucleoid is a nucleus".
- Fungal walls are chitin, not cellulose; bacterial walls are peptidoglycan. Fungi are not plants and don't photosynthesise.
- Saprophytes digest food outside their cells (extracellular digestion) and then absorb it; they don't "eat" or engulf it.
- Rhizoids are hyphae, not roots; they absorb food, not water from soil.
- An endospore is a survival structure, not reproduction (S4).
- Don't use the kingdom name "Monera"; bacteria are in the domain Bacteria (see "Domains of life").
- Sexual reproduction in Rhizopus (zygospores, + and − strains) and the stages of bacterial conjugation were on the previous syllabus; the specification asks only for structure. G4 mentions that fungi also reproduce sexually in one phrase; B10 names the sex pilus only. Don't describe either process.
- Bacterial growth curves, aseptic technique and culturing belong to "Microorganisms in industry" and "Investigation: growth of microorganisms". Pathogenic species and diseases belong to "Pathogens"; the nitrogen-cycle steps to "The nitrogen cycle".
- Viruses are not bacteria or fungi; their structure is in "Viruses".
- The diagram cues in B11, S2 and R9 are the only ones wanted. No other diagram cues.
