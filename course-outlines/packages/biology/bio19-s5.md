# Package: bio19-s5 — Conduction across the synaptic cleft and its disruption

- **loId:** `bio19-s5` · **chapterId:** `bio19` · **syllabusRef:** 2.4.8
- **Syllabus (all bold, Higher Level only):** "conduction of nerve impulses: neurotransmitters
  activated by ions, released into cleft for short time, transmitting the impulse to the next
  neuron; neurotransmitter inactivated by an enzyme after transmission, reabsorbed by the
  presynaptic neuron to make new neurotransmitter substance; disruptions to this mechanism
  leading to acute and chronic disorders which are treated through drugs that inhibit enzymes
  that degrade neurotransmitters or inhibit reuptake of neurotransmitters at the synapse,
  creating an enhanced effect (similar processes for psychoactive drugs and painkillers)."
  Outcome 8: "model impulse travel across a synaptic cleft, consider the impacts of
  disruptions to impulse travel."
- **Level:** every block in this subsection ends its heading with " (Higher Level only)".
- **Prepared by:** a higher model, 2026-10-20.

## 1. Must cover

Release and inactivation
- [ ] C1 Release is triggered by ions (calcium ions entering the knob) and lasts only a short time, so each impulse produces a brief burst of neurotransmitter. Refer to "Neurotransmission" for the release stages; don't repeat them.
- [ ] C2 After it has passed on the impulse, the neurotransmitter is inactivated by an enzyme in the cleft. Example: acetylcholinesterase breaks acetylcholine down into choline and ethanoic acid (acetate).
- [ ] C3 The breakdown products are reabsorbed by the presynaptic neuron and used to make new neurotransmitter. Some neurotransmitters, e.g. serotonin and dopamine, are mainly reabsorbed whole by transporter proteins in the presynaptic membrane (reuptake).
- [ ] C4 Why inactivation matters: if the neurotransmitter stayed in the cleft, the postsynaptic neuron would keep firing, so the signal couldn't be switched off and repeated signals couldn't be told apart.
- [ ] C5 Model: write the whole cycle as a numbered loop (arrival → ion entry → release → diffusion → binding → new impulse → enzyme breakdown or reuptake → reabsorption → resynthesis and storage in vesicles), and give a "Diagram:" cue for a synapse showing the enzyme in the cleft and reuptake arrows into the presynaptic knob.

Disruptions and the disorders they cause
- [ ] D1 Disruption can come from too little or too much neurotransmitter, faulty receptors, or blocked breakdown or reuptake. Short-term (acute) or long-term (chronic) disorders can result.
- [ ] D2 Acute example: nerve agents and organophosphate insecticides inhibit acetylcholinesterase, so acetylcholine builds up at synapses with muscles, causing continuous muscle contraction, paralysis and, in severe cases, death from failure of the breathing muscles.
- [ ] D3 Chronic example: in Alzheimer's disease, neurons that use acetylcholine are lost, contributing to memory loss.
- [ ] D4 Chronic example: depression is associated with altered signalling by serotonin and noradrenaline. Its causes are complex; don't say it is "caused by low serotonin".
- [ ] D5 Chronic example: in Parkinson's disease, dopamine-producing neurons in the brain die, causing tremor, stiffness and slow movement.

Treatments: two drug strategies, both creating an enhanced effect
- [ ] E1 Strategy 1, enzyme inhibitors: a drug inhibits the enzyme that breaks down a neurotransmitter, so the neurotransmitter stays in the cleft longer and its effect is enhanced. Examples: acetylcholinesterase inhibitors (e.g. donepezil) used to treat the symptoms of Alzheimer's disease; monoamine oxidase (MAO) inhibitors, antidepressants that slow the breakdown of serotonin, noradrenaline and dopamine.
- [ ] E2 Strategy 2, reuptake inhibitors: a drug blocks the transporter that reabsorbs a neurotransmitter, so more stays in the cleft for longer. Example: selective serotonin reuptake inhibitors (SSRIs, e.g. fluoxetine) used to treat depression.
- [ ] E3 Neither strategy cures the disorder; they treat symptoms by enhancing the remaining signalling.

Psychoactive drugs and painkillers work in similar ways
- [ ] G1 A psychoactive drug alters brain function, affecting mood, perception or behaviour.
- [ ] G2 Cocaine blocks the reuptake of dopamine, so dopamine builds up in the cleft, producing euphoria. (Its effect on mood and addiction belongs to "Dopamine and endorphins"; one sentence here.)
- [ ] G3 Nicotine mimics acetylcholine, binding to and activating some of its receptors.
- [ ] G4 Opioid painkillers such as morphine bind to the same receptors as endorphins, mimicking them and reducing the sensation of pain.
- [ ] G5 A drug may instead block receptors, preventing transmission: curare blocks acetylcholine receptors on muscles, causing paralysis.
- [ ] G6 Consider impacts: summarise in a comparison block how each type of disruption (blocked release, blocked receptors, blocked breakdown, blocked reuptake, mimics) changes impulse travel: less or no transmission vs prolonged or enhanced transmission.

## 3. Traps

- Every block is Higher Level only; mark every heading.
- No electrochemistry (no potentials, no millivolts).
- Don't repeat the release stages from "Neurotransmission"; start from C1.
- Endorphins' roles and lifestyle effects on dopamine belong to "Dopamine and endorphins". Here they appear only as G2 and G4.
- Don't claim any drug cures Alzheimer's, Parkinson's or depression.
- Don't call depression simply a "chemical imbalance".
