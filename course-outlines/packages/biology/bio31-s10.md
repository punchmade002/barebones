## 1. Must cover

The concept of the niche (one block)
- [ ] N1 The niche of a species is its role (functional position) in its ecosystem: what it feeds on and what feeds on it, where in the habitat it lives and feeds, when it is active, the range of abiotic conditions (temperature, moisture, light, pH) it can tolerate, its interactions with other species, and its effects on its environment.
- [ ] N2 Habitat vs niche: the habitat is where an organism lives (its "address"); the niche is how it lives there (its "profession"). Many species share a habitat, but each has its own niche, e.g. in an oak woodland, caterpillars feed on the leaves, woodlice and earthworms feed on dead leaves on the floor, blue tits eat caterpillars in the canopy, and fungi decompose fallen wood.
- [ ] N3 A species' niche is the result of its adaptations: its structural, behavioural and physiological features suit it to a particular way of life, and they also limit it to that way of life.
- [ ] N4 Competitive exclusion: two species can't occupy exactly the same niche in the same place for long. If they try, they compete for the same resources, and the better-adapted species wins and the other declines or is excluded locally. Example: the grey squirrel and the red squirrel have similar niches in Irish woodland (tree-living seed and nut eaters); in broadleaf woodland the grey squirrel, which is larger and can feed efficiently on acorns that red squirrels digest poorly, outcompetes the red squirrel, which survives better in conifer woodland.
- [ ] N5 Niche differentiation: similar species can live together if their niches differ, e.g. by feeding on different foods, at different heights or at different times of day. Example: in the same grassland, the kestrel hunts small mammals by day and the barn owl hunts them at night. (Kestrel and barn owl both occur in Ireland.)
- [ ] N6 Specialist and generalist niches: a specialist has a narrow niche (one food or habitat), which makes it efficient but vulnerable to change, e.g. the caterpillars of the marsh fritillary butterfly feed mainly on one plant, devil's-bit scabious, so the butterfly depends on the wet grasslands and bogs where that plant grows. A generalist has a broad niche, e.g. the red fox eats rabbits, mice, birds, insects, earthworms, fruit and scraps, and lives in countryside and towns, so it copes well with change.
- [ ] N7 An invasive species is often one that finds a niche unfilled, or fills an existing niche better than the native species, in its new home (e.g. the grey squirrel; case in "Biodiversity loss and conservation"). One bullet.

Examples: niches and adaptations (one block per example, or two examples per block; each: ecosystem → niche → adaptations, each adaptation with what it does)
- [ ] E1 Sundew (raised and blanket bogs). Niche: a producer that photosynthesises in waterlogged, acidic bog where the peat is very poor in nitrogen, and supplements its nitrogen by catching and digesting insects. Adaptations: leaves covered in red hairs (tentacles), each tipped with a drop of sticky fluid that glistens and attracts and traps small insects; the tentacles bend over the trapped insect; glands secrete enzymes that digest it, and the leaf absorbs the products, including nitrogen compounds. Small rosette of leaves close to the ground in an open, sunny habitat.
- [ ] E2 Limpet (rocky shore). Niche: a primary consumer (grazer) that scrapes the thin film of algae off rocks between the tides, eaten by birds such as oystercatchers and by crabs. Adaptations: a low, conical shell that waves flow over without dislodging it; a strong muscular foot that clamps it to the rock against waves and predators; at low tide it clamps down onto its own "home scar", a groove ground into the rock to match its shell, which seals in water and prevents drying out; a rough, tongue-like radula that rasps algae from rock; it feeds when covered by the tide and returns to its home scar (behavioural).
- [ ] E3 Barn owl (farmland and grassland). Niche: a nocturnal predator (secondary or tertiary consumer) of small mammals such as mice, rats and shrews, nesting in old buildings and hollow trees. Adaptations: soft feathers with fringed, comb-like edges on the flight feathers that make its flight almost silent; a heart-shaped facial disc of feathers that funnels sound to the ears; ears at slightly different heights on each side of the head, so it can locate prey by sound alone in darkness; large forward-facing eyes for binocular vision in dim light; sharp, curved talons and hooked beak to grip and tear prey.
- [ ] E4 Bumblebee or honeybee (grassland, meadows, hedgerows, gardens). Niche: a primary consumer that feeds on nectar (energy) and pollen (protein), and a pollinator of flowering plants, transferring pollen from flower to flower. Adaptations: a long, tube-like tongue (proboscis) to reach nectar inside flowers; a hairy body to which pollen sticks; pollen baskets (stiff hairs on the hind legs) to carry pollen back to the nest; eyes that see ultraviolet patterns on petals that guide them to nectar; honeybees communicate the location of flowers to others by a dance (behavioural). Plant side of pollination is in "Flower structure and pollination".
- [ ] E5 Earthworm (soil of grassland and woodland). Niche: a detritivore that eats dead leaves and soil organic matter, recycling nutrients, aerating and draining the soil with its burrows and mixing the soil layers; food for blackbirds, thrushes, badgers and foxes. Adaptations: a long, thin, segmented body without legs for burrowing; tiny bristles (chaetae) on each segment that grip the burrow wall; mucus that lubricates movement and binds burrow walls; a thin, moist skin through which it exchanges gases, so it lives in damp soil and comes to the surface mainly at night or in wet weather; it pulls leaves into its burrow, feeding below ground away from predators.
- [ ] E6 Marram grass (sand dunes). Niche: a pioneer producer that colonises loose, dry, salty sand and binds it into dunes, allowing other plants to settle later. Adaptations: leaves that roll into a tube in dry conditions, with stomata on the inner surface in grooves lined with hairs, trapping moist air and reducing water loss by transpiration; long roots that reach water deep in the sand; underground stems (rhizomes) that spread and bind the sand; it grows upward when buried by blowing sand. (Plant water-loss detail in "Transport in Plants".)

Summary of the niche concept for exam use (one bullet, or the lead of the first block)
- [ ] S1 To describe an organism's niche, state: its ecosystem and habitat; its trophic level and what it eats; what eats it; when and where it is active; the conditions it tolerates; and two or three adaptations, each matched to the part of its niche it suits.

## 3. Traps

- Habitat and niche are different (N2); students often treat them as the same.
- The niche is not just "what it eats": include where, when, conditions tolerated and interactions.
- Don't teach fundamental vs realised niche or Gause's experiments by name; they are not on the specification. N4 states the principle only.
- Adaptation types and natural selection belong to "The theory of evolution by natural selection"; don't redefine them. Predator–prey adaptations and symbiosis types in a chosen ecosystem belong to "Biotic factors and relationships"; the examples here are about niches.
- Don't say adaptations appear "because the organism needs them"; they arise by natural selection.
- Grey and red squirrel case detail (squirrelpox, introduction in 1911) is in "Biodiversity loss and conservation"; N4 covers only the niche overlap.
- No diagram cues are required; optionally one: "Diagram: a sundew leaf with sticky tentacles curved over a trapped insect, labelled." No other diagram cues.

## 4. What exams ask (topics only)

- Define niche; distinguish between niche and habitat.
- Describe the niche of a named organism and explain how two adaptations suit it to its niche.
- Explain what happens when two species have the same niche.
- Give examples of structural and behavioural adaptations (legacy questions asked for adaptations with their benefits).

## 5. Practice questions to write

1. 6 marks: "Explain the difference between a habitat and a niche, using one organism as an example." Model answer: N1–N2 with one organism's habitat and niche (e.g. limpet: rocky shore; grazer of algae, eaten by oystercatchers and crabs).
2. 12 marks: "Outline the concept of the niche. Describe the niche of one named animal and one named plant and explain how three adaptations suit each to its niche." Model answer: N1, N3; two of E1–E6, each with niche and three matched adaptations.
3. 9 marks: "Red squirrels and grey squirrels occupy similar niches. Explain what happens when they live in the same woodland, and why the red squirrel survives better in some woodlands than others." Model answer: N4 (competitive exclusion; grey larger and feeds efficiently on acorns; reds persist in conifer woods), N7 (invasive species).
