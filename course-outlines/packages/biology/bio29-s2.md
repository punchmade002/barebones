## 1. Must cover

The circulatory system (one short block)
- [ ] O1 (assumed) The human circulatory system consists of the heart (a pump), blood vessels (arteries, capillaries and veins, a closed network of tubes) and blood (the transport medium). It is a closed system: blood stays inside vessels, and materials leave or enter it only across capillary walls.
- [ ] O2 Why it is needed: in a large, many-celled animal, diffusion alone is far too slow to move materials between the exchange surfaces (lungs, gut, kidneys) and cells deep in the body; the circulatory system carries materials quickly by mass flow, so every cell is within a short diffusion distance of a capillary.
- [ ] O3 Materials transported (one bullet): oxygen (lungs → all cells); carbon dioxide (cells → lungs); digested nutrients such as glucose, amino acids, vitamins and minerals (small intestine → liver → cells); urea (liver → kidneys); hormones (endocrine glands → target organs); water and salts; antibodies and white blood cells (to sites of infection); clotting factors and platelets; heat (from active organs such as the liver and muscles to the whole body and the skin).

The double (two-circuit) circulation (one process block)
- [ ] D1 Humans have a double circulation: in one complete circuit of the body, blood passes through the heart twice. The two circuits are the pulmonary circuit (heart → lungs → heart) and the systemic circuit (heart → body organs → heart). The right side of the heart pumps the pulmonary circuit and the left side pumps the systemic circuit (sides in "Investigation: the heart").
- [ ] D2 Pulmonary circuit, step by step: 1. deoxygenated blood is pumped by the right ventricle into the pulmonary artery; 2. the pulmonary artery divides into a branch to each lung; 3. in the lung capillaries the blood picks up oxygen and loses CO₂ (see "Gas exchange and the breathing mechanism"); 4. oxygenated blood returns in the pulmonary veins to the left atrium.
- [ ] D3 Systemic circuit, step by step: 1. oxygenated blood passes from the left atrium through the bicuspid valve to the left ventricle; 2. the left ventricle pumps it into the aorta; 3. arteries branching from the aorta carry it to every organ (except the lungs' gas-exchange tissue), where it passes through capillaries, giving up oxygen and nutrients and collecting CO₂ and wastes; 4. deoxygenated blood returns through veins to the superior and inferior venae cavae and into the right atrium; 5. it passes through the tricuspid valve to the right ventricle, and the pulmonary circuit begins again.
- [ ] D4 Full route of a red blood cell (one bullet, as a chain): vena cava → right atrium → tricuspid valve → right ventricle → semilunar valve → pulmonary artery → lung capillaries → pulmonary vein → left atrium → bicuspid valve → left ventricle → semilunar valve → aorta → artery → arteriole → capillaries of an organ → venule → vein → vena cava.
- [ ] D5 Advantages of a double circulation (one bullet each): (a) blood returning from the lungs has lost much of its pressure in the lung capillaries; returning it to the heart lets it be pumped out again at high pressure, so it reaches all body organs quickly; (b) the two circuits can run at different pressures: high in the systemic circuit to drive blood through the many organs, lower in the pulmonary circuit so the thin-walled lung capillaries are not damaged and fluid is not forced into the alveoli; (c) the septum keeps oxygenated and deoxygenated blood separate, so the blood reaching the organs is fully oxygenated. Together these deliver oxygen fast enough for the high respiration rate of a warm-blooded, active animal.
- [ ] D6 Diagram: a schematic of the double circulation: the heart drawn as four boxes (right atrium and ventricle, left atrium and ventricle) with the lungs above and the body organs below; arrows for the pulmonary artery, pulmonary veins, aorta and venae cavae; shade oxygenated blood red and deoxygenated blood blue; label pulmonary circuit and systemic circuit.

Pathways of blood circulation: the named vessels (one block, one bullet per organ; Ordinary and Higher)
- [ ] V1 (assumed) A circulation pathway is the route from the heart to one organ and back. Each organ is supplied by an artery that branches from the aorta and drained by a vein that joins a vena cava, and is usually named after the organ.
- [ ] V2 Lungs (pulmonary pathway): pulmonary artery (from the right ventricle, deoxygenated) and pulmonary veins (to the left atrium, oxygenated). In adults these are the only artery carrying deoxygenated blood and the only veins carrying oxygenated blood.
- [ ] V3 Head and brain: carotid arteries (in the neck) supply it; jugular veins drain it to the superior vena cava.
- [ ] V4 Arms: subclavian arteries and veins.
- [ ] V5 Kidneys: renal arteries bring blood containing urea to be filtered; renal veins return filtered blood (low in urea) to the inferior vena cava (filtration in "The urinary system").
- [ ] V6 Legs: iliac arteries and veins.
- [ ] V7 Heart muscle: coronary arteries and cardiac veins (detail in "Heartbeat"; one phrase here).
- [ ] V8 Liver and gut: see the portal pathway block. The liver receives blood from two vessels: the hepatic artery (oxygenated blood from the aorta) and the hepatic portal vein (nutrient-rich blood from the gut); blood leaves the liver in the hepatic vein, which joins the inferior vena cava.
- [ ] V9 Diagram: a simple plan of the body's main vessels: heart, lungs, head, arms, liver, gut, kidneys and legs, with the aorta and venae cavae and each organ's named artery and vein, including the hepatic artery, hepatic portal vein and hepatic vein.

The portal pathway (one block)
- [ ] PT1 A portal system is a vein that carries blood from one capillary network to a second capillary network, instead of straight back to the heart. So the blood passes through two sets of capillaries between leaving the heart and returning to it.
- [ ] PT2 The hepatic portal vein: blood from the capillaries in the villi of the small intestine (and the walls of the stomach and large intestine) collects in the hepatic portal vein, which carries it to a second set of capillaries (the sinusoids) in the liver. After a meal its blood is rich in glucose, amino acids and other absorbed nutrients.
- [ ] PT3 Why it matters (one bullet): absorbed water-soluble nutrients, and any absorbed toxins or drugs, reach the liver before the rest of the body, so the liver can process them and keep the composition of the blood reaching the body steady. How the liver does this (glycogen storage, deamination) and the villi are taught in "Absorption, transport and storage"; name them only.
- [ ] PT4 The hepatic portal vein carries deoxygenated blood (it has already passed through the gut capillaries), so the liver gets its oxygen from the hepatic artery (V8).

Modelling interactions with other body systems (one block; one bullet per system, each naming where the exchange happens and what is transferred)
- [ ] I1 Breathing system: in the lung capillaries the blood takes up oxygen and releases CO₂; the circulatory system carries oxygen to every respiring cell and CO₂ back. Without circulation, the gas exchanged in the lungs could not reach the tissues.
- [ ] I2 Digestive system: absorbed nutrients enter the blood capillaries of the villi (fats enter lacteals; one phrase) and are carried by the hepatic portal vein to the liver and then to all cells; the blood supplies the gut wall with the oxygen it needs for active absorption.
- [ ] I3 Urinary system: the renal arteries bring blood containing urea, excess water and salts to the kidneys, which filter it; the kidneys regulate the water and salt content of the blood, and so its volume (see "The urinary system").
- [ ] I4 Endocrine system: hormones are secreted directly into the blood and carried to target organs (e.g. insulin from the pancreas to the liver; ADH from the pituitary to the kidneys); adrenaline in turn increases heart rate (see "Glands, locations and hormones").
- [ ] I5 Immune system: blood carries white blood cells and antibodies to sites of infection, and platelets and clotting factors seal wounds (see "Blood" and "Innate and adaptive immunity").
- [ ] I6 Musculoskeletal system: red bone marrow makes the blood cells; active muscles receive extra blood (oxygen and glucose) and the contracting skeletal muscles squeeze veins, helping return blood to the heart (see "Blood vessels").
- [ ] I7 Nervous system: the medulla oblongata adjusts the heart rate and the diameter of arterioles, so blood is sent where it is needed (see "Heartbeat"); the brain itself needs a constant blood supply of oxygen and glucose.
- [ ] I8 Temperature regulation: blood carries heat from active organs; widening or narrowing of skin arterioles controls heat loss (taught in "Homeostasis as a stimulus-response model"; one phrase).
- [ ] I9 A model of these interactions (the outcome asks students to model them): a labelled flow diagram with the heart and blood at the centre and arrows to and from the lungs, gut and liver, kidneys, endocrine glands, muscles and brain, each arrow labelled with the materials transferred (O₂, CO₂, nutrients, urea, hormones, heat). Diagram: draw this model. State one limitation: the model shows what moves but not the vessels' structure or the rates of transfer, and it treats each organ as one box.
- [ ] I10 Worked example (one bullet): during exercise, muscles respire faster; the breathing rate rises to take in more O₂ and remove more CO₂ (see "CO₂ as a controlling factor in breathing"); the heart rate rises and arterioles to the muscles widen, so more blood reaches them; arterioles to the gut narrow; heat from the muscles is carried to the skin and lost.

## 3. Traps

- Arteries carry blood away from the heart, veins towards it; the hepatic portal vein is a vein because it carries blood towards the heart, by way of the liver, though it does not enter the heart itself.
- Don't call the pulmonary vein "deoxygenated" or the pulmonary artery "oxygenated".
- "Double circulation" means the blood passes through the heart twice per circuit, not that there are two hearts.
- The hepatic portal vein runs from the gut to the liver, not from the liver to the heart (that is the hepatic vein). Students often confuse the hepatic artery, hepatic portal vein and hepatic vein.
- Fetal circulation, the placenta and the umbilical vessels belong to "Pregnancy" (Human Reproduction); don't add them. The hypophyseal (pituitary) portal system is not on the specification; give the hepatic portal system only.
- The lymphatic system is not on this specification; I2 mentions lacteals in one phrase only (their role in fat absorption is in "Absorption, transport and storage").
- The hepatic portal vein is also taught in "Absorption, transport and storage" (its role in carrying absorbed nutrients to the liver). Here it is one of the three pathways of the circulation: keep PT1–PT4 short and don't re-teach absorption or liver functions.
- Heart chambers and valves belong to "Investigation: the heart"; tissue fluid and capillary exchange to "Blood vessels"; the absorption process to "Absorption, transport and storage". Use these by name.
- Cardiac cycle is not on the specification; don't say when each chamber contracts.
- The diagram cues D6, V9 and I9 are the only ones wanted. No other diagram cues.

## 4. What exams ask (topics only)

- Trace the path of a red blood cell from a named vein (e.g. the vena cava) to the aorta, or from the lungs to a named organ.
- Explain what is meant by double circulation and give its advantages.
- Name the vessel that carries blood from a named organ, or that carries blood rich in nutrients, or with little CO₂; explain why the hepatic portal vein is unusual.
- Explain how the circulatory system interacts with the breathing, digestive and urinary systems.
