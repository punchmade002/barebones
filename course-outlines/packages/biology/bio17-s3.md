## 1. Must cover

What a mutation is (one block)
- [ ] D1 [HL] A mutation is a permanent change in the DNA: in the base sequence of a gene (a point, or gene, mutation) or in the structure of a chromosome (a chromosomal mutation). A mutation that changes a gene produces a new allele.
- [ ] D2 [HL] Causes: mutations arise spontaneously, mainly as uncorrected errors in base pairing during DNA replication (see "Modelling DNA replication and information flow"), and their rate is increased by mutagens: ionising radiation (X-rays, gamma rays, radioactive substances) and ultraviolet light; chemicals such as those in tobacco smoke; and some viruses (detail in "Causes, prevention and treatment of cancer"). Repair enzymes correct most damage.
- [ ] D3 [HL] Body-cell (somatic) mutations affect only the individual and its cells descended from the mutated cell, and are not inherited; they can lead to cancer (see "Cancer"). Mutations in cells that form gametes (germ-line mutations) can be passed to offspring and are present in every cell of the child.
- [ ] D4 [HL] Effects: most mutations are neutral (in non-coding DNA, or silent); many are harmful (they change or stop a protein); a few are beneficial in a particular environment, e.g. lactase persistence (described in "Variation"; name it only); mutations giving antibiotic resistance help bacteria survive antibiotics. Mutation is the only source of new alleles and so the ultimate source of variation for natural selection (see "Variation").

Point mutations: how they occur and their effects (one process block, with the worked model)
- [ ] P1 [HL] A point mutation is a change in the nucleotide sequence at one point in a gene, usually of one or a few base pairs. It occurs when a wrong nucleotide pairs during replication and is not corrected, or a nucleotide is added or missed out; at the next replication the change is copied into both strands and becomes permanent.
- [ ] P2 [HL] Substitution: one base is replaced by another. Only one codon changes. Three possible effects: silent (the new codon codes for the same amino acid, because the code is degenerate; see "The genetic code"), so the protein is unchanged; missense (a different amino acid), which may or may not change the protein's shape and function; nonsense (the codon becomes a stop codon), so the polypeptide is cut short and usually does not work.
- [ ] P3 [HL] Insertion or deletion of one base (or any number not a multiple of three): every codon after the change is read differently (a frameshift), because the code is read in threes without gaps from the start codon. Many amino acids after the change are wrong, and a stop codon often appears early or is lost, so the protein usually doesn't work. A frameshift near the start of a gene is more damaging than one near the end.
- [ ] P4 [HL] Insertion or deletion of three bases adds or removes one amino acid without a frameshift; the rest of the protein is normal, but even one missing amino acid can change the folding (cystic fibrosis, P6).
- [ ] P5 [HL] Worked model (the question gives the codon table). Normal mRNA AUG UUU GGC AAA UAA → methionine–phenylalanine–glycine–lysine, stop.
  - Substitution, silent: GGC → GGA, still glycine; protein unchanged.
  - Substitution, missense: UUU → UCU, serine instead of phenylalanine.
  - Substitution, nonsense: AAA → UAA, a stop codon; the chain ends after glycine.
  - Insertion of G after AUG: AUG GUU UGG CAA AUA A… → methionine–valine–tryptophan–glutamine–isoleucine…; every amino acid after the start is changed and the original stop codon is lost from the reading frame.
  - Deletion of the first U of UUU: AUG UUG GCA AAU AA… → methionine–leucine–alanine–asparagine…; frameshift.
  - Deletion of the whole codon UUU: AUG GGC AAA UAA → methionine–glycine–lysine; one amino acid missing, the rest unchanged.
  - Word analogy, for revision: THE CAT ATE THE RAT; substitution THE CAT ATE THE HAT (one word changed); insertion THE CXA TAT ETH ERA T (nonsense after the change); deletion THE ATA TET HER AT.

Known examples of point mutations (one block)
- [ ] E1 [HL] Sickle-cell anaemia: a substitution in the gene for beta-globin (one of the polypeptides of haemoglobin), on chromosome 11. One base change alters the codon for the sixth amino acid from GAG to GUG (in mRNA; CTC to CAC in the DNA strand being read), so valine replaces glutamic acid. The changed haemoglobin (haemoglobin S) molecules stick together into long fibres when oxygen is low, distorting red blood cells into a rigid sickle shape; sickled cells block small blood vessels, causing pain and organ damage, and are destroyed quickly, causing anaemia. It is recessive: people with two copies have the disease; people with one copy (sickle-cell trait) are usually healthy and are more resistant to malaria, which is why the allele is common in parts of Africa where malaria is common (an example of natural selection).
- [ ] E2 [HL] Cystic fibrosis: caused by mutations in the CFTR gene on chromosome 7, which codes for a chloride-ion channel protein in cell membranes. The most common mutation, called F508del (delta F508), is a deletion of three bases, which removes one amino acid (phenylalanine) at position 508 of the protein; the protein folds wrongly, is broken down by the cell and does not reach the membrane, giving thick, sticky mucus in the lungs and pancreas. It is recessive. Ireland has the highest rate of cystic fibrosis in the world. Newer drugs (CFTR modulators) help the faulty protein fold and work, which shows how knowing the mutation leads to treatment.
- [ ] E3 [HL] Cystic fibrosis is one of the conditions tested for in the newborn heel-prick screening offered to every baby in Ireland (see "Prenatal and postnatal care"). One bullet.

Chromosomal mutations: how they occur (one process block, with the lettered model)
- [ ] C1 [HL] A chromosomal mutation is a change in the structure of one or more chromosomes that affects many genes at once. It occurs when chromosomes break (e.g. caused by ionising radiation or chemicals, or by errors when chromosomes are copied or separated in meiosis) and the broken pieces are lost or rejoin in the wrong place. Large changes can be seen under the light microscope in a karyotype.
- [ ] C2 [HL] Model: write a chromosome as A B C • D E F G H, where letters are genes (or groups of genes) and • is the centromere, and a different, non-homologous chromosome as M N O • P Q R.
- [ ] C3 [HL] Deletion: a section breaks off and is lost; the genes on it are missing. A B C • D E F G H → A B C • D E H (F and G lost). Usually harmful, because a whole set of genes is missing.
- [ ] C4 [HL] Duplication: a section is copied twice, so its genes are repeated. A B C • D E F G H → A B C • D E F E F G H. Extra copies can upset the balance of gene products; over evolutionary time, duplicated genes can mutate to take on new functions.
- [ ] C5 [HL] Inversion: a section breaks out, turns through 180 degrees and rejoins, so its genes are in reverse order. A B C • D E F G H → A B C • D G F E H. No genes are lost, so it often has no effect on the person, unless a break falls inside a gene; it can cause problems in meiosis, leading to gametes with missing or extra genes.
- [ ] C6 [HL] Translocation: a section of one chromosome breaks off and joins a different, non-homologous chromosome; in a reciprocal translocation two chromosomes swap sections. A B C • D E F G H and M N O • P Q R → A B C • D E F Q R and M N O • P G H. If no genes are lost the person may be healthy, but a break can join two genes into a new hybrid (fusion) gene, and carriers can produce gametes with unbalanced sets of genes.
- [ ] C7 [HL] Diagram: each of the four mutations drawn as a chromosome with lettered bands and a centromere, before and after, with the translocation shown between two chromosomes of different sizes.

Known examples of chromosomal mutations (one block)
- [ ] K1 [HL] Deletion: cri du chat syndrome (French for "cry of the cat"), caused by deletion of part of the short arm of chromosome 5. Babies have a high-pitched cry like a cat's, a small head and low birth weight, and later intellectual disability and delayed development.
- [ ] K2 [HL] Duplication: Charcot–Marie–Tooth disease type 1A, caused by duplication of a small section of chromosome 17 containing the PMP22 gene; the extra copy disrupts the insulating myelin sheath of nerves in the limbs, causing gradual weakness of the muscles of the feet and lower legs.
- [ ] K3 [HL] Inversion: an inversion within the gene for blood-clotting factor VIII on the X chromosome breaks the gene and causes nearly half of all cases of severe haemophilia A (haemophilia as a sex-linked trait is in "Sex-linked traits"). By contrast, a common inversion in chromosome 9 usually has no effect, because no genes are broken.
- [ ] K4 [HL] Translocation: the Philadelphia chromosome in chronic myeloid leukaemia (CML), a cancer of white blood cells. A reciprocal translocation between chromosomes 9 and 22, in a bone-marrow stem cell, joins part of the ABL gene (from chromosome 9) to the BCR gene on chromosome 22, forming a BCR-ABL fusion gene on a shortened chromosome 22 (the Philadelphia chromosome). The fusion gene codes for an enzyme that is permanently switched on and drives the cell to divide uncontrollably (an oncogene; see "Cancer"). It is a somatic mutation, not inherited. The chromosome was discovered in Philadelphia, USA, in 1960 by Peter Nowell and David Hungerford, and Janet Rowley showed in 1973 that it arises by translocation; the drug imatinib, which blocks the BCR-ABL enzyme, is in "Causes, prevention and treatment of cancer".

Changes in chromosome number (one short block; not one of the four structural changes)
- [ ] N1 [HL] Some conditions are caused by a change in the number of chromosomes, not in their structure, so they are not deletion, duplication, inversion or translocation. They usually result from non-disjunction: a homologous pair (or two copies of a chromosome) fails to separate during meiosis, so a gamete has one chromosome too many or too few.
- [ ] N2 [HL] Down syndrome: in about 95% of cases, three copies of chromosome 21 (trisomy 21), giving 47 chromosomes, after non-disjunction, usually during egg formation; the risk rises with the mother's age. Features include a characteristic facial appearance, some degree of intellectual disability and a higher risk of heart defects. A few per cent of cases are caused instead by a translocation that attaches extra chromosome-21 material to another chromosome; only those cases involve a structural change.
- [ ] N3 [HL] Other examples of number changes, one phrase each: Turner syndrome (45, X: one X and no second sex chromosome, female) and Klinefelter syndrome (47, XXY, male).
- [ ] N4 [HL] A karyotype is a photograph of a cell's chromosomes, taken during mitosis when they are condensed, and arranged in homologous pairs by size and shape; it shows changes in number (e.g. three chromosome 21s) and large structural changes (e.g. the shortened chromosome 22).

Point and chromosomal mutations compared (one comparison block, paired points)
- [ ] V1 [HL] Scale: a point mutation changes one or a few bases in one gene; a chromosomal mutation changes the arrangement or number of copies of a large section with many genes.
- [ ] V2 [HL] How they arise: point mutations mainly from mispairing during replication; chromosomal mutations from breakage and wrong rejoining of chromosomes, often during meiosis.
- [ ] V3 [HL] Detection: point mutations need DNA sequencing (see "DNA sequencing"); large chromosomal mutations can be seen in a karyotype under the light microscope.
- [ ] V4 [HL] Examples: sickle-cell anaemia (substitution) and cystic fibrosis (three-base deletion); cri du chat (deletion), Charcot–Marie–Tooth type 1A (duplication), severe haemophilia A (inversion), the Philadelphia chromosome in CML (translocation).

## 3. Traps

- Every block is Higher Level only; mark every heading.
- Down syndrome (trisomy 21) is a change in chromosome number, not a deletion, duplication, inversion or translocation; never list it as an example of the four structural types. Only the minority of translocation Down syndrome cases involve a structural change. Turner and Klinefelter syndromes are also number changes.
- Sickle-cell is a substitution (not a deletion); the cystic fibrosis F508del change is a deletion of three bases, so it is not a frameshift. Don't call it a single-base deletion.
- A point mutation in the specification's sense includes small deletions and insertions, not only substitutions.
- A substitution changes at most one amino acid (or none, or makes a stop codon); a single-base insertion or deletion causes a frameshift. Don't say a substitution causes a frameshift.
- Translocation is between non-homologous chromosomes. The Philadelphia chromosome is the shortened chromosome 22, not chromosome 9; CML is caused by a somatic mutation and is not inherited.
- Cri du chat involves chromosome 5 (short arm), not chromosome 21 or a whole missing chromosome.
- Only germ-line mutations are inherited; mutations in skin or lung cells are not passed to children.
- The gene → protein → trait chains (CFTR protein function; haemoglobin's role) belong to "Genes, proteins and traits"; give the mutation and its consequence without repeating that block. Mutagens and carcinogens in detail belong to "Causes, prevention and treatment of cancer"; variation and natural selection to "Variation" and "The theory of evolution by natural selection"; inheritance of recessive conditions to "Monohybrid crosses"; sex-linked haemophilia to "Sex-linked traits".
- Don't teach crossing over, the stages of meiosis, or mutation rates.
- Diagram cues: C7 only (point mutations are shown as written sequences in P5). No other diagram cues.
