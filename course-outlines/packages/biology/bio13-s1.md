## 1. Must cover

Catalysts and enzymes (one block)
- [ ] C1 (assumed) A catalyst is a substance that speeds up a chemical reaction without being used up or permanently changed by it, so it can be used again and again. A catalyst does not change which products form or how much product is made in the end; it only makes the reaction reach that point faster.
- [ ] C2 An enzyme is a biological catalyst: a catalyst made by a living cell. Almost all enzymes are globular proteins, folded into a precise three-dimensional shape (protein structure is in "Structures of biomolecules"). Because they are proteins, enzymes are made at ribosomes, following the instructions in a gene.
- [ ] C3 Properties of enzymes, one bullet: they are needed only in small amounts, because each molecule is reused many times (catalase, among the fastest, breaks down millions of hydrogen peroxide molecules per second); they are unchanged at the end of the reaction; they are specific (C9); their activity is affected by temperature and pH, and they are denatured by heat and extremes of pH (detail in "Investigation: rate of enzyme activity").
- [ ] C4 Naming: most enzyme names end in "-ase", and many are named after their substrate: maltase acts on maltose, lipase on lipids, protease on proteins, sucrase on sucrose. Some older names, such as pepsin and trypsin, don't follow this rule.
- [ ] C5 Where enzymes act: most work inside the cell that made them (intracellular), e.g. the enzymes of respiration in the cytoplasm and mitochondria and of photosynthesis in chloroplasts. Some are secreted to work outside the cell (extracellular), e.g. digestive enzymes released into the gut (detail in "The Digestive System") and the enzymes fungi and bacteria release onto their food.

How enzymes speed up reactions: activation energy (one block)
- [ ] A1 (assumed) Activation energy is the minimum energy that reacting molecules need before a reaction can start: the energy needed to break or strain existing bonds. It is an energy "hill" that must be climbed even when the reaction releases energy overall.
- [ ] A2 Without a catalyst, most reactions of metabolism have such a high activation energy that, at body temperature (37 °C), they would happen far too slowly to keep a cell alive. Heating would supply the energy, but high temperatures would damage cells and their proteins.
- [ ] A3 An enzyme provides an alternative route for the reaction with a lower activation energy, so many more molecules have enough energy to react at the temperature of the cell, and the reaction is many times faster. The overall energy change of the reaction (energy released or taken in) stays the same.
- [ ] A4 Diagram: an energy-profile graph with "energy" on the y-axis and "progress of reaction" on the x-axis; reactants at a higher level on the left, products lower on the right; two humps, a tall one labelled "without enzyme" and a lower one labelled "with enzyme", with the activation energy of each marked by a vertical arrow from the reactants to the top of its hump.

How enzymes work: active site and enzyme–substrate complex (one process block)
- [ ] M1 The substrate is the molecule an enzyme acts on; the products are the molecules made. Example: catalase acts on its substrate hydrogen peroxide: 2H₂O₂ → 2H₂O + O₂.
- [ ] M2 The active site is a small region of the enzyme, a pocket or groove on its surface, where the substrate binds and the reaction takes place. Its shape (and the chemical charges in it) is complementary to the substrate: the two fit together.
- [ ] M3 Step 1: the substrate collides with the enzyme and binds to the active site, forming an enzyme–substrate complex. Enzyme and substrate molecules meet by random movement, so anything that increases collisions (more substrate, more enzyme, higher temperature) increases the rate, up to a point.
- [ ] M4 Step 2: while the substrate is held in the active site, the enzyme lowers the activation energy, for example by straining the bonds that must break, or by holding two substrate molecules close together in the right position to react. The substrate is converted to products: an enzyme–product complex.
- [ ] M5 Step 3: the products have a different shape, no longer fit, and are released. The active site is free and unchanged, ready to bind another substrate molecule.
- [ ] M6 Summary in words: enzyme + substrate → enzyme–substrate complex → enzyme + products.
- [ ] M7 Breaking down and building up: the same mechanism works for catabolic reactions, where one substrate is split into smaller products (e.g. amylase splits starch into maltose), and for anabolic reactions, where two or more substrates are joined into a larger product (e.g. DNA polymerase joins nucleotides into a new DNA strand when DNA is copied; ATP synthase joins ADP and phosphate into ATP).
- [ ] M8 Diagram: three stages in a row: enzyme with a labelled active site and a substrate of complementary shape approaching; enzyme–substrate complex; enzyme unchanged with two products leaving. Label enzyme, active site, substrate, enzyme–substrate complex, products. (A matching diagram for a joining reaction is optional.)

Enzymes as selective catalysts: specificity (one block)
- [ ] S1 Specificity means that each enzyme catalyses only one reaction, or one type of reaction, because only a substrate with the complementary shape fits its active site. This is what "selective catalysts" means: amylase digests starch but not protein or fat; pepsin digests protein but not starch.
- [ ] S2 The lock-and-key model is the simplest explanation: the substrate (key) fits only the active site (lock) of the right shape. It was later refined to the induced-fit model, in which the active site changes shape slightly as the substrate binds (the history of the two models is in "Models"; the Higher Level detail is in "Enzyme structure and specificity"). One bullet only.
- [ ] S3 Because enzymes are specific, a cell needs thousands of different enzymes, one for each of its reactions, and it can control a reaction by making, or not making, the one enzyme that catalyses it.
- [ ] S4 Shape is essential: anything that changes the shape of the active site, such as high temperature or extreme pH (denaturation, see "Investigation: rate of enzyme activity") or a change in the enzyme's amino-acid sequence caused by a mutation in its gene, stops the substrate fitting, so the enzyme stops working.

The importance of enzymes in metabolism (one block)
- [ ] I1 Every reaction of metabolism is catalysed by its own specific enzyme. Without enzymes, the reactions that keep cells alive (respiration, photosynthesis, protein synthesis, DNA copying, digestion) would be far too slow at the temperatures and pH inside living things.
- [ ] I2 Metabolic pathways: most processes are a sequence of reactions, each catalysed by a different enzyme, in which the product of one reaction is the substrate of the next. Respiration and photosynthesis are such pathways (detail in "Respiration" and "Photosynthesis"). Diagram: a pathway written as A → B → C → D with "enzyme 1", "enzyme 2", "enzyme 3" written over the arrows.
- [ ] I3 Enzymes control metabolism: because each step needs its own enzyme, a cell controls which reactions happen, and how fast, by switching on or off the genes for particular enzymes and by activating or inhibiting enzymes already made. If one enzyme in a pathway is missing, the pathway stops at that step: its substrate builds up and the later products are not made.
- [ ] I4 Many enzymes need a non-protein helper to work: a mineral ion (e.g. magnesium for many enzymes that use ATP; see "Minerals") or a coenzyme made from a vitamin. One bullet.
- [ ] I5 Some drugs and poisons work by blocking an enzyme: e.g. some insecticides and nerve agents inhibit the enzyme that breaks down a neurotransmitter (detail in "Conduction across the synaptic cleft and its disruption"). One bullet; no types of inhibition.
- [ ] I6 Health link, enzyme deficiencies (one bullet each): Lactose intolerance: many adults make little lactase, the enzyme that digests lactose (milk sugar), so undigested lactose passes to the large intestine, where bacteria ferment it, causing bloating, wind, cramps and diarrhoea; lactose-free milk is made using lactase (see "Industrial enzymes"). Phenylketonuria (PKU): an inherited lack of the enzyme that converts the amino acid phenylalanine into another amino acid (tyrosine), so phenylalanine builds up in the blood and damages the developing brain; it is detected by the heel-prick test given to newborn babies in Ireland and is controlled by a diet low in phenylalanine.

## 3. Traps

- An enzyme lowers the activation energy; it does not "give" the reaction energy, raise the temperature, or change the overall energy released.
- Enzymes are not used up and are not "living"; they are proteins made by living cells and work outside cells too (e.g. in the gut, a test tube or a factory).
- The active site is a part of the enzyme, not of the substrate. The substrate's shape is complementary to the active site, not "the same as" it.
- Denatured is not the same as inactive at low temperature: say only that high temperature and extreme pH change the active site's shape. The rate graphs, optimums and the low-temperature explanation belong to "Investigation: rate of enzyme activity"; don't add them here.
- The induced-fit steps, the bonds holding the 3D shape and how the active site forms from folding are Higher Level and belong to "Enzyme structure and specificity"; S2 gives one sentence. This block is not Higher Level.
- Types of enzyme inhibition (competitive, non-competitive) and enzyme kinetics are not on the specification. Don't teach them.
- Named digestive enzymes, their sites and pH belong to "Chemical breakdown" in "The Digestive System"; use amylase and pepsin only as examples of specificity.
- Industrial uses belong to "Industrial enzymes"; I6 mentions lactose-free milk in a phrase only.
- Mutations as processes belong to "Mutations"; S4 is one phrase.
