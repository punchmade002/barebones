## 1. Must cover

Origin of white blood cells (one block, lead plus bullets)
- [ ] O1 (assumed) White blood cells (leucocytes) are the cells of the immune system. Unlike red blood cells, they have a nucleus. All develop from stem cells in the red bone marrow. They travel in the blood and lymph, and many leave the capillaries to enter tissues where pathogens are.
- [ ] O2 Two broad groups: phagocytes (neutrophils and monocytes/macrophages), which engulf pathogens, and lymphocytes (B cells, T cells and natural killer cells), small cells with a large round nucleus filling most of the cell and little cytoplasm.
- [ ] O3 B lymphocytes mature in the bone marrow; T lymphocytes leave the marrow and mature in the thymus, a gland in the chest behind the breastbone (T for thymus). During maturation, lymphocytes that would attack the body's own cells are removed, so mature lymphocytes respond only to non-self antigens.

Monocytes (one block)
- [ ] MO1 Monocytes are the largest white blood cells, with a large kidney- or bean-shaped nucleus. They circulate in the blood for a few days, then move into tissues and develop into macrophages, which can live for months.
- [ ] MO2 Roles: macrophages carry out phagocytosis of pathogens, dead cells and debris (stages in "Innate and adaptive immunity"); they are antigen-presenting cells, displaying fragments of engulfed pathogens on their surface to activate helper T cells, so they link innate and adaptive immunity; they release chemicals that cause inflammation and fever and attract other white blood cells.
- [ ] MO3 Diagram: a monocyte (large cell, kidney-shaped nucleus) beside a lymphocyte (small cell, large round nucleus, thin rim of cytoplasm); label nucleus, cytoplasm, cell membrane.

Natural killer cells (one block)
- [ ] NK1 Natural killer (NK) cells are lymphocytes of the innate system. They are not specific to one antigen and need no earlier exposure, so they act within hours of an infection, before B and T cells have responded.
- [ ] NK2 How they recognise targets: healthy body cells display normal self-marker (MHC) proteins on their surface. Many virus-infected cells and cancer cells display fewer of these markers or display stress molecules; NK cells kill cells with this "missing self" pattern. NK cells also kill cells coated with antibodies.
- [ ] NK3 How they kill: they release perforin, a protein that makes pores in the target cell's membrane, and enzymes (granzymes) that enter through the pores and trigger the cell to self-destruct (apoptosis, programmed cell death). The dying cell breaks into fragments that macrophages engulf, so the virus inside is destroyed without spreading. They also release chemicals that activate macrophages.

B lymphocytes (one block)
- [ ] BL1 B lymphocytes carry antibody molecules in their membrane as receptors; each B cell and its descendants make only one antibody shape. They are responsible for antibody-mediated (humoral) immunity (clonal selection, plasma cells and memory B cells are taught in "Innate and adaptive immunity"; use without restating).
- [ ] BL2 Plasma cells, the antibody-secreting form of B cells, are larger than resting B cells, with extensive rough endoplasmic reticulum to make and secrete large amounts of antibody protein (see "Structures and organelles of animal and plant cells").
- [ ] BL3 Antibodies act on pathogens and toxins outside cells, in blood, tissue fluid and mucus, not on pathogens hidden inside cells.

T lymphocytes (one block, one bullet per type)
- [ ] TL1 All T cells have T-cell receptors on their surface that recognise one antigen, but only when it is displayed on a cell's surface (presented), never as a free molecule. They don't make antibodies.
- [ ] TL2 Helper T cells carry the surface marker CD4. Role: they are the coordinators of the adaptive response, signalling with cytokines (which cells they activate, and in what order, is in "Innate and adaptive immunity"; don't restate). Because almost every adaptive response depends on them, HIV, which infects and destroys CD4 helper T cells, leaves the body unable to fight many infections (see "Viruses: replication").
- [ ] TL3 Killer T cells (cytotoxic T cells) carry the surface marker CD8. Role: they bind to body cells displaying the matching antigen (virus-infected cells, cancer cells, cells of transplanted tissue) and kill them by releasing perforin and granzymes, triggering apoptosis, the same method as NK cells (NK3), but only for cells showing their one specific antigen. Each killer T cell can kill many target cells in turn.
- [ ] TL4 Suppressor T cells (now usually called regulatory T cells) are the brakes of the immune system: as well as ending a response once an infection is cleared (in "Innate and adaptive immunity"), they suppress lymphocytes that react to the body's own antigens, helping to prevent autoimmune disease and allergy (autoimmune disease is in "Emerging diseases and society").
- [ ] TL5 Memory T cells form from the clone of activated T cells and remain in the blood and lymph tissue for years or decades. On re-exposure to the same antigen they quickly produce new helper and killer T cells, giving a faster, stronger cell-mediated response (memory B cells do the same for antibodies). Memory cells are why vaccination works.

Comparing the roles (one comparison block)
- [ ] C1 Innate vs adaptive: monocytes/macrophages and NK cells are part of innate immunity (non-specific, act quickly, no memory); B and T lymphocytes are adaptive (specific to one antigen, slower on first exposure, form memory cells).
- [ ] C2 How each destroys pathogens: macrophages engulf and digest (phagocytosis); NK and killer T cells kill infected body cells by perforin and apoptosis; B cells (as plasma cells) secrete antibodies; helper T cells kill nothing themselves but signal to other cells; suppressor T cells regulate; memory cells kill nothing but enable a rapid future response.
- [ ] C3 Where the target is: antibodies from B cells act on pathogens and toxins outside cells; killer T and NK cells act on pathogens inside body cells (by killing the cell); macrophages engulf free pathogens.
- [ ] C4 Recognition: B cells recognise free antigens with membrane antibodies; T cells recognise only antigens presented on cell surfaces; NK cells recognise missing or abnormal self-markers, not a specific antigen; macrophages recognise general features of pathogens and antibody-coated particles.
- [ ] C5 Summary list, one bullet per cell (innate or adaptive; specific or not; main role; matures in): monocyte/macrophage: innate; non-specific; phagocytosis and antigen presentation; bone marrow, then tissues. NK cell: innate; non-specific; kills infected and cancer cells; bone marrow. B lymphocyte: adaptive; specific; antibodies (via plasma cells) and memory; bone marrow. Helper T: adaptive; specific; activates B cells, killer T cells and macrophages (CD4); thymus. Killer T: adaptive; specific; kills infected, cancer and transplant cells (CD8); thymus. Suppressor T: adaptive; ends responses, prevents attack on self; thymus. Memory B and T: adaptive; specific; long-lived, fast secondary response.
- [ ] C6 Cells working together, worked example (influenza virus in the airways; one bullet, in sequence words only, referring to "Innate and adaptive immunity" for detail): infected cells release interferons and NK cells kill some of them within hours; macrophages engulf viruses and present antigens; helper T cells are activated; killer T cells destroy infected cells while plasma cells release antibodies that neutralise free viruses; suppressor T cells end the response; memory cells remain, but because flu antigens change (see "Viruses: replication"), they may not recognise next year's strain.

## 3. Traps

- Every block is Higher Level only; mark every heading.
- Don't retell the cell-mediated sequence step by step or clonal selection; refer to "Innate and adaptive immunity". C6 is a single summary bullet.
- NK cells are lymphocytes but belong to the innate system; they are not T cells and don't have antigen-specific receptors.
- Killer T cells don't engulf pathogens; they kill whole infected body cells. Macrophages engulf. Helper T cells don't kill anything.
- T cells never make antibodies; plasma cells (from B cells) do. B cells mature in the bone marrow, not the thymus.
- "Suppressor T cell" is the specification's term: use it, noting "regulatory T cell" once.
- Neutrophils are not named in this outcome; they appear only in "Innate and adaptive immunity" (phagocytosis) and in O2.
- Don't name lysosomes (not on the specification). Rough ER is in "Structures and organelles of animal and plant cells" (Higher Level); BL2 uses it in one phrase.
- Diagram cues: MO3 only. No other diagram cues.

## 5. Practice questions to write

1. 6 marks, Higher: "Name the four types of T lymphocyte and state one role of each." Model answer: TL2–TL5, one role each (helper activates B and killer T cells; killer destroys infected cells; suppressor ends the response; memory gives a rapid response on re-infection).
2. 9 marks, Higher: "Compare the roles of natural killer cells and killer T cells." Model answer: similarity: both kill infected and cancer cells by perforin and apoptosis (NK3, TL3); differences: NK innate, non-specific, act within hours, detect missing self-markers, no memory (NK1–NK2) vs killer T adaptive, specific to one presented antigen, activated by helper T cells, memory cells formed (TL1, TL3, TL5).
3. 12 marks, Higher: "Compare the roles of monocytes, B lymphocytes and helper T lymphocytes in the immune response." Model answer: MO2 (phagocytosis, antigen presentation), BL1–BL3 (antibodies against pathogens outside cells), TL2 (cytokines that activate B and killer T cells; CD4; HIV); then paired comparisons from C1–C4.
