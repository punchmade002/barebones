## 1. Must cover

Nephron structure (one structure-and-function block; parts in the order fluid passes through them)
- [ ] N1 A nephron is one of the about one million microscopic tubules in each kidney; it is the kidney's functional unit, where blood is filtered and urine is formed. Its wall is one layer of cells thick throughout.
- [ ] N2 Bowman's capsule: the cup-shaped, double-walled closed end of the nephron, in the cortex, wrapped around the glomerulus. Its inner wall is made of special cells with gaps between them, so filtered fluid passes easily into the space inside the capsule.
- [ ] N3 Proximal convoluted tubule (PCT): the first, long, coiled ("convoluted") section after the capsule, in the cortex. "Proximal" means nearer the capsule.
- [ ] N4 Loop of Henle: a long U-shaped loop that dips down into the medulla, with a descending limb going down and an ascending limb coming back up to the cortex.
- [ ] N5 Distal convoluted tubule (DCT): the second coiled section, back in the cortex ("distal" means further from the capsule). It empties into a collecting duct.
- [ ] N6 Collecting duct: a tube shared by many nephrons that runs down through the medulla (through a pyramid) and opens into the renal pelvis. The syllabus does not list it, but it is needed to explain water reabsorption and ADH; some textbooks don't count it as part of the nephron.

Blood supply of the nephron (one block)
- [ ] B1 A branch of the renal artery divides into arterioles. An afferent arteriole (afferent = carrying towards) brings blood to each nephron and divides into the glomerulus, a knot of capillaries inside Bowman's capsule.
- [ ] B2 The capillaries rejoin to form the efferent arteriole (efferent = carrying away), which is narrower than the afferent arteriole. Because blood enters the glomerulus through a wider vessel than it leaves by, pressure builds up inside the glomerulus. This high pressure drives filtration. The glomerulus is unusual: a capillary network between two arterioles, not between an arteriole and a venule.
- [ ] B3 The efferent arteriole then branches into a second capillary network that surrounds the PCT, loop of Henle and DCT closely. Substances reabsorbed from the tubule pass into these capillaries; the capillaries also supply the tubule cells with oxygen and glucose for respiration (they need ATP for active transport) and carry away their carbon dioxide.
- [ ] B4 These capillaries join into venules, then a branch of the renal vein, which carries the filtered blood back to the circulation.
- [ ] B5 Diagram: one nephron with its blood supply, labelled afferent arteriole, glomerulus, efferent arteriole, Bowman's capsule, proximal convoluted tubule, descending limb, loop of Henle, ascending limb, distal convoluted tubule, collecting duct, capillary network around the tubules, branch of renal artery, branch of renal vein; draw a line marking the boundary between cortex and medulla, with the capsule, PCT and DCT above it and the loop and most of the collecting duct below. Mark arrows for filtration (glomerulus into capsule) and reabsorption (tubule into capillaries).

Filtration in the glomerulus and Bowman's capsule (one process block)
- [ ] F1 Filtration (also called ultrafiltration, filtration under pressure): the high blood pressure in the glomerulus forces small molecules out of the blood, through the pores in the capillary walls and the inner wall of Bowman's capsule, into the capsule.
- [ ] F2 Filtered (pass into the filtrate): water, glucose, amino acids, salts (e.g. sodium and chloride ions), urea, vitamins and some hormones. Not filtered (stay in the blood): red and white blood cells, platelets and plasma proteins, because they are too large to pass through.
- [ ] F3 Filtration is not selective: it sorts molecules by size only, so useful substances (glucose, amino acids) are filtered out with the waste. The glomerular filtrate therefore has the same composition as blood plasma minus the proteins.
- [ ] F4 Scale: the kidneys filter about 180 litres of fluid a day but produce only about 1.5 litres of urine, so about 99% of the filtrate is reabsorbed. This is why reabsorption is essential.
- [ ] F5 Structure suits function: the wide afferent and narrow efferent arterioles raise pressure; the many capillaries in each glomerulus, and about a million glomeruli, give a large filtering surface; the capillary wall and the capsule's inner wall are thin and porous.

Reabsorption along the tubule (one process block; one bullet per part)
- [ ] R1 Reabsorption is the return of useful substances from the filtrate into the blood in the capillaries around the tubule. It is selective: the body keeps what it needs.
- [ ] R2 PCT, the main site of reabsorption: all the glucose and all the amino acids, most of the salts (about two-thirds of the sodium) and most of the water (about two-thirds) are reabsorbed here. Glucose, amino acids and sodium ions are taken up by active transport, using ATP; water follows by osmosis; some urea diffuses back into the blood.
- [ ] R3 Structure of the PCT suits reabsorption: it is long and coiled (time and surface area); its cells have microvilli on the side facing the filtrate, greatly increasing the surface area; its cells have many mitochondria to supply ATP for active transport; it is closely surrounded by capillaries; its wall is one cell thick (short distance).
- [ ] R4 Loop of Henle: the ascending limb pumps salts (sodium and chloride ions) out into the tissue fluid of the medulla, and its wall does not let water through. This makes the medulla very salty (a high solute concentration, which is highest deep in the medulla). The descending limb lets water through, so water leaves it by osmosis into the salty medulla and is reabsorbed into the blood. The main job of the loop is to create this salty medulla, which lets the collecting duct reabsorb water later.
- [ ] R5 DCT: more salts and water are reabsorbed, adjusting the amounts to the body's needs. The DCT also secretes some substances from the blood into the filtrate, such as hydrogen ions (to control blood pH), potassium ions and some drugs. Secretion is the opposite direction to reabsorption. One bullet for secretion.
- [ ] R6 Collecting duct: as it passes down through the salty medulla, water leaves it by osmosis and is reabsorbed into the blood. How much water is reabsorbed is controlled by ADH (detail in "ADH and osmoregulation"). What remains is urine: water, urea, excess salts and other wastes. Urine then drains into the renal pelvis.
- [ ] R7 Glucose in urine (link): the carriers in the PCT can reabsorb glucose only up to a limit. In untreated diabetes the blood glucose is so high that more glucose is filtered than the PCT can reabsorb, so glucose appears in the urine.

Comparing plasma, filtrate and urine (one comparison block, paired points)
- [ ] C1 Protein: present in plasma; absent from filtrate and urine (too large to be filtered).
- [ ] C2 Glucose and amino acids: present in plasma and filtrate at the same concentration; absent from urine (all reabsorbed in the PCT).
- [ ] C3 Urea: same concentration in plasma and filtrate (freely filtered); much more concentrated in urine, because most of the water is reabsorbed but most of the urea is not.
- [ ] C4 Salts: similar in plasma and filtrate; in urine the amount varies with intake, because salt reabsorption is adjusted.
- [ ] C5 Skill (one bullet): when given a data table of plasma, filtrate and urine, explain each difference using C1–C4: absent from filtrate = not filtered (too large); absent from urine = reabsorbed; more concentrated in urine = water reabsorbed and the substance not.

Summary (one block)
- [ ] S1 One bullet per part, in order: glomerulus and Bowman's capsule (filtration under pressure) → PCT (reabsorption of all glucose and amino acids, most salts and water) → loop of Henle (makes the medulla salty; water reabsorbed from descending limb) → DCT (adjusts salts and water; secretion) → collecting duct (water reabsorbed under ADH control) → renal pelvis.

## 3. Traps

- Every block is Higher Level only; mark every heading. Keep the key terms (glomerulus, Bowman's capsule, PCT, loop of Henle, DCT, collecting duct, afferent and efferent arteriole, glomerular filtrate, filtration, reabsorption, secretion) to this subsection.
- Glomerulus (capillary knot) vs Bowman's capsule (cup of the nephron around it): students swap them. Filtration is from the glomerulus into the capsule.
- The afferent arteriole is the wider one. Don't say the efferent is wider.
- Filtration is non-selective (by size); reabsorption is selective. Don't say glucose is "not filtered".
- Water reabsorption is always passive (osmosis); salts, glucose and amino acids are reabsorbed actively in the PCT. Don't say water is actively pumped.
- Don't teach the countercurrent multiplier by name, the vasa recta, podocyte or fenestration names, the juxtaglomerular apparatus, aldosterone, renin, or numerical solute concentrations. They are beyond the specification.
- The regions of the kidney, the organs, dialysis and urine testing belong to "The urinary system"; ADH's mechanism and the feedback loop to "ADH and osmoregulation"; the gland that releases ADH to "Glands, locations and hormones". Refer to them by title.
- Don't give the filtrate and urine volumes more than once; they belong to F4.
- The diagram cue in B5 is the only one wanted. No other diagram cues.

## 5. Practice questions to write

1. 6 marks, Higher: "Explain why blood in the glomerulus is at high pressure and why this is important." Model answer: B2 (afferent wider than efferent) and F1 (pressure forces small molecules into Bowman's capsule).
2. 9 marks, Higher: "Glucose is present in the glomerular filtrate but not in the urine of a healthy person. Protein is present in blood plasma but not in the filtrate. Explain each of these observations." Model answer: F2–F3 (glucose small enough to be filtered; protein too large), R2 (all glucose reabsorbed in the PCT by active transport); may add R7.
3. 12 marks, Higher: "Draw a labelled diagram of a nephron and its blood supply, and outline what happens in the proximal convoluted tubule, the loop of Henle and the distal convoluted tubule." Model answer: B5 diagram with at least eight labels; R2–R3, R4 and R5, one or two points each. Must not describe ADH beyond naming it.
