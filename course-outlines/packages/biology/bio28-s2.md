## 1. Must cover

Why CO₂ is the controlling factor (one block)
- [ ] W1 Carbon dioxide is produced by every respiring cell. If it is not removed it accumulates in the blood, where it reacts with water to form carbonic acid, which releases hydrogen ions: CO₂ + H₂O ⇌ H₂CO₃ ⇌ H⁺ + HCO₃⁻ (carbonic acid → hydrogen ions + hydrogencarbonate ions). The reaction is speeded up by the enzyme carbonic anhydrase in red blood cells. So a rise in CO₂ makes the blood more acidic (its pH falls).
- [ ] W2 Blood pH is normally kept within a narrow range around 7.4 (slightly alkaline). A fall in pH alters the shape of enzymes and other proteins and disturbs cell function (enzymes and pH are in the chapter "Enzymes"), so the CO₂ level must be controlled. Breathing is the body's fastest way of adjusting it.
- [ ] W3 In a healthy person at sea level, the CO₂ level, not the oxygen level, is the main stimulus for breathing. The body is far more sensitive to a small rise in CO₂ than to a fall in O₂: oxygen receptors only strongly increase breathing when the oxygen level falls a lot (e.g. at high altitude). This is why the urge to breathe when holding the breath comes from rising CO₂, not falling O₂.

Detecting the change: chemoreceptors (one structure block)
- [ ] C1 A chemoreceptor is a receptor that detects the concentration of a chemical.
- [ ] C2 Central chemoreceptors lie in the medulla oblongata, close to the breathing centre. CO₂ diffuses from the blood into the fluid surrounding the brain (cerebrospinal fluid), where it forms carbonic acid and lowers the pH; the receptors respond to this rise in hydrogen ions. They are responsible for most of the response to a rise in CO₂.
- [ ] C3 Peripheral chemoreceptors lie in the carotid bodies (small structures in the wall of each carotid artery in the neck, which carry blood to the brain) and the aortic bodies (in the wall of the arch of the aorta). They detect a rise in CO₂ (and H⁺) and also a large fall in O₂ in the arterial blood, and send impulses along sensory nerves to the breathing centre.
- [ ] C4 Diagram: outline of the head, neck and chest showing the medulla oblongata (breathing centre and central chemoreceptors), the carotid bodies on the carotid arteries, the aortic bodies on the aortic arch, nerves to the breathing centre, and nerves from the breathing centre to the diaphragm and intercostal muscles.

The control loop (one process block: numbered steps, as a stimulus-response sequence)
- [ ] L1 1. Stimulus: CO₂ in the blood rises (e.g. muscles respire faster during exercise). 2. Receptors: the central and peripheral chemoreceptors detect the rise in CO₂ (through the fall in pH). 3. Control centre: impulses reach the breathing centre in the medulla oblongata. 4. The breathing centre sends impulses more often along the motor nerves to the effectors: the diaphragm and the intercostal muscles (and, in heavy breathing, the abdominal muscles). 5. Response: the muscles contract more strongly and more often, so breathing becomes deeper and faster. 6. More CO₂ is exhaled from the alveoli and more oxygen taken in, so the CO₂ level in the blood falls back towards normal and the pH rises. 7. The receptors detect the return to normal, the breathing centre sends fewer impulses, and breathing returns to the resting rate.
- [ ] L2 This is negative feedback: the response (removing CO₂) cancels the change (the rise in CO₂) that caused it, keeping the blood CO₂ level and pH near a set point. It works in both directions: if CO₂ falls below normal, the breathing centre reduces the rate and depth of breathing, so CO₂ builds up again.
- [ ] L3 The cardiac centre in the medulla responds to the same signals: a rise in CO₂ also increases the heart rate, so blood carries CO₂ to the lungs and oxygen to the muscles faster (heart-rate control is in "Heartbeat"). One bullet.
- [ ] L4 Diagram: a feedback loop flow chart: CO₂ rises → chemoreceptors → breathing centre in the medulla → diaphragm and intercostal muscles → faster, deeper breathing → CO₂ falls → back to normal (with the loop arrow returning to the start); and a matching loop for CO₂ falling.

Applications (one block; one bullet each)
- [ ] A1 Exercise: muscles respire faster and release more CO₂ into the blood; the rise is detected and breathing rate and depth increase within seconds, removing CO₂ and supplying the extra oxygen. Breathing stays fast for a time after exercise stops, while the extra CO₂ is removed and the oxygen debt from anaerobic respiration (lactic acid) is repaid (see "Respiration as a catabolic process").
- [ ] A2 Holding the breath: CO₂ builds up in the blood, the chemoreceptors' signal to the breathing centre becomes stronger and stronger, and eventually the automatic control overrides the voluntary control by the cerebrum, forcing a breath. This is why a person can't hold their breath until they lose consciousness at rest.
- [ ] A3 Hyperventilation (deliberate fast, deep breathing): removes extra CO₂, so the CO₂ level falls below normal and the urge to breathe is reduced; the person may feel dizzy. Before swimming underwater this is dangerous: the urge to breathe comes too late, so the oxygen level can fall far enough to cause blacking out before the swimmer feels the need to surface.
- [ ] A4 Breathing air with a raised CO₂ content (e.g. in a poorly ventilated, crowded room, or rebreathing air from a bag) makes breathing faster and deeper, by the same loop.
- [ ] A5 Comparison with plants (one bullet only): in plants a rise in the CO₂ concentration inside the leaf also acts as a controlling signal, causing stomata to close; the mechanism is in "CO₂ as a controlling factor in plant stomata" (Transport in Plants). In both cases the CO₂ concentration regulates gas exchange with the air, but in humans it is detected by chemoreceptors and acts through nerves and muscles, while in plants it acts directly on the guard cells.

## 3. Traps

- Every block is Higher Level only; mark every heading.
- CO₂ is the main controlling factor, not oxygen. Don't say "low oxygen makes us breathe faster" as the main mechanism.
- The receptors detect CO₂ mainly through its effect on pH (hydrogen ions); say both.
- The breathing centre is in the medulla oblongata, not the cerebrum, cerebellum or hypothalamus. The hypothalamus controls temperature and water balance (see "Homeostasis as a stimulus-response model").
- Breathing rate rises because CO₂ rises, not because the muscles "need oxygen" directly; the oxygen supply rises as a consequence.
- Don't teach stretch receptors in the lungs (the Hering–Breuer reflex), the pneumotaxic or apneustic centres, or oxygen dissociation curves; they are not on the specification.
- The Ordinary Level statements (the breathing centre in the medulla sets the rhythm; a CO₂ rise increases rate and depth) are in "Gas exchange and the breathing mechanism"; don't repeat the breathing mechanism itself here.
- How the blood carries CO₂ (mostly as hydrogencarbonate in the plasma) is in "Blood"; W1 gives the reaction only because it explains the pH change.
- Stomatal opening and closing, guard cells and their mechanism belong to "CO₂ as a controlling factor in plant stomata" (Transport in Plants); A5 is one bullet.
- The diagram cues C4 and L4 are the only ones wanted. No other diagram cues.

## 4. What exams ask (topics only)

- Explain how the concentration of CO₂ in the blood controls the rate of breathing; name where it is detected and the part of the brain involved.
- Explain why breathing rate increases during exercise and stays high afterwards.
- Explain why a person cannot hold their breath indefinitely.
- Describe the control of breathing as an example of negative feedback.

## 5. Practice questions to write

1. 6 marks, Higher: "Name the part of the brain that controls breathing and state where in the body the concentration of carbon dioxide is detected." Model answer: medulla oblongata (breathing centre); central chemoreceptors in the medulla and peripheral chemoreceptors in the carotid and aortic bodies (C2, C3).
2. 12 marks, Higher: "Outline the role of carbon dioxide concentration in controlling breathing during exercise, and explain why this is an example of negative feedback." Model answer: A1 stimulus, W1 (CO₂ lowers pH), L1 steps 2–7, L2. Must state that the response reduces the CO₂ level that caused it. Must not give oxygen as the main stimulus.
3. 9 marks, Higher: "Explain why (i) a person cannot hold their breath for very long and (ii) hyperventilating before swimming underwater is dangerous." Model answer: A2 (rising CO₂ overrides voluntary control) and A3 (CO₂ lowered, urge to breathe delayed, oxygen falls, blackout).
