## 1. Must cover

The effect of CO₂ concentration on stomata (one block)
- [ ] C1 [HL] The concentration of CO₂ in the air spaces inside the leaf is one of the factors that controls the width of the stomatal pores (along with light and water supply).
- [ ] C2 [HL] A low CO₂ concentration inside the leaf causes the stomata to open (or open wider).
- [ ] C3 [HL] A high CO₂ concentration inside the leaf causes the stomata to close (or partly close).
- [ ] C4 [HL] Link to the guard-cell mechanism: guard cells respond to the CO₂ concentration around them. Low CO₂ leads to potassium ions (K⁺) being taken into the guard cells, water entering by osmosis and the guard cells becoming turgid, so the pore opens; high CO₂ leads to K⁺ leaving the guard cells, water following by osmosis and the guard cells becoming flaccid, so the pore closes. One bullet, using the mechanism from "Transport of water and minerals".
- [ ] C5 [HL] The response is to the concentration inside the leaf, which depends on the balance between photosynthesis (using CO₂) and respiration (releasing it), not simply on the concentration in the outside air.

Why the response makes sense: day and night (one block)
- [ ] D1 [HL] In the light, photosynthesis uses CO₂ faster than respiration produces it, so the CO₂ concentration in the air spaces falls. The low CO₂ opens the stomata, letting in more CO₂ for photosynthesis exactly when it is needed.
- [ ] D2 [HL] In darkness there is no photosynthesis; respiration continues, so CO₂ builds up in the air spaces. The high CO₂ closes the stomata. The plant doesn't need CO₂ in the dark, so closing saves water that would otherwise be lost by transpiration.
- [ ] D3 [HL] This is a negative feedback loop: when internal CO₂ is low the stomata open, CO₂ enters and its concentration rises; as it rises, the stomata begin to close. CO₂ concentration therefore helps match the opening of the stomata to the leaf's demand for CO₂, keeping the balance between taking in CO₂ and losing water. (Negative feedback is defined in "Homeostasis as a stimulus-response model".)
- [ ] D4 [HL] The CO₂ response works together with the other controls: light opens the stomata directly as well as by lowering CO₂ through photosynthesis; shortage of water closes the stomata even when CO₂ is low, because preventing wilting takes priority over photosynthesis. So on a hot, dry afternoon the stomata may close although the leaf needs CO₂, and photosynthesis slows.
- [ ] D5 [HL] Experimental evidence: if a leaf in the light is given air with extra CO₂, its stomata close partly; if a leaf in the dark is given CO₂-free air, its stomata open partly. This shows CO₂ acts on the stomata on its own, separately from light.

CO₂, stomata and transpiration (one block)
- [ ] T1 [HL] Because CO₂ controls stomatal width, it affects transpiration: raising the CO₂ concentration around a leaf closes the stomata partly and reduces the rate of transpiration; lowering it opens them and increases the rate. This is why CO₂ concentration is one of the factors in "Investigation: rate of transpiration".
- [ ] T2 [HL] Sustainability link: the CO₂ concentration of the atmosphere is rising, mainly from burning fossil fuels. Plants grown in raised CO₂ tend to open their stomata less, so they lose less water per unit of sugar made. Leaves of the same species collected over the last two centuries and kept in herbarium collections show fewer stomata per unit area as CO₂ has risen. One bullet, with no figures.
- [ ] T3 [HL] Technology link: glasshouse growers add CO₂ to raise photosynthesis (see "Investigation: rate of photosynthesis"); the extra CO₂ also partly closes the stomata, so the plants lose less water. One phrase.

CO₂ as a controlling factor in plants and in humans compared (one comparison block, paired points)
- [ ] H1 [HL] What is detected: in plants, the CO₂ concentration inside the leaf, detected by the guard cells themselves; in humans, the CO₂ concentration of the blood (through its effect on blood pH), detected by chemoreceptors in the medulla oblongata and in the carotid and aortic bodies, which signal the breathing centre in the medulla (detail in "CO₂ as a controlling factor in breathing"). Plants have no nerves: the response is local, in each stoma.
- [ ] H2 [HL] Response to high CO₂: in plants the stomata close, reducing gas exchange; in humans the rate and depth of breathing increase, increasing gas exchange.
- [ ] H3 [HL] Response to low CO₂: in plants the stomata open; in humans breathing slows.
- [ ] H4 [HL] Why the responses are opposite: for a plant in the light, CO₂ is a raw material for photosynthesis, so low internal CO₂ signals "more is needed" and high internal CO₂ signals "none is being used, save water"; for a human, CO₂ is a waste product of respiration that makes the blood more acidic, so high CO₂ signals "remove more".
- [ ] H5 [HL] What is similar: in both, CO₂ concentration is the main controlling signal for gas exchange, and the control works by negative feedback, returning the CO₂ level towards normal. In both, the controlled structures (stomata; the diaphragm and intercostal muscles that ventilate the alveoli) adjust how much gas passes across the exchange surface.

## 3. Traps

- Every block is Higher Level only; mark every heading. Key terms here (CO₂ as a controlling factor, negative feedback as applied to stomata) are Higher Level material.
- The direction is easily reversed: high CO₂ closes stomata; low CO₂ opens them. In humans high CO₂ speeds breathing up. Don't write "high CO₂ opens stomata to let it out".
- It is the CO₂ concentration inside the leaf that matters (C5), which falls in the light because of photosynthesis; don't say stomata open because "the air outside has low CO₂".
- Don't re-teach the guard-cell mechanism (inner wall, K⁺ uptake using ATP, osmosis) beyond the single bullet C4; it is in "Transport of water and minerals".
- Don't re-teach the human breathing mechanism, the respiratory centre or blood pH control; they are in "Gas exchange and the breathing mechanism" and "CO₂ as a controlling factor in breathing". H1–H5 give only what the comparison needs.
- Don't name abscisic acid or blue-light receptors (not on the specification); D4 says "shortage of water" and "light" only.
- Don't put this content in "Transport of CO₂ and photosynthetic products"; that subsection cross-refers here.
- The investigation of CO₂ and transpiration rate is in "Investigation: rate of transpiration"; T1 is one bullet.
- No diagram cues: the guard-cell diagram is in "Transport of water and minerals". No other diagram cues.

## 4. What exams ask (topics only)

- Name the gas whose concentration influences the diameter of the stomata (carbon dioxide).
- State the effect of a high (or low) CO₂ concentration on stomata, and explain the benefit to the plant.
- Outline the role of CO₂ concentration as a controlling factor in plant stomata and in the human breathing system, and compare them.

## 5. Practice questions to write

1. 6 marks, Higher: "State the effect of a high concentration of carbon dioxide inside a leaf on its stomata, and explain why the CO₂ concentration inside a leaf is usually high at night." Model answer: C3 (stomata close); D2 (no photosynthesis in the dark, respiration continues, CO₂ builds up; closing saves water).
2. 9 marks, Higher: "Explain how changes in carbon dioxide concentration inside a leaf help to control the opening of stomata during the day, and describe this as a negative feedback loop." Model answer: D1 (photosynthesis lowers internal CO₂, stomata open); C4 in one sentence; D3 (CO₂ enters, rises, stomata begin to close; matches supply to demand while limiting water loss).
3. 12 marks, Higher: "Compare the role of carbon dioxide concentration as a controlling factor in plant stomata and in the human breathing system." Model answer: H1–H5 (what detects it; opposite responses to high and low CO₂; reason: raw material vs waste product; both negative feedback controlling gas exchange); C2–C3.
