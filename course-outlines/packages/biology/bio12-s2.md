## 1. Must cover

Evolution and adaptation (one block)
- [ ] E1 Evolution is the change in the inherited characteristics of a population over many generations. In genetic terms, it is a change in how common particular alleles are in a population. Over long periods it can produce new species.
- [ ] E2 An adaptation is an inherited feature that increases an organism's chance of surviving and reproducing in its environment. Adaptations can be structural (a cactus's leaves reduced to spines, reducing water loss), behavioural (birds migrating to warmer regions in winter) or physiological (the chemistry of the body, e.g. lactase made by adults; see "Variation").
- [ ] E3 Natural selection is the process by which individuals with inherited characteristics better suited to their environment survive and reproduce more than others, so those characteristics become more common in the next generation. It is the main mechanism of evolution.

The rationale: how the theory was reached (one block)
- [ ] R1 Charles Darwin (1809–1882), an English naturalist, sailed around the world on HMS Beagle from 1831 to 1836. He observed that species varied from place to place, e.g. the finches and the giant tortoises of the Galápagos Islands differed from island to island and resembled species on the nearby South American mainland; and that fossils of extinct animals in South America resembled living species there.
- [ ] R2 Influences: the geologist Charles Lyell argued that the Earth is very old and is shaped by slow processes over vast time, giving time for gradual change; Thomas Malthus argued that populations tend to grow faster than their food supply, so there is a struggle for existence; breeders of pigeons, dogs and farm animals showed that selection over generations could change a species greatly (artificial selection).
- [ ] R3 Alfred Russel Wallace (1823–1913), working in the Malay Archipelago, reached the same idea independently and wrote to Darwin. Papers by both were read together at the Linnean Society of London on 1 July 1858. Darwin published On the Origin of Species in November 1859, giving the evidence in detail.
- [ ] R4 The rationale: Darwin and Wallace needed an explanation for observations that the idea of fixed, separately created species did not explain: variation within species; the fit between organisms and their environments; species on islands resembling those on the nearest mainland; and fossils of extinct species resembling living ones.

The basis: observations and inferences (one process block, numbered)
- [ ] B1 1. Overproduction: organisms produce more offspring than can survive (e.g. a single cod releases millions of eggs; an oak tree produces thousands of acorns), yet population sizes stay fairly stable over time.
- [ ] B2 2. Struggle for existence: so there is competition for limited resources (food, water, space, mates), and many individuals die from predation, disease or harsh conditions before reproducing.
- [ ] B3 3. Variation: individuals of a species differ, and many of the differences are inherited (from sexual reproduction and mutation; see "Variation").
- [ ] B4 4. Survival of the fittest: individuals whose inherited variations suit them better to the environment are more likely to survive and to reproduce. "Fittest" means best adapted to that environment, with the greatest reproductive success, not the strongest. (The phrase was coined by Herbert Spencer and later used by Darwin.)
- [ ] B5 5. Inheritance: the survivors pass the favourable alleles to their offspring, so these alleles and characteristics become more common in the next generation, and the less favourable ones less common.
- [ ] B6 6. Over many generations, the population becomes better adapted to its environment. Different populations in different environments change in different ways, and given time and isolation they may become separate species (speciation is in "Evidence for evolution").
- [ ] B7 Selection acts on individuals (they survive or not), but it is populations that evolve; an individual's genes don't change during its life.
- [ ] B8 Diagram: a flow chart of the six steps B1–B6, or a three-panel sequence for the peppered moth (W1): mixed population on a soot-darkened tree → birds eat the visible pale moths → next generation mostly dark.

Worked example of natural selection: the peppered moth (one block)
- [ ] W1 The peppered moth (Biston betularia) rests on tree trunks by day and is eaten by birds. It has a pale, speckled form and a dark (melanic) form, the difference being controlled by one gene; the dark allele arose by mutation.
- [ ] W2 Before the Industrial Revolution, tree trunks in England were pale, covered in lichens, and almost all moths were pale, which were camouflaged; the rare dark moths were easily seen and eaten.
- [ ] W3 In the 1800s, air pollution from coal killed the lichens and blackened tree trunks with soot near industrial cities such as Manchester. Now the dark moths were camouflaged and the pale ones were eaten more, so dark moths survived and reproduced more, and the dark form became the most common in industrial areas.
- [ ] W4 After clean-air laws in the 1950s reduced pollution, the trunks became paler again and the pale form has again become the most common. Selection changed direction when the environment changed: evolution is not a fixed march in one direction.

Explaining common origins and predicting future directions (one block)
- [ ] P1 Common origins: the theory explains why all living things share features (cells, DNA, the same genetic code, ATP): they are all descended from common ancestors, with the more closely related species sharing a more recent common ancestor. This is the basis of classification by phylogeny (see "Classification").
- [ ] P2 Highly specialised organisms are explained as the result of natural selection over many generations in a particular, stable environment, producing close adaptation to a narrow way of life. Example: the giant panda's diet is almost entirely bamboo, and the koala's almost entirely eucalyptus leaves.
- [ ] P3 Predicting future directions: because natural selection depends on the environment, the theory predicts that if the environment changes, organisms will either adapt (if the variation exists and there is time), move, or become extinct. Highly specialised organisms, which depend on one food or habitat and often reproduce slowly, are most at risk of extinction when that resource or habitat is lost. Generalists, which can use many foods and habitats, are more likely to survive change.
- [ ] P4 The theory also predicts that organisms with short generation times and large populations will evolve fastest, e.g. bacteria evolving resistance to antibiotics, insects evolving resistance to pesticides, and the influenza virus changing so much each year that the flu vaccine must be updated annually. These predictions are borne out (see "Evidence for evolution" for antibiotic resistance).

Natural and artificial selection (one block, then one comparison)
- [ ] A1 Artificial selection (selective breeding) is the process by which humans choose which individuals breed, so that characteristics useful or attractive to humans become more common over generations.
- [ ] A2 Examples: all dog breeds, from the Chihuahua to the Great Dane, were bred from wolves; cabbage, broccoli, cauliflower, kale, Brussels sprouts and kohlrabi were all bred from the wild cabbage (Brassica oleracea); dairy cows have been bred for high milk yield; modern wheat for large grains that stay on the plant until harvested.
- [ ] A3 Darwin used artificial selection as evidence that selection over generations can produce large changes, and as an analogy for natural selection (R2).
- [ ] A4 Comparison (paired points): who or what selects: the environment (predators, climate, food supply, disease) vs humans. Characteristics favoured: those that increase survival and reproduction in the wild vs those wanted by humans, which may harm the organism (e.g. flat-faced dog breeds such as pugs and bulldogs have breathing problems). Speed: usually slow, over many generations vs relatively fast, because humans choose strongly. Outcome: adaptation to the environment and, eventually, new species vs new breeds and varieties of the same species (all dogs are one species). Genetic variation: usually maintained in large wild populations vs often reduced, because a few chosen individuals are bred and inbred, making the population more vulnerable to disease (the Irish potato example is in "Variation"; refer, don't repeat). Similarity: both depend on inherited variation and on some individuals reproducing more than others.

Discussing the theory: difficulties then and answers since (one block)
- [ ] D1 Darwin did not know how characteristics were inherited or where new variation came from. Many people then believed in blending inheritance, which would dilute any favourable variation in a few generations. Mendel's work on inheritance (published in 1866, but widely recognised only from 1900) showed that inheritance is by discrete units (genes) that are not blended; later, the discovery of DNA and mutation explained the source of new variation (see "Variation").
- [ ] D2 Combined with genetics in the early 20th century, the theory became the modern synthesis: evolution is a change in allele frequencies in populations, caused mainly by natural selection acting on variation that comes from mutation and sexual reproduction.
- [ ] D3 Critics in Darwin's time said there were too few transitional fossils, and that the Earth was too young for such slow change. Since then many transitional fossils have been found, and radiometric dating shows the Earth is about 4.6 billion years old (detail in "Evidence for evolution").
- [ ] D4 The theory caused controversy because it conflicted with the religious belief, then widespread, that species were created separately in their present form. Science tests explanations against evidence; why evolution is accepted as the best explanation is in "The importance of the theory".

## 3. Traps

- Don't say organisms change "because they need to" or "try to adapt", or that an individual evolves. Variation comes first, at random; selection then favours some of it (B7).
- "Fittest" is not "strongest" (B4).
- Evolution does not have a goal and doesn't always lead to more complex organisms (W4, P3).
- Don't say humans evolved from chimpanzees; humans and chimpanzees share a common ancestor.
- Artificial selection is not genetic engineering: it selects among existing variation without changing DNA directly (genetic engineering is in "Genetic engineering").
- The evidence for evolution (fossils, comparative anatomy, embryology, molecular evidence, antibiotic resistance, speciation, the Galápagos finches' beaks) belongs to "Evidence for evolution". R1 mentions the finches only as Darwin's observation; don't describe beak changes here.
- The concept of a theory, its acceptance, alternatives such as Lamarck's, and the importance of the theory belong to "The importance of the theory". D4 names the conflict only.
