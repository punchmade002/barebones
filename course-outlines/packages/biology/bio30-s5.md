## 1. Must cover

How each factor affects the rate (one block, the theory)
- [ ] T1 The rate of transpiration is the volume (or mass) of water lost by a plant per unit time. It depends on how steep the water vapour concentration gradient is between the air spaces of the leaf (saturated with water vapour) and the outside air, how fast water evaporates, and how wide the stomata are open.
- [ ] T2 Air movement: wind blows away the layer of humid air that builds up around the leaf, keeping the outside air dry and the gradient steep, so the rate increases. In still air the humid layer builds up and the rate falls.
- [ ] T3 Temperature: warmer water molecules have more kinetic energy, so they evaporate from the mesophyll cell walls and diffuse out faster; warm air can also hold more water vapour, so the gradient is steeper. The rate increases with temperature. At very high temperatures the plant may begin to wilt and close its stomata, and the rate then falls.
- [ ] T4 Surface area: more leaves, or larger leaves, means more stomata and more evaporating surface, so the rate increases roughly in proportion to the leaf area.
- [ ] T5 Cuticle: a waxy cuticle is waterproof, so only a little water is lost through it; most is lost through the stomata. Leaves with a thick cuticle lose less water than leaves with a thin one; a leaf surface covered with an artificial waterproof layer (petroleum jelly) loses almost none through that surface.
- [ ] T6 Light intensity: stomata open in the light and close in darkness, so the rate increases with light intensity (until the stomata are fully open) and is very low in darkness. A lamp also warms the leaf, which raises the rate further unless temperature is controlled.
- [ ] T7 CO₂ concentration: a high CO₂ concentration around the leaf partly closes the stomata, so the rate falls; a low CO₂ concentration opens them wider, so the rate rises. (Higher Level explanation in "CO₂ as a controlling factor in plant stomata".)
- [ ] T8 Humidity (one bullet; not named by the specification but needed to control the other factors): humid air reduces the gradient, so the rate falls. This matters whenever a shoot is enclosed in a plastic bag (the CO₂ method), because the air in the bag becomes humid.

Planning (one block)
- [ ] A1 Aim: to investigate the effect of one factor (air movement, temperature, surface area, cuticle, light intensity or CO₂ concentration) on the rate of transpiration of a leafy shoot. Change one factor per investigation.
- [ ] A2 Hypotheses (examples to write, each resting on T2–T7): "Increasing air movement increases the rate of transpiration"; "The rate of transpiration increases as temperature increases"; "The rate increases as leaf area increases"; "Covering the lower surface of a leaf reduces water loss more than covering the upper surface"; "The rate increases as light intensity increases"; "Increasing the CO₂ concentration around a shoot reduces its rate of transpiration".
- [ ] A3 Variables (example: light intensity): independent variable is light intensity (lamp distance, or light meter reading in lux); dependent variable is the rate of water uptake (distance moved by the air bubble per minute, converted to volume per minute); controlled variables are the same shoot (same species, same number and size of leaves), temperature (thermometer by the shoot, heat shield), air movement (no fans, windows closed), humidity (same room), CO₂ (same room air), time allowed to adjust, and length of each timing. For the other factors, swap the independent variable and keep the rest constant.
- [ ] A4 Controls: a baseline reading for the same shoot in normal room conditions (still air, room temperature, normal light) to compare each change against; a leafless stem in the potometer (all leaves removed and the cut surfaces sealed) shows that almost all the water is lost through the leaves; for the cuticle investigation, an untreated leaf; for the CO₂ investigation, an identical bag with no CO₂ added or removed.
- [ ] A5 The potometer measures water uptake, not water loss. The two are almost equal, because nearly all the water a shoot takes up is lost by transpiration; only a tiny amount is used in photosynthesis or to keep cells turgid. The mass-loss method measures water loss directly.
- [ ] A6 Equipment and materials: bubble potometer (a capillary tube with a scale, a reservoir or syringe with a tap for resetting the bubble, and rubber tubing or a bung to hold the shoot), clamp stand, a leafy shoot of a woody shrub with a stiff stem (e.g. laurel or privet), sharp knife or scalpel, a basin or sink of water, petroleum jelly (Vaseline), paper towels, stopwatch, ruler, thermometer, bench lamp (LED preferred) and metre stick, light meter (optional), electric fan, heat shield (a flat-sided tank or beaker of water), clear plastic bag and string or tape, soda lime (to absorb CO₂), a CO₂ source (a flask of marble chips with dilute hydrochloric acid connected by tubing, or a CO₂ cylinder), graph paper (for leaf area), and for the alternative methods an electronic balance, cobalt chloride paper, forceps and clear tape.

Setting up the potometer (one block, method)
- [ ] P1 1. Cut a leafy shoot from the plant, then immediately recut the stem at a slant under water, a few centimetres further up, so no air enters the xylem (an air bubble would break the water columns; see "Transport of water and minerals"). 2. Submerge the potometer in the basin of water so it fills completely with no air bubbles. 3. Fit the shoot into the rubber tubing or bung under water, so the joint is airtight. 4. Take the assembled potometer out of the water and clamp it upright, with the capillary tube horizontal. 5. Seal all joints with petroleum jelly to prevent leaks. 6. Gently dry the leaves with a paper towel, since water on their surface would evaporate and add to the loss.
- [ ] P2 7. Introduce one air bubble into the capillary tube by lifting its open end out of the water for a moment, then return it to a beaker of water. 8. Leave the shoot for about 5 minutes to adjust to the conditions. 9. Record the starting position of the bubble on the scale, start the stopwatch, and record the distance the bubble moves towards the shoot in a set time (e.g. 5 minutes). 10. Return the bubble to the start of the scale by opening the reservoir tap (or pushing water in from the syringe). 11. Repeat at least three times under the same conditions and calculate the mean.
- [ ] P3 Diagram: a bubble potometer clamped on a stand: a leafy shoot sealed into the top with petroleum jelly, the water-filled tube, a reservoir with a tap, a horizontal capillary tube with a scale and an air bubble, the open end dipping into a beaker of water; label each part and draw an arrow for the direction the bubble moves.

Calculating the rate (one skills block, with worked examples)
- [ ] C1 Rate of water uptake = distance moved by the bubble ÷ time, e.g. in mm per minute. Use this when all readings use the same potometer.
- [ ] C2 To give a volume, use the volume of a cylinder: volume = πr² × distance moved, where r is the radius of the capillary tube. Worked example: radius 0.5 mm, bubble moves 30 mm in 5 minutes. Volume = 3.14 × 0.5² × 30 = 3.14 × 0.25 × 30 = 23.6 mm³ in 5 minutes, so the rate is 23.6 ÷ 5 = 4.7 mm³ per minute.
- [ ] C3 To compare shoots of different sizes, divide by the leaf area: draw round each leaf on 1 cm² graph paper, count the squares that are at least half covered and add up the total for all the leaves. Worked example: 4.7 mm³ per minute ÷ 50 cm² = 0.094 mm³ per minute per cm².
- [ ] C4 Mass-loss method: rate = mass lost ÷ time (g per hour); percentage mass loss = (mass lost ÷ starting mass) × 100, which compares leaves of different sizes. Worked example: a leaf of 2.0 g that weighs 1.5 g after 24 hours has lost 0.5 g, which is 25%.

Method: air movement (one block)
- [ ] M1 1. Take the baseline reading in still air (P2). 2. Place an electric fan at a fixed distance (e.g. 1 m) from the shoot on its lowest setting, wait 5 minutes and take three readings. 3. Repeat on the medium and highest settings, or with the fan at decreasing distances. 4. Check that the fan doesn't change the temperature by reading the thermometer near the shoot. Expected: the rate increases as air movement increases.

Method: temperature (one block)
- [ ] M2 1. Take readings with the potometer in places at different, steady temperatures, e.g. a cool room, the laboratory, and a warm room, measuring the air temperature beside the shoot with a thermometer each time. 2. Allow the shoot about 10 minutes to reach each temperature before taking three readings. 3. Don't use a fan heater blowing on the shoot, because it changes air movement as well as temperature; if a heater is used, place it at a distance behind a heat shield. 4. Keep light intensity the same in each place (same lamp, no direct sunlight). Expected: the rate increases with temperature.

Method: surface area (one block)
- [ ] M3 1. Take readings with all the leaves on the shoot. 2. Remove one or two leaves by cutting them off at the base of the leaf stalk, seal the cut surfaces with petroleum jelly, and take new readings. 3. Repeat until no leaves remain. 4. Measure the area of each removed leaf on graph paper (C3), so the total leaf area left at each stage is known. Alternatively compare shoots with different total leaf areas. Expected: the rate falls as the leaf area falls, roughly in proportion; the leafless stem gives almost no movement of the bubble. Plot rate against leaf area.

Method: presence or absence of cuticle (one block)
- [ ] M4 1. Pick four similar leaves of the same size from the same plant. 2. Leave leaf A untreated (the control); coat the upper surface of leaf B with a thin layer of petroleum jelly; coat the lower surface of leaf C; coat both surfaces of leaf D. The petroleum jelly acts as an artificial, thicker waterproof cuticle. 3. Seal the cut end of each leaf stalk with petroleum jelly. 4. Weigh each leaf on an electronic balance. 5. Hang the leaves on a string across the room, in the same conditions. 6. Reweigh each leaf every day for several days, and calculate the percentage mass loss (C4).
- [ ] M5 Expected results: A loses the most mass; B loses nearly as much as A, because the upper surface has a thick cuticle and few stomata; C loses much less, because the stomata on the lower surface are blocked; D loses almost none. Conclusion: the cuticle greatly reduces water loss, and most water is lost through the stomata on the lower surface.
- [ ] M6 Variation using the potometer: coat the upper surfaces, then the lower surfaces, of all the leaves on the potometer shoot and take readings after each, compared with the untreated reading. Or compare shoots of plants with a thick, shiny waxy cuticle (e.g. laurel) and plants with thin, soft leaves of similar area. One bullet.
- [ ] M7 Cobalt chloride paper (supporting method): dry cobalt chloride paper is blue and turns pink when it absorbs water. Using forceps, place a small square on the upper surface and one on the lower surface of a leaf still on a plant, and cover each with clear tape; time how long each takes to turn pink. Expected: the paper on the lower surface turns pink first, showing more water vapour leaves through the lower surface, where most stomata are.

Method: light intensity (one block)
- [ ] M8 1. Work in a darkened room. 2. Place a lamp at a set distance from the shoot (e.g. 10 cm), with a heat shield between them, wait 5 minutes and take three readings. 3. Repeat at 20, 30, 40 and 50 cm, measuring the distance from bulb to shoot with a metre stick (or measure the light intensity with a light meter, since intensity doesn't fall in simple proportion to distance; see "Investigation: rate of photosynthesis"). 4. Take a final set of readings in darkness (lamp off, shoot covered with a black bag or box). 5. Check the thermometer beside the shoot at each distance. Expected: the rate is highest with the lamp closest and very low in darkness, when the stomata close.

Method: CO₂ concentration (one block)
- [ ] M9 1. Take readings with the shoot in a clear plastic bag sealed loosely around the stem, with nothing added (the control for the effect of the bag and its humidity). 2. Raised CO₂: pass CO₂ into the bag through a tube from a flask of marble chips and dilute hydrochloric acid (or from a CO₂ cylinder), tie the bag, wait 10 minutes and take three readings. 3. Lowered CO₂: place a small open dish of soda lime, which absorbs CO₂, inside the bag (not touching the leaves), tie the bag, wait 10 minutes and take three readings. 4. Keep the shoot in bright light throughout, at a constant temperature, and keep each run short, because humidity builds up inside the bag. Expected: raised CO₂ lowers the rate; lowered CO₂ raises it slightly. The effect is smaller and harder to detect than the effects of light or air movement, so careful repeats and comparison with secondary data are needed.

Safety (one block)
- [ ] H1 Cut away from the body on a tile, and take care with the knife or scalpel; glass capillary tubes break easily, so handle them gently and report breakages.
- [ ] H2 Electricity and water: keep the lamp, the fan and their cables away from the basin and the potometer; dry hands before touching switches. Lamp bulbs (except LEDs) get very hot; let them cool before moving them. Keep fingers clear of the fan blades.
- [ ] H3 Cobalt chloride paper is toxic and can cause cancer; handle it only with forceps, wear gloves, and wash hands afterwards.
- [ ] H4 Soda lime is corrosive (it contains sodium hydroxide): wear safety glasses and gloves and don't touch it. Dilute hydrochloric acid is an irritant: wear safety glasses.
- [ ] H5 Wash hands after handling plants (some, such as laurel, are poisonous if eaten) and petroleum jelly.

Results and conclusions (one block)
- [ ] D1 Record primary data in a table: the independent variable (e.g. lamp distance in cm, fan setting, temperature in °C, leaf area in cm²), the three readings of distance moved in the set time, the mean, and the rate in mm per minute or mm³ per minute. For the cuticle investigation, record the starting mass, each day's mass and the percentage mass loss for each leaf.
- [ ] D2 Plot the mean rate (y-axis) against the independent variable (x-axis), with units. Use a bar chart for categories (fan settings, treatments A–D, still vs moving air) and a line graph for continuous variables (temperature, leaf area, light intensity).
- [ ] D3 Expected patterns: the rate rises with air movement, temperature, leaf area and light intensity; it falls with a thick or artificial cuticle (especially on the lower surface) and with raised CO₂. At high light intensity the rate levels off once the stomata are fully open.
- [ ] D4 Conclusion: state the relationship shown by the primary data, explain it using T2–T7 (gradient, evaporation, stomatal opening), and say whether it supports the hypothesis. Use the mean of the repeats and compare with other groups' results.
- [ ] D5 Diagram: four leaves hanging on a string, labelled A untreated, B upper surface coated, C lower surface coated, D both coated, with a bar chart of expected percentage mass loss (A highest, B slightly lower, C much lower, D lowest).

Using secondary data (one block)
- [ ] S1 Secondary data are results collected by others: other groups' class results, published graphs and tables, weather data (temperature, humidity and wind speed from a weather station or Met Éireann), or results from electronic sensors and data loggers measuring humidity or mass loss. Pooling class results increases the sample size and checks reproducibility.
- [ ] S2 Interpreting a graph of transpiration over 24 hours: the rate is very low at night (stomata closed, cooler, often still air); it rises after dawn as the stomata open, light increases and the air warms; it peaks around the early afternoon, when light and temperature are highest and the air is driest; it falls in the evening. On a hot, dry afternoon it may dip if the plant begins to wilt and closes its stomata.
- [ ] S3 Using secondary data for the CO₂ factor: because the effect of CO₂ is small in a school laboratory, published results from controlled-atmosphere experiments (plants grown at different CO₂ concentrations, with all other conditions fixed) can be used to support the conclusion that higher CO₂ reduces stomatal opening and transpiration.
- [ ] S4 Comparing primary and secondary data: check whether your results show the same trend as the published data; if they differ, look for an uncontrolled variable or a source of error (below), and evaluate the sources for reliability (see "Recognising bias and evaluating sources").

Sources of error and limitations (one block)
- [ ] E1 The potometer measures water uptake, not loss (A5); a small amount is used by the plant.
- [ ] E2 Air leaks at the joints, or air entering the cut stem, stop or slow the bubble and give a falsely low rate; cut under water and seal with petroleum jelly.
- [ ] E3 Changing one factor often changes another: a lamp heats the shoot, a fan or heater moves air, and a plastic bag raises humidity. Use a heat shield, keep heaters away, use a control bag and check the thermometer.
- [ ] E4 Room conditions (temperature, humidity, sunlight through windows, people moving) change during a lesson; take readings close together in time and repeat.
- [ ] E5 Too little time to adjust after each change gives a rate that still reflects the previous condition; wait about 5–10 minutes.
- [ ] E6 A cut shoot behaves differently from a whole plant and may wilt during a long investigation; use a healthy shoot and a short investigation, or a fresh shoot for each run (and measure its leaf area).
- [ ] E7 Reading the bubble position by eye on a scale is imprecise; read at eye level and repeat at least three times, calculating a mean.

## 3. Traps

- A potometer measures the rate of water uptake, not transpiration directly; say so in any description (A5, E1).
- The shoot must be cut and fitted under water; students often forget why (air in the xylem breaks the water column).
- Change only one factor at a time. The usual errors: a lamp that also heats the shoot; a heater that also moves air; a bag that also raises humidity. Each must be controlled (E3).
- Light affects transpiration because it opens the stomata, not because light "evaporates water". Temperature, not light, speeds evaporation.
- High CO₂ lowers the rate of transpiration (stomata partly close); students often predict the opposite. The full reason is Higher Level and belongs to "CO₂ as a controlling factor in plant stomata"; T7 gives one sentence only.
- In the cuticle investigation, coating the lower surface reduces water loss more than coating the upper surface, because the lower surface has more stomata and a thinner cuticle.
- The bubble moves towards the shoot, because the shoot takes water from the tube.
- Humidity is not named by the specification; teach it only as T8 and as a controlled variable, not as a separate method block.
- The inverse-square relationship of light intensity with distance is taught in "Investigation: rate of photosynthesis"; M8 refers to it in a phrase.
- Stomatal structure and opening, transpiration and the transpiration pull belong to "Transport of water and minerals"; the cuticle and leaf structure to "Plant structures for transport". Use them without re-teaching.
- No other diagram cues beyond P3 and D5.

## 4. What exams ask (topics only)

- Name the apparatus used to measure the rate of transpiration (water uptake) and describe how it is set up, including cutting the shoot under water and sealing the joints.
- State how one named factor (light intensity, air movement, temperature, leaf area, cuticle, CO₂) was varied, how the rate was measured, and two variables kept constant.
- Predict and explain the effect of a named factor on the rate of transpiration.
- Explain why a potometer measures water uptake rather than water loss.
- Calculate a rate from bubble movement or mass loss, and interpret a graph or table of results, including secondary data such as a 24-hour transpiration graph.

## 5. Practice questions to write

1. 6 marks: "State the effect of (i) increased air movement and (ii) increased light intensity on the rate of transpiration, and explain each effect." Model answer: T2 (rate increases; humid air removed, steeper gradient) and T6 (rate increases; stomata open wider).
2. 12 marks: "Describe how you would use a potometer to investigate the effect of light intensity on the rate of transpiration. Include how you would set up the apparatus, vary light intensity, measure the rate, and keep two other factors constant, and state one limitation of the method." Model answer: P1–P2 (cut and fit under water, seal with petroleum jelly, dry leaves, introduce bubble, adjust 5 minutes, distance in a set time, reset, three repeats); M8 (lamp distances, darkness, heat shield, light meter); A3 controlled variables (temperature, air movement); A5 or E1–E3 limitation.
3. 9 marks: "Four similar leaves were treated with petroleum jelly as follows: A none, B upper surface, C lower surface, D both surfaces. They were weighed and hung up for three days. Predict the order of percentage mass loss, from greatest to least, and explain your prediction. Calculate the percentage mass loss of a leaf that fell from 2.0 g to 1.5 g." Model answer: M4–M5 (A > B > C > D; cuticle waterproof, most stomata on the lower surface); C4 (25%).
