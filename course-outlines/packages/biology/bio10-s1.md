## 1. Must cover

Nucleic acids and nucleotides (one structure-and-function block)
- [ ] U1 Nucleic acids (DNA and RNA) are large molecules (polymers) built from many small units called nucleotides joined in a chain. DNA is deoxyribonucleic acid; RNA is ribonucleic acid. They contain the elements carbon, hydrogen, oxygen, nitrogen and phosphorus.
- [ ] U2 Each nucleotide has three parts: a five-carbon (pentose) sugar, a phosphate group, and a nitrogenous base attached to the sugar.
- [ ] U3 The sugar is deoxyribose in DNA and ribose in RNA. Deoxyribose has one fewer oxygen atom than ribose ("deoxy" means "without oxygen").
- [ ] U4 There are four bases in each nucleic acid. DNA: adenine (A), thymine (T), cytosine (C) and guanine (G). RNA: adenine, uracil (U), cytosine and guanine; uracil takes the place of thymine.
- [ ] U5 (assumed) Adenine and guanine are larger, double-ringed bases (purines); cytosine, thymine and uracil are smaller, single-ringed bases (pyrimidines). One sentence.
- [ ] U6 Function of a nucleotide: it is the building block of DNA and RNA, and its base is one "letter" of the genetic information. The order (sequence) of bases along a strand carries the information. (Modified nucleotides also carry energy: see "ATP".)
- [ ] U7 Diagram: one nucleotide drawn as a circle (phosphate) joined to a pentagon (sugar) joined to a rectangle (base), labelled phosphate, deoxyribose or ribose, and nitrogenous base.

How nucleotides join into a strand (one block)
- [ ] J1 Nucleotides join by bonds between the sugar of one nucleotide and the phosphate of the next, made in condensation reactions (a molecule of water is removed for each bond). The repeating sugar–phosphate–sugar–phosphate chain is the sugar–phosphate backbone.
- [ ] J2 The bases stick out from the backbone, one per sugar. The backbone is the same in every DNA molecule; only the sequence of bases differs, so the bases carry the information and the backbone gives strength and support.

DNA: the double helix and base pairing (one structure-and-function block)
- [ ] D1 DNA is double-stranded: two strands of nucleotides lie side by side, with the sugar–phosphate backbones on the outside and the bases pointing inwards, like the rails and rungs of a ladder.
- [ ] D2 The bases of the two strands pair up: adenine always pairs with thymine (A–T) and cytosine always pairs with guanine (C–G). These are complementary base pairs. Each pair is one purine with one pyrimidine, so every rung is the same width.
- [ ] D3 The bases in a pair are held together by hydrogen bonds: two between A and T, three between C and G. Each hydrogen bond is weak, so the strands can be separated (unzipped) when DNA is copied or read; but there are millions of them, so the molecule as a whole is stable.
- [ ] D4 The two linked strands are twisted around each other into a spiral called a double helix, like a twisted ladder.
- [ ] D5 Because pairing is fixed, the two strands are complementary: the sequence of one strand decides the sequence of the other. Worked example: the strand ATGCCTAG pairs with TACGGATC.
- [ ] D6 (assumed) Base-pairing calculations: in double-stranded DNA the amount of A equals T, and C equals G. Worked example: if 20% of the bases in a DNA sample are adenine, thymine is also 20%, leaving 60% shared equally between cytosine and guanine, so each is 30%.
- [ ] D7 Function of DNA: it stores the genetic information (genes) that controls the cell's activities, by coding for proteins; it is copied before a cell divides so each new cell receives the full set, and it is passed from parents to offspring. (Genes and proteins: "Genes, proteins and traits".)
- [ ] D8 Where DNA is found: in eukaryotic cells, mostly in the nucleus, in the chromosomes; small amounts in mitochondria and chloroplasts. In bacteria, in a loop in the cytoplasm and in small rings called plasmids.
- [ ] D9 The double-helix model was published by James Watson and Francis Crick in 1953, using X-ray diffraction images of DNA made by Rosalind Franklin (working with Maurice Wilkins). One bullet, as a Nature of Science link.
- [ ] D10 Diagram: a short section of DNA drawn as a straight ladder (untwisted) of about four base pairs, labelled sugar (deoxyribose), phosphate, sugar–phosphate backbone, base pairs A–T and C–G, hydrogen bonds; plus a sketch of the twisted double helix.

RNA (one structure-and-function block)
- [ ] N1 RNA is usually single-stranded: one chain of nucleotides with its own sugar–phosphate backbone. RNA molecules are much shorter than DNA molecules.
- [ ] N2 RNA nucleotides contain ribose instead of deoxyribose, and uracil instead of thymine. Uracil pairs with adenine (A–U) whenever RNA pairs with DNA or with another RNA.
- [ ] N3 A single strand can fold back on itself where its bases pair up, giving some RNA molecules a fixed shape.
- [ ] N4 Function of RNA: it uses the information in DNA to make proteins. The three main types are named only here: messenger RNA (mRNA) carries a copy of a gene from the nucleus to a ribosome; transfer RNA (tRNA) brings amino acids to the ribosome; ribosomal RNA (rRNA) forms part of the ribosome. Their roles in protein synthesis belong to "DNA Replication, Protein Synthesis and Mutations".
- [ ] N5 Where RNA is found: it is made in the nucleus and works in the cytoplasm; it is found in the nucleus (including the nucleolus), the cytoplasm and the ribosomes.
- [ ] N6 Diagram: a short single RNA strand of about four nucleotides labelled ribose, phosphate, backbone, and bases including uracil.

DNA and RNA compared (one comparison block, paired points)
- [ ] C1 Strands: DNA double-stranded helix; RNA usually single-stranded.
- [ ] C2 Sugar: DNA deoxyribose; RNA ribose.
- [ ] C3 Bases: DNA A, T, C, G; RNA A, U, C, G (uracil instead of thymine). Pairing: A–T in DNA; A–U in RNA.
- [ ] C4 Size and life span: DNA very long and stable, kept for the life of the cell; RNA molecules shorter and broken down after use.
- [ ] C5 Location: DNA mainly in the nucleus (also mitochondria and chloroplasts); RNA in the nucleus, cytoplasm and ribosomes.
- [ ] C6 Function: DNA stores the genetic information; RNA carries and uses it to make proteins.

## 3. Traps

- Don't teach "antiparallel" strands or 3′ and 5′ ends: the specification asks only for a double-stranded helix with A–T and C–G pairs.
- Bases pair A–T and C–G, never A–G or C–T; in RNA it is A–U. Uracil is never in DNA; thymine is never in RNA.
- Hydrogen bonds hold the two strands together (between bases); the backbone bonds within a strand are strong covalent bonds. Don't say the backbone is held by hydrogen bonds.
- DNA and RNA are nucleic acids, not proteins; a nucleotide is not the same as a base (the base is one of its three parts).
- How DNA is replicated, and transcription and translation, belong to "DNA Replication, Protein Synthesis and Mutations" (and DNA replication in the cell cycle to "Cell Division and Cancer"); D3, D7 and N4 mention them in a phrase only.
- Chromosome structure (histones, chromatin, chromatids), coding and non-coding DNA, and mitochondrial and chloroplast inheritance belong to "Genetic Inheritance"; D8 gives location only.
- Viruses containing DNA or RNA belong to "Viruses, Classification and Domains of Life".
- The structure of tRNA belongs to "DNA Replication, Protein Synthesis and Mutations".
- "Biomolecules — The Chemicals of Life" names nucleic acids as a biomolecule group and refers here for detail; this subsection holds the detail.
- No other diagram cues beyond U7, D10 and N6.

## 4. What exams ask (topics only)

- Name the sugar in DNA (or RNA); name the components of a nucleotide.
- Give structural differences between DNA and RNA (strands, sugar, thymine vs uracil).
- Name the base that pairs with a given base; give a complementary strand.
- Name locations in a eukaryotic cell where RNA but no DNA is found (cytoplasm, ribosomes).
- Draw a labelled diagram of a section of DNA.
