## 1. Must cover

Fertile periods (one block)
- [ ] P1 Fertility is the ability to produce offspring. A fertile period is the time during which intercourse can lead to pregnancy.
- [ ] P2 Males are continuously fertile: from puberty, sperm are made every day for the rest of life (declining slowly in old age), and every ejaculation contains millions of sperm (see "Gametes and fertilisation").
- [ ] P3 Females are cyclically fertile: from puberty to menopause, usually one egg is released per cycle and it survives only about 12–24 hours, while sperm can survive up to about five days. So the fertile window in each cycle is about six days: the five days before ovulation and the day of ovulation. In a regular 28-day cycle this is roughly days 9 to 14, but because ovulation day varies (see "The menstrual cycle"), the window can't be predicted exactly from the calendar alone.
- [ ] P4 Female fertility also changes over a lifetime: it is highest in the twenties and falls with age, more steeply after about 35, because the number and quality of the remaining eggs decline (see "Gametes and fertilisation"), and it ends at the menopause (see "Hormones in reproduction").

Contraception: what it is (one short block)
- [ ] C1 Contraception is the deliberate prevention of pregnancy. Methods work by preventing ovulation, preventing sperm from reaching the egg (preventing fertilisation), or preventing implantation. They are grouped as natural, mechanical (barrier), surgical and chemical (hormonal and spermicidal).
- [ ] C2 Effectiveness: with perfect use, the male condom is about 98% effective; the pill, the implant, the coils and sterilisation are each more than 99% effective. With typical (real-life) use, methods that depend on the user remembering or using them correctly every time (natural methods, condoms, the pill) fail more often, while long-acting methods (implant, coils, sterilisation) stay above 99%.
- [ ] C3 Only condoms (male and female) protect against sexually transmitted infections (STIs). People using another method need condoms as well to reduce the risk of STIs.

Natural methods (one block)
- [ ] N1 Abstinence (not having intercourse) is the only method that is completely effective against pregnancy and STIs.
- [ ] N2 Fertility awareness (natural family planning): the couple avoid intercourse during the fertile window, found by combining signs: 1. calendar (rhythm) method, recording cycle lengths to estimate when ovulation happens; 2. body temperature, measured each morning before getting up, which rises by a small amount (a few tenths of a degree Celsius) just after ovulation and stays up for the rest of the cycle, because of progesterone; 3. cervical mucus, which becomes thin, clear, stretchy and slippery around ovulation. Home kits that detect the hormone surge before ovulation in urine can also be used (see "The menstrual cycle").
- [ ] N3 Use and limits of natural methods: no drugs, devices or side effects, and acceptable to people whose religious beliefs rule out artificial contraception (e.g. the teaching of the Catholic Church); but they need training, daily records and regular cycles, are much less reliable with typical use, are hard to use after childbirth, when breastfeeding or near the menopause, and give no protection against STIs. Withdrawal of the penis before ejaculation is unreliable, because some sperm may be released before ejaculation. Breastfeeding delays ovulation but is not reliable contraception (see "Hormones in reproduction").

Mechanical (barrier and device) methods (one block)
- [ ] B1 Male condom: a thin latex (or non-latex) sheath placed over the erect penis that collects the semen, so sperm can't enter the vagina. It also blocks the exchange of body fluids and so reduces the spread of STIs, including HIV. Cheap, widely available, no hormones; can tear or slip; must be put on before any contact.
- [ ] B2 Female condom: a soft pouch placed in the vagina that lines it and collects semen; also protects against STIs. Diaphragm or cap: a dome of soft silicone placed over the cervix before intercourse, used with a spermicide and left in for several hours afterwards; it doesn't protect well against STIs.
- [ ] B3 Copper coil (copper intrauterine device, IUD): a small T-shaped device with copper, placed in the uterus by a doctor or nurse. Copper is toxic to sperm and eggs, preventing fertilisation, and changes the endometrium so an embryo is unlikely to implant. It lasts for 5–10 years, contains no hormones, and can be removed at any time with a quick return to fertility. It can also be used as emergency contraception if fitted within five days. It can make periods heavier, and gives no protection against STIs. (Grouped here as mechanical because it is a device; the hormonal coil is in the chemical block.)

Surgical methods (one block)
- [ ] S1 Vasectomy (male sterilisation): under local anaesthetic, each vas deferens is cut and tied or sealed, so sperm can't leave the testes (see "The male reproductive system"). The man still ejaculates semen (the fluids come from the glands, which are untouched) and his testosterone and sex drive are unaffected; the sperm that are still made are broken down and reabsorbed. It isn't effective at once, because sperm remain in the ducts beyond the cut; semen tests are needed after some weeks to confirm there are no sperm.
- [ ] S2 Tubal ligation (female sterilisation): each fallopian tube is cut, tied or blocked with a clip, usually by keyhole surgery under general anaesthetic, so the egg and sperm can't meet. Ovulation and the menstrual cycle continue as before (the egg is reabsorbed). It is effective at once.
- [ ] S3 Use and limits: very effective and permanent, with nothing to remember; but it should be treated as irreversible (reversal is difficult and often fails), carries the small risks of any surgery, and gives no protection against STIs. It suits people who are sure they want no more children.
- [ ] S4 Diagram: outline of the male system with the cut in each vas deferens marked, and of the female system with the cut or clip in each fallopian tube marked; label vas deferens, testis, fallopian tube, ovary, uterus, and "cut and tied".

Chemical methods (one block)
- [ ] H1 The combined pill contains synthetic oestrogen and progesterone and prevents ovulation (how, in "Hormonal manipulation"); it also thickens the cervical mucus so sperm can't pass. It is taken daily, usually with a break or dummy pills each month, during which a withdrawal bleed happens. The progestogen-only pill (synthetic progesterone only) works mainly by thickening the cervical mucus, and in some types also stops ovulation; it suits women who can't take oestrogen.
- [ ] H2 Long-acting hormonal methods release a synthetic progesterone continuously: the implant (a small flexible rod placed under the skin of the upper arm, lasting about 3 years), the injection (every few months), and the hormonal coil (intrauterine system, IUS, placed in the uterus, lasting several years, which thickens the cervical mucus and thins the endometrium). The contraceptive patch and vaginal ring release oestrogen and progesterone, like the combined pill.
- [ ] H3 Emergency contraception: emergency hormonal pills, taken as soon as possible after unprotected intercourse (one type within 3 days, another within 5 days), work mainly by delaying ovulation; the copper coil fitted within 5 days is the most effective emergency method (B3). They are not for regular use.
- [ ] H4 Spermicides: chemicals in a gel or cream that kill sperm; they are not reliable alone and are used with a diaphragm or cap.
- [ ] H5 Use and limits of hormonal methods: very effective; periods often become lighter, more regular and less painful; the combined pill lowers the risk of cancers of the ovary and of the endometrium (womb). But they can cause side effects (e.g. headaches, mood changes, irregular bleeding with progesterone-only methods); the combined pill slightly raises the risk of blood clots (deep vein thrombosis) and stroke, especially in smokers and women over 35, so it isn't prescribed to everyone; hormonal methods give no protection against STIs; and the pill must be taken correctly every day (vomiting or some medicines can stop it working).

Preventing STIs (one block)
- [ ] I1 An STI (sexually transmitted infection) is an infection passed on mainly by sexual contact. Examples: bacterial – chlamydia, gonorrhoea, syphilis (treated with antibiotics); viral – HIV, genital herpes, human papillomavirus (HPV, which causes genital warts and most cervical cancers) and hepatitis B (can be controlled but not usually cured). Pathogens in "Pathogens".
- [ ] I2 Link to infertility: chlamydia and gonorrhoea often cause no symptoms, especially in women, but can spread to the uterus and fallopian tubes (pelvic inflammatory disease), scarring and blocking the tubes; this is a major preventable cause of female infertility and raises the risk of ectopic pregnancy (see "The female reproductive system"). In men they can inflame the epididymis.
- [ ] I3 Prevention: correct use of condoms every time; fewer sexual partners; regular testing and treatment of infected people and their partners, which breaks the chain of transmission (many infected people have no symptoms); vaccination against HPV (see "Causes, prevention and treatment of cancer") and hepatitis B; for HIV, anti-HIV drugs taken before possible exposure (pre-exposure prophylaxis, PrEP), and treatment of people with HIV, which reduces their virus to levels at which it isn't passed on sexually. Gonorrhoea is becoming resistant to antibiotics, which makes prevention more important (resistance in "Emergence and spread of infectious disease").

Freezing eggs (one block)
- [ ] F1 Egg freezing (oocyte cryopreservation) is a way of preserving fertility for later. 1. Hormone injections stimulate the ovaries to mature several eggs at once. 2. The eggs are collected with a fine needle through the vaginal wall, guided by ultrasound, under sedation. 3. They are frozen very rapidly (vitrification), so that ice crystals, which would damage the cells, don't form, and stored in liquid nitrogen at −196 °C. 4. Later they are thawed and fertilised by IVF or ICSI (below).
- [ ] F2 Uses: before cancer treatment (chemotherapy or radiotherapy can destroy the eggs in the ovaries), or by women who wish to delay having children. Sperm, and embryos made in IVF, can also be frozen; sperm freezing is offered to men before cancer treatment.
- [ ] F3 Limits: success depends strongly on the woman's age when the eggs are frozen (younger eggs have fewer chromosome errors and survive freezing better), so freezing gives no guarantee of a baby; not every egg survives thawing; the process involves hormone treatment and a procedure, and it is expensive.

Infertility and its causes (one block)
- [ ] IN1 Infertility is the failure to become pregnant after 12 months of regular unprotected intercourse. The World Health Organization estimated in 2023 that about 1 in 6 people worldwide experience infertility at some point in their lives. Causes are found in the man, the woman, both, or neither (unexplained infertility).
- [ ] IN2 Male causes: low sperm count; sperm that swim poorly or are abnormally shaped (e.g. a faulty tail); a blockage of the epididymis or vas deferens (e.g. after infection or vasectomy); undescended testes; low testosterone or low pituitary hormones; and lifestyle and environmental factors that reduce sperm production, such as smoking, heavy drinking, obesity, anabolic steroids (see "Hormonal manipulation") and overheating of the testes. Diagnosed by semen analysis (sperm count, movement and shape under the microscope).
- [ ] IN3 Female causes: failure to ovulate, caused by hormone imbalance, e.g. polycystic ovary syndrome (PCOS), or by being very underweight or overweight; blocked or damaged fallopian tubes, e.g. after pelvic inflammatory disease from an STI (I2); endometriosis, in which tissue like the endometrium grows outside the uterus and can cause scarring around the tubes and ovaries; problems of the uterus or cervical mucus; and age (P4). Diagnosed by blood hormone tests, ultrasound of the ovaries and uterus, and tests that show whether the tubes are open.

Treatments for infertility (one block per treatment; each block gives the biology, the steps and who it is for)
- [ ] T1 Stimulating ovulation (for women who don't ovulate regularly, e.g. with PCOS): fertility drugs, as tablets or injections, cause the ovaries to mature and release eggs; the woman is monitored by ultrasound to see how many follicles develop, and intercourse or insemination is timed to ovulation. Risks: more than one egg may be released, so twins or triplets are more likely; the ovaries can over-respond (ovarian hyperstimulation syndrome), becoming swollen and painful, which is occasionally serious. Lifestyle change, e.g. weight loss, can restore ovulation in some women.
- [ ] T2 Artificial insemination (intrauterine insemination, IUI): semen from the partner or a donor is collected, the sperm are washed and concentrated, and they are placed directly into the uterus through the cervix with a thin tube, at the time of ovulation. This places many active sperm close to the fallopian tubes. For: mildly low sperm count or motility, cervical mucus that blocks sperm, difficulty with intercourse, and single women or female couples using donor sperm. The woman's fallopian tubes must be open. The same technique is widely used in farming, e.g. to breed cattle from selected bulls.
- [ ] T3 In vitro fertilisation (IVF; "in vitro" means "in glass", i.e. outside the body): 1. hormone injections stimulate the ovaries to mature several eggs; 2. the eggs are collected with a needle guided by ultrasound; 3. the eggs are mixed with sperm in a dish in the laboratory, where fertilisation takes place; 4. the embryos are grown in an incubator for 2–5 days (up to the blastocyst stage; see "Pregnancy"); 5. usually one (sometimes two) embryos are placed in the uterus through the cervix with a thin tube; 6. a pregnancy test about two weeks later shows whether an embryo has implanted. Spare good-quality embryos can be frozen for later attempts. For: blocked or damaged fallopian tubes, unexplained infertility, endometriosis, failure of other treatments, and couples needing preimplantation genetic testing (see "Prenatal and postnatal care").
- [ ] T4 History (one bullet): the first baby conceived by IVF, Louise Brown, was born in England on 25 July 1978, after work by the scientist Robert Edwards and the gynaecologist Patrick Steptoe; Edwards was awarded the Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine in 2010. Millions of babies have since been born by IVF worldwide.
- [ ] T5 Intracytoplasmic sperm injection (ICSI): the same as IVF, except that in step 3 a single sperm is chosen and injected directly into the cytoplasm of each egg with a very fine glass needle, under a microscope. Because the sperm doesn't have to swim to or enter the egg itself, ICSI overcomes severe male infertility: very low sperm count, sperm that can't swim or can't penetrate the egg, sperm taken directly from the testis or epididymis by a needle (e.g. after a vasectomy or a blockage), and previous failure of fertilisation in IVF. It is also used with frozen eggs (F1).
- [ ] T6 Diagram: a flow chart of IVF with ICSI as a branch: stimulate ovaries → collect eggs → (IVF: mix eggs and sperm in a dish | ICSI: inject one sperm into each egg) → grow embryos 2–5 days → transfer an embryo to the uterus → pregnancy test.

How ovulation-stimulating drugs work (Higher Level only; one block)
- [ ] T7 [HL] Some fertility tablets block oestrogen's negative feedback on the brain and pituitary, so the pituitary releases more FSH and LH, which stimulate follicle development and ovulation (feedback in "The menstrual cycle").
- [ ] T8 [HL] Injected fertility drugs contain FSH (often with LH) and act directly on the ovaries to make follicles develop; a final injection of a hormone that acts like LH triggers the eggs' final maturation and ovulation, imitating the LH surge. The same injections are used to stimulate the ovaries in IVF and egg freezing.

Discussing fertility control and infertility treatment (one comparison block per strategy group; each bullet an actual argument with its biology)
- [ ] D1 Contraception, for: lets people choose whether and when to have children and how many, and space pregnancies, which improves the health of mothers and babies; prevents unintended pregnancy, including in teenagers; condoms reduce the spread of STIs, including HIV, a major public-health benefit; hormonal methods can treat heavy or painful periods and the combined pill lowers the risk of ovarian and endometrial cancer; at a global scale, access to contraception slows population growth, reducing pressure on food, water and habitats (a Sustainability link; see "The cross-cutting themes").
- [ ] D2 Contraception, against or limits: hormonal methods have side effects and, for some women, a small increased risk of blood clots; no method except condoms prevents STIs, so a person relying on the pill may still catch one; user-dependent methods often fail in real life; sterilisation is effectively irreversible and may be regretted; some people object to artificial contraception on religious or moral grounds; synthetic oestrogen from the pill, along with natural oestrogens, passes through sewage treatment into rivers, where it has been linked to the feminisation of male fish, an environmental effect.
- [ ] D3 Infertility treatment, for: allows people with infertility to have a child who is genetically their own, relieving much distress; ICSI lets many men with very few or poorly swimming sperm become fathers; egg and sperm freezing preserve fertility for people having cancer treatment; preimplantation testing in IVF lets couples who carry a serious genetic condition avoid passing it on; donor eggs or sperm allow people without their own gametes to have children; research in IVF has increased understanding of fertilisation and early development.
- [ ] D4 Infertility treatment, against or limits: stimulating ovulation and transferring more than one embryo raise the chance of twins and triplets, which are more often born premature and underweight and carry more risk for the mother, which is why single-embryo transfer is now favoured; ovarian hyperstimulation can be dangerous; success rates per cycle are limited and fall sharply with the woman's age, so treatment can mean repeated cycles with much stress and cost, and unequal access for those who can't pay; ICSI bypasses the natural selection of sperm, so a genetic cause of the father's infertility (e.g. some changes on the Y chromosome) can be passed to a son; spare frozen embryos raise questions about how long they are stored and whether they may be used in research or discarded (see "Ethics of genetic technologies"); children conceived with donor gametes may want to know their genetic origins, which raises questions about donor anonymity; treatment allows parenthood at older ages, when pregnancy carries more risk (e.g. a higher chance of chromosome conditions; see "Mutations").
- [ ] D5 Freezing eggs, for and against: for, it preserves a woman's own eggs before cancer treatment and gives women more choice over when to have children; against, success is uncertain and lower when eggs are frozen at older ages, it involves hormone treatment, egg collection and high costs, and it may give a false sense of security that leads to delayed attempts at pregnancy.

## 3. Traps

- Only condoms protect against STIs; the pill, coils, sterilisation and natural methods don't (C3).
- Vasectomy doesn't stop ejaculation or testosterone production, and isn't effective immediately; tubal ligation doesn't stop periods (S1–S2).
- IVF: fertilisation happens in the dish, not in the uterus; the embryo, not the egg, is transferred. ICSI is a form of IVF, not an alternative to it. Artificial insemination puts sperm in the uterus, not eggs, and needs open fallopian tubes.
- The ICSI acronym: intracytoplasmic sperm injection (into the cytoplasm), not "intracellular".
- Keep FSH and LH out of the Ordinary blocks; T1 says "fertility drugs"; the hormone names are in T7–T8.
- Don't re-explain how the combined pill blocks ovulation; that is in "Hormonal manipulation" (H1 gives one phrase).
- Don't call the copper coil hormonal or the hormonal coil (IUS) copper.
- Don't give success rates for IVF, prices, or numbers of IVF babies beyond "millions".
- The ethics of embryo research, stem cells, genetic testing of embryos and genetic data belong to "Ethics of genetic technologies"; D3–D4 state the points briefly and cross-refer.
- Don't describe Irish law or services (no free-contraception scheme, no public IVF funding, no Assisted Human Reproduction legislation).
- Place the Higher Level block (T7–T8) straight after the block on stimulating ovulation (T1).

## 5. Practice questions to write

1. 6 marks: "Explain why a man is fertile continuously but a woman is fertile for only a few days in each cycle." Model answer: P2 (sperm made every day from puberty); P3 (one egg per cycle, surviving 12–24 hours; sperm survive up to five days; window of about six days).
2. 12 marks: "Name one natural, one mechanical, one surgical and one chemical method of contraception. For each, explain how it prevents pregnancy and state whether it protects against STIs." Model answer: e.g. N2 (avoid fertile window; no), B1 (condom collects semen; yes), S1 or S2 (cut ducts; no), H1 (prevents ovulation, thickens mucus; no).
3. 12 marks: "Describe in vitro fertilisation and discuss the use and biological implications of IVF and ICSI as treatments for infertility." Model answer: T3 steps, T5 what ICSI adds and who it helps, then at least two points from D3 and two from D4 (e.g. genetically related child, overcoming male infertility; multiple births, falling success with age, passing on Y-linked infertility, spare embryos), and a balanced conclusion.
