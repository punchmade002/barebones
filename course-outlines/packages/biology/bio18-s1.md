## 1. Must cover

What the musculoskeletal system is (one block)
- [ ] O1 The musculoskeletal system is the skeleton (bones, and the cartilage and ligaments that join them at joints) together with the skeletal muscles and the tendons that attach them to bones. Muscles provide the force; bones act as rigid levers; joints are the pivots where movement happens. Name the parts only here; each is described in the later subsections.
- [ ] O2 The human adult skeleton has 206 bones, divided into the axial skeleton (80 bones: the skull, vertebral column, ribs and sternum, forming the central axis of the body) and the appendicular skeleton (126 bones: the pectoral and pelvic girdles and the limbs attached to them). Detail is in "The axial skeleton" and "The appendicular skeleton".
- [ ] O3 (assumed) Bone is a living tissue (a connective tissue): bone cells lie in a hard matrix of calcium phosphate, which gives hardness and resists compression, and collagen fibres, which give some flexibility and resist bending and snapping. Bones have a blood supply and nerves, can grow and repair themselves, and change shape slowly in response to the forces put on them.

Functions of the skeleton (one block; one bullet per function, each with an example)
- [ ] F1 Support: the skeleton is a rigid framework that holds the body up, gives it its shape, and supports soft organs; e.g. the vertebral column supports the head and trunk, the leg bones carry the body's weight.
- [ ] F2 Protection: bones surround and protect delicate organs; e.g. the skull (cranium) protects the brain, the ribs and sternum protect the heart and lungs, the vertebrae protect the spinal cord, and the pelvic girdle protects the bladder and reproductive organs.
- [ ] F3 Movement: bones provide attachment points for muscles and act as levers that the muscles pull on, moving the body at joints (detail in "Muscles and joints").
- [ ] F4 Making blood cells: red bone marrow, found in spongy bone, makes red blood cells, white blood cells and platelets. In adults it is mainly in flat bones (skull, ribs, sternum, vertebrae, pelvis) and the ends of long bones such as the femur and humerus. Yellow marrow, which fills the hollow shafts of long bones, stores fat.
- [ ] F5 Storing minerals: bone stores calcium and phosphate. When blood calcium falls, calcium is released from bone (under the control of parathyroid hormone; see "Glands, locations and hormones"), so the skeleton helps keep blood calcium constant for nerve and muscle function.

Types of muscle (one comparison block)
- [ ] U1 (assumed) Skeletal muscle: attached to bones by tendons; under voluntary (conscious) control, as well as reflex control; its cells look striped (striated) under the microscope; contracts quickly and powerfully but tires (fatigues). It is the muscle of the musculoskeletal system.
- [ ] U2 (assumed) Smooth muscle: in the walls of the gut, blood vessels, bladder and uterus; involuntary (not under conscious control); not striped; contracts slowly and can stay contracted for a long time, e.g. peristalsis in the gut.
- [ ] U3 (assumed) Cardiac muscle: only in the wall of the heart; involuntary; striped; contracts rhythmically throughout life without tiring. (The heart is in "The Circulatory System and Blood".) Only skeletal muscle is part of the musculoskeletal system.

The muscle as the effector of a response (one process block)
- [ ] E1 A skeletal muscle is made of bundles of long cells called muscle fibres. Each fibre contains many nuclei and many mitochondria, and is packed with protein filaments of two kinds: thin filaments of actin and thick filaments of myosin, arranged in repeating units (which give the striped appearance).
- [ ] E2 Skeletal muscle contracts only when stimulated by a motor neuron. A voluntary movement starts in the brain; a reflex movement starts in the spinal cord (see "The reflex action"). Impulses travel along the motor neuron to its ending on the muscle fibre, a synapse called the neuromuscular junction.
- [ ] E3 At the neuromuscular junction, the neurotransmitter acetylcholine is released, crosses the gap and binds to receptors on the muscle fibre, which starts an impulse in the fibre (synapse detail in "Neurotransmission").
- [ ] E4 The impulse causes calcium ions (Ca²⁺) to be released from stores inside the fibre. The calcium allows the myosin filaments to attach to the actin filaments and pull them inwards, so the filaments slide past each other and the fibre shortens. Energy for this comes from ATP (see "ATP"), made in the many mitochondria by respiration.
- [ ] E5 When the impulses stop, calcium is pumped back into its stores, the filaments detach, and the muscle relaxes. A relaxed muscle doesn't lengthen by itself: it is pulled back to its full length by an opposing muscle (antagonistic pairs, in "Muscles and joints").
- [ ] E6 A muscle can only pull (by contracting); it can never push. The force is passed through a tendon to a bone, which moves at a joint: the response.
- [ ] E7 Strength of response: the more motor neurons that fire and the more muscle fibres that contract, the stronger the contraction, so the nervous system can make a movement gentle or forceful. A slight, continuous partial contraction of muscles (muscle tone) holds the body's posture without conscious effort.
- [ ] E8 Diagram: a flow diagram of a voluntary response: brain → spinal cord → motor neuron → neuromuscular junction → skeletal muscle (contracts) → tendon → bone moves at joint; and a sketch of a skeletal muscle cut open to show bundles of muscle fibres, with one fibre enlarged to show actin and myosin filaments, labelled.

Responses the system carries out (one block)
- [ ] R1 Movement of the whole body (locomotion: walking, running, swimming) to find food, escape danger or move to better conditions, and movement of parts of the body (grasping, turning the head towards a sound).
- [ ] R2 Maintaining posture and balance: muscles of the back, neck and legs make constant small adjustments in response to information from receptors, keeping the body upright.
- [ ] R3 Other responses that depend on skeletal muscle: breathing (the diaphragm and intercostal muscles moving the ribs; see "Gas exchange and the breathing mechanism"), speech, facial expression, chewing and swallowing, and shivering, in which rapid involuntary contractions release heat when the body is cold.

Dependence on other systems (one block)
- [ ] D1 Nervous system: every contraction of a skeletal muscle is started by a motor neuron; receptors in muscles and joints send information back to the brain about the position of the limbs, so movements can be coordinated.
- [ ] D2 Circulatory and breathing systems: active muscles need more oxygen and glucose for respiration and must get rid of more carbon dioxide and heat, so heart rate and breathing rate rise during exercise. When oxygen can't be supplied fast enough, muscle fibres respire anaerobically, producing lactic acid and much less ATP, and soon tire (fatigue); the lactic acid is removed afterwards, when oxygen is available again, which is one reason breathing stays fast after exercise (see "Respiration"). Don't say lactic acid is the only cause of fatigue.
- [ ] D3 Endocrine system: adrenaline increases blood flow to skeletal muscles and raises blood glucose, preparing them for action (see "Glands, locations and hormones").
- [ ] D4 Health link: regular weight-bearing exercise (e.g. walking, running) strengthens bones and muscles; a diet with enough protein (for muscle), calcium and vitamin D (for bone; see "Minerals" and "Vitamins") supports the system.

## 3. Traps

- Don't repeat the general stimulus–response pathway, the one-line overview of each system, or the "systems working together in danger" example from "Structures and systems for response"; refer to it by title.
- The sliding-filament mechanism is assumed knowledge here at the level of E1–E5 only: no sarcomere bands (A, I, H, Z), troponin, tropomyosin or cross-bridge cycle; the specification doesn't ask for them.
- Muscles pull, never push. A relaxed muscle is lengthened by its antagonist, not by itself.
- Tendons join muscle to bone; ligaments join bone to bone. Their structure and functions belong to "Muscles and joints"; here name them only.
- Individual bones and their functions belong to "The axial skeleton" and "The appendicular skeleton"; F1–F2 give only the examples needed to illustrate each function.
- Neurotransmitter release, synapse structure and reflex arcs belong to "The Nervous System" ("Neurotransmission", "The reflex action"); E2–E3 give two sentences.
- Disorders of the musculoskeletal system (arthritis, osteoporosis, fractures, sprains, muscular dystrophy) and bone growth were on the previous syllabus and aren't on this specification. Don't teach them.
- Plant responses belong to "Structures and systems for response".
- The diagram cues in E8 are the only ones wanted here. No other diagram cues.
