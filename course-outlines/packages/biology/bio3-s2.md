# Package: bio3-s2 — Science, scientists and society

  contribution that scientists make to scientific discovery and invention, and evaluate its

## 1. Must cover

How scientists contribute (one block)
- [ ] C1 A discovery is finding something that already exists in nature but was not known, e.g. the structure of DNA, or that a mould kills bacteria. An invention is creating a new device, process or product that did not exist before, e.g. the electron microscope, PCR, or a vaccine. Discoveries often lead to inventions (the discovery of penicillin's effect led to the invention of methods to mass-produce it).
- [ ] C2 Basic (pure) research aims to understand nature with no particular use in mind; applied research aims to solve a practical problem. Many applications come from basic research done decades earlier, e.g. the enzymes used in PCR and in DNA profiling came from the study of bacteria (detail in "Generating a DNA profile").
- [ ] C3 Today most discoveries are made by teams, often international, working in universities, hospitals, public research institutes and companies, funded by governments, the EU, charities and industry (see "Science as a global enterprise"). Who funds research influences which questions are studied.
- [ ] C4 Scientists also contribute by advising governments (e.g. public health advice in a pandemic), assessing risks of new technologies, communicating with the public, and warning about problems (as Rachel Carson did, case study 3).

The four factors for evaluating impact (one block; each factor defined, with the questions to ask)
- [ ] F1 Economic: costs and benefits in money and jobs. Ask: who pays for the research and the product; who profits (companies, patents); does it create jobs or industries; does it save money (e.g. fewer days lost to illness, higher crop yields); can poorer people and countries afford it?
- [ ] F2 Social: effects on people's health, lives, communities and how people live. Ask: who benefits and who is harmed; does it change life expectancy or quality of life; does it increase or reduce inequality; how does the public accept it?
- [ ] F3 Sustainability: meeting present needs without harming the ability of future generations to meet theirs. Ask: what are the effects on the environment, biodiversity, resources and climate; are the benefits long-lasting; does it cause problems such as resistance or pollution?
- [ ] F4 Ethical: whether it is right or wrong. Ask: is it fair (justice); does it do good and avoid harm; were people informed and did they consent; were animals used, and was their use justified; who decides? Ethics questions are answered by reasoning about values, informed by evidence; science alone can't settle them.
- [ ] F5 Ethical principles used in biology and medicine (assumed): the four principles of medical ethics are autonomy (respect people's right to decide for themselves, through informed consent), beneficence (do good), non-maleficence (do no harm) and justice (treat people fairly and share benefits and burdens fairly). For animal research, the 3Rs (proposed by Russell and Burch in 1959): Replacement (use non-animal methods where possible), Reduction (use as few animals as possible), Refinement (minimise pain and distress). The precautionary principle: where an action might cause serious harm and the science is uncertain, take care or don't proceed until there is more evidence. International research conventions (e.g. the Declaration of Helsinki) are in "Science as a global enterprise".

Case studies (one block each; each block: the scientists and what they did → impact under the four factors, positive and negative → a one-line judgement)
- [ ] P1 Case study 1, penicillin. What happened: in 1928 Alexander Fleming, at St Mary's Hospital in London, noticed that a mould (Penicillium) contaminating a culture plate of Staphylococcus bacteria had killed the bacteria around it. Howard Florey and Ernst Chain at the University of Oxford purified penicillin and showed it cured infections, from 1940; it was mass-produced during the Second World War. Fleming, Florey and Chain shared the Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine in 1945.
- [ ] P2 Penicillin impact. Social: bacterial infections that were often fatal (infected wounds, pneumonia, sepsis) became curable; surgery and childbirth became much safer; it began the era of antibiotics. Economic: created a large pharmaceutical industry; healthier workers and fewer deaths; antibiotics are cheap to make. Sustainability: overuse and misuse of antibiotics in medicine and farming have selected for resistant bacteria, such as MRSA (natural selection; see "Evidence for evolution"), threatening to make infections untreatable again; Fleming warned of this in his Nobel lecture in 1945. Ethical: unequal access in poorer countries; use of antibiotics to make farm animals grow faster (banned in the EU since 2006) raises resistance for everyone; prescribing responsibly (antibiotic stewardship) is now an ethical duty. Judgement: one of the most beneficial discoveries in medicine, whose benefits are now threatened by the way it has been used.
- [ ] I1 Case study 2, insulin. What happened: in 1921 at the University of Toronto, Frederick Banting and Charles Best extracted insulin from the pancreases of dogs and showed it lowered blood glucose in diabetic dogs; in 1922 it was first used to treat a person with type 1 diabetes, a 14-year-old boy, Leonard Thompson. Banting and John Macleod received the Nobel Prize in 1923. The discoverers sold the patent to the University of Toronto for a token sum so insulin could be made widely available. Insulin was extracted from cattle and pig pancreases until 1982, when human insulin made by genetically modified bacteria became the first genetically engineered medicine approved for use (detail in "Genetic engineering").
- [ ] I2 Insulin impact. Social: type 1 diabetes changed from a fatal disease to a manageable condition. Economic: a large global insulin industry; in some countries, notably the USA, high insulin prices mean some patients can't afford enough. Sustainability: making insulin in bacteria in fermenters gives an unlimited, consistent supply without relying on slaughterhouse animals. Ethical: experiments on dogs (assess using the 3Rs); the choice to give up profit from the patent; animal-derived insulin raised religious and vegetarian concerns that human insulin from bacteria removes; fair access is a justice issue. Judgement: overwhelmingly positive, but access is unequal.
- [ ] D1 Case study 3, DDT and Rachel Carson. What happened: in 1939 Paul Müller discovered that the chemical DDT is a powerful insecticide; he received the Nobel Prize in 1948. DDT was used to control insects that spread malaria and typhus, and to protect crops. In 1962 the American biologist Rachel Carson published Silent Spring, presenting evidence that DDT and other pesticides were harming wildlife, especially birds. DDT is not broken down quickly, so it builds up in food chains, reaching high concentrations in top predators such as birds of prey, where it causes thin eggshells that break. The USA banned most uses of DDT in 1972; the Stockholm Convention (2001) restricts it worldwide but allows its use to control malaria-carrying mosquitoes.
- [ ] D2 DDT impact. Social: millions of people protected from malaria and typhus; increased food production. Economic: cheap and effective, so it raised crop yields; chemical companies attacked Carson's work. Sustainability: persistence and build-up in food chains harmed birds of prey and other wildlife; insects evolved resistance to DDT; Silent Spring helped start the modern environmental movement. Ethical: weighing human lives saved from malaria against harm to wildlife and long-term ecological damage; the precautionary principle; the right of the public to be told the risks. Judgement: shows that the same scientific contribution can have major benefits and major harms, and that scientists also contribute by evaluating technologies. (Pyramids and food chains are in "Energy and nutrient transfer".)
- [ ] V1 Case study 4, ivermectin and William C. Campbell (an Irish scientist). What happened: William Campbell, born in 1930 in Ramelton, Co. Donegal, and a graduate of Trinity College Dublin, worked at the drug company Merck in the USA. Satoshi Ōmura in Japan isolated a soil bacterium (a Streptomyces species) that produced useful compounds; Campbell's team found that one of them, avermectin, killed parasitic worms, and developed the drug ivermectin from it. Ivermectin is used against river blindness (onchocerciasis), caused by a parasitic worm spread by the bites of blackflies, and against lymphatic filariasis. Campbell and Ōmura shared the 2015 Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine with Tu Youyou, who discovered the antimalarial drug artemisinin.
- [ ] V2 Ivermectin impact. Social: has protected many millions of people in Africa and Latin America from river blindness and other parasitic diseases. Economic: first sold as a veterinary drug for livestock and pets, which made it highly profitable; healthier people can work and attend school. Ethical: since 1987 Merck has donated the human form (Mectizan) free, for as long as needed, through the Mectizan Donation Program, a model of a company sharing a discovery with people who can't pay. Sustainability: heavy use in livestock has led to resistance in some livestock parasites, and residues in animal dung can harm dung-feeding insects. Judgement: a discovery from basic research on soil microorganisms with an enormous health benefit, shared ethically.
- [ ] TH1 Case study 5, thalidomide (a warning case). What happened: thalidomide was sold from 1957, first in West Germany and then in many countries including Ireland and the UK, as a sedative and was given to pregnant women for morning sickness. It caused severe birth defects, especially shortened or missing limbs, in more than 10,000 babies before it was withdrawn in 1961. It had not been tested for effects on the developing foetus. In the USA, Frances Kelsey, a reviewer at the Food and Drug Administration, refused to approve it because the safety evidence was inadequate.
- [ ] TH2 Thalidomide impact. Social: lifelong disability for survivors and their families; loss of public trust in medicines. Ethical: patients were harmed by a drug released without adequate testing; survivors campaigned for decades for compensation and an apology. Economic: compensation costs; new regulation made developing drugs slower and more expensive. Lasting effect: much stricter laws on testing drugs before sale, including testing for effects in pregnancy, and systems to report side effects. Thalidomide is now used, under strict controls, to treat leprosy complications and a blood cancer (multiple myeloma). Judgement: shows why scientists and regulators must test new technologies carefully and why independent review matters.

## 3. Traps

- The lenses Health, Sustainability and Technology belong to "The cross-cutting themes"; don't redefine them here. Sustainability here is one of the four factors (F3).
- Ethics of genetic technologies (GM crops, gene editing, DNA data, cloning, stem cells) belongs to "Ethics of genetic technologies"; don't use those as case studies here.
- How antibiotic resistance evolves belongs to the Evolution and Immunity chapters; P2 states the effect only.
- Media arguments and the MMR case belong to "Evaluating evidence".
- Fleming did not purify or mass-produce penicillin; Florey and Chain did. Don't say Fleming "invented" penicillin.
- Don't say DDT is banned everywhere: the Stockholm Convention allows its use against malaria mosquitoes.
- Don't say Campbell discovered the bacterium: Ōmura isolated it.
