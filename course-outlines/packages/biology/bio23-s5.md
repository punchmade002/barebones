## 1. Must cover

From zygote to implantation (one process block)
- [ ] Z1 Fertilisation in the fallopian tube gives a diploid zygote, the first cell of the new individual (see "Gametes and fertilisation"). One bullet.
- [ ] Z2 The zygote divides repeatedly by mitosis as cilia and muscle contractions move it down the fallopian tube; the cells don't grow between divisions, so the cells become smaller while the ball stays about the size of the egg. After about five days it reaches the uterus as a small ball of cells.
- [ ] Z3 Implantation: about 6–7 days after fertilisation the ball of cells sinks into the thick, spongy endometrium and embeds itself there, with enzymes from its outer cells digesting a way in. From now on the woman is pregnant. Implantation needs an endometrium kept thick by progesterone (see "Hormones in reproduction").
- [ ] Z4 After implantation, a hormone from the embryo keeps the corpus luteum alive, so the endometrium is kept and menstruation stops; a missed period is often the first sign of pregnancy, and pregnancy tests detect this hormone in urine (hCG; detail in "Hormones in reproduction"). One bullet.
- [ ] Z5 If the ball of cells implants in the fallopian tube instead, it is an ectopic pregnancy (see "The female reproductive system"). One phrase only.

Placenta, umbilical cord and amnion (one structure-and-function block)
- [ ] PL1 Placenta formation: after implantation, the outer cells of the embryo grow many finger-like projections (villi) into the endometrium; these, with the mother's endometrium and blood spaces around them, form the placenta, a disc-shaped organ attached to the wall of the uterus. It is fully formed by about the end of the third month (12 weeks) and keeps growing until birth.
- [ ] PL2 The umbilical cord connects the foetus to the placenta. It contains two umbilical arteries, which carry deoxygenated blood with wastes from the foetus to the placenta, and one umbilical vein, which carries oxygenated blood with nutrients from the placenta to the foetus. (Note: the vein carries oxygenated blood, the reverse of the adult pattern for most arteries and veins; see "Circulation".)
- [ ] PL3 The mother's and the foetus's blood don't mix. They are separated by a thin barrier of the villi, only a few cells thick. This protects the foetus from the mother's high blood pressure and from her immune cells, and matters because their blood groups may differ (see "Blood").
- [ ] PL4 How the placenta's structure suits exchange: the villi give a very large surface area; the barrier is very thin; the foetus's capillaries in the villi lie close to pools of the mother's blood; and blood flow on both sides keeps the concentration gradients high. Substances cross by diffusion, and some (e.g. glucose, amino acids) by facilitated diffusion or active transport (see "Diffusion, osmosis and active transport").
- [ ] PL5 Functions of the placenta: 1. supplies oxygen, glucose, amino acids, fatty acids, vitamins, minerals and water to the foetus (it acts as the foetus's lungs and gut); 2. removes carbon dioxide and urea, which pass into the mother's blood to be excreted by her lungs and kidneys (it acts as the foetus's kidneys); 3. passes antibodies from mother to foetus, giving passive natural immunity for the first months after birth (see "Acquired immunity, prevention and treatment"); 4. secretes hormones, taking over the production of progesterone and oestrogen from the corpus luteum (see "Hormones in reproduction"); 5. forms a barrier to many pathogens and to the mother's blood cells.
- [ ] PL6 Harmful substances can cross the placenta: alcohol (can cause foetal alcohol spectrum disorder, with damage to the brain and growth); nicotine and carbon monoxide from smoking (reduce oxygen supply; linked to low birth weight and premature birth); many drugs, legal and illegal (e.g. the thalidomide case in "Science, scientists and society"); and some viruses, e.g. rubella (German measles), which can cause deafness, blindness and heart defects if caught in early pregnancy, and HIV. This is why antenatal advice matters (see "Prenatal and postnatal care").
- [ ] PL7 Amnion: a thin membrane that forms a fluid-filled sac (the amniotic sac) around the embryo. Amniotic fluid cushions the foetus against shocks, keeps its temperature steady, lets it move and develop its muscles and bones, and prevents it sticking to the membranes. Foetal cells in the fluid can be sampled for testing (amniocentesis, in "Prenatal and postnatal care").
- [ ] PL8 Diagram: a foetus in the uterus, showing the uterus wall and endometrium, the placenta attached to the uterus wall, the umbilical cord with its arteries and vein, the amnion and amniotic fluid, and the cervix with its mucus plug. Label each.

Growth and development of the baby (one block, Ordinary and Higher)
- [ ] G1 The baby develops by repeated mitosis (cell number), cell growth, and cell differentiation (cells becoming specialised for different jobs), so that one zygote gives rise to all the tissues and organs. One bullet; uses the terms from "The cell cycle" and "Levels of organisation" without redefining.
- [ ] G2 Length of pregnancy (gestation): about 38 weeks from fertilisation, which is counted in practice as about 40 weeks from the first day of the woman's last period. It is divided into three trimesters of about three months each.
- [ ] G3 Outline of development: in the first two months all the main organs form, and the heart begins beating by the end of the first month; by about 12 weeks the baby is recognisably human with all organs present, though tiny; during the rest of pregnancy the organs grow and mature, the baby moves (felt by the mother from about 16–20 weeks), and fat is laid down under the skin in the last weeks. The lungs are among the last organs to mature, which is why babies born very early have difficulty breathing (see "Prenatal and postnatal care").
- [ ] G4 The first two months are when the baby is most at risk from harmful substances (PL6), because that is when the organs are forming; damage then can cause structural birth defects.

Early development: morula and blastocyst (Higher Level only; one block)
- [ ] E1 [HL] Zygote: the single diploid cell formed at fertilisation.
- [ ] E2 [HL] Morula: after about 3–4 days of division the zygote has become a solid ball of about 16 or more cells, still inside the zona pellucida, looking like a mulberry (the meaning of "morula"). It is still in the fallopian tube or just entering the uterus.
- [ ] E3 [HL] Blastocyst: by about day 5 the cells have rearranged into a hollow ball with a fluid-filled cavity. It has two parts: an outer layer of cells (the trophoblast), which will form the embryo's part of the placenta and its membranes, and a small cluster inside, the inner cell mass, which will form the embryo itself. The blastocyst hatches out of the zona pellucida and is the stage that implants (Z3), trophoblast first.
- [ ] E4 [HL] The cells of the inner cell mass can develop into any type of body cell: they are embryonic stem cells. Their use and the ethical questions it raises belong to "Ethics of genetic technologies". One bullet.
- [ ] E5 [HL] Diagram: the sequence zygote → 2 cells → 4 cells → morula → blastocyst, with the blastocyst in section showing the trophoblast, inner cell mass and fluid-filled cavity; and a side view of the fallopian tube and uterus showing where each stage is (fertilisation in the tube, morula in the tube, blastocyst implanting in the endometrium).

Germ layers, embryo and foetus (Higher Level only; one block)
- [ ] E6 [HL] Germ layers: during the third week after fertilisation the cells of the inner cell mass move and arrange themselves into three layers, the germ layers, each of which gives rise to particular tissues and organs.
- [ ] E7 [HL] What each germ layer forms. Ectoderm (outer layer): the epidermis of the skin, hair and nails, and the whole nervous system (brain, spinal cord, nerves) and parts of the eye. Mesoderm (middle layer): muscles, bones and cartilage, the heart, blood and blood vessels, the kidneys and the reproductive organs. Endoderm (inner layer): the lining of the digestive system and of the breathing system (trachea, bronchi, lungs), and the liver and pancreas. Memory aid: ecto = outer skin and nerves; meso = movement, blood and support; endo = inner linings.
- [ ] E8 [HL] Embryo: the developing individual from implantation (about the second week after fertilisation) until the end of the eighth week, during which all the main organs form (organ formation). By the end of week 8 the embryo is about 3 cm long and has all its major organs. Foetus: from the ninth week until birth; no new organs form, but the existing ones grow, mature and begin to work. The distinction: embryo = organs forming; foetus = organs present and growing.
- [ ] E9 [HL] Summary sequence, one bullet: zygote (day 0, fallopian tube) → morula (days 3–4) → blastocyst (about day 5; implants about days 6–7) → germ layers form (week 3) → embryo (to end of week 8; organs form) → foetus (week 9 to birth; growth and maturation).

Labour and birth (one process block, in stages)
- [ ] L1 Labour is the process by which the baby, then the placenta, is pushed out of the uterus. It usually begins at about 40 weeks of pregnancy, when the baby is normally head down. Hormonal changes start it, and the hormone oxytocin drives the contractions by positive feedback (detail in "Hormones in reproduction"; positive feedback in "Homeostasis as a stimulus-response model").
- [ ] L2 Stage 1 (dilation): the muscle of the uterus wall begins to contract regularly, and the contractions become stronger and more frequent. They pull the cervix open until it is fully dilated, about 10 cm wide. The mucus plug comes away, and the amnion usually bursts, releasing the amniotic fluid ("the waters break"). This is the longest stage, often many hours in a first birth.
- [ ] L3 Stage 2 (delivery, or expulsion): strong contractions of the uterus, helped by the mother pushing with her abdominal muscles, push the baby, usually head first, out through the cervix and the vagina (birth canal). The umbilical cord is then clamped and cut; the stump later dries and falls off, leaving the navel.
- [ ] L4 At birth the baby must start breathing for itself: the first breaths inflate the lungs, and its circulation changes so that blood goes to the lungs for oxygen instead of relying on the placenta. One bullet; no detail of the foetal circulation.
- [ ] L5 Stage 3 (afterbirth): shortly after the birth, further contractions separate the placenta and membranes from the uterus wall and push them out. Contraction of the uterus also closes the blood vessels where the placenta was attached, limiting bleeding.
- [ ] L6 (assumed) A Caesarean section is the delivery of the baby through a surgical cut in the abdomen and uterus, used when a vaginal birth would be unsafe for the mother or baby (e.g. a baby lying the wrong way, or a placenta blocking the cervix). One bullet.

## 3. Traps

- Mark E1–E9 Higher Level only. Don't put the words morula, blastocyst, germ layer, trophoblast or inner cell mass in the Ordinary blocks. Z2–Z3 say "ball of cells".
- Fertilisation happens in the fallopian tube; implantation happens in the endometrium of the uterus. The blastocyst, not the zygote, implants.
- The mother's and foetus's blood never mix; substances are exchanged across the placenta. Don't say "the mother's blood flows into the baby through the cord".
- The umbilical vein carries oxygenated blood to the foetus; the arteries carry deoxygenated blood away from it (PL2).
- The placenta is formed by both the embryo (villi) and the mother (endometrium).
- Embryo vs foetus: the boundary is the end of week 8 (E8). Some sources use "embryo" for all stages before birth; this specification uses both terms, so keep the distinction.
- Pregnancy length: 38 weeks from fertilisation vs 40 weeks from the last period (G2); give both.
- Positive feedback in labour (L1) is defined in "Homeostasis as a stimulus-response model"; the oxytocin mechanism is in "Hormones in reproduction". Don't explain it here beyond one phrase.
- Don't teach the foetal circulation (shunts), gastrulation by name beyond "germ layers form", the chorion/allantois, or the names of the membranes other than the amnion.
- Diagram cues PL8 and E5 only. No other diagram cues.

## 4. What exams ask (topics only)

- State where fertilisation and implantation occur; define implantation.
- Give functions of the placenta, the umbilical cord and the amniotic fluid; explain why the blood of mother and foetus does not mix.
- Name substances that pass from mother to foetus and from foetus to mother; name harmful substances that can cross.
- [HL] Describe the stages from zygote to blastocyst; name the germ layers and an organ or tissue from each; distinguish embryo and foetus.
- Describe the stages of labour and birth.

## 5. Practice questions to write

1. 6 marks: "Give three functions of the placenta." Model answer: three of PL5 (supply of oxygen and nutrients; removal of carbon dioxide and urea; transfer of antibodies; hormone production; barrier).
2. 12 marks, Higher: "Describe the development of a human from the zygote to the end of the embryo stage, referring to the morula, blastocyst and germ layers." Model answer: E1–E3 (with days and the inner cell mass and trophoblast), Z3 implantation, E6–E7 (three layers, one product each), E8 (organs formed by end of week 8).
3. 9 marks: "Describe the three stages of labour." Model answer: L2 (contractions, cervix dilates to about 10 cm, waters break), L3 (pushing, baby delivered through the vagina, cord cut), L5 (placenta expelled); oxytocin named in one phrase (L1).
