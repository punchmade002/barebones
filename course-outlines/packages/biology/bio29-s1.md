## 1. Must cover

The heart's structures and their functions (one structure-and-function block, one bullet per structure; then a diagram bullet)
- [ ] H1 (assumed) Position and size: the heart is a hollow, muscular organ about the size of a clenched fist, in the thorax between the two lungs, behind the sternum, with its pointed lower end (the apex) tilted to the left. It is enclosed in the pericardium, a double membrane with fluid between its layers that reduces friction as the heart beats and prevents it from over-stretching.
- [ ] H2 Atria (singular atrium): the two upper chambers. They receive blood returning to the heart from the veins and pump it the short distance into the ventricles below, so they have thin walls. Each has an ear-like flap on the outside (the auricle).
- [ ] H3 Ventricles: the two lower chambers. They pump blood out of the heart into the arteries, so they have thick muscular walls. The left ventricle's wall is much thicker than the right ventricle's, because the left pumps blood all round the body (the systemic circuit) against high resistance, while the right pumps blood only to the nearby lungs (the pulmonary circuit) at lower pressure, which protects the delicate lung capillaries.
- [ ] H4 Septum: the thick wall of muscle dividing the heart into left and right sides. It keeps oxygenated blood (left side) separate from deoxygenated blood (right side), so the blood pumped to the body carries as much oxygen as possible. Two separate pumps: the right side pumps deoxygenated blood to the lungs; the left side pumps oxygenated blood to the body.
- [ ] H5 Veins entering the heart (thin-walled, bring blood to the atria): the venae cavae (the superior or anterior vena cava from the head and arms; the inferior or posterior vena cava from the rest of the body) bring deoxygenated blood into the right atrium; the pulmonary veins (four in humans) bring oxygenated blood from the lungs into the left atrium.
- [ ] H6 Arteries leaving the heart (thick-walled, carry blood away from the ventricles): the pulmonary artery leaves the right ventricle and divides to take deoxygenated blood to the two lungs; the aorta, the largest artery, leaves the left ventricle and arches over the heart, taking oxygenated blood to the body. The coronary arteries branch from the base of the aorta to supply the heart muscle itself (detail in "Heartbeat").
- [ ] H7 Valves keep blood flowing in one direction only. Atrioventricular (AV) valves lie between each atrium and its ventricle: the tricuspid valve (three flaps) on the right and the bicuspid (mitral) valve (two flaps) on the left. They let blood pass from atrium to ventricle and close when the ventricle contracts, so blood is not pushed back into the atrium.
- [ ] H8 The flaps of the AV valves are attached by tough cords, the chordae tendineae ("heart strings"), to small papillary muscles on the ventricle wall. These hold the flaps and stop them turning inside out (into the atria) under the high pressure when the ventricles contract.
- [ ] H9 Semilunar valves lie at the base of the pulmonary artery and of the aorta. Each has three half-moon-shaped pockets. Blood pushed out by the ventricle flattens the pockets against the artery wall; when the ventricle relaxes, blood falling back fills the pockets, which meet and close the opening, so blood can't flow back into the ventricle.
- [ ] H10 Cardiac muscle tissue: found only in the heart wall. Its cells are striated (striped), branched and joined end to end by intercalated discs, which let electrical impulses pass quickly from cell to cell so the muscle contracts as a coordinated unit. Each cell has one nucleus and very many mitochondria, supplying ATP by aerobic respiration, so the heart beats throughout life without tiring (fatigue). It is myogenic: it contracts by itself without needing a nerve impulse (the pacemaker is in "Heartbeat"). (Cardiac muscle as one of three muscle types is in "The musculoskeletal system as a system for response"; use without restating.)
- [ ] H11 Diagram: a vertical section through the heart viewed from the front (so the heart's left side is on the right of the page): label right and left atria, right and left ventricles (left wall thicker), septum, tricuspid and bicuspid valves, chordae tendineae, papillary muscles, semilunar valves, superior and inferior venae cavae, pulmonary artery, pulmonary veins, aorta; arrows for the direction of blood flow; shade or label the oxygenated (left) and deoxygenated (right) sides.
- [ ] H12 Diagram: cardiac muscle as seen under the microscope (from a prepared slide or micrograph): branched, striated cells with a central nucleus and intercalated discs; label striations, nucleus, intercalated disc, branch.

Planning (one block)
- [ ] P1 Aim: to identify the external and internal structures of a mammalian heart by dissection and relate each structure to its function, supporting conclusions with primary data (observations and measurements) and secondary data (diagrams, models, micrographs, images of the human heart).
- [ ] P2 Hypotheses (examples to write): "The wall of the left ventricle is thicker than the wall of the right ventricle, because it pumps blood around the whole body." "Arteries leaving the heart have thicker walls than veins entering it." "The valves allow blood to flow in one direction only."
- [ ] P3 Variables for the measured part (wall thickness): independent variable: the chamber (left ventricle, right ventricle, atrium); dependent variable: the wall thickness in mm; controlled variables: measure at the same level (about halfway between the base and the apex), with the same ruler or calipers, on the same heart, with the cut made at right angles to the wall. Repeat each measurement three times at different points and calculate the mean; pool class results from several hearts to check repeatability.
- [ ] P4 Secondary data: a labelled diagram or model of the human heart to compare with the dissected heart; micrographs or a prepared slide of cardiac muscle (H10, H12); published images or measurements of human hearts. The dissected heart is a model for the human heart: mammalian hearts have the same four chambers, valves and vessels.
- [ ] P5 Equipment: a fresh or thawed sheep's, pig's or ox's heart (from a butcher, ideally with the vessels left long); dissecting board or tray; scalpel; sharp dissecting scissors; forceps; a blunt seeker or probe; a glass rod or drinking straw; ruler or calipers; wash bottle or beaker of water; disposable gloves; lab coat or apron; safety glasses; paper towels; disinfectant; a labelled diagram of the heart; camera or phone to record observations.

Method (one block, numbered steps)
- [ ] M1 1. Put on gloves, lab coat and safety glasses. Rinse the heart under cold running water to remove blood clots, and dry it with paper towel.
- [ ] M2 2. Identify the front (ventral) surface: it has a coronary artery running diagonally across it and the pulmonary artery leaves from the front. Place the heart with the front facing you, so the heart's left side is on your right. Squeeze both sides: the left ventricle feels firmer and thicker.
- [ ] M3 3. Examine the outside. Identify the atria (soft, ear-like flaps at the top) and ventricles (firm, at the bottom), the apex, the coronary arteries and fat on the surface, and the vessels at the top. Arteries (aorta and pulmonary artery) have thick, rubbery walls and stay open; veins (venae cavae, pulmonary veins) have thin, floppy walls and collapse. Draw and label what you see; photograph it.
- [ ] M4 4. Find which vessel joins which chamber: push a blunt probe or glass rod gently into each vessel to see which chamber it enters (vena cava → right atrium; pulmonary artery ← right ventricle; pulmonary veins → left atrium; aorta ← left ventricle). Butchers often trim the vessels short; use the diagram (secondary data) to identify any that are missing.
- [ ] M5 5. Open the right side: using scissors, cut from the right atrium down the outside wall of the right ventricle towards the apex, and open the chambers. Observe the tricuspid valve (three flaps), the chordae tendineae and the papillary muscles; pull gently on a cord to see how it holds a flap.
- [ ] M6 6. Push the probe from the right ventricle up into the pulmonary artery, then cut along the artery to open it, and observe the three pockets of the semilunar valve at its base.
- [ ] M7 7. Open the left side in the same way: cut through the left atrium and down the left ventricle. Observe the bicuspid valve (two flaps) and its chordae tendineae. Follow the probe from the left ventricle into the aorta, cut it open and observe its semilunar valve; look just above the valve pockets for the small openings of the coronary arteries.
- [ ] M8 8. Valve test (optional): before opening the arteries, hold the heart upright and pour water from a wash bottle into the aorta or pulmonary artery from above. The semilunar pockets fill and close, so the water does not pass down into the ventricle, showing the valves allow one-way flow. Similarly, water poured into a ventricle through the atrium lifts the AV valve flaps up towards a closed position.
- [ ] M9 9. Cut a transverse section across both ventricles about halfway between the base and the apex. Observe the septum between them and measure the wall thickness of the left ventricle, the right ventricle and the septum with a ruler (P3). Compare with the thickness of an atrium wall.
- [ ] M10 10. Examine a prepared slide of cardiac muscle under the light microscope (or a micrograph) and draw it (H12).
- [ ] M11 11. Record observations in a table and on labelled drawings; dispose of the heart and clean up (S3–S4).

Safety and ethics (one block)
- [ ] S1 Scalpels and scissors are very sharp: cut on the dissecting board, away from your body and fingers, and never towards another person; hold the tissue with forceps, not fingers, near the blade; carry sharp instruments pointing down; report any cut at once.
- [ ] S2 Raw meat can carry bacteria (e.g. Salmonella) that cause food poisoning: wear gloves, don't touch your face, never eat or drink in the laboratory, keep the heart on the tray, and wash hands thoroughly with soap afterwards.
- [ ] S3 Disinfect the board, instruments and bench after the dissection; wrap the heart and remove it as directed by the teacher (not left in an open bin).
- [ ] S4 Wear safety glasses, as fluid can spray when cutting or pouring water through vessels.
- [ ] S5 Ethics: use a heart from an animal already killed for food (a by-product of the food industry), so no animal is killed for the dissection; treat the tissue with respect. A student who objects can use a model, a virtual dissection or videos (secondary data), in line with the 3Rs of animal use (see "Science, scientists and society").

Results (one block; primary data, then secondary data)
- [ ] R1 Expected observations: four chambers; atria thin-walled and small, ventricles thick-walled; the left ventricle wall clearly thicker than the right (in measurements, typically several times thicker); the septum thick and muscular; the tricuspid valve with three flaps on the right and the bicuspid valve with two on the left, both held by chordae tendineae to papillary muscles; three-pocket semilunar valves at the base of the aorta and pulmonary artery; the aorta with a much thicker, more elastic wall than the venae cavae; the coronary arteries on the surface and their openings just above the aortic valve.
- [ ] R2 Results table to record: structure; where found; description (colour, texture, thickness, number of flaps); measured wall thickness (mm; three readings and mean); function (from H2–H10).
- [ ] R3 Secondary data: the dissected heart matches the human heart diagram in chambers, valves and vessels; differences seen are due to the butcher's trimming (missing atria or vessels) or species size. Micrographs show the branched, striated cardiac muscle cells and intercalated discs, which can't be seen with the naked eye.

Conclusion (one block, relating structure to function)
- [ ] C1 The left ventricle has the thickest wall because it must generate the highest pressure, to pump blood all round the body; the right ventricle pumps only to the lungs.
- [ ] C2 Atria have thin walls because they pump blood only into the ventricles below them.
- [ ] C3 The valves (AV valves with chordae tendineae, and semilunar valves) allow blood to flow only from atria to ventricles to arteries, never backwards; the water test supports this.
- [ ] C4 The septum separates oxygenated and deoxygenated blood. Arteries have thick walls to withstand high pressure; veins have thin walls (detail in "Blood vessels"). State whether each hypothesis in P2 is supported, citing the measurements and observations.

Sources of error and limitations (one block)
- [ ] E1 Butchers often remove the atria or cut the vessels very short, so some structures may be missing or hard to identify; vessels and valves may be damaged by cutting.
- [ ] E2 Wall-thickness measurements vary with where and at what angle the cut is made, and soft tissue compresses under the ruler; measure at the same level at right angles, repeat three times and use the mean.
- [ ] E3 Heart muscle relaxes and blood clots after death, and freezing and thawing changes texture, so the chambers are not in their living shape.
- [ ] E4 A sheep's or pig's heart is a model: its size and some features differ from a human heart (e.g. the number of pulmonary vein openings), so conclusions about humans need secondary data.
- [ ] E5 One heart per group is a small sample; pooling class data improves repeatability.

## 3. Traps

- Left and right refer to the heart's (the body's) own sides: in a front-view diagram, the left side of the heart is on the right of the page. Students often label them the wrong way round.
- The tricuspid valve is on the right and the bicuspid on the left ("tRIcuspid on the RIght" is a common reminder).
- Arteries carry blood away from the heart and veins towards it; it is not about oxygen. The pulmonary artery carries deoxygenated blood and the pulmonary veins oxygenated blood.
- The chordae tendineae stop the AV valve flaps turning inside out; they do not open or close the valves.
- The atria do not pump blood to the body or lungs; only the ventricles do.
- Cardiac cycle (systole, diastole, its phases, heart sounds) is not on this specification: don't describe the sequence of chamber contraction or valve opening in time, and don't use the words systole or diastole.
- The full route of blood through both circuits belongs to "Circulation"; the pacemaker (SA node), heart-rate control and the coronary arteries and cardiac veins in detail belong to "Heartbeat"; the wall layers of arteries, veins and capillaries to "Blood vessels". H5–H6 name each great vessel and the chamber it joins only.
- Cardiac muscle as one of three muscle types belongs to "The musculoskeletal system as a system for response"; H10 gives its own features only.
- Don't name the scalpel blade size, a specific supplier or a particular textbook procedure.
- The diagram cues H11 and H12 are the only ones wanted. No other diagram cues.

## 4. What exams ask (topics only)

- In a heart dissection: name an instrument used; how the front of the heart is identified; how to tell the left ventricle from the right; how to find the vessels; a safety precaution.
- Give a biological explanation of the septum; explain why the left ventricle wall is thicker than the right.
- Name the valves and give their function; state the role of the chordae tendineae.
- Label a diagram of the heart, including chambers, valves and the vessels entering and leaving.
- Relate the features of cardiac muscle to its function.
