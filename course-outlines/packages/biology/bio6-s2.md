# Package: bio6-s2 — Domains of life

- **loId:** `bio6-s2` · **chapterId:** `bio6` · **syllabusRef:** 1.1.3
- **Syllabus:** "domains of life including archaea, bacteria, eukaryota." Outcome 3: "outline
  the domains of life system based on genetic characteristics." Ordinary and Higher level.
  The outline needs what a domain is, what genetic evidence the system rests on, and the main
  features of each domain.
- **Prepared by:** a higher model, 2026-10-22.

## 1. Must cover

What the domain system is (one block)
- [ ] D1 A domain is the highest (broadest) rank in the classification of living things, above kingdom. All cellular life is placed in three domains: Bacteria, Archaea and Eukarya (the specification calls it eukaryota).
- [ ] D2 The system was developed by the American microbiologist Carl Woese. In 1977, with George Fox, he showed that archaea are a separate group from bacteria; in 1990 Woese and colleagues formally proposed the three domains.
- [ ] D3 Before this, all prokaryotes were placed in one group (the kingdom "Monera" in the five-kingdom system). Archaea look like bacteria under the microscope, so the difference could only be found from their molecules.
- [ ] D4 Genetic basis: Woese compared the base sequences of ribosomal RNA (rRNA), specifically the RNA of the small ribosome subunit. Every cell has ribosomes, the rRNA does the same job in all of them, and its sequence changes very slowly over evolutionary time, so it can be compared across all living things. The more similar two organisms' rRNA sequences, the more closely related they are.
- [ ] D5 The results showed three deep branches of life, not two (prokaryote and eukaryote). Archaea differ from bacteria about as much as either differs from eukaryotes, and in several genetic features archaea are more similar to eukaryotes than to bacteria. So archaea and eukaryotes share a more recent common ancestor with each other than either does with bacteria.
- [ ] D6 Diagram: a three-domain tree: one common ancestor at the base; one branch to Bacteria; a second branch that splits into Archaea and Eukarya. Label the four eukaryotic kingdoms (Protista, Fungi, Plantae, Animalia) on the Eukarya branch.

The three domains (comparison block, one bullet per domain, then a table)
- [ ] B1 Bacteria: prokaryotic (no nucleus or membrane-bound organelles); single circular chromosome without histone proteins; cell walls contain peptidoglycan; found almost everywhere. Includes decomposers, nitrogen-fixing bacteria, photosynthetic cyanobacteria and pathogens (e.g. Escherichia coli, Streptococcus). Many are killed by antibiotics.
- [ ] A1 Archaea: prokaryotic, single-celled, similar in size and shape to bacteria; but cell walls lack peptidoglycan; their membrane lipids have a different chemical structure from those of all other organisms; their DNA is wound around histone-like proteins; their enzymes for copying DNA and making RNA are more like those of eukaryotes; most antibiotics that kill bacteria don't affect them.
- [ ] A2 Archaea habitats: many are extremophiles living in extreme conditions: hot springs and deep-sea hydrothermal vents (thermophiles), very salty water such as salt lakes (halophiles, e.g. Halobacterium), and places with no oxygen where methanogens make methane, e.g. bogs, rice paddies and the digestive systems of cattle. They are also common in ordinary soil and the oceans. No archaea are known to cause disease in humans.
- [ ] E1 Eukarya: all organisms whose cells have a nucleus enclosed by a membrane and membrane-bound organelles (e.g. mitochondria); several linear chromosomes with histones; larger ribosomes. Unicellular or multicellular. Contains the kingdoms Protista, Fungi, Plantae and Animalia.
- [ ] T1 Comparison table, rows: nucleus (absent / absent / present); membrane-bound organelles (no / no / yes); peptidoglycan in cell wall (yes / no / no); membrane lipids (bacterial-type / unique archaeal type / bacterial-type); histones (no / yes, histone-like / yes); ribosome size (70S / 70S / 80S in cytoplasm); affected by common antibiotics (many are / generally not / not); examples.
- [ ] T2 (assumed) The S number (Svedberg unit) measures how fast a particle settles in a centrifuge; a larger number means a larger particle. One bullet.

Why it matters (one bullet group)
- [ ] W1 The domain system shows that the biggest division in life is not plant versus animal, or even prokaryote versus eukaryote, but these three lineages. Animals, plants and fungi are just three small branches of one domain.
- [ ] W2 It is based on genetic evidence, which reflects evolutionary relationships, rather than on appearance; it is an example of how new technology (gene sequencing) changes classification, discussed further in "Classification".

## 3. Traps

- "Monera" is not used in this specification. D3 names it only as the old system it replaced.
- Archaea are not "ancient bacteria" and not a type of bacterium, although the old name was "archaebacteria". Don't call them bacteria.
- Don't say archaea live only in extreme environments.
- Viruses are in no domain (see "Viruses"); prions too.
- Kingdom features (Protista, Fungi, Plantae, Animalia) belong to "Classification". E1 only names them.
- The detailed structure of a bacterial cell belongs to "Microorganisms"; the comparison of prokaryotic and eukaryotic cell structure to "Prokaryotic and eukaryotic cells". B1 and A1 give only the features that separate the domains.
- Diagram cues: only D6. No other diagram cues.

## 4. What exams ask (topics only)

This outcome is new to the 2025 specification; the past papers have no questions on it. Expect:
- Name the three domains and give a feature of each.
- State the type of evidence the domain system is based on, and why rRNA is suitable.
- Give two differences between archaea and bacteria.
- Explain why archaea were once classified with bacteria.
