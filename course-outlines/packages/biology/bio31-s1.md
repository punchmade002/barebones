## 1. Must cover

Ecology and the levels of organisation in ecology (one block)
- [ ] E1 Ecology is the study of the interactions between living organisms, and between organisms and their non-living environment. The word comes from the Greek oikos, "home". (assumed)
- [ ] E2 A population is all the members of one species living in a particular area at the same time, e.g. all the daisies in a field, or all the red squirrels in a woodland. Members of a population can interbreed (species is defined in "Classification"). (assumed)
- [ ] E3 A community is all the populations of all the different species living and interacting in an area at the same time, e.g. all the plants, animals, fungi and microorganisms of a woodland. A community is the living (biotic) part of an ecosystem only. (assumed)
- [ ] E4 The ecological levels, smallest to largest: organism → population → community → ecosystem → biosphere. Each level includes the one before; from ecosystem upward the non-living environment is included. They continue the levels in "Levels of organisation" (cell → tissue → organ → organ system → organism).
- [ ] E5 Diagram: nested boxes or a stepped sequence showing one rabbit (organism), a group of rabbits (population), rabbits with grass, foxes and other species (community), the community with soil, water, air and light (ecosystem), and the Earth's living layer (biosphere); label each level.

The biosphere (one block, or with ecosystems)
- [ ] S1 The biosphere is the part of the Earth where life exists: the lower atmosphere, the land surface and soil, and the water (fresh water and oceans). It is the sum of all the Earth's ecosystems.
- [ ] S2 It is a thin layer compared with the size of the Earth: life is found from the deep ocean floor to a few kilometres up in the air (birds, spores, bacteria carried on the wind). Most life is concentrated in a narrow zone near the surface, where light, liquid water and suitable temperatures are present.
- [ ] S3 The biosphere is a single, closed system for matter (atoms are recycled, not added) but an open system for energy: energy enters as sunlight and leaves as heat. Detail in "Energy and nutrient transfer"; nutrient cycles in "The carbon cycle" and "The nitrogen cycle".

Ecosystems (one block)
- [ ] Y1 An ecosystem is a community of organisms interacting with each other and with their non-living (abiotic) environment in a particular area. It has living (biotic) and non-living (abiotic) parts that affect each other.
- [ ] Y2 Biotic factors are the living parts of the environment and the effects organisms have on each other: food supply, predation, competition, disease, parasites, pollinators, decomposers. Abiotic factors are the non-living physical and chemical conditions: light, temperature, water, oxygen, soil type and pH, minerals, wind, slope, aspect. One bullet each (assumed); measuring them belongs to "Abiotic factors" in "Investigating an Ecosystem"; how they limit populations is in "Carrying capacity and limiting factors".
- [ ] Y3 Ecosystems have no fixed size: a rock pool, a hedgerow, a lake, a raised bog or an ocean can each be treated as an ecosystem. Their boundaries are often gradual (e.g. a woodland edge merging into grassland).
- [ ] Y4 Every ecosystem needs a continual input of energy, almost always sunlight captured by producers in photosynthesis, while its matter is recycled by decomposers. The roles of producers, consumers and decomposers are taught in "Energy and nutrient transfer"; name them only here.
- [ ] Y5 Examples of Irish ecosystems: grassland (the most widespread in Ireland, mostly farmed pasture and meadow); woodland (e.g. the native oak woodlands of Killarney National Park); peatlands (raised bogs in the midlands, blanket bogs in the uplands and the west); freshwater (lakes such as Lough Derg, and rivers); coastal and marine (rocky shores, sandy shores and sand dunes, salt marshes, the open sea). One bullet.

Habitats (one block)
- [ ] H1 A habitat is the place where an organism lives, which provides the food, shelter and conditions it needs, e.g. a woodlouse's habitat is damp leaf litter; a limpet's is the rocky shore; a frog's is a pond and the damp vegetation around it.
- [ ] H2 A microhabitat is a small area within a habitat with its own conditions, e.g. the underside of a stone on a rocky shore, a rock pool, a rotting log, or the bark of a tree. Conditions there (shade, moisture, temperature) can differ greatly from the surroundings. (assumed)
- [ ] H3 One ecosystem contains many habitats: a woodland contains the canopy, the trunk, the leaf litter and the soil, each with different species. A habitat is the organism's "address"; its role there is its niche, taught in "The niche". One phrase only.

Biodiversity (one block)
- [ ] B1 Biodiversity (biological diversity) is the variety of life in an area, or on Earth, at all its levels. It is usually described at three levels: genetic diversity, species diversity and ecosystem (habitat) diversity.
- [ ] B2 Genetic diversity is the variety of alleles (genes) within a species or population, e.g. the different breeds of cattle, or the variation in disease resistance among wild ash trees. It allows a population to adapt by natural selection when conditions change; low genetic diversity left the Irish potato crop of the 1840s vulnerable to blight (detail in "Variation").
- [ ] B3 Species diversity is the variety of species in an area. It has two parts: species richness (the number of different species present) and species evenness (how evenly the individuals are spread among those species). Two areas with the same species can differ in diversity if one is dominated by a single species. Measuring it by the Simpson's Diversity Index is in "Simpson's Diversity Index" (Higher Level).
- [ ] B4 Ecosystem (habitat) diversity is the variety of different ecosystems and habitats in a region, e.g. Ireland's bogs, woodlands, grasslands, lakes, rivers and coasts. A region with many habitats supports more species.
- [ ] B5 Biodiversity is not spread evenly: it is generally higher in warm, wet, stable environments (tropical rainforests and coral reefs are the most species-rich ecosystems) and lower in cold or extreme ones. Ireland has fewer species than Britain or mainland Europe, partly because it became an island after the last ice age, before many species had recolonised it from the south.
- [ ] B6 Why biodiversity matters, its loss, and conservation are taught in "Biodiversity loss and conservation"; here one bullet only: biodiversity provides food, medicines and materials, supports processes such as pollination and nutrient cycling, and makes ecosystems more stable.

## 3. Traps

- B5: the writer must not name species "missing" from Ireland. Use only: Ireland has fewer species than Britain and mainland Europe, partly because it became an island after the last ice age before many species recolonised it.
- Habitat is a place; ecosystem includes the community and its abiotic environment; community excludes the abiotic environment. Students often call an ecosystem a habitat.
- A population is one species; a community is many species. Don't say "a population of animals".
- Biodiversity is more than the number of species: include genetic and ecosystem diversity, and evenness as well as richness.
- Do not define the niche here (belongs to "The niche"), nor producers, consumers, trophic levels or food chains (belong to "Energy and nutrient transfer").
- Sampling and measuring biotic and abiotic factors belong to "Investigating an Ecosystem"; nutrient cycles to "Nutrient Cycling". Name them, don't teach them.
- Causes of biodiversity loss, Irish case studies and conservation belong to "Biodiversity loss and conservation".
- The only diagram cue is E5. No other diagram cues.

## 4. What exams ask (topics only)

- Define ecology, biosphere, ecosystem, habitat, population and community.
- Distinguish between a habitat and an ecosystem, or a population and a community.
- Explain what is meant by biodiversity; name its three levels.
- Give examples of biotic and abiotic factors in a named ecosystem.
