## 1. Must cover

The immune system and its two parts (one block)
- [ ] I1 Immunity is the body's ability to resist infection by pathogens. The immune system consists of physical and chemical barriers, white blood cells (made from stem cells in the bone marrow), the lymphatic system (lymph nodes, spleen, thymus) and defence proteins in the blood such as antibodies and complement.
- [ ] I2 The immune system works by telling "self" from "non-self": the body's own cells carry their own marker molecules on their surface, which the immune system normally ignores; foreign cells and molecules are attacked.
- [ ] I3 Innate immunity (non-specific) is present from birth, acts against any pathogen in the same way, and acts within minutes to hours. Adaptive immunity (also called acquired or specific immunity) develops during life after exposure to a particular pathogen, acts against that pathogen only, takes days to develop the first time, and remembers it. Innate defences are found in all animals and in plants; adaptive immunity with antibodies and lymphocytes is found only in vertebrates.

Innate response, first line: barriers (one block)
- [ ] B1 Skin: a tough outer layer of dead, keratin-filled cells that pathogens can't easily cross; oil (sebum) from glands makes the surface slightly acidic, which slows bacterial growth. Breaks in the skin are sealed by blood clotting, which stops blood loss and blocks pathogen entry.
- [ ] B2 Mucous membranes line the breathing, digestive, urinary and reproductive tracts. Their sticky mucus traps microorganisms and dust; in the trachea and bronchi, cilia beat the mucus up to the throat, where it is swallowed or coughed out. Coughing and sneezing are reflexes that expel irritants.
- [ ] B3 Chemical barriers: hydrochloric acid in the stomach (about pH 2) kills most microorganisms swallowed with food; lysozyme, an enzyme in tears, saliva and mucus, breaks down bacterial cell walls; the acidic conditions in the vagina inhibit many pathogens.
- [ ] B4 Harmless microorganisms living on the skin and in the gut and vagina (the normal microbiota) compete with pathogens for space and nutrients (detail in "The microbiome"; one sentence here).

Innate response, second line: when pathogens get in (one block)
- [ ] N1 Phagocytosis: phagocytes are white blood cells that engulf and digest pathogens. Neutrophils are the most numerous white blood cells and the first to arrive; macrophages (formed from monocytes, see "White blood cells") are larger and longer-lived. Stages: 1. the phagocyte is attracted by chemicals released by pathogens and damaged cells; 2. it binds the pathogen; 3. it surrounds it with extensions of its membrane and takes it into a vesicle; 4. digestive enzymes are released into the vesicle and break the pathogen down; 5. harmless products are absorbed or released.
- [ ] N2 Diagram: the stages of phagocytosis in four sketches: phagocyte approaching a bacterium; membrane extensions surrounding it; bacterium enclosed in a vesicle; bacterium being digested. Label phagocyte, nucleus, bacterium, vesicle, digestive enzymes.
- [ ] N3 Inflammation: damaged cells and mast cells release histamine and other chemicals, which widen local blood vessels and make capillaries leakier. More blood flow brings phagocytes and defence proteins, and fluid leaks into the tissue. Result: redness, heat, swelling and pain at the site. Pus is a mixture of dead phagocytes, pathogens and tissue fluid.
- [ ] N4 Fever: chemicals released by white blood cells reset the temperature set point in the hypothalamus upwards (see "Homeostasis as a stimulus-response model"), raising body temperature. A higher temperature slows the reproduction of many pathogens and speeds up the reactions of the immune system.
- [ ] N5 Defence proteins: complement, a group of blood proteins, coats pathogens so phagocytes engulf them more easily and can punch holes in bacterial membranes, bursting them; interferons are proteins released by virus-infected cells that make neighbouring cells resist virus replication. Natural killer cells also act in the innate response (see "White blood cells", Higher Level; one phrase here).
- [ ] N6 Plants have innate defences only: physical barriers (cuticle, bark, cell walls), protective chemicals, and the death of infected cells to wall off a pathogen (detail of plant protective adaptations in "Structures and systems for response"). One bullet.

Adaptive response: antigens and antibodies (one block)
- [ ] A1 An antigen is a molecule, usually a protein or polysaccharide on the surface of a pathogen or a toxin it releases, that the immune system recognises as foreign and that triggers a specific immune response, including antibody production. Each pathogen carries several antigens; its own cells' markers are "self" antigens that are normally tolerated.
- [ ] A2 An antibody (immunoglobulin) is a Y-shaped protein made by plasma cells (derived from B lymphocytes) that binds to one specific antigen. It has four polypeptide chains (two long heavy chains and two short light chains) held together by disulfide bonds. The tips of the two arms are the variable regions, which differ between antibodies and form two identical antigen-binding sites; the rest is the constant region, the same in all antibodies of a class.
- [ ] A3 Specificity: the shape of the antigen-binding site is complementary to the shape of one antigen, so each antibody binds only that antigen, like a key in a lock (compare enzyme specificity in "Enzyme structure and specificity").
- [ ] A4 Diagram: an antibody as a Y shape, labelled heavy chain, light chain, variable region, antigen-binding site (two), constant region, disulfide bonds, with an antigen fitting one binding site.
- [ ] A5 How antibodies destroy or disable pathogens (one bullet each): agglutination: because each antibody has two binding sites, antibodies link pathogens together into clumps that can't spread and are easily engulfed; neutralisation: antibodies cover the parts of viruses that attach to cells, or bind to toxins (antitoxins), so they can't act; opsonisation: antibodies coat pathogens, marking them for phagocytes, which have receptors for the antibody's constant region; activating complement, which bursts bacterial cells.

Adaptive response: how antibodies are produced and remembered (one block)
- [ ] L1 Lymphocytes are the white blood cells of adaptive immunity. There are two main kinds: B lymphocytes (B cells), which produce antibodies (the antibody-mediated or humoral response, acting on pathogens and toxins in blood and tissue fluid), and T lymphocytes (T cells), which act directly on infected body cells (the cell-mediated response; Higher Level block below).
- [ ] L2 Clonal selection: the body has millions of different B cells, each with one type of antibody fixed in its membrane as a receptor, made before any infection. When a pathogen enters, only the B cells whose receptors fit its antigen bind to it; these are "selected" and, with chemical signals from helper T cells, divide rapidly by mitosis to form a clone of identical cells.
- [ ] L3 The clone differentiates into plasma cells and memory B cells. Plasma cells secrete large amounts of the matching antibody into the blood (thousands of molecules per second) for a few days, then die. Memory cells stay in the body for years, ready to respond to the same antigen.
- [ ] L4 Primary response (first exposure): there is a delay of several days (often about a week) while the matching B cells are selected and cloned, so antibody levels rise slowly and stay fairly low; the person may become ill during this time.
- [ ] L5 Secondary response (later exposure to the same antigen): memory cells recognise the antigen at once and quickly produce many plasma cells, so antibodies appear within a few days, reach a much higher level and last longer. The pathogen is usually destroyed before symptoms appear: the person is immune. This is the basis of vaccination (see "Acquired immunity, prevention and treatment").
- [ ] L6 Diagram: a graph of antibody concentration (y-axis) against time in days (x-axis), with a first exposure giving a small, slow primary peak and a second exposure giving a faster, much taller, longer secondary peak; label both exposures, the lag phase, and the primary and secondary responses.

[HL] The cell-mediated immune response (one block, heading marked " (Higher Level only)")
- [ ] CM1 The cell-mediated response is the part of adaptive immunity carried out by T lymphocytes acting directly on cells, not by antibodies. It is needed because antibodies can't reach pathogens once they are inside body cells (viruses and some bacteria, e.g. the TB bacterium). It also destroys cancer cells and cells of transplanted organs, which is why transplant patients take drugs that suppress the immune system.
- [ ] CM2 Antigen presentation: an infected body cell displays fragments of the pathogen's antigens on its surface, held in its self-marker proteins (MHC proteins). Macrophages and other antigen-presenting cells that have engulfed a pathogen display its antigens in the same way. This is how T cells, which can't recognise free antigens, detect infection.
- [ ] CM3 Activation of helper T cells: a helper T cell whose receptor fits the displayed antigen binds to the antigen-presenting cell and is activated. It divides to form a clone of helper T cells (clonal selection, as for B cells).
- [ ] CM4 Helper T cells coordinate the response by releasing chemical signals (cytokines): these stimulate the matching killer T cells to divide; stimulate the matching B cells to divide into plasma cells and memory cells (L2–L3: B cells need this help to respond fully); and attract and activate macrophages. So helper T cells link the cell-mediated and antibody responses.
- [ ] CM5 Killer T cells (cytotoxic T cells) of the clone bind to body cells displaying the antigen and kill them, destroying the pathogens inside before they can multiply (how they kill is in "White blood cells").
- [ ] CM6 When the infection is cleared, suppressor T cells switch off the response, stopping the activity of killer T cells and B cells so healthy tissue isn't damaged. Memory T cells remain, so a second infection triggers a faster, larger cell-mediated response.
- [ ] CM7 Role of B lymphocytes in the cell-mediated response: B cells act as antigen-presenting cells (they take in the antigen they bind and display it to helper T cells), and the antibodies their plasma cells release mark infected cells and pathogens for destruction. Model: a flow diagram, "pathogen → antigen presented by macrophage or infected cell → helper T cell activated and cloned → cytokines → killer T cells cloned → infected cells killed; B cells cloned → plasma cells → antibodies; memory T and B cells formed; suppressor T cells end response". Diagram: draw this flow diagram.

Distinguishing innate and acquired immunity (one comparison block, paired points)
- [ ] D1 Paired points, innate vs acquired (adaptive): present from birth vs develops after exposure to a pathogen; non-specific (same response to every pathogen) vs specific (each response against one antigen); response the same each time vs memory, so a second response is faster and stronger; acts within minutes to hours vs takes days on first exposure; barriers, phagocytes (neutrophils, macrophages), inflammation, fever, complement, interferons, natural killer cells vs B and T lymphocytes and antibodies; in all animals and plants vs in vertebrates only.
- [ ] D2 The two systems work together: macrophages of the innate system present antigens to start the adaptive response, and antibodies of the adaptive system make phagocytosis and complement more effective (A5).

## 3. Traps

- Mark only the cell-mediated block (CM1–CM7) Higher Level only. Antigens, antibodies, clonal selection, plasma and memory cells and primary/secondary responses (A1–A5, L1–L6) are Ordinary and Higher level.
- Antibodies are made by plasma cells (derived from B cells), not by T cells or phagocytes. An antigen is the foreign molecule; an antibody is the body's protein. Students often swap them.
- "Antibiotic" is not "antibody": antibiotics are drugs that kill bacteria.
- B cells do not mature in the thymus; T cells do. Detail of where each cell matures, their appearance, surface markers (CD4, CD8), perforin, natural killer cells and the comparison table of cell roles belong to "White blood cells". Don't restate them here; CM5 and N5 refer there.
- Ways of acquiring immunity (natural/artificial, active/passive), vaccines, herd immunity and drugs belong to "Acquired immunity, prevention and treatment". L5 mentions vaccination in one phrase.
- HIV's attack on helper T cells belongs to "Viruses: replication"; autoimmune disease to "Emerging diseases and society"; the microbiota to "The microbiome"; blood clotting and white blood cell numbers to "Blood" (The Circulatory System and Blood).
- Don't name lysosomes (not on the specification): say "digestive enzymes in vesicles".
- Don't teach antibody classes (IgG, IgA etc.) here; "Acquired immunity, prevention and treatment" names IgG and IgA for passive immunity only. Don't teach MHC classes, interleukin names or the complement pathways.
- Diagram cues: N2, A4, L6 and the CM7 flow diagram only. No other diagram cues.
