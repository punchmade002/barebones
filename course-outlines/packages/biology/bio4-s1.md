# Package: bio4-s1 — Models

- **loId:** `bio4-s1` · **chapterId:** `bio4` · **syllabusRef:** U4.1
- **Syllabus:** "generating and using models; the evolving nature of models." Outcome U4.1:
  "appreciate that models are simplified representations of complex systems or phenomena with
  underlying assumptions; can be modified as more data becomes available from the
  system/phenomenon; can predict the behaviour of a system/phenomenon." The specification's
  footnote defines models as "representations of ideas, structures, processes, or systems
  through a variety of means such as words, diagrams, equations, physical models or
  simulations". Its glossary defines the verb "model": represent an idea, structure, process or
  system in these ways; "use models to describe, explain, make predictions and solve problems,
  recognising that all models have limitations". The specification also says "modelling is at
  the heart of what biologists do". Ordinary and Higher level.
- **Prepared by:** a higher model, 2026-10-22. Many outcomes in later chapters use the verb
  "model" (e.g. model the carbon cycle, model a monohybrid cross), so this subsection teaches
  what a model is and how to judge one, with worked examples; the biology of each example is
  taught in its own chapter.

## 1. Must cover

What a model is (one block)
- [ ] D1 A model is a simplified representation of an idea, structure, process or system, used to describe, explain or predict how it behaves.
- [ ] D2 Models are needed because real biological systems are too small (molecules, cells), too large (ecosystems, the biosphere), too slow (evolution), too fast (nerve impulses), too complex (the human body), or too dangerous or unethical to study directly.
- [ ] D3 Every model is simplified: it keeps the features that matter for its purpose and leaves out others. A model is judged by how useful it is for its purpose, not by whether it is a perfect copy; no model is complete.
- [ ] D4 Every model rests on assumptions: things taken to be true so the model can work (e.g. a Punnett square assumes fertilisation is random). If an assumption doesn't hold, the model's predictions fail.
- [ ] D5 Models can be modified as more data become available: when new observations don't fit, the model is changed or replaced (worked examples 1 and 2 below).
- [ ] D6 Models can predict: a good model predicts how the system will behave in new conditions, and testing those predictions is how models are checked (worked example 3).

Types of model, each with a biological example (one block)
- [ ] TY1 Words (verbal or descriptive models): e.g. a written description of natural selection (variation, competition, survival of the better adapted, inheritance).
- [ ] TY2 Diagrams: e.g. a food web, a diagram of the carbon cycle or nitrogen cycle, a labelled diagram of a cell, a flowchart of a negative-feedback loop, a pyramid of numbers.
- [ ] TY3 Equations: e.g. the balanced equation for photosynthesis, 6CO₂ + 6H₂O → C₆H₁₂O₆ + 6O₂, which models what goes in and comes out but not the many steps in between; the reproduction number R₀ in models of disease spread.
- [ ] TY4 Physical (concrete) models: e.g. Watson and Crick's model of DNA; a bell-jar model of the lungs; a plastic model of the heart; ball-and-stick models of molecules.
- [ ] TY5 Simulations (often computer models): e.g. computer models of how an infectious disease spreads through a population, climate models, and computer models of protein folding; simulations can run "what if" scenarios quickly and safely.
- [ ] TY6 (assumed) Model organisms are a different use of the word: species studied in detail because they are easy to keep and breed and share much biology with others, e.g. the bacterium Escherichia coli, yeast, the fruit fly Drosophila melanogaster, the plant Arabidopsis thaliana, and the house mouse. Assumption: what is true of them is true of other organisms; limitation: results don't always transfer to humans.

How models are generated and used (one block)
- [ ] G1 Generating a model: 1. observe the system and collect data; 2. identify its key parts and how they interact; 3. represent them simply, often by analogy with something familiar (e.g. "lock and key"); 4. use the model to make predictions; 5. test the predictions against new data; 6. keep, modify or replace the model.
- [ ] G2 Evaluating a model (questions to ask): what does it show, and for what purpose? What does it leave out? What assumptions does it make? Does it fit the data? Can it explain and predict? How could it be improved? Answers about a model should always name at least one strength and one limitation.
- [ ] G3 A model is not the same as a theory: a theory is a broad, well-tested explanation; a model is a representation that may be used to explain part of a theory or to make its predictions concrete (theories are in "The nature of scientific knowledge"). Two different models of the same thing can both be useful for different purposes.

Worked example 1, enzyme action: lock and key → induced fit (one block; the Higher Level detail is in "Enzyme structure and specificity")
- [ ] E1 Lock-and-key model (proposed by Emil Fischer in 1894): the enzyme's active site has a rigid shape that exactly fits its substrate, as only one key fits a lock. It explains why enzymes are specific (each catalyses one reaction).
- [ ] E2 Its assumption is that the active site is rigid. New data did not fit: some enzymes act on several similar substrates, and later structural studies (e.g. X-ray crystallography) showed that some enzymes change shape when the substrate binds; e.g. the enzyme hexokinase closes around glucose.
- [ ] E3 Induced-fit model (proposed by Daniel Koshland in 1958): the active site is flexible; when the substrate enters, the active site changes shape slightly to mould tightly around it, like a glove around a hand, which strains the substrate's bonds and helps the reaction. It replaced lock and key as the better model, though lock and key is still useful as a simple first explanation of specificity.
- [ ] E4 Diagram: two rows of three stages each: lock and key (enzyme with rigid active site + substrate → enzyme–substrate complex → products released, enzyme unchanged) and induced fit (the same, with the active site changing shape as the substrate binds). Label active site, substrate, enzyme–substrate complex, products.

Worked example 2, the cell membrane: sandwich model → fluid mosaic model (one block)
- [ ] F1 Earlier model (Hugh Davson and James Danielli, 1935): a phospholipid bilayer sandwiched between two continuous layers of protein. Assumption: proteins form uniform layers on each surface.
- [ ] F2 New data that did not fit: membrane proteins vary greatly in type and amount between membranes; freeze-fracture electron microscopy, which splits a membrane down the middle of the bilayer, showed proteins embedded in and passing through the bilayer; and in 1970 Larry Frye and Michael Edidin fused a mouse cell and a human cell with differently labelled membrane proteins and saw the proteins mix over the surface of the combined cell, showing that membrane components move.
- [ ] F3 Fluid mosaic model (Seymour Singer and Garth Nicolson, 1972): a phospholipid bilayer, with the hydrophilic (water-attracting) phosphate heads facing the watery fluid inside and outside the cell and the hydrophobic (water-repelling) fatty-acid tails facing inwards; proteins are scattered in it like tiles in a mosaic, some on the surface and some passing right through (channel and carrier proteins); the phospholipids and many proteins move sideways, so the membrane is fluid. Animal cell membranes also contain cholesterol, and many proteins and lipids carry carbohydrate chains (glycoproteins and glycolipids) on the outer surface.
- [ ] F4 What it explains and predicts: why small non-polar molecules such as O₂ and CO₂ diffuse straight through the bilayer while ions and glucose need protein channels or carriers; why membranes can fuse and re-seal (e.g. in endocytosis). (Transport is taught in "Diffusion, osmosis and active transport".)
- [ ] F5 Limitations: it is still being refined; membranes are not uniformly fluid, some proteins are held in place by the cytoskeleton, membranes are more crowded with protein than the usual diagram shows, and small regions of the membrane (lipid rafts) differ in composition.
- [ ] F6 Diagram: a section of membrane showing the phospholipid bilayer (heads and tails labelled hydrophilic and hydrophobic), a channel protein passing through, a surface protein, a glycoprotein with its carbohydrate chain on the outside, and cholesterol.

Worked example 3, the DNA double helix: a model that predicted (one block; DNA structure itself is in "Nucleotides, DNA and RNA")
- [ ] N1 In 1953 James Watson and Francis Crick, at the University of Cambridge, built a physical model of DNA from metal plates and rods. They used X-ray diffraction images of DNA made by Rosalind Franklin and Raymond Gosling (including the image known as Photo 51) at King's College London, data from Maurice Wilkins, and Erwin Chargaff's finding that in DNA the amount of adenine equals that of thymine and the amount of cytosine equals that of guanine.
- [ ] N2 The model: two strands wound into a double helix, with a sugar–phosphate backbone on the outside and bases paired inside, adenine with thymine and cytosine with guanine. It explained Chargaff's data and the X-ray pattern.
- [ ] N3 Prediction: complementary base pairing suggested that each strand could act as a template for a new strand, so DNA could copy itself (Watson and Crick noted this in their 1953 paper). In 1958 Matthew Meselson and Franklin Stahl showed experimentally that DNA replication is semi-conservative, as the model predicted. (Replication is taught in "DNA replication and mitosis in the cell cycle".)
- [ ] N4 Limitations: the model shows one short, straight, static section; in cells DNA is extremely long, is wound around histone proteins and coiled into chromosomes, and moves and unwinds; atoms are shown as solid shapes.
- [ ] N5 Watson, Crick and Wilkins received the Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine in 1962. Franklin had died in 1958, and the prize is not awarded after death. Her contribution is now widely recognised; the case is also an example of how credit is shared in science.

Further models in the course (one block; each model with one assumption or limitation; one bullet each)
- [ ] X1 Bell-jar model of breathing: a bell jar (the chest), balloons (lungs), a rubber sheet (diaphragm), a tube (trachea); pulling the sheet down increases the volume in the jar, lowering the pressure, so air enters the balloons. Limitations: the jar's walls are rigid, whereas the ribs move; there are no intercostal muscles; the rubber sheet is pulled down, whereas the diaphragm contracts and flattens; the balloons have no alveoli (see "Gas exchange and the breathing mechanism").
- [ ] X2 Punnett square: predicts the expected ratios of offspring genotypes and phenotypes. Assumptions: one gene controls the trait; each gamete carries one allele; fertilisation is random; all offspring survive. Limitations: ratios are only probabilities, so small families often differ; doesn't apply to traits controlled by many genes or the environment (see "Monohybrid crosses" and "Benefits and limitations of Mendelian genetics").
- [ ] X3 Food chains, food webs and pyramids of numbers: model feeding relationships and energy flow. Limitations: real feeding relationships are more complex; pyramids of numbers can be inverted (e.g. many insects on one tree) (see "Energy and nutrient transfer" and "Pyramids of numbers").
- [ ] X4 Carbon and nitrogen cycle diagrams: model how matter moves between living things and the environment; they show processes but not their rates or amounts (see "The carbon cycle" and "The nitrogen cycle").
- [ ] X5 Disease-spread models using R₀ (the average number of people one infected person infects in a population with no immunity): predict that an outbreak grows if R₀ is above 1 and dies out if below 1, and how many people must be immune to stop spread. Assumptions: people mix randomly and R₀ stays constant; in reality behaviour, vaccination, seasons and new variants change it, so predictions are updated as new data come in (see "Emergence and spread of infectious disease").
- [ ] X6 Homeostasis as a stimulus–response model, with negative feedback (see "Homeostasis as a stimulus-response model").

## 3. Traps

- Induced fit is examined at Higher Level in "Enzyme structure and specificity". Here E1–E4 are an Ordinary-level illustration of how a model changes; don't add 3D-structure, activation-energy or specificity detail beyond these items, and don't mark the block Higher Level.
- Don't say Franklin was "ignored" or that Watson and Crick "stole" her work; give only the facts in N1 and N5.
- Don't say the fluid mosaic model is "proven" or final (F5).
- Don't call a Punnett square "the law"; Mendel's laws are separate (in "Mendel's Laws").
- The bell-jar model's biology (breathing mechanism) belongs to "Gas exchange and the breathing mechanism"; X1 is the model's parts and limitations only.
- Writing a scientific model of one's own investigation results (graphs, lines of best fit) belongs to "Producing and analysing data".
- Diagram cues: E4 and F6 only. No other diagram cues.

## 4. What exams ask (topics only)

No legacy questions on models as such. Expect:
- What is a model? Give two features of models (simplified, assumptions, can be modified, can predict).
- Describe a named model and give one strength and one limitation.
- Explain how a named model was modified as new evidence became available (lock and key to induced fit; sandwich to fluid mosaic).
- Given an unfamiliar model (a diagram or simulation), identify its assumptions and limitations.
