# Package: bio2-s6 — Communicating results

- **loId:** `bio2-s6` · **chapterId:** `bio2` · **syllabusRef:** U2.6
- **Syllabus:** students learn about "communicating results to a range of audiences". Outcome 6:
  "organise and communicate their research and investigative findings in a variety of ways fit
  for purpose and audience, using relevant scientific terminology and representations."
  Ordinary and Higher level (no Higher Level only blocks).
- **Prepared by:** a higher model, 2026-10-22. The spec glossary defines "organise" as
  "arrange; to systematise or methodise". The spec's Digital technology section lists using
  digital technology to "collect, record, analyse and display data" and to "develop and improve
  investigative research, communication, and report writing skills"; include this in I4. How to
  draw tables and graphs is in "Producing and analysing data"; here they are named only as
  representations to choose between.

## 1. Must cover

The scientific report (one block, sections in order)
- [ ] W1 A report of an investigation is organised in standard sections so that a reader can follow it and repeat the work: title; aim or question; hypothesis and prediction; background (the biological theory behind the hypothesis, with sources); equipment and materials; method; risk assessment and ethical considerations; results (tables and graphs); analysis (calculations, patterns, anomalies); conclusion; evaluation (limitations, sources of error, improvements); references.
- [ ] W2 The method is written as numbered steps with exact quantities, concentrations, times and temperatures, usually in the past tense and impersonal ("10 cm³ of hydrogen peroxide was added"), with enough detail for someone else to reproduce it.
- [ ] W3 A scientific paper written for other scientists begins with an abstract: a short summary of the aim, method, main results and conclusion, so readers can decide quickly whether it is relevant.
- [ ] W4 Results sections present data; they don't explain them. Explanation belongs in the analysis and conclusion.

Fit for purpose and audience (one comparison block)
- [ ] A1 The same findings are communicated differently depending on who will read them and why: the audience's background knowledge, what they need to know, and what they will do with it.
- [ ] A2 Scientists and examiners: full report or paper; technical terms used precisely; full data, methods and limitations; references.
- [ ] A3 The general public: plain language with technical terms explained or avoided; the main finding and why it matters first; simple graphs or infographics; uncertainty stated honestly without exaggeration. Formats: poster, article, presentation, video, infographic, social media post.
- [ ] A4 Decision-makers (e.g. a school board, a council, a health service): a short summary of the evidence, its reliability and its implications, with recommendations.
- [ ] A5 A poster or presentation is selective: a clear title, the question, a brief method, one or two key graphs, the conclusion, with little text. A full report includes everything.

Scientific terminology (one block)
- [ ] V1 Use precise scientific terms with their correct meanings: e.g. "independent variable", "control", "precise", "accurate", "reliable", "anomalous" (defined in this chapter's other subsections), and biological terms such as "denatured" rather than "killed" for an enzyme, "osmosis" rather than "soaking up water".
- [ ] V2 Use SI units and their symbols consistently: g and kg, cm³ and dm³, °C, s and min, mm and µm; write a unit with every quantity.
- [ ] V3 Write scientific names of organisms in binomial form, genus with a capital letter and species with a lower-case letter, in italics in print or underlined by hand, e.g. Homo sapiens.
- [ ] V4 Avoid vague words ("a lot", "it worked", "the results were good"); state what changed, by how much and in which direction.

Representations (one block)
- [ ] P1 Choose the representation that best shows the point to the audience: a table to give exact values; a graph to show a trend or comparison (graph types in "Producing and analysing data"); a labelled diagram to show apparatus or a structure; a flow chart to show the steps of a method or process; a photograph as a record of a result (e.g. a colour change, colonies on an agar plate).
- [ ] P2 A diagram of apparatus is drawn in two dimensions as a cross-section, in pencil, with ruled label lines that touch the part labelled and don't cross. Biological drawings use clear single lines, no shading, labels to the side, and a title with magnification or scale where relevant.
- [ ] P3 Every table, graph and figure has a title or caption and is referred to in the text.

Integrity in communicating (one block)
- [ ] I1 Report all results honestly, including anomalies and results that don't support the hypothesis, and state the limitations.
- [ ] I2 Acknowledge every source: cite it in the text and give a reference list. A reference gives at least the author or organisation, the year, the title and where it was published (for a website, the web address and the date it was accessed).
- [ ] I3 Don't exaggerate findings: a small school investigation can't show something is true in general, and communication to the public should not overstate certainty.
- [ ] I4 Digital tools support communication: spreadsheets to record, process and graph data; presentation and word-processing software for reports and posters; data loggers to collect and display data.

## 3. Traps

- Don't repeat the rules for drawing tables and graphs; they are in "Producing and analysing data".
- Peer review and publication as part of how science is checked belong to "Science as a global enterprise"; don't describe peer review here.
- Evaluating media reports of science belongs to "Evaluating evidence"; evaluating sources for bias belongs to "Recognising bias and evaluating sources".
- Plagiarism is defined in "Designing and conducting investigations"; I2 gives how to reference only.
- The report format for the assessment component is prescribed by the SEC (see "The Biology in Practice Investigation"); W1 is a general scientific report structure, not the SEC's format.
- Diagram cues: none beyond the drawing conventions in P2. No other diagram cues.

## 4. What exams ask (topics only)

- Why scientists publish their results (to share knowledge and allow others to check them; detail in "Science as a global enterprise").
- New to this specification, expect: choose and justify a way to present given data to a named audience; rewrite a technical finding for the public; identify the sections of a scientific report.

## 5. Practice questions to write

1. 6 marks: "State three ways a report of an investigation for other scientists differs from a poster presenting the same findings to the public." Model answer: three pairs from A2, A3 and A5 (technical terms vs plain language; full data and method vs key graph; references and limitations vs main message).
2. 9 marks: "Explain why the method section of a scientific report must be written in detail, and list the information it should include." Model answer: W2 (so others can repeat it and check reproducibility), quantities, concentrations, times, temperatures, numbered steps, replicates.
3. 9 marks: "Describe three ways a student can show integrity when communicating the results of an investigation." Model answer: I1, I2, I3.
