# Package: bio7-s4 — Investigation: plant and animal cells

- **loId:** `bio7-s4` · **chapterId:** `bio7` · **syllabusRef:** 1.3.3
- **Syllabus:** Outcome 3: "investigate, using primary data gathered with a light microscope
  and secondary data gathered from scanning electron microscope imagery, the structures and
  organelles of animal and plant cells and relate them to their functions." Ordinary and
  Higher level. Write it as one or more Investigation blocks (NOTES_STANDARD block type):
  onion epidermis (plant) and cheek cells (animal) as the primary data, SEM images as the
  secondary data.
- **Prepared by:** a higher model, 2026-10-22. How to use the microscope and the general
  wet-mount technique (coverslip at 45°, why stain) are in "Microscopy"; refer to them and
  don't repeat the reasons.

## 1. Must cover

Aim, hypothesis and variables (one block)
- [ ] A1 Aim: to observe and compare the structures of plant cells (onion epidermis) and animal cells (human cheek cells) using a light microscope, and to use scanning electron microscope (SEM) images to see structures the light microscope can't show.
- [ ] A2 Hypothesis: plant cells will show a cell wall and a large vacuole, which animal cells lack; both will show a cell membrane, cytoplasm and nucleus.
- [ ] A3 Independent variable: the type of cell (plant or animal). Dependent variable: the structures observed (present or absent, shape, arrangement). Controlled variables: the same microscope and total magnification (×100 and ×400), the same light setting, a fresh thin specimen one cell layer thick, the same volume of stain and staining time.
- [ ] A4 Control: an unstained wet mount of each specimen in water, viewed in the same way, to show what the stain adds (which structures become visible) and that the structures seen are in the cells and not produced by the stain.

Equipment and reagents (one bullet group)
- [ ] E1 Light microscope; clean glass slides and coverslips; onion; scalpel or knife and white tile; forceps; mounted needle; dropper (pipette); filter paper or paper towel; iodine solution; methylene blue stain; sterile cotton buds; beaker of disinfectant for used buds; distilled water; gloves and safety glasses; pencil and paper for drawings.

Method A: onion epidermis cells (plant), numbered steps
- [ ] O1 1. Place a few drops of water in the centre of a clean slide. 2. Cut an onion into quarters and remove one fleshy scale leaf. 3. Snap the scale leaf and use forceps to peel the thin, transparent epidermis from its inner (concave) surface. 4. Cut a piece about 1 cm square and lay it flat in the water, unfolding it with the mounted needle so there are no folds or overlaps. 5. Add one or two drops of iodine solution and leave it for a few minutes to stain. 6. Lower a coverslip at 45° with the mounted needle (as in "Microscopy"). 7. Blot excess liquid from the edges with filter paper. 8. Prepare an unstained slide the same way using water only (control). 9. Examine under low power (×100), then high power (×400), as in "Microscopy". 10. Make a labelled pencil drawing of a few cells and record the total magnification.

Method B: cheek cells (animal), numbered steps
- [ ] K1 1. Gently rub the inside of your own cheek with a clean, sterile cotton bud. 2. Smear the bud across the centre of a clean slide in a thin layer. 3. Immediately put the used bud into the beaker of disinfectant. 4. Add one or two drops of methylene blue and leave for one to two minutes. 5. Lower a coverslip at 45° and blot the excess with filter paper. 6. Prepare an unstained slide with a drop of water (control). 7. Examine under low power, then high power; look for flat, separate cells, avoiding clumps. 8. Make a labelled pencil drawing and record the magnification.
- [ ] K2 Alternative where a school doesn't use cheek cells: a prepared (commercial) slide of human cheek cells or another animal tissue, examined in the same way.
- [ ] K3 Optional extension for chloroplasts: mount a single leaf of a moss or of Elodea (Canadian pondweed) in a drop of water, without stain; the green chloroplasts are visible, which onion bulb cells don't have.

Safety (one bullet group)
- [ ] S1 Use only your own cheek cells, with a new sterile cotton bud; put used buds, and slides after use, into disinfectant to prevent spreading infection; wash hands afterwards.
- [ ] S2 Iodine solution and methylene blue stain skin and clothes and can irritate eyes: wear gloves and safety glasses and wipe up spills.
- [ ] S3 Cut away from your fingers on a white tile with the scalpel; carry scalpels pointing down.
- [ ] S4 Hold slides and coverslips by their edges; coverslips are very thin and break easily; dispose of broken glass in the glass bin, not the ordinary bin.
- [ ] S5 Keep water away from the microscope's electric lamp and cable; never point a microscope mirror at direct sunlight (eye damage).

Expected results: primary data (one block)
- [ ] R1 Onion epidermis: cells are regular, brick-shaped or rectangular, fitted closely together in rows; each cell is outlined by a clear, rigid cell wall; the nucleus is stained yellow-brown by iodine and is often seen near the edge of the cell; the cytoplasm stains pale yellow and forms a thin layer pushed to the edge by a large central vacuole, which looks clear; no chloroplasts, because the bulb grows underground and doesn't photosynthesise.
- [ ] R2 Cheek cells: cells are flat, irregular in shape and rounded or many-sided, lying singly or in small overlapping groups; each has a cell membrane but no cell wall; the nucleus is small, central and stained dark blue; the cytoplasm stains pale blue; no large vacuole and no chloroplasts.
- [ ] R3 Unstained controls: cells are almost transparent; the onion cell walls are faintly visible but the nucleus is hard to see; the cheek cells are barely visible. So stains make the nucleus and cytoplasm visible by adding contrast.
- [ ] R4 Structures the light microscope can't show: ribosomes, the internal detail of mitochondria, and nuclear pores. Mitochondria are too small to see clearly at ×400.
- [ ] R5 Diagram: pencil drawings of (i) a few onion epidermis cells labelled cell wall, cell membrane (pressed against the wall), cytoplasm, vacuole and nucleus, and (ii) two or three cheek cells labelled cell membrane, cytoplasm and nucleus, each with the total magnification (e.g. ×400) written beside it.

Secondary data: SEM images (one block)
- [ ] D1 Sources: published SEM images (micrographs) of onion epidermis and of cheek (epithelial) cells, and of other cells, from textbooks or reputable scientific image libraries, each with a scale bar or magnification. Record the source of each image.
- [ ] D2 What SEM images add: a three-dimensional view of the cell surface, at much higher magnification and resolution than the light microscope; e.g. the regular, box-like shape of the onion cells and the outlines of their walls, and the flat, thin, overlapping shape of cheek cells and the texture of their surfaces.
- [ ] D3 Limits of SEM images: they show surfaces only, not internal organelles (those need TEM images, see "Microscopy"); they are of dead, metal-coated cells, so preparation can create artefacts; any colour is false colour added afterwards.
- [ ] D4 (assumed) Measuring size from an image: actual size = image size ÷ magnification. Measure the image with a ruler in mm and convert to µm (1 mm = 1000 µm). Worked example: an onion cell measures 40 mm long on an image magnified ×200: 40 ÷ 200 = 0.2 mm = 200 µm. With a scale bar, measure the bar, then: actual size = (image size of the cell ÷ image length of the bar) × the length the bar represents.
- [ ] D5 Comparing primary and secondary data: the light microscope shows living-cell features in colour (with stain) and at low cost; the SEM confirms the shapes and surfaces in 3D at much greater detail. Using both gives a fuller and more reliable picture than either alone.

Conclusion and relating structures to functions (one block)
- [ ] C1 Both cell types have a cell membrane, cytoplasm and a nucleus, so both are eukaryotic.
- [ ] C2 The onion cells have a cell wall (giving their fixed, regular shape and support) and a large vacuole (keeping the cell turgid); the cheek cells have neither, so they have a flexible, irregular shape. This supports the hypothesis.
- [ ] C3 Relating to function: the cheek cells are lining (epithelial) cells, flat and thin, forming a protective layer that is continually rubbed off and replaced, which is why they come away easily; the onion epidermis is a protective outer layer of tightly fitting cells with strong walls; the onion has no chloroplasts because the bulb is a food store, not a photosynthetic organ.

Sources of error and improvements (one bullet group)
- [ ] X1 Air bubbles (black-edged circles) can be mistaken for cells or hide them: lower the coverslip at 45°.
- [ ] X2 Folded or overlapping tissue, or a thick smear, makes cells hard to see: use a flat piece of epidermis one cell layer thick and a thin smear.
- [ ] X3 Too much stain darkens everything and hides detail; too little gives poor contrast: use one or two drops and blot the excess.
- [ ] X4 The specimen drying out under the lamp distorts cells: add a drop of water at the edge of the coverslip.
- [ ] X5 Poor focus or wrong light settings: focus with the fine focus at high power and adjust the diaphragm. Drawings depend on the observer, so compare with other students' slides and with the SEM images; examine several cells and several fields of view, not just one.

## 3. Traps

- The specification requires secondary data from SEM imagery; don't replace it with TEM. TEM may be mentioned only in D3.
- Don't claim onion cells show chloroplasts or that cheek cells show a cell wall or vacuole.
- Don't say iodine stains the onion cells blue-black: that is the starch test (onion epidermis has little or no starch).
- The detail of mitochondria, ribosomes and nuclear pores is not visible at ×400 (R4). Don't name the ER or Golgi apparatus in this subsection (Higher Level material, covered in "Structures and organelles of animal and plant cells").
- The specification has no magnification or actual-size formula: T3 in "Microscopy" and D4 here are assumed knowledge. Keep D4 to the one formula and worked example.
- Don't repeat microscope parts, focusing steps or the reasons for the coverslip angle; refer to "Microscopy".
- Organelle functions in full belong to "Structures and organelles of animal and plant cells"; C3 relates only what this investigation shows.
- Diagram cues: only R5. No other diagram cues.
