# Package: bio24-s2 — Investigation: insect- and wind-pollinated plants

- **loId:** `bio24-s2` · **chapterId:** `bio24` · **syllabusRef:** 2.5.7
- **Syllabus:** "adaptations of these structures dependent on whether the plant is insect or
  wind pollinated." Outcome 7: "investigate and compare the structures of insect and wind
  pollinated plants and relate them to their functions, use primary and secondary data to
  support conclusions." Ordinary and Higher level. No Higher Level only material.
- **Prepared by:** a higher model, 2026-10-29. Write as one full investigation in the
  NOTES_STANDARD "Investigation" block type: a comparison block (the adaptations, as paired
  points), a planning block, a method block, a safety block, a results block, a
  conclusion-with-secondary-data block and an errors block. This is a comparative observation
  study, not a controlled experiment: there is no treatment to apply, so say so, and give the
  "variables" as the type of pollination (what differs) and the features observed and measured
  (what is recorded). The flower parts and their basic functions are taught in "Flower
  structure and pollination"; use the names without redefining them.

## 1. Must cover

Adaptations compared (one Comparison block, paired points: insect-pollinated vs wind-pollinated)
- [ ] C1 Petals: large, brightly coloured, often with nectar guides, to attract insects vs small, green or dull, or absent, as there is no animal to attract and petals would block pollen in the wind.
- [ ] C2 Scent and nectar: usually scented with nectaries producing nectar as a reward vs no scent and no nectaries.
- [ ] C3 Position of anthers: inside the flower, held firmly on short or stiff filaments where a visiting insect brushes against them vs hanging outside the flower on long, thin, flexible filaments, loosely attached (often swinging freely), so the wind shakes pollen out.
- [ ] C4 Stigma: small, sticky and inside the flower, where the insect's body touches it vs large, feathery (branched) and hanging outside the flower, giving a big surface area to catch pollen from the air.
- [ ] C5 Pollen amount: relatively small amounts, because insects carry it directly from flower to flower vs very large amounts, because most pollen carried by the wind never reaches a stigma of the same species.
- [ ] C6 Pollen grains: larger, often with a sticky or oily coat and a rough or spiky surface that clings to insect hairs vs small, light, dry and smooth, so they are carried easily and far by the wind and don't clump.
- [ ] C7 Flowers as a whole: usually showy, single or in visible clusters vs small, inconspicuous, often in catkins or spikes held high above the leaves; many wind-pollinated trees (e.g. hazel) flower before their leaves open, so leaves don't block the pollen.
- [ ] C8 Examples to name: insect-pollinated: buttercup, wallflower, foxglove, primrose; wind-pollinated: grasses (e.g. ryegrass), hazel, oak, birch, plantain.
- [ ] C9 Diagram: side-by-side labelled drawings of an insect-pollinated flower (half-flower of buttercup or wallflower: large petal, nectary, anther inside the flower, small sticky stigma) and a wind-pollinated grass flower (feathery stigmas and anthers on long filaments hanging outside small green bracts). Label the bracts only as "bracts (small green scales)".
- [ ] C10 Diagram: an insect-pollen grain (larger, spiky or sculptured surface) beside a wind-pollen grain (smaller, smooth), as seen under the microscope.

Planning (one block)
- [ ] A1 Aim: to compare the structures of an insect-pollinated flower and a wind-pollinated flower, and relate each structure to its function in pollination.
- [ ] A2 Hypothesis (example to write): "The insect-pollinated flower will have larger, coloured petals, nectaries and a sticky stigma inside the flower; the wind-pollinated flower will have small or no petals, anthers and feathery stigmas hanging outside the flower, and smaller, smoother pollen grains."
- [ ] A3 What differs and what is recorded: the factor being compared is the method of pollination (insect or wind). The features recorded are qualitative (colour, scent, presence of nectar, whether the stigma is sticky or feathery, whether anthers and stigma are inside or outside the flower, pollen surface) and quantitative (number of each part, lengths of filaments and styles, petal size, pollen grain diameter).
- [ ] A4 Keeping the comparison fair: use fully open, mature flowers in which the anthers are shedding pollen; examine several flowers of each species (e.g. at least three) and average the measurements; measure every flower in the same way with the same instruments and microscope magnification. No control in the experimental sense is needed because no factor is changed; the two flower types are compared with each other.
- [ ] A5 Equipment: fresh insect-pollinated flowers (e.g. buttercup or wallflower) and wind-pollinated flowers (e.g. a flowering grass, or hazel catkins in late winter); white tile; scalpel or sharp blade; forceps; mounted needle; hand lens (×10) or stereo (dissecting) microscope; ruler (mm) or graph paper; microscope slides and coverslips; dropper with water; compound light microscope; clear sticky tape; gloves; pencil and paper.

Method (one block, numbered steps)
- [ ] M1 1. Examine each whole flower with the hand lens. Record its colour, size, and whether it has a scent. Note whether the anthers and stigmas are inside the flower or hang outside it.
- [ ] M2 2. Remove the parts of the insect-pollinated flower from the outside in, using forceps, laying each ring (sepals, petals, stamens, carpels) in order on the white tile. Count the parts in each ring and record them.
- [ ] M3 3. Look at the base of the petals for nectaries (shiny or moist patches). Touch the stigma lightly with a mounted needle to see if it is sticky.
- [ ] M4 4. Measure the length of the filaments, style and petals with a ruler (or on graph paper) for each flower. Repeat for at least three flowers of each species and calculate the means.
- [ ] M5 5. Repeat steps 1 to 4 with the wind-pollinated flower, using the stereo microscope for the small grass flowers. Note the bracts, the long hanging filaments and the feathery stigmas.
- [ ] M6 6. Cut a vertical section of the insect-pollinated flower with the scalpel on the tile (a half-flower) and draw it, labelled.
- [ ] M7 7. Pollen: tap a ripe anther over a drop of water on a clean slide (or press clear sticky tape onto the anther and stick the tape on a slide), add a coverslip, and examine under the light microscope at low then high power. Draw a few grains of each type at the same magnification and compare their size and surface; estimate their diameter (e.g. against the field of view or an eyepiece scale). Note how easily pollen comes off the anther and whether it clumps (sticky) or spreads (dry).
- [ ] M8 8. Record all observations and measurements in a results table and make labelled drawings with the magnification stated.

Safety (one block)
- [ ] H1 Cut away from the body on a white tile with a sharp scalpel; carry and store blades safely.
- [ ] H2 Pollen can trigger hay fever or asthma; students with pollen allergies should avoid handling wind-pollinated flowers or wear a mask and gloves, and the room should be ventilated.
- [ ] H3 Some plants are poisonous or irritant: foxglove is poisonous if eaten, and buttercup sap can irritate skin. Wear gloves, don't put plant material near the mouth, and wash hands after the practical.
- [ ] H4 Collect only common plants, with the landowner's permission; don't pick protected or rare wild flowers.
- [ ] H5 Microscope slides and coverslips are fragile glass; dispose of breakages in a glass bin.

Results (one block)
- [ ] D1 Results table: one column for each species (insect-pollinated, wind-pollinated), with rows for petal colour and size, scent, nectaries, number of stamens, filament length, anther position, stigma type and position, style length, amount of pollen released, pollen size and surface.
- [ ] D2 Expected primary data: the insect-pollinated flower has large coloured petals, nectaries, scent, anthers on short or stiff filaments inside the flower, a small sticky stigma, and larger, sticky, spiky or sculptured pollen grains; the wind-pollinated flower has small green bracts or no petals, no nectar or scent, anthers dangling outside the flower on long filaments that shed clouds of dry pollen when tapped, feathery stigmas outside the flower, and smaller, smooth, dry pollen grains.

Conclusion and secondary data (one block)
- [ ] S1 Conclusion: relate each observed structure to its function (use C1 to C7): e.g. the feathery stigma of the grass gives a large surface to catch airborne pollen; the sticky stigma of the buttercup collects pollen rubbed off an insect's body. State whether the results support the hypothesis.
- [ ] S2 Secondary data used to support the conclusions: scanning electron micrographs (SEM images) of pollen grains, which show the surface detail that a light microscope can't resolve (spikes and ridges on insect pollen; smooth surfaces on grass pollen); published pollen counts, which show the huge numbers of grass pollen grains in the air in early summer; labelled diagrams and descriptions of other species in textbooks or plant identification guides.
- [ ] S3 Primary data are data the student collects by observing and measuring; secondary data are collected by others (one bullet, linking to "Producing and analysing data").

Sources of error and limitations (one block)
- [ ] E1 Only a few flowers of one or two species are examined, so the results may not represent all insect- or wind-pollinated plants; examine more flowers and more species, and compare with secondary data.
- [ ] E2 Flowers at different stages (bud, open, withering) differ in size and pollen release; use flowers at the same stage.
- [ ] E3 Grass flowers are very small, so measurements with a ruler are imprecise; use a stereo microscope or graph paper.
- [ ] E4 Pollen size estimated against the field of view is approximate; a calibrated eyepiece scale gives more precise sizes. The light microscope can't show fine surface detail, hence the use of SEM images.
- [ ] E5 Judgements of colour, scent and stickiness are qualitative and subjective; have two people record them.
- [ ] E6 Damage during dissection can break parts or knock off pollen; handle flowers gently with forceps.

## 3. Traps

- This is a comparison, not a controlled experiment; don't invent an independent variable that is "changed" or a control group. Do require fair comparison (A4).
- Feathery stigmas belong to wind-pollinated flowers (e.g. grasses); sticky stigmas to insect-pollinated flowers. Don't call a wind-pollinated stigma "sticky" as its main feature.
- Wind pollen is light and smooth, not "sticky" or "heavy"; insect pollen is not "light and dry".
- Don't use dandelion as an insect-pollinated example (it is a composite flower head and often sets seed without pollination) or wheat (mainly self-pollinated).
- Don't teach grass-flower terms (lemma, palea, lodicule); call them bracts.
- The parts of a flower and the general definition of pollination belong to "Flower structure and pollination"; don't redefine them.
- Pine and other conifers are not flowering plants; don't use them as wind-pollinated flower examples.
- The diagram cues C9 and C10 are all that are wanted. No other diagram cues.

## 4. What exams ask (topics only)

- From a diagram, decide whether a flower is insect- or wind-pollinated and give reasons from its structure.
- Give two (or more) differences between insect- and wind-pollinated flowers.
- Explain why wind-pollinated plants produce large amounts of pollen.
- Describe how to examine and compare the two flower types in the laboratory, including pollen under the microscope.
