## 1. Must cover

What a pyramid of biomass is (one block)
- [ ] B1 Biomass is the total mass of living material, usually measured as dry mass (mass after all water is removed), in a given area or volume at a given time, e.g. grams per square metre (g/m²) for land or grams per cubic metre (g/m³) for water.
- [ ] B2 A pyramid of biomass shows the total dry mass of the organisms at each trophic level of a food chain or web, at one time, per unit area. Each level is a horizontal bar whose width is proportional to its biomass, with producers at the base.
- [ ] B3 Dry mass is used because the water content of organisms varies greatly (e.g. lettuce leaves are mostly water, wood much less) and changes from hour to hour, so fresh (wet) mass would be misleading.
- [ ] B4 How biomass is measured: 1. sample the organisms at each trophic level in a measured area (e.g. a quadrat); 2. dry them in an oven at about 80–100 °C, below the temperature at which organic matter burns; 3. weigh, return to the oven, and reweigh until the mass stays constant (all water gone); 4. scale up to the whole area. This kills the organisms, so only small samples are taken, and the biomass of large organisms such as trees is estimated from their measurements.
- [ ] B5 Pyramids of biomass are almost always upright: biomass decreases at each trophic level, because only part of the food eaten becomes the consumer's body; the rest is respired (lost as CO₂ and water), egested or excreted (from "Energy and nutrient transfer").
- [ ] B6 Diagram: a pyramid of numbers and a pyramid of biomass for the same food chain side by side, oak tree → caterpillars → blue tits → sparrowhawk: the numbers pyramid has a narrow base (one tree), while the biomass pyramid is upright with the widest bar for the tree.

Inverted pyramids of biomass (one block)
- [ ] I1 In some aquatic ecosystems, at certain times, the biomass of the producers (phytoplankton) is smaller than that of the primary consumers (zooplankton) that eat them, so the pyramid is inverted at the base.
- [ ] I2 Reason: phytoplankton are tiny, reproduce very rapidly and are eaten almost as fast as they are produced, so at any one moment the standing crop (the biomass present) is small, even though over a year they produce far more biomass than the zooplankton. The pyramid measures biomass present at one moment, not the rate at which it is produced.
- [ ] I3 Diagram: an inverted pyramid of biomass for phytoplankton → zooplankton, with the producer bar narrower than the primary consumer bar, labelled.

Comparing pyramids of numbers and pyramids of biomass (one comparison block, paired points)
- [ ] C1 Similarities: both show the trophic levels of a food chain or web as stacked bars with producers at the base; both are usually upright; both are measured at one time and change with the seasons; both rely on sampling; both are models that show why there are fewer, or less mass of, top consumers; both usually leave out decomposers.
- [ ] C2 What is measured: number of individuals (numbers) vs total dry mass per unit area (biomass).
- [ ] C3 Size of organisms: ignored, so one tree counts as one (numbers) vs taken into account, so a large tree counts for its full mass (biomass). So a biomass pyramid gives a truer picture of how much food is available at each level.
- [ ] C4 Shape: often inverted or spindle-shaped where one large producer feeds many herbivores or where parasites are involved (numbers) vs almost always upright on land, including for tree-based chains and parasite chains; inverted only in some aquatic ecosystems at some times (biomass).
- [ ] C5 Ease of collecting data: counting is quicker and does not harm the organisms (numbers) vs drying and weighing is slow and destructive, so it needs small samples, and large organisms must be estimated (biomass).
- [ ] C6 Drawing to scale: very large differences in numbers (millions of grass plants, one fox) make numbers pyramids hard to draw to scale; biomass values are usually less extreme, so biomass pyramids are easier to draw to scale.
- [ ] C7 What neither shows well: the rate of production over time (biomass shows only the standing crop), so short-lived, fast-reproducing organisms are under-represented; and the energy content, because a gram of fat contains about twice the energy of a gram of carbohydrate or protein, so equal biomass doesn't mean equal energy.
- [ ] C8 Judgement for the study of ecosystems: pyramids of numbers are useful for a quick, non-destructive survey of feeding relationships, especially where organisms are similar in size; pyramids of biomass better show the amount of material passing up a food chain and the effect of energy loss, so they are more useful for comparing ecosystems. A pyramid of energy (which shows the energy flowing through each level over a period of time and is never inverted) overcomes the limitations of both; name it only.

Worked example (one Skills block)
- [ ] W1 Sample data (invented for practice) for a woodland food chain, per 100 m²: oak trees 1 (dry biomass 2 000 kg); caterpillars 5 000 (dry biomass 5 kg); blue tits 4 (dry biomass 0.04 kg); sparrowhawk 0.1 of a bird's territory, so draw as the smallest bar. Pyramid of numbers: narrow base, very wide caterpillar bar, then narrow. Pyramid of biomass: upright, decreasing from 2 000 kg to 5 kg to 0.04 kg. Interpretation: the tree is one organism but contains far more food than all the caterpillars, so the biomass pyramid shows the food available correctly, and it is upright.

## 3. Traps

- Every block is Higher Level only; mark every heading. Biomass, dry mass, pyramid of biomass and standing crop are Higher Level key terms.
- Biomass is dry mass, not fresh mass, and is per unit area at one time.
- Pyramids of biomass are not "never" inverted: the aquatic case (I1) exists. Pyramids of energy are the ones that are never inverted (C8 names them only; they are not on the specification).
