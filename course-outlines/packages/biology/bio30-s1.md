## 1. Must cover

Why plants need transport, and the root and shoot systems (one block)
- [ ] R1 (assumed) A large plant has a small surface area to volume ratio (see "Levels of organisation"), so diffusion alone can't supply its cells. Its roots take in water and minerals from the soil, but these are needed in the leaves; its leaves make sugar, but this is needed in the roots, growing tips, flowers, fruits and storage organs. The two transport tissues, xylem and phloem, link them.
- [ ] R2 The root system (everything below ground) anchors the plant, absorbs water and minerals through the root hairs, and often stores food (e.g. the swollen root of a carrot). The shoot system (stem, leaves, buds and flowers) holds the leaves up to the light, carries out photosynthesis and gas exchange, transports materials between roots and leaves, and reproduces.
- [ ] R3 The two systems are joined by continuous vascular tissue: the xylem and phloem of the root run up through the stem and branch out into every leaf as the veins, so there is an unbroken pipeline from root hair to leaf cell.

The three tissue types (one structure-and-function block, one bullet per tissue)
- [ ] T1 Dermal tissue is the outer protective layer. In young roots, stems and leaves it is the epidermis, usually one cell thick. In leaves and stems the epidermis is covered by a waxy, waterproof cuticle that cuts water loss and keeps out pathogens; it contains stomata (pores, each between two guard cells) for gas exchange. In roots it has no cuticle and some epidermal cells grow out as root hairs, for absorbing water and minerals. In woody stems the epidermis is replaced by bark, pierced by lenticels.
- [ ] T2 Ground tissue makes up the bulk of the plant between the dermal and vascular tissue. It is mostly made of living, thin-walled cells with large vacuoles. Functions depend on position: photosynthesis (the mesophyll of the leaf, and green stems), storage of starch and water (root cortex, stem pith, potato tubers), and support, because its cells are kept firm (turgid) by water pressure.
- [ ] T3 Vascular tissue is the transport tissue: xylem carries water and dissolved minerals; phloem carries dissolved food (mainly sucrose). Xylem and phloem lie together in groups called vascular bundles in the stem and leaf, and in a central core in the root. Xylem also supports the plant.

Xylem (one structure-and-function block)
- [ ] X1 Xylem carries water and dissolved minerals from the roots, up the stem, to the leaves, in one direction only (upwards).
- [ ] X2 It is made of two kinds of cell, both dead and hollow when mature, with no cytoplasm, nucleus or cell membrane, so water flows through them without obstruction. Xylem vessels are long continuous tubes formed from a column of cells joined end to end whose end walls have broken down. Tracheids are single long, narrow cells with tapering ends; water passes from one tracheid to the next through thin areas in the walls called pits.
- [ ] X3 Their cell walls are thickened with lignin, a hard, waterproof substance, laid down in rings, spirals or as a solid layer with pits. Lignin stops the vessels collapsing inwards when the water in them is under tension (pulled from above; see "Transport of water and minerals") and makes xylem the main supporting tissue of woody plants: wood is xylem.
- [ ] X4 Pits in the side walls let water move sideways between neighbouring vessels and tracheids, and out to surrounding cells.
- [ ] X5 Diagram: a longitudinal view of a xylem vessel (a long open tube with no end walls, spiral or ring thickening of lignin and pits) beside a tracheid (tapering ends, pits); label lignin, pit, no end wall, hollow lumen.

Phloem (one structure-and-function block)
- [ ] P1 Phloem carries dissolved food, mainly sucrose and also amino acids, from the leaves (where it is made) to the parts that use or store it. It can carry food upwards or downwards.
- [ ] P2 Sieve tubes are columns of living cells (sieve tube elements) joined end to end. Their end walls are perforated with many small holes, forming sieve plates, through which the sap flows from one cell to the next. A mature sieve tube element has a thin layer of cytoplasm but no nucleus and very few organelles, leaving room for the sap to flow.
- [ ] P3 Each sieve tube element has a companion cell beside it, joined to it by many fine connections through the walls. The companion cell has a nucleus, dense cytoplasm and many mitochondria; it keeps the sieve tube element alive and supplies the energy (ATP) needed to load sucrose into the sieve tube (see "Transport of CO₂ and photosynthetic products").
- [ ] P4 Phloem cell walls are cellulose only, not lignified, so phloem gives little support. In a woody stem the phloem lies just inside the bark.
- [ ] P5 Diagram: a longitudinal view of two sieve tube elements end to end with a sieve plate between them and a companion cell alongside; label sieve tube element, sieve plate, cytoplasm, companion cell, nucleus (in the companion cell only).

Xylem and phloem compared (one comparison block, paired points)
- [ ] C1 Cells: xylem vessels and tracheids are dead and hollow; sieve tube elements and companion cells are living.
- [ ] C2 Walls: xylem walls are thickened with lignin; phloem walls are cellulose only.
- [ ] C3 End walls: xylem vessels have none; sieve tubes have sieve plates.
- [ ] C4 Contents: xylem carries water and minerals; phloem carries sucrose and amino acids in solution.
- [ ] C5 Direction: xylem upwards only, from roots to leaves; phloem up or down, from where food is made or stored to where it is used.
- [ ] C6 Support: xylem supports the plant; phloem gives little support.

The root (one structure-and-function block)
- [ ] O1 Functions: anchors the plant in the soil, absorbs water and minerals, transports them to the stem, and may store food.
- [ ] O2 Root tip: a root cap protects the delicate growing tip as it pushes through the soil; just behind it is meristematic tissue whose cells divide, so the root grows in length (meristems are named in "Levels of organisation"). Root hairs develop a little further back, on the region that has stopped growing, so they are not torn off as the root moves forward. One bullet.
- [ ] O3 Root hairs: each is a long, thin outgrowth of a single epidermal cell. There are thousands of them, giving a huge surface area for absorption; the wall is thin and has no cuticle, so water passes in easily; the large vacuole holds cell sap that is more concentrated than soil water, so water enters by osmosis; many mitochondria supply ATP for the active transport of mineral ions. Root hairs grow between soil particles, in close contact with the film of soil water.
- [ ] O4 Root cortex: a wide zone of ground tissue made of large, thin-walled, loosely packed living cells with air spaces between them. Water and minerals pass across it from the root hairs to the xylem; it stores food (often as starch); the air spaces let oxygen from soil air reach the cells for respiration. Its innermost single layer of cells, the endodermis, surrounds the vascular tissue and controls what passes into the xylem (one phrase only).
- [ ] O5 Vascular tissue of the root is in the centre: the xylem forms a star or X shape, with the phloem in groups between its arms. A central core resists the pulling forces on a root as the shoot is blown about, as the core of a rope does.
- [ ] O6 Diagram: a transverse section of a young root, labelled epidermis (dermal tissue), root hair, cortex (ground tissue), endodermis, xylem and phloem (vascular tissue). Label the three tissue types by name as well as the structures.

The stem (one structure-and-function block)
- [ ] S1 Functions: supports the leaves and flowers, holding the leaves up to the light and the flowers up for pollination; carries water and minerals up in the xylem and food up or down in the phloem between roots and leaves; may store food (e.g. the potato tuber is a swollen underground stem) and, if green, photosynthesise.
- [ ] S2 Epidermis with a waxy cuticle on the outside (dermal tissue) protects the stem and reduces water loss; in a woody stem it is replaced by bark.
- [ ] S3 Cortex just inside the epidermis and pith in the centre (both ground tissue): living cells that store food and water, and help support the stem when turgid.
- [ ] S4 Vascular bundles arranged in a ring near the outside of the stem; in each bundle the phloem is on the outside and the xylem on the inside. A ring of strong tissue near the edge resists bending as the stem is blown sideways, as a hollow tube does.
- [ ] S5 Diagram: a transverse section of a young (non-woody) stem, labelled epidermis and cuticle (dermal tissue), cortex and pith (ground tissue), vascular bundle with phloem outside and xylem inside (vascular tissue).

Lenticels (one block)
- [ ] L1 A lenticel is a small raised pore in the bark of a woody stem (also found on some roots, on potato tubers, and as the speckles on apple skins). It is a patch of loosely packed cells with air spaces between them.
- [ ] L2 Function: bark is waterproof and airtight, so the living cells beneath it (including the phloem) would be cut off from air. Lenticels allow gas exchange by diffusion: oxygen in for respiration and carbon dioxide out. A small amount of water vapour is also lost through them.
- [ ] L3 Lenticels stay open all the time; unlike stomata they have no guard cells and cannot close. Examples: the horizontal lines on the bark of birch and cherry trees.

The leaf (one structure-and-function block)
- [ ] F1 Functions: photosynthesis; gas exchange (CO₂ in, oxygen out, in the light); transpiration, which pulls water up the plant (see "Transport of water and minerals").
- [ ] F2 External structure: a thin, flat, broad blade gives a large surface area to absorb light and CO₂ and a short distance for gases to diffuse; the leaf stalk (petiole) holds the blade out towards the light and carries the vascular tissue into it; the midrib and veins are vascular bundles that bring water and minerals and take away sucrose, and their network supports the blade.
- [ ] F3 Waxy cuticle on the upper (and lower) epidermis: waterproof, reduces water loss by evaporation, transparent so light passes through; it is usually thicker on the upper surface, which faces the sun.
- [ ] F4 Upper epidermis: one layer of transparent cells with no chloroplasts, letting light through to the cells below; few or no stomata.
- [ ] F5 Palisade mesophyll (ground tissue): one or two layers of tall, column-shaped cells packed with chloroplasts, just below the upper epidermis where light is strongest; most photosynthesis takes place here.
- [ ] F6 Spongy mesophyll (ground tissue): irregular, loosely packed cells with fewer chloroplasts and large air spaces between them. The air spaces connect to the stomata and let CO₂, oxygen and water vapour diffuse through the leaf; the moist cell surfaces are where CO₂ dissolves before entering the cells and where water evaporates.
- [ ] F7 Vascular bundle (vein): xylem on the upper side brings water and minerals; phloem on the lower side carries sucrose away.
- [ ] F8 Lower epidermis: contains most of the stomata. Each stoma (plural stomata) is a pore between two bean-shaped guard cells, which, unlike other epidermal cells, contain chloroplasts. The guard cells open and close the pore (mechanism in "Transport of water and minerals"). Stomata on the shaded lower surface lose less water than they would on the sunny upper surface.
- [ ] F9 Diagram: a transverse section of a leaf, labelled cuticle, upper epidermis (dermal tissue), palisade mesophyll and spongy mesophyll with air spaces (ground tissue), vascular bundle with xylem above and phloem below (vascular tissue), lower epidermis, stoma and guard cells.
- [ ] F10 Diagram: a surface view of the lower epidermis showing a stoma between two guard cells (with chloroplasts) surrounded by irregular epidermal cells; label stoma, guard cell, chloroplast, epidermal cell.

## 3. Traps

- Xylem is dead; phloem is living. Sieve tube elements have no nucleus; the companion cell has one. Students often swap these.
- Xylem carries water and minerals, never sugar; phloem carries sucrose (and amino acids), not glucose and not water "upwards only". Phloem moves food in both directions.
- Lenticels are not stomata: they are in bark, have no guard cells and can't close.
- Root hairs are extensions of single epidermal cells, not separate cells and not tiny roots; they have no cuticle and no chloroplasts.
- The palisade layer, not the spongy layer, does most photosynthesis; the spongy layer is for gas diffusion.
- Not on this specification (previous-syllabus or beyond-scope content): monocot vs dicot comparisons; the microscope practical of preparing a stem section (the previous syllabus's mandatory activity); cambium, secondary growth and annual rings; the zones of the root tip as a separate topic (O2 is one bullet only); the Casparian strip; parenchyma, collenchyma and sclerenchyma as named cell types; modified storage organs (bulbs, corms, rhizomes, tap roots) as a classification. R2 and S1 name a carrot and a potato in a phrase only. Plasmodesmata are not on the specification: in P3 say "fine connections through the walls".
- Osmosis and active transport are defined in "Diffusion, osmosis and active transport"; O3 uses them without redefining.
- How stomata open and close, transpiration, root pressure and cohesion–tension belong to "Transport of water and minerals"; CO₂ diffusion and phloem transport (translocation, sources and sinks) belong to "Transport of CO₂ and photosynthetic products". Here, name the job in a phrase.

## 4. What exams ask (topics only)

- Draw a labelled transverse section of a leaf; mark where most photosynthesis occurs and explain why; state other ways the leaf is adapted for photosynthesis.
- Draw a transverse section of a root (or stem) and label the dermal, ground and vascular tissues.
- Give the function of xylem and of phloem; name the tissue type they belong to; state where they are in a root or stem.
- Relate the structure of a root hair (or xylem vessel, or sieve tube) to its function.
- Name a plant structure through which gases are exchanged other than stomata (lenticels).
