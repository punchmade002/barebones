## 1. Must cover

Species and how information passes between generations (one process block, in this order)
- [ ] R1 Species: a group of organisms with similar features that can interbreed in natural conditions to produce fertile offspring. Example: a horse and a donkey can mate, but their offspring, the mule, is sterile, so horses and donkeys are different species. Limitation: the definition can't be applied to organisms that reproduce only asexually, or to fossils (how new species form is in "Evidence for evolution").
- [ ] R2 Heredity: the passing of characteristics (traits), by means of genes, from parents to offspring, i.e. from one generation to the next. Genetics is the study of heredity and of variation.
- [ ] R3 Chromosome (the term in this list): the structure that carries genes from one generation to the next; full structure in "Chromosome structure and genes". In information transfer, what matters is that body cells carry chromosomes in homologous pairs, and each gamete carries one chromosome from each pair.
- [ ] R4 Gamete: a sex cell, e.g. a sperm or egg in animals; in flowering plants the male gamete nucleus in the pollen grain and the egg cell in the ovule. Gametes are haploid (one set of chromosomes, one chromosome of each pair): a human gamete has 23 chromosomes. They are made by meiosis (in "Mitosis and meiosis").
- [ ] R5 Sexual reproduction: reproduction involving the fusion of two gametes, usually from two parents. The offspring receive half their chromosomes from each parent, so they are genetically different from both parents and from each other (how this creates variation is in "Variation"). Contrast: asexual reproduction involves one parent and no gametes, and produces genetically identical offspring (clones).
- [ ] R6 Fertilisation: the fusion of the nucleus of a male gamete with the nucleus of a female gamete to form a diploid zygote, the first cell of the new organism. It restores the diploid number: in humans 23 + 23 = 46 chromosomes. The zygote then divides by mitosis, so every body cell carries the same set.
- [ ] R7 Diagram: a life-cycle flow diagram: parent (2n = 46) → meiosis → gametes (n = 23, sperm and egg) → fertilisation → zygote (2n = 46) → mitosis → offspring; label haploid, diploid, meiosis, fertilisation, mitosis.

Genes and alleles (one block)
- [ ] A1 Allele: one of the alternative forms of a gene, found at the same locus on homologous chromosomes. Alleles of a gene differ slightly in base sequence (they arise by mutation) and so may produce different versions of the trait. Example: the gene for seed shape in peas has an allele for round seeds (R) and one for wrinkled seeds (r).
- [ ] A2 A diploid organism has two alleles for each autosomal gene, one on each homologous chromosome, one inherited from each parent. A population may have more than two alleles of a gene, but each individual carries only two, e.g. the human ABO blood-group gene has three alleles (A, B and O) in the population.
- [ ] A3 (assumed) Homozygous: having two identical alleles of a gene (RR or rr); a homozygous organism is also called pure-breeding, because it passes on the same allele in every gamete. Heterozygous: having two different alleles of a gene (Rr); a heterozygous organism is also called a hybrid for that gene. (Used in "Monohybrid crosses" without redefining.)
- [ ] A4 Diagram: a pair of homologous chromosomes with one gene locus marked; one chromosome carries R and the other r (heterozygous); a second pair carries r and r (homozygous); label homologous chromosomes, locus, allele.

Genotype and phenotype (one block)
- [ ] P1 Genotype: the genetic make-up of an organism, i.e. the alleles it has for a particular trait, written with letters, e.g. RR, Rr or rr.
- [ ] P2 Phenotype: the observable (physical, chemical or behavioural) characteristics of an organism that result from its genotype, e.g. round or wrinkled seeds; blood group A.
- [ ] P3 Phenotype = genotype + environment. The genotype sets the possible range; the environment affects how it is expressed. Examples: identical twins have the same genotype but may differ in mass because of diet and exercise; hydrangea plants of the same variety have blue flowers in acid soil and pink flowers in alkaline soil; a plant with the genes for chlorophyll is pale yellow if grown in the dark.
- [ ] P4 Different genotypes can give the same phenotype (RR and Rr are both round) when one allele is dominant (D1). So the phenotype doesn't always reveal the genotype; how to find it out (the test cross) is in "Monohybrid crosses".

Dominance and recessive alleles (one block)
- [ ] D1 Dominant allele: an allele that is expressed in the phenotype of a heterozygote; it masks the effect of the other allele. Written with a capital letter (R). A dominant trait shows in both RR and Rr.
- [ ] D2 Recessive allele: an allele that is expressed in the phenotype only when no dominant allele is present, i.e. only in the homozygous condition (rr). Written with the lower-case of the same letter (r). In a heterozygote it is present but hidden, and can still be passed on.
- [ ] D3 Why: often the dominant allele codes for a working protein and the recessive allele for a non-working one. One working copy makes enough protein, so Rr looks the same as RR. Example: in peas, R codes for a working starch-branching enzyme; rr seeds make less starch and wrinkle ("Genes, proteins and traits" has the full chain; refer, don't repeat).
- [ ] D4 Dominant does not mean "more common" or "better". Example: the allele causing Huntington's disease is dominant but rare, and harmful. Cystic fibrosis and albinism are caused by recessive alleles, so two carriers (heterozygotes) can have an affected child.
- [ ] D5 (assumed) Carrier: a heterozygous individual who carries a recessive allele for a condition but does not show it. One sentence; used in "Sex-linked traits".

Gene expression (one block)
- [ ] E1 Gene expression: the process by which the information in a gene is used to make its product (a protein, or a functional RNA), so that the gene affects the phenotype. A gene that is being used is "switched on" (expressed); one that is not is "switched off".
- [ ] E2 Every body cell has the same genes, but each cell type expresses only some of them, which is why cells become specialised (the insulin and haemoglobin examples are in "Genes, proteins and traits"; refer, don't repeat).
- [ ] E3 Expression is controlled: genes are switched on or off by regulatory DNA (in "Chromosome structure and genes"), by signals such as hormones, and by the environment, e.g. some genes are switched on only in response to a stimulus. How the environment and behaviour change expression is in "Genetic and epigenetic mechanisms".

Further key terms (one block, Higher Level only; one bullet each, definition plus example and cross-reference only)
- [ ] H1 Epigenetic expression: changes in gene expression that are not caused by a change in the DNA base sequence, but by chemical tags on the DNA or on the histones that switch genes on or off; they can be caused by the environment and behaviour, can be passed on when cells divide, and some are reversible. Full treatment, comparison and examples in "Genetic and epigenetic mechanisms".
- [ ] H2 Incomplete dominance: neither allele is dominant over the other, so the heterozygote has a phenotype intermediate between the two homozygotes. Example: red-flowered and white-flowered snapdragons give pink-flowered heterozygotes. Contrast with complete dominance, where the heterozygote looks like the dominant homozygote. Crosses in "Incomplete dominance".
- [ ] H3 Linkage: genes located on the same chromosome are linked; they tend to be inherited together because they don't separate into different gametes. Linked genes do not assort independently. Detail in "Linkage".
- [ ] H4 Sex-linkage: a gene located on a sex chromosome (in humans almost always the X chromosome) is sex-linked, so the trait's inheritance pattern differs between males and females. Examples: red–green colour blindness and haemophilia, which are much more common in males. Detail in "Sex-linked traits".

## 3. Traps

- Only H1–H4 are Higher Level; keep them in their own block. Don't mark any other term.
- No crosses, Punnett squares, ratios, test-cross method, Mendel's laws or Mendel's experiments here: they belong to "Monohybrid crosses", "Incomplete dominance", "Mendel's Laws", "Dihybrid crosses", "Linkage", "Sex determination" and "Sex-linked traits". H2–H4 give the definition and one example only; don't explain why linked genes stay together, how sex is determined, or the X-linked inheritance pattern.
- Gene vs allele: a gene is the section of DNA for a trait (seed shape); alleles are its alternative forms (round, wrinkled). Genotype vs phenotype: letters vs observable characteristic. Students often write "the dominant gene"; it is the dominant allele.
- Don't say recessive alleles are "weaker", "less common" or "disappear"; they remain and can be passed on (D2, D4).
- Gamete vs zygote: gamete is haploid, zygote diploid. Fertilisation is fusion of the nuclei, not just the meeting of the gametes.
- Mitosis and meiosis, and haploid and diploid as cell-cycle terms, belong to "Mitosis and meiosis" and "The cell cycle"; use the terms without explaining the divisions.
- Codominance and the ABO cross are not on the specification; A2 uses the ABO gene only as an example of multiple alleles. Don't explain blood-group dominance.
- Epigenetic mechanisms (methylation, histone changes) and examples belong to "Genetic and epigenetic mechanisms"; H1 names "chemical tags" only.
- No other diagram cues beyond R7 and A4.
