## 1. Must cover

Population size and carrying capacity (one block)
- [ ] P1 A population's size changes through four processes: births (natality) and immigration (individuals arriving) increase it; deaths (mortality) and emigration (individuals leaving) decrease it. A population grows when births plus immigration exceed deaths plus emigration. (assumed)
- [ ] P2 Every population could grow very fast if nothing stopped it, because organisms produce more offspring than are needed to replace themselves (as in "The theory of evolution by natural selection"). In reality resources run short and growth slows and stops.
- [ ] P3 The carrying capacity of an environment is the maximum population size of a species that it can support indefinitely, given the food, water, space and other resources available. It is often given the symbol K.
- [ ] P4 A limiting factor is any factor that restricts the size, growth or distribution of a population. The factor in shortest supply relative to need sets the limit, even if all others are plentiful (the same principle as limiting factors in photosynthesis in "Investigation: rate of photosynthesis"). Carrying capacity is set by the limiting factors acting together.
- [ ] P5 Carrying capacity is not fixed: it changes when conditions change (a wet or dry year, a new predator or disease, habitat loss or restoration), and it differs between species in the same habitat. If a population rises above carrying capacity, it damages its resources (e.g. overgrazing) and then falls.

Abiotic limiting factors (one block; one bullet per factor with an Irish example)
- [ ] A1 Water: all organisms need water. On the dry, free-draining sand of coastal sand dunes, water shortage limits which plants can grow and how many; marram grass, which has rolled leaves and deep roots, survives where most plants can't. In a dry summer, grass growth slows and fewer grazing animals can be supported.
- [ ] A2 Oxygen: aquatic animals depend on oxygen dissolved in water, which is scarce compared with air. Salmon, trout and stonefly nymphs need well-oxygenated, cool, fast-flowing water, so oxygen limits their numbers in slow, warm or polluted rivers (decomposing organic pollution uses up oxygen). In waterlogged bog soil, low oxygen limits roots and decomposers, so few plant species can grow there.
- [ ] A3 Space: organisms need space to attach, shelter, nest or hold territory. On a rocky shore, bare rock is limited, so barnacles, mussels and seaweeds compete for space to attach; seabirds nest only on cliffs and islands with suitable ledges; many songbirds, such as robins, defend territories, so the number of territories in a woodland limits the breeding population.
- [ ] A4 Temperature: it affects enzyme activity, growth and survival (see "Enzymes"). Cold winters kill many small birds such as wrens; low temperatures in the uplands shorten the growing season, limiting plant growth; warm water holds less dissolved oxygen, so temperature also limits fish.
- [ ] A5 Soil: soil type, pH, minerals, water content and air content limit plants and so the animals that feed on them. Acidic, nitrogen-poor bog peat supports only specialised plants (Sphagnum, heather, sundews); thin limestone soils of the Burren, Co. Clare support lime-loving plants; fertile, well-drained lowland soils support productive grassland.
- [ ] A6 Light (the specification doesn't list it, but it is a key limiting factor for plants; one bullet): under a closed woodland canopy in summer, little light reaches the floor, so few plants grow there; woodland plants such as bluebells grow and flower in spring before the leaves of the trees open.

Biotic limiting factors (one block; one bullet per factor with an Irish example)
- [ ] B1 Food: the supply of food usually sets the carrying capacity for animals. For grazers it is the amount of grass or browse; seed-eaters such as squirrels and wood mice do better in years when trees produce large seed crops; birds of prey such as barn owls are limited by the number of small mammals.
- [ ] B2 Disease: disease spreads faster in dense populations, so it limits large populations most. Squirrelpox virus, carried by grey squirrels, kills red squirrels; myxomatosis, a viral disease, killed most of Ireland's rabbits after it arrived in the 1950s; ash dieback is killing ash trees.
- [ ] B3 Competition: organisms competing for the same limited resource (food, light, water, space, mates) reduce each other's share, so fewer survive or breed. Competition between members of the same species (intraspecific) and between different species (interspecific) both limit populations; e.g. grey squirrels outcompete red squirrels for food in broadleaf woods; on a woodland floor, taller plants shade out shorter ones. (Detail in "Biotic factors and relationships".)
- [ ] B4 Predation: predators kill and eat prey, keeping prey numbers below what the food supply alone would allow. Foxes take rabbits and the eggs and chicks of ground-nesting birds such as the curlew; pine martens prey on grey squirrels, reducing their numbers where martens are common. Predator numbers are in turn limited by the number of prey.
- [ ] B5 Absence of predators: Ireland's deer (native red deer, and sika deer introduced to Powerscourt, Co. Wicklow, in 1860) have had no natural predator since wolves died out in Ireland more than two centuries ago. Their numbers are limited mainly by food, so in parts of Co. Wicklow they have risen high enough to browse away tree seedlings and stop woodland regenerating; culling by people is used to keep them below the level that damages habitats.

Density-dependent and density-independent factors (one block) (assumed)
- [ ] D1 Density-dependent factors have a greater effect as the population becomes more crowded: food shortage, competition, disease and predation (mostly biotic). They slow growth as a population approaches carrying capacity, so they regulate population size.
- [ ] D2 Density-independent factors affect a population regardless of its size: a severe frost, a flood, a storm, a drought or a fire (mostly abiotic). They can cause sudden crashes at any population size.
- [ ] D3 Human activity changes carrying capacity: draining wet grassland and bogs removes curlew breeding habitat, lowering its carrying capacity; rewetting a bog or planting hedgerows raises it for the species that depend on them; garden feeders increase food for some birds.

Illustrating carrying capacity in one Irish ecosystem (one worked-example block)
- [ ] W1 Rabbits in grassland: food (grass) is the main density-dependent limit; as rabbit numbers rise, grass is grazed shorter and competition increases. Predation by foxes, stoats and birds of prey removes many young. Disease (myxomatosis) causes crashes when the population is dense. Space for burrows (well-drained soil, e.g. on banks and slopes) limits where warrens can be dug. Wet winters (an abiotic, density-independent factor) can flood burrows and kill young. The carrying capacity of the field for rabbits is the population that these factors together allow it to support over time.
- [ ] W2 Diagram: a simple graph of a population rising and levelling off at a horizontal dashed line labelled "carrying capacity (K)", with arrows labelled "limiting factors (environmental resistance)" pressing down on the curve. The shapes of population curves are detailed in "Population curves".
