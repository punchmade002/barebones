## 1. Must cover

Glycolysis (one block, process; numbered stages)
- [ ] G1 Glycolysis ("sugar splitting") is the first stage of both aerobic and anaerobic respiration. It takes place in the cytoplasm (cytosol), is controlled by about ten enzymes in sequence, and needs no oxygen.
- [ ] G2 1. Activation: two ATP molecules are used to add phosphate groups to the glucose (C₆), making it more reactive. This is an investment of energy.
- [ ] G3 2. Splitting: the six-carbon sugar is split into two three-carbon molecules.
- [ ] G4 3. Oxidation: hydrogen (electrons and protons) is removed from each three-carbon molecule by dehydrogenase enzymes and picked up by NAD⁺, forming NADH: two NADH per glucose.
- [ ] G5 4. ATP generation: four ATP are made as phosphate groups are transferred to ADP. Net gain: 4 made − 2 used = 2 ATP per glucose.
- [ ] G6 5. Product: two molecules of pyruvate (pyruvic acid, C₃) per glucose. Pyruvate still holds most of the energy of the glucose.
- [ ] G7 Summary: glucose + 2 ADP + 2 Pi + 2 NAD⁺ → 2 pyruvate + 2 ATP (net) + 2 NADH. No CO₂ is released in glycolysis.
- [ ] G8 Glycolysis happens in almost every living cell, prokaryotic and eukaryotic, which suggests it evolved early in the history of life, before there was oxygen in the atmosphere. One bullet.
- [ ] G9 Other six-carbon sugars (e.g. fructose) and the glucose from glycogen or starch enter the same pathway. One phrase.

Transfer molecules in glycolysis (one block)
- [ ] T1 ATP: used (2) to activate glucose and made (4) by transferring phosphate from the sugar molecules directly to ADP; the net 2 ATP is all the energy anaerobic respiration releases per glucose.
- [ ] T2 NAD⁺: the cell has only a small supply. Glycolysis converts NAD⁺ to NADH, so it stops if NADH isn't turned back into NAD⁺.
- [ ] T3 The fate of NADH depends on oxygen: with oxygen, it passes its electrons to the electron transport chain in the mitochondrion (see "Second-stage process"); without oxygen, it passes its hydrogen to pyruvate (fermentation, below). Either way NAD⁺ is regenerated.

Fermentation pathways (one block, process)
- [ ] F1 When oxygen is absent (or too scarce), pyruvate is not passed to the mitochondrion. Instead, in the cytoplasm, NADH passes its hydrogen to pyruvate (or a product made from it). This regenerates NAD⁺, so glycolysis can keep running and keep making its 2 ATP per glucose. The purpose of fermentation is to regenerate NAD⁺, not to make more ATP: no ATP is made after glycolysis.
- [ ] F2 Lactic acid fermentation (in animal muscle cells and lactic acid bacteria such as those used to make yogurt): pyruvate (C₃) takes hydrogen from NADH and becomes lactic acid (lactate, C₃). One step; no CO₂ is released. Overall: glucose → 2 lactic acid, net 2 ATP.
- [ ] F3 Alcoholic (ethanol) fermentation (in yeast, and in plant cells short of oxygen, e.g. waterlogged roots): pyruvate (C₃) first loses one carbon as CO₂, forming a two-carbon compound, which then takes hydrogen from NADH and becomes ethanol (C₂). Two steps; one CO₂ released per pyruvate. Overall: glucose → 2 ethanol + 2 CO₂, net 2 ATP.
- [ ] F4 "Depending on organism and conditions": which product is made depends on the organism's enzymes (animals and many bacteria make lactic acid; yeast and plants make ethanol), and whether fermentation happens at all depends on the conditions: yeast and muscle respire aerobically when oxygen is available and switch to fermentation when it is not (detail of the conditions in "Investigation: conditions for fermentation").
- [ ] F5 Why fermentation yields little energy: glucose is only partly broken down; lactic acid and ethanol still contain much of the original energy (ethanol is a fuel, e.g. for biofuels).
- [ ] F6 Diagram: glycolysis and fermentation as one flow chart. Glucose (C₆) → (2 ATP used, 4 ATP made, 2 NAD⁺ → 2 NADH) → 2 pyruvate (C₃); then three branches: (a) with oxygen → mitochondrion (second stage); (b) no oxygen, animals and some bacteria → lactic acid (C₃), with NADH → NAD⁺; (c) no oxygen, yeast and plants → CO₂ released → ethanol (C₂), with NADH → NAD⁺. Show the NAD⁺ loop returning to glycolysis. Label cytoplasm and mitochondrion.

## 3. Traps

- Every block is Higher Level only; mark every heading. Key terms (glycolysis, pyruvate, net ATP, NADH, lactic acid fermentation, alcoholic fermentation, regeneration of NAD⁺) are Higher Level; "glycolysis" and "pyruvate" also appear at Ordinary Level in "Respiration as a catabolic process", so don't repeat that subsection's outline.
- Net gain is 2 ATP, not 4: two are used at the start.
- Glycolysis releases no CO₂. In respiration, CO₂ is released only in the second stage and in alcoholic fermentation.
- Glycolysis needs no oxygen but happens in aerobic respiration too; it is not "the anaerobic stage only".
- Fermentation makes no extra ATP: its job is to regenerate NAD⁺ (F1). Students often say lactic acid formation "releases energy".
- Lactic acid has three carbons, like pyruvate; ethanol has two, because one carbon is lost as CO₂.
- Specification wording "six-carbon carbohydrate": glucose is the usual one; G9 notes others. Use "glucose" in examples.
- The equations and everyday examples of fermentation (baking, brewing, yogurt, oxygen debt) belong to "Respiration as a catabolic process"; refer to them, don't repeat them.
- Pyruvate's fate in the mitochondrion (acetyl CoA, citric acid cycle) belongs to "Second-stage process"; T3 names it only.
- FAD/FADH₂ and ATP totals for aerobic respiration are not on the specification.
- No other diagram cues beyond F6.

## 4. What exams ask (topics only)

- Name the first stage of respiration, its location, and its product (pyruvate, a 3-carbon molecule).
- State whether oxygen is needed for glycolysis; state the net ATP gain.
- Name the products of fermentation in yeast and in human muscle; explain how fermentation lets glycolysis continue.
- Name the 3-carbon molecule that enters the mitochondrion.
