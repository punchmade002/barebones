## 1. Must cover

Taxonomy and the hierarchy (one block)
- [ ] H1 Classification is the grouping of organisms according to their shared characteristics; taxonomy is the science of naming, describing and classifying organisms.
- [ ] H2 Organisms are placed in a hierarchy of ranks (taxa), from broadest to narrowest: domain, kingdom, phylum, class, order, family, genus, species. Moving down the ranks, each group has fewer members, which share more features and are more closely related.
- [ ] H3 Worked example, human: domain Eukarya, kingdom Animalia, phylum Chordata, class Mammalia, order Primates, family Hominidae, genus Homo, species Homo sapiens.
- [ ] H4 Species: a group of organisms with similar features that can interbreed to produce fertile offspring. A horse and a donkey can mate, but their offspring (a mule) is infertile, so they are different species. Limitation: the definition can't be applied to organisms that reproduce only asexually (e.g. bacteria) or to fossils.
- [ ] H5 Binomial nomenclature: every species has a two-part scientific name, the genus name (capital first letter) followed by the species name (lower case), printed in italics or underlined when handwritten, e.g. Homo sapiens (human), Escherichia coli (a gut bacterium), Rhizopus stolonifer (black bread mould). It was introduced by the Swedish botanist Carl Linnaeus, whose Species Plantarum (1753) used it for all plants.
- [ ] H6 Species in the same genus are closely related: the lion (Panthera leo) and tiger (Panthera tigris) share the genus Panthera.

Prokaryotic and eukaryotic as a means of classifying (one bullet group)
- [ ] P1 The first division of cellular life is by cell type. Prokaryotic cells have no nucleus and no membrane-bound organelles; eukaryotic cells have a nucleus surrounded by a membrane, and membrane-bound organelles. Cell structure in detail is in "Prokaryotic and eukaryotic cells".
- [ ] P2 Two kingdoms are prokaryotic (Bacteria, Archaea), each forming its own domain; four are eukaryotic (Protista, Fungi, Plantae, Animalia), all in the domain Eukarya. The distinction became clear only once the electron microscope showed cell interiors.

The six kingdoms (one block, one bullet per kingdom: cell type, cell number, cell wall, nutrition, examples)
- [ ] K-B Bacteria: prokaryotic; unicellular; cell walls of peptidoglycan; autotrophic (photosynthetic or chemosynthetic) or heterotrophic (saprophytic or parasitic); reproduce by binary fission. Examples: Escherichia coli, Lactobacillus (used to make yoghurt), Streptococcus.
- [ ] K-A Archaea: prokaryotic; unicellular; cell walls without peptidoglycan; many live in extreme environments. Examples: methanogens in cattle digestive systems; Halobacterium in salt lakes.
- [ ] K-Pr Protista: eukaryotic; mostly unicellular, though some are multicellular algae such as seaweeds (kelp); no tissues of the kind found in plants and animals; some photosynthetic (algae), some heterotrophic; the group includes eukaryotes that don't belong in the other three kingdoms. Examples: Amoeba, Paramecium, Plasmodium (causes malaria), Phytophthora infestans (causes potato blight, which caused the Great Famine in Ireland, 1845–1849).
- [ ] K-F Fungi: eukaryotic; mostly multicellular, made of thread-like hyphae forming a mycelium, but yeasts are unicellular; cell walls of chitin; heterotrophic, secreting enzymes onto food and absorbing the products (saprophytic or parasitic); store carbohydrate as glycogen; reproduce by spores; no chlorophyll. Examples: Rhizopus (bread mould), yeast (Saccharomyces cerevisiae), mushrooms, Penicillium.
- [ ] K-Pl Plantae: eukaryotic; multicellular; cell walls of cellulose; chloroplasts; autotrophic by photosynthesis; store carbohydrate as starch; mostly don't move. Examples: mosses, ferns, conifers, flowering plants such as the daisy and oak.
- [ ] K-An Animalia: eukaryotic; multicellular; no cell walls; heterotrophic, taking in (ingesting) food; store carbohydrate as glycogen; most can move and have nerves and muscles. Examples: sponges, earthworms, insects, fish, mammals.
- [ ] K-T Comparison table with the six kingdoms as columns and rows: cell type, unicellular/multicellular, cell wall material, nutrition, storage carbohydrate, example.
- [ ] K-V Viruses are not in any kingdom or domain, because they are not cells (see "Viruses").

Phylogeny (one block)
- [ ] Y1 Phylogeny is the evolutionary history of a group of organisms; phylogenetic classification groups organisms by how closely related they are by descent from common ancestors, rather than by appearance alone.
- [ ] Y2 A phylogenetic tree shows these relationships: each branch point (node) represents a common ancestor; the closer two species' branches join, the more recent their common ancestor and the more closely related they are.
- [ ] Y3 Genus and species in phylogeny: species in the same genus share a very recent common ancestor; genera in the same family share an older one. Example: the lion and tiger (genus Panthera) are more closely related to each other than either is to the domestic cat (Felis catus), but all three are in the cat family (Felidae); the dog (Canis familiaris, also written Canis lupus familiaris) is in a different family (Canidae), and all four are in the order Carnivora.
- [ ] Y4 Diagram: a phylogenetic tree for lion, tiger, domestic cat and dog, with the nodes for genus Panthera, family Felidae and order Carnivora labelled.
- [ ] Y5 Evidence used to work out phylogeny: shared anatomical structures with the same basic plan (e.g. the five-digit limb of mammals, birds, reptiles and amphibians); embryology; fossils; and, most powerfully, comparisons of DNA base sequences and protein amino-acid sequences: the fewer the differences, the more closely related. Each type of evidence for evolution is in "Evolution: The Origins of Life"; one bullet here.
- [ ] Y6 Appearance can mislead: whales and dolphins look like fish but are mammals (they breathe air with lungs, are warm-blooded and feed their young on milk).

Classification systems evolve (one block, in date order)
- [ ] S1 Early systems had two kingdoms, plants and animals; Linnaeus in the 18th century classified by visible features.
- [ ] S2 Microscopes revealed single-celled organisms that fitted neither kingdom; Ernst Haeckel proposed a third kingdom, Protista, in 1866.
- [ ] S3 In 1969 Robert Whittaker proposed five kingdoms (Monera, Protista, Fungi, Plantae, Animalia). Fungi were separated from plants because they have chitin walls, no chlorophyll and absorb their food; Monera held all prokaryotes.
- [ ] S4 rRNA sequencing by Carl Woese showed that prokaryotes form two very different groups, giving three domains (1990) and the six kingdoms used in this course, with Monera split into Bacteria and Archaea (detail in "Domains of life").
- [ ] S5 The reason systems change: new technology (light microscope, electron microscope, biochemistry, DNA sequencing) provides new evidence, and classification is revised to reflect evolutionary relationships more accurately. Classification is a model that scientists keep testing and improving.

Emerging evidence (one bullet group)
- [ ] N1 DNA sequencing and whole-genome comparisons are now the main evidence for classification and have moved many organisms. Examples: Phytophthora (potato blight) was long classed as a fungus because it grows as threads, but its DNA and its cellulose cell walls show it belongs with the protists (water moulds); DNA evidence placed the giant panda in the bear family.
- [ ] N2 Environmental DNA sequencing (sequencing all the DNA in a soil or water sample) has revealed huge numbers of microorganisms that have never been grown in a laboratory, adding new branches to the tree of life.
- [ ] N3 Example of debate: Asgard archaea, first described in 2015 from DNA in sediment near the Loki's Castle hydrothermal vents in the Arctic Ocean, share many genes with eukaryotes. Some scientists argue that eukaryotes arose from within the archaea, which would mean only two primary domains; this is still debated.
- [ ] N4 DNA barcoding: a short standard gene sequence is used to identify species quickly, e.g. to identify species from tiny samples or to detect mislabelled food.

Using classification principles: dichotomous keys (skills block with a worked example)
- [ ] K1 A dichotomous key identifies an organism by a series of steps, each giving a choice between two contrasting statements (a couplet) about a visible feature; each statement leads either to a name or to the number of the next couplet.
- [ ] K2 Worked example, a key to six invertebrates: 1. Legs present → go to 2; legs absent → go to 4. 2. Three pairs of legs → ladybird (an insect); more than three pairs of legs → go to 3. 3. Four pairs of legs → spider; seven pairs of legs → woodlouse. 4. Shell present → snail; no visible shell → go to 5. 5. Body divided into rings (segments) → earthworm; body not divided into rings → slug.
- [ ] K3 Using it: an animal with eight legs goes 1 (legs present) → 2 (more than three pairs) → 3 (four pairs) → spider. Write the route for one more organism (e.g. the slug: 1 → 4 → 5 → slug).
- [ ] K4 Constructing a key: list the organisms; choose a feature that splits them into two groups; keep splitting each group in two until each organism is on its own. Use features that are easy to observe and don't vary between individuals or seasons (e.g. number of legs, not size or colour); a key for n organisms needs n − 1 couplets.
- [ ] K5 Classifying an unknown organism to a kingdom: use the K-T features in order: does it have a nucleus? (no: bacteria or archaea, separated by cell-wall chemistry or rRNA); if yes, is it multicellular with tissues? cell wall material? autotrophic or heterotrophic? Worked example: a newly found organism is eukaryotic, made of hyphae with chitin walls, and absorbs food from dead leaves → kingdom Fungi.

The importance of classification (one bullet group)
- [ ] I1 It organises the enormous diversity of life into manageable groups, making it easier to study and to find information.
- [ ] I2 Scientific names are universal and unique, avoiding the confusion of common names: the "robin" of Europe (Erithacus rubecula) and the "robin" of North America (Turdus migratorius) are different species in different genera.
- [ ] I3 It shows evolutionary relationships, so features of a new organism can be predicted from its relatives, e.g. relatives of a plant that produces a useful drug may produce similar compounds.
- [ ] I4 Health: correctly identifying a pathogen decides the treatment, e.g. a bacterial infection may respond to an antibiotic but a fungal or viral one won't.
- [ ] I5 Agriculture and the environment: identifying pests, invasive species and endangered species is needed for control and conservation and for measuring biodiversity.

## 3. Traps

- Six kingdoms only: Bacteria, Archaea, Protista, Fungi, Plantae, Animalia. Never use "Monera" except as the historical system in S3. Don't use "Protoctista" as the kingdom name; the specification says protista.
- Fungi are not plants: no chlorophyll, chitin (not cellulose) walls, glycogen (not starch).
- Many older Irish textbooks put Phytophthora with the fungi. It is a protist (N1); say so.
- Genus name capital, species name lower case; never write "Homo Sapiens".
- The domain system's evidence (rRNA) is in "Domains of life"; S4 summarises it in one bullet.
- Evidence for evolution (fossils, comparative anatomy, embryology) is taught in "Evolution: The Origins of Life"; Y5 lists it only as a tool for classification.
- Detailed fungal structure (Rhizopus) and bacterial structure belong to "Microorganisms".
- The key in K2 must be reproduced exactly; don't add or change features.
- Diagram cues: only Y4 (and the key in K2, written as text). No other diagram cues.
