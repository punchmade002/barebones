# Package: bio8-s7 — Investigation: food tests

- **loId:** `bio8-s7` · **chapterId:** `bio8` · **syllabusRef:** 1.2.5
- **Syllabus:** "testing for the presence of nutrients in food including carbohydrates,
  protein, and lipids." Outcome 5: "investigate qualitatively the presence of nutrients in a
  range of food samples, use primary data to support conclusions." Ordinary and Higher level.
  No Higher Level only material.
- **Prepared by:** a higher model, 2026-10-22. Write as one full investigation in the
  NOTES_STANDARD "Investigation" block types: a planning block, one method block per test
  (iodine for starch, Benedict's for reducing sugar, biuret for protein, two lipid tests), a
  safety block, a results-and-conclusion block, and an errors block. The specification names
  no reagent. The four tests below are the standard school tests; the grease-spot (brown
  paper) test was the previous syllabus's lipid test and Sudan III is the common alternative,
  so teach both. Measuring how much reducing sugar is present belongs to "Investigation:
  reducing sugars, quantitatively".

## 1. Must cover

Planning (one block)
- [ ] A1 Aim: to find out which nutrients (starch, reducing sugar, protein, lipid) are present in a range of food samples. Qualitative means recording whether a nutrient is present or absent (from a colour change or other observation), not how much.
- [ ] A2 Hypothesis (example to write): "Potato contains starch but not lipid; milk contains reducing sugar (lactose), protein and lipid." Students predict for each food from what it is.
- [ ] A3 Variables: independent variable is the food sample tested; dependent variable is the result of each test (the colour or observation: present or absent); controlled variables are the volume of food solution and of reagent, the concentration of reagents, the water-bath temperature and heating time for Benedict's, and clean apparatus for every test (to avoid carry-over).
- [ ] A4 Controls: for every test, a negative control (distilled water in place of the food, showing the colour when the nutrient is absent) and a positive control (a known pure sample: starch solution, glucose solution, egg-white (albumen) solution, vegetable oil), showing the colour when it is present. Results for foods are judged against these.
- [ ] A5 Range of food samples (example set): potato, bread, rice or flour (starch); glucose, honey, grapes, fruit juice, milk (reducing sugar); egg white, milk, meat or cheese (protein); butter, vegetable oil, nuts, crisps, cheese (lipid); table sugar (sucrose: a sugar that gives a negative Benedict's result).
- [ ] A6 Equipment and materials: test tubes and rack, test-tube holder, droppers or graduated pipettes or syringes, spotting tile, mortar and pestle (or knife and chopping board), beaker of distilled water, filter funnel and filter paper, hot water bath (a beaker of near-boiling water from a kettle, or an electric water bath) and thermometer, brown paper, labels, safety glasses, gloves. Reagents: iodine solution (iodine dissolved in potassium iodide), Benedict's solution, biuret reagent (or sodium hydroxide solution and dilute copper sulfate solution), Sudan III stain.
- [ ] A7 Preparing the samples (method step common to all tests): 1. Crush or grind a solid food with a little distilled water using a mortar and pestle. 2. Add more water, stir, and filter or let the solid settle, using the liquid (food solution) for the tests. 3. Liquid foods (milk, juice) can be tested directly or diluted. 4. Label each tube with the food and the test.

Test for starch: iodine (one block)
- [ ] S1 Method: 1. Place a few drops of food solution (or a small piece of solid food) on a white spotting tile, or 2 cm³ of food solution in a test tube. 2. Add a few drops of iodine solution. 3. Observe the colour at once. 4. Repeat with water (negative control) and starch solution (positive control). No heating.
- [ ] S2 Result: the yellow-brown (orange-brown) iodine turns blue-black if starch is present; it stays yellow-brown if starch is absent.

Test for reducing sugar: Benedict's (one block)
- [ ] R1 A reducing sugar is one that can reduce (give electrons to) the blue copper(II) ions in Benedict's solution. All monosaccharides (glucose, fructose, galactose) and the disaccharides maltose and lactose are reducing sugars. Sucrose is not a reducing sugar.
- [ ] R2 Method: 1. Place 2 cm³ of food solution in a test tube. 2. Add an equal volume (2 cm³) of Benedict's solution and mix; it is blue. 3. Place the tube in a hot water bath (near boiling, at least about 80 °C) for about 5 minutes. Never heat the tube directly in a Bunsen flame. 4. Observe the colour. 5. Run water (negative control) and glucose solution (positive control) in the same water bath at the same time.
- [ ] R3 Result: if a reducing sugar is present, the blue solution turns green, then yellow, orange and finally a brick-red precipitate forms; it stays blue if none is present. The reducing sugar reduces blue copper(II) ions to copper(I) oxide, the brick-red precipitate. The colour reached gives a rough idea of how much sugar is present (green = a little, brick red = a lot), which is the basis of "Investigation: reducing sugars, quantitatively".
- [ ] R4 Fehling's test is an alternative: equal volumes of Fehling's solutions A and B are mixed with the sample and heated in a water bath; blue turns to a brick-red precipitate if a reducing sugar is present. One bullet.
- [ ] R5 Extension (one bullet): table sugar (sucrose) stays blue. To show it is made of reducing sugars, boil a sucrose solution with a few drops of dilute hydrochloric acid to hydrolyse it to glucose and fructose, neutralise with sodium hydrogencarbonate until fizzing stops, then do the Benedict's test: it now turns brick red. (A non-reducing sugar test.)

Test for protein: biuret (one block)
- [ ] P1 Method: 1. Place 2 cm³ of food solution in a test tube. 2. Add 2 cm³ of sodium hydroxide solution and mix (or add biuret reagent, which already contains it). 3. Add a few drops of dilute copper sulfate solution, one drop at a time, shaking gently. 4. Leave for a few minutes and observe. No heating. 5. Run water (negative control) and egg-white solution (positive control).
- [ ] P2 Result: the blue colour turns purple (violet or lilac) if protein is present; it stays blue if protein is absent. The copper ions react with the peptide bonds in the protein, so the test detects peptide bonds, not single amino acids.

Tests for lipid (one block; two alternative tests)
- [ ] L1 Grease-spot (brown paper) test, method: 1. Rub a small amount of food on a piece of brown paper (or place a drop of liquid food on it). 2. Place a drop of water on another part of the paper as a control. 3. Leave the paper to dry (or warm it gently away from flames). 4. Hold it up to the light.
- [ ] L2 Grease-spot result: a translucent (see-through) greasy spot that does not disappear when the paper dries shows lipid is present. The water spot also looks translucent at first but disappears as it dries, which is why the paper must be dried before judging.
- [ ] L3 Sudan III test, method: 1. Place 2 cm³ of food solution (or a little crushed food in 2 cm³ of water) in a test tube. 2. Add a few drops of Sudan III, a red dye that dissolves in lipid but not water. 3. Shake gently and leave to stand for a minute. 4. Run water (negative control) and vegetable oil in water (positive control).
- [ ] L4 Sudan III result: if lipid is present, a red-stained oily layer forms on top of the water (lipid is less dense than water and doesn't mix with it); with no lipid, the dye spreads through the water or settles as red specks with no red oily layer.

Safety (one block)
- [ ] H1 Wear safety glasses throughout: sodium hydroxide (biuret test) is corrosive and especially harmful to the eyes; Benedict's and copper sulfate solutions are harmful if swallowed or splashed. Wash any splash off skin with plenty of water.
- [ ] H2 Heat Benedict's tests only in a water bath, never directly over a flame, because the liquid can boil suddenly and spurt out of the tube. Use a test-tube holder, point the tube away from people, and take care with near-boiling water.
- [ ] H3 Sudan III stain is usually dissolved in ethanol (an alcohol), which is highly flammable: keep it away from flames. Don't warm brown paper near a flame.
- [ ] H4 Iodine solution stains skin and clothes; wear gloves if possible.
- [ ] H5 Never taste or eat food in the laboratory; be aware of food allergies (e.g. to nuts) among the class. Clean up spills and wash hands after the practical.

Recording results and conclusion (one block)
- [ ] D1 Record primary data in a results table: one row per food, one column per test, recording the colour observed and "present" or "absent" (or +/−); include the control rows.
- [ ] D2 Example of expected results: potato: starch present; glucose, honey, grapes: reducing sugar present; table sugar: Benedict's negative (sucrose is non-reducing); egg white: protein present; vegetable oil and butter: lipid present; milk: reducing sugar, protein and lipid all present; distilled water: all negative.
- [ ] D3 Conclusion: state which nutrients each food contains, supported by the results compared with the controls, and say whether this supports the hypothesis. The tests show presence only, not the amount.

Sources of error and limitations (one block)
- [ ] E1 Colour judgments are subjective; compare with the controls against a white background, and have two people check.
- [ ] E2 Coloured foods (e.g. dark fruit juice) can mask a colour change; dilute or filter them, or use a control of the food without the reagent.
- [ ] E3 Contamination: reusing a dropper or test tube can carry a nutrient from one sample to another, giving a false positive.
- [ ] E4 A very small amount of nutrient may not give a visible change (false negative); some nutrients are inside cells, so the food must be well crushed.
- [ ] E5 Benedict's detects reducing sugars only, so a food rich in sucrose can give a negative result even though it contains sugar.
- [ ] E6 Repeat each test (e.g. three times) to check that results are repeatable.
- [ ] E7 Diagram: a test tube of food solution with Benedict's standing in a beaker of hot water with a thermometer, labelled.

## 3. Traps

- Benedict's must be heated (in a water bath); the iodine, biuret and Sudan III tests are not heated. Students often mix this up.
- The starch result is blue-black, not "black" or "purple"; the protein result is purple (violet), not "blue-black".
- Iodine solution's starting colour is yellow-brown (orange-brown), not "red" or "clear".
- Benedict's detects reducing sugar, not "all sugars" and not "glucose only".
- In the grease-spot test, let the paper dry before judging, because water also leaves a temporary translucent spot.
- Don't heat the brown paper over a flame.
- The amount of reducing sugar and the use of standard glucose solutions belong to "Investigation: reducing sugars, quantitatively"; R3 mentions the colour scale in one sentence only.
- The ethanol emulsion test for lipid is not needed; teach the two lipid tests given.
- Using iodine and Benedict's to follow amylase activity belongs to "Enzymes"; don't add it here.
- The diagram cue in E7 is the only one wanted. No other diagram cues.

## 4. What exams ask (topics only)

- Name the reagent for a named food test, the colour before and after, and whether heat is needed.
- Describe how to carry out the test for reducing sugar (including the water bath) or for fat (including letting the paper dry).
- Name a control for a food test and explain its purpose.
- Name a food test that requires heat (the reducing-sugar test).
- Interpret a table of food-test results to say which nutrients a food contains.

## 5. Practice questions to write

1. 6 marks: "Name the reagent used to test for (i) starch and (ii) protein, and state the colour that shows a positive result for each." Model answer: S2 (iodine, blue-black) and P2 (biuret, purple/violet).
2. 12 marks: "Describe how you would test a food sample for the presence of reducing sugar. Include a control and a safety precaution, and state the result you would expect if reducing sugar were present." Model answer: A7, R2 (with water bath), A4 control, H2 safety, R3 result. Must say not to heat directly.
3. 9 marks: "A student tested milk, starch solution and table sugar with Benedict's solution. Only milk turned brick red. Explain these results and suggest how the student could show that table sugar is made of reducing sugars." Model answer: R1 (lactose in milk is a reducing sugar; starch is a polysaccharide, not a reducing sugar; sucrose is non-reducing), E5, and R5 for the second part.
