## 1. Must cover

The concept of a microbiome (one block)
- [ ] C1 A microbiome is the whole community of microorganisms (bacteria, archaea, fungi, protists and viruses) living together in a particular environment, such as the human gut, the skin or the soil, together with their genes and the products they make. The organisms themselves are often called the microbiota; many people use the two words for the same thing.
- [ ] C2 A microbiome is an ecological community: its members compete, feed on each other's products and interact with their host or surroundings, and its make-up changes with conditions (food, pH, oxygen, temperature, water). A diverse microbiome (many species) is generally more stable and recovers better after a disturbance.
- [ ] C3 Most of these microorganisms can't be grown on agar in the laboratory, so microbiomes are studied mainly by extracting all the DNA from a sample (e.g. faeces or soil) and sequencing it to identify which organisms and genes are present (sequencing in "DNA sequencing"; computer analysis in "Bioinformatics: genome databases").
- [ ] C4 Most microorganisms are not pathogens; the majority in a healthy microbiome are harmless (commensals) or beneficial (mutualists) (relationships in "Biotic factors and relationships").

The human microbiome and how it is acquired (one block)
- [ ] H1 Humans carry microbiomes in the gut, mouth, nose, skin and vagina. The largest is in the gut, and most of the gut's microorganisms live in the large intestine (colon), where food moves slowly and there is almost no oxygen, so most are anaerobic bacteria.
- [ ] H2 Number: a 2016 estimate found the body contains about as many bacterial cells (about 38 trillion) as human cells (about 30 trillion), a ratio of roughly 1 : 1; the older, often-quoted figure of ten bacteria for every human cell is now thought to be an overestimate. The gut contains hundreds of species, and each person's combination is different.
- [ ] H3 Acquiring it: a baby's gut is colonised at birth and soon after. Babies born vaginally pick up microorganisms from the mother's vagina and gut; babies born by caesarean section are first colonised mostly by skin and hospital microorganisms, so their early gut microbiome is different. Breast milk contains sugars (oligosaccharides) that the baby can't digest but that feed beneficial gut bacteria such as Bifidobacterium. The microbiome changes with diet, weaning, antibiotics, environment (e.g. pets, farm animals, siblings) and age, becoming more stable in childhood.
- [ ] H4 Interdependence: the host provides a warm, moist, food-rich, stable home; the microorganisms provide digestion, vitamins and protection (blocks below). This mutualism is an example of how organisms depend on each other.

The gut microbiome and digestion (one block)
- [ ] D1 Fermenting fibre: human enzymes can't digest dietary fibre (e.g. cellulose and other plant cell-wall polysaccharides, and resistant starch), so it reaches the large intestine undigested. There, gut bacteria ferment it anaerobically, using their own enzymes.
- [ ] D2 The main products are short-chain fatty acids (SCFAs): acetate, propionate and butyrate. They are absorbed by the colon: butyrate is the main energy source of the cells lining the colon, keeping the lining healthy; the others are carried to the liver and body cells and used for energy. Gases (CO₂, hydrogen and, in some people, methane made by archaea) are by-products.
- [ ] D3 Vitamins: gut bacteria make vitamin K (needed for blood clotting) and some B vitamins, part of which the body absorbs.
- [ ] D4 Gut bacteria also break down some substances that human enzymes can't, e.g. some plant chemicals and some drugs, and convert bile salts, affecting how fats are absorbed.
- [ ] D5 Herbivores depend even more on their microbiome: cattle, sheep and rabbits rely on microorganisms to digest cellulose (see "Biotic factors and relationships"). The digestive system itself is in "The Digestive System", and the large intestine's absorption of water in "Absorption, transport and storage".

The gut microbiome, immune signalling and protection (one block)
- [ ] I1 Protection against pathogens (colonisation resistance): the normal gut microbiota occupy the space and attachment sites on the gut lining and use up the nutrients that pathogens would need; some make acids that lower the pH and some make antibacterial proteins (bacteriocins) that kill competing bacteria. The skin and vaginal microbiota protect in the same way: in the vagina, Lactobacillus bacteria make lactic acid, keeping the pH low, which stops many pathogens growing (innate defences in "Innate and adaptive immunity").
- [ ] I2 When antibiotics kill many normal gut bacteria, pathogens can take over: e.g. the bacterium Clostridioides difficile can multiply and cause severe diarrhoea; the yeast Candida can overgrow, causing thrush (see "Pathogens"). Repeated C. difficile infection can be treated by faecal microbiota transplant, restoring a healthy microbiome from a donor (see "Microorganisms in industry").
- [ ] I3 Immune signalling: the gut wall contains a large part of the body's immune tissue. Molecules from gut microorganisms (e.g. parts of their cell walls, and SCFAs such as butyrate) are detected by immune cells in the gut lining and send them signals. This "trains" the immune system in early life to tolerate harmless microorganisms and food while still responding to pathogens; e.g. butyrate promotes suppressor T cells (the specification's term; also called regulatory T cells), which damp down inflammation (T cells in "White blood cells").
- [ ] I4 The microbiome also helps maintain the gut barrier: it stimulates the lining to make mucus and keeps the cells tightly joined, so bacteria and their products don't leak into the blood and trigger inflammation.
- [ ] I5 Evidence and limits: animals raised completely free of microorganisms (germ-free mice) have poorly developed gut immune tissue. A less diverse or altered gut microbiome has been linked to inflammatory bowel disease, allergies, asthma and some autoimmune diseases, which fits the hygiene hypothesis (see "Emerging diseases and society"). These are mostly correlations: it is often not known whether the altered microbiome causes the disease or results from it.

The gut microbiome and metabolic health (one block)
- [ ] M1 Metabolic health means healthy body mass, blood glucose, blood fats and blood pressure, without obesity or type 2 diabetes. The gut microbiome affects it in several ways: how much energy is extracted from food (more efficient fermentation of fibre releases more energy as SCFAs); signals from SCFAs that affect appetite hormones and how the body stores fat and controls blood glucose; and inflammation from a leaky gut barrier, which is linked to insulin resistance.
- [ ] M2 Evidence: in a 2006 study, germ-free mice given gut microorganisms from obese mice gained more body fat than germ-free mice given microorganisms from lean mice, on the same diet; this shows the microbiome can affect fat storage, at least in mice. In humans, people with obesity or type 2 diabetes often have a less diverse gut microbiome, but this is a correlation, partly explained by differences in diet.
- [ ] M3 Diet shapes the microbiome: a diet high in varied fibre from vegetables, fruit, pulses and whole grains feeds fibre-fermenting bacteria and is linked to a more diverse microbiome and more SCFAs; a diet high in sugar, fat and processed food and low in fibre is linked to lower diversity.

Disruption and promoting a healthy microbiome (one block)
- [ ] P1 Dysbiosis is an imbalance in the microbiome, e.g. a loss of diversity or of beneficial species, or overgrowth of harmful ones. Causes include antibiotics (which kill beneficial as well as harmful bacteria), a low-fibre diet, infection, stress and some medicines.
- [ ] P2 Ways to promote a healthy gut microbiome: eat plenty of varied fibre (D1, M3); eat fermented foods containing live microorganisms (e.g. live yogurt, kefir); take antibiotics only when needed and complete the course as prescribed (also reduces antibiotic resistance; see "Emergence and spread of infectious disease").
- [ ] P3 Probiotics are live microorganisms taken in food or supplements (e.g. Lactobacillus and Bifidobacterium species) to benefit health. Prebiotics are food components that humans can't digest but that feed beneficial gut bacteria (e.g. some fibres in onions, garlic, leeks, bananas and oats). Evidence that probiotics help is good for some conditions (e.g. reducing diarrhoea caused by antibiotics) but weak or mixed for many claims; most don't permanently change the microbiome.

The soil microbiome: nutrient cycling (one block)
- [ ] S1 Soil contains an enormous variety of microorganisms: a single gram of fertile soil can contain hundreds of millions to billions of bacteria and many metres of fungal hyphae, with thousands of species. Together they cycle many nutrients, not just one.
- [ ] S2 Carbon: bacteria and fungi decompose dead plants, animals and dung, respiring the organic carbon and releasing CO₂; part of the dead matter is turned into stable humus, which stores carbon in the soil for many years (see "The carbon cycle"). Fungi are the main decomposers of wood and of the tough lignin and cellulose of dead plants.
- [ ] S3 Nitrogen: decomposers release ammonium from proteins in dead matter; nitrogen-fixing bacteria (free-living and in legume root nodules) add nitrogen from the air; nitrifying bacteria convert ammonium to nitrate, the main form plants absorb; denitrifying bacteria in waterlogged soils convert nitrate back to nitrogen gas (and some nitrous oxide, N₂O) (steps in "The nitrogen cycle"; no organism names needed).
- [ ] S4 Phosphorus and sulfur: some soil bacteria and fungi release phosphate from organic matter and from insoluble minerals; mycorrhizal fungi carry phosphate and water to plant roots in exchange for sugars (see "Biotic factors and relationships"). Decomposers release sulfur from proteins as sulfate, which plants absorb.
- [ ] S5 Methane: in waterlogged soils and bogs, where there is no oxygen, methane-producing archaea release methane; in drier, aerated soils other bacteria oxidise methane, so soils can be a source or a sink of methane.

The soil microbiome: other roles (one block)
- [ ] S6 Soil structure: fungal hyphae bind soil particles together, and bacteria make sticky polysaccharides, forming crumbs (aggregates) with air spaces between them, which improves drainage and aeration and reduces erosion.
- [ ] S7 Plant health: a healthy, diverse soil microbiome suppresses some plant diseases (by competition and antibiotic production), and some bacteria and fungi promote plant growth (e.g. nitrogen fixers, mycorrhizae).
- [ ] S8 Sustainability links: intensive farming (heavy use of artificial fertiliser and pesticides, frequent ploughing, compaction) can reduce soil microbial diversity; practices such as adding manure or compost, growing clover, reduced ploughing and crop rotation support it (ethical and sustainability issues in "Ethics and sustainability of nutrient cycling").

Climate warming and soil microbiomes (one block)
- [ ] W1 Faster decomposition: a rise in temperature speeds up the enzyme-controlled respiration of soil microorganisms (up to their optimum), so they decompose soil organic matter faster and release more CO₂. Long-term field experiments, in which forest soil plots are warmed by buried heating cables and compared with unheated plots, have measured extra loss of soil carbon as CO₂ from the warmed plots.
- [ ] W2 Positive feedback: soils store more carbon than the atmosphere and all plants together. Extra CO₂ released by warming adds to the greenhouse effect, which causes more warming, which speeds decomposition further. This feedback is a major uncertainty in climate predictions.
- [ ] W3 Permafrost: in the Arctic, frozen soils (permafrost) store huge amounts of undecomposed organic matter. As they thaw, microorganisms decompose it, releasing CO₂ and, in waterlogged thawed soil, methane, a more powerful greenhouse gas.
- [ ] W4 Peat bogs: Irish bogs store carbon as peat because waterlogging stops decomposition. Warmer, drier summers (and draining) lower the water table, letting air in, so aerobic microorganisms decompose the peat and release CO₂; warmer waterlogged peat also releases more methane (carbon sinks in "Climate change and biological strategies").
- [ ] W5 Nitrogen and other cycles: warming can speed up the release of ammonium and nitrate (more nutrients for plants in the short term, but more leaching of nitrate), and increase the nitrous oxide (a greenhouse gas) released by nitrifying and denitrifying bacteria.
- [ ] W6 Drought and changes in community: drought reduces microbial activity, and rewetting dry soil causes a sudden burst of CO₂ release; warming and drought change which species dominate (e.g. the balance of bacteria and fungi), which can change how fast nutrients are cycled.
- [ ] W7 Uncertainty: in some long-term experiments the extra CO₂ release declined after some years, because the easily decomposed carbon was used up or the microbial community adjusted (acclimatised); how large the long-term feedback will be is still being researched.

Exploring microbiomes: an investigation and secondary data (one block)
- [ ] E1 A simple investigation of soil microbial activity and temperature: 1. put equal masses (e.g. 20 g) of the same fresh soil into sealed containers, each with a small open tube of hydrogencarbonate indicator (or limewater) suspended above the soil, not touching it; 2. set up a control with the same mass of soil that has been sterilised by heating in an oven (so its microorganisms are dead); 3. keep sets at different temperatures (e.g. 5 °C, 15 °C, 25 °C) in the dark; 4. record the time for the indicator to change from red to yellow (more CO₂) or the limewater to turn milky. Expected result: the colour changes fastest at the highest temperature and not (or very little) in the sterilised control, showing that soil microorganisms release CO₂ by respiration and do so faster when warmer. Keep it in the dark so no photosynthesis by algae or seedlings removes CO₂. Wash hands after handling soil.
- [ ] E2 Hydrogencarbonate indicator colours (one bullet): red (orange-red) at normal air CO₂ levels; yellow when CO₂ rises; purple when CO₂ falls.
- [ ] E3 Secondary data to explore: published results of microbiome sequencing studies (e.g. comparisons of the gut microbiomes of people on different diets, or of babies born vaginally and by caesarean section), long-term soil-warming experiments, and soil carbon measurements. When evaluating them, check the sample size, whether the study was in humans or animals, and whether it shows correlation or cause (evaluation in "Evaluating evidence").

## 3. Traps

- A microbiome is a community of many kinds of microorganisms, not just bacteria, and not a single species.
- Most gut microorganisms are in the large intestine, not the stomach (which is too acidic) or the small intestine.
- Gut bacteria ferment fibre; humans don't digest it. Don't say humans digest cellulose.
- Links between the microbiome and obesity, diabetes, allergies or mood are mostly correlations; don't state them as proven causes. Don't make claims about the gut–brain axis or mental health; the specification doesn't ask for them.
- Probiotics (live microorganisms) and prebiotics (food for them) are different.
- The 10 : 1 bacteria-to-human-cell ratio is out of date; use H2.
- Warming speeds up decomposition only up to the enzymes' optimum; drought slows microbial activity.
- The carbon- and nitrogen-cycle diagrams and step names belong to "The carbon cycle" and "The nitrogen cycle"; greenhouse gases, carbon sinks and strategies to cut emissions belong to "Climate change and biological strategies". Here, only what the microbiome does and how warming changes it.
- Pathogens, and innate defences in general, belong to "Pathogens" and "Innate and adaptive immunity"; faecal microbiota transplant as a medical use is named in "Microorganisms in industry" and explained here (I2).
- Don't name the microorganisms of the nitrogen cycle (the specification says names are not required).
- No diagram cues are wanted.
