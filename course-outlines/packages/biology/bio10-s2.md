## 1. Must cover

Definitions (one block)
- [ ] G1 A gene is a section of DNA that contains the instructions (code) for making a particular protein (strictly, a polypeptide). Genes are carried on chromosomes; each chromosome holds many genes. (Chromosome structure and the term "locus" belong to "Genetic Inheritance".)
- [ ] G2 (assumed) A protein is a chain of amino acids, folded into a particular shape. About 20 different amino acids are used to make proteins. Amino-acid structure belongs to "Biomolecules — The Chemicals of Life"; one sentence here.
- [ ] G3 A trait is an inherited characteristic of an organism, e.g. eye colour, blood group, seed shape in peas, or whether a person can make a particular enzyme.

From gene to protein (one process block)
- [ ] P1 The sequence of bases in a gene determines the sequence of amino acids in the protein it codes for. (How bases are read in groups is in "The genetic code", Higher Level.)
- [ ] P2 The sequence of amino acids determines how the chain folds, so it determines the protein's three-dimensional shape; the shape determines the protein's function, e.g. the active site of an enzyme or the binding site of a receptor.
- [ ] P3 The cell's machinery reads the gene: the code is copied into a messenger molecule (mRNA), which travels from the nucleus to a ribosome, where the amino acids are joined in the correct order. One bullet, naming the steps only; transcription and translation are described in "DNA Replication, Protein Synthesis and Mutations".
- [ ] P4 So information flows in one direction: DNA (gene) → mRNA → protein → trait.

From protein to trait (one block)
- [ ] T1 Proteins produce traits because they do most of the work and build much of the structure of cells: enzymes control the chemical reactions that make substances such as pigments; structural proteins build hair, skin, muscle and connective tissue (e.g. keratin, collagen); transport and channel proteins move substances; hormones such as insulin and receptor proteins carry signals.
- [ ] T2 Different versions of a gene (alleles) have slightly different base sequences, so they can give a protein with a different amino-acid sequence, a different shape, or no working protein at all; this produces a different version of the trait. (Alleles, dominance and crosses belong to "Genetic Inheritance".)
- [ ] T3 Every body cell contains the same genes, but each cell type uses (expresses) only some of them, e.g. the insulin gene is used to make insulin only in cells of the pancreas, and haemoglobin is made only in developing red blood cells. (Gene expression and epigenetics belong to "Genetic Inheritance".)

Worked examples of gene → protein → trait (one block; give each as a three-step chain)
- [ ] E1 Albinism (one form): the gene for the enzyme tyrosinase → tyrosinase is needed to make the dark pigment melanin → people with two non-working copies make little or no melanin, so they have very pale skin, white or very fair hair and light-sensitive eyes.
- [ ] E2 Cystic fibrosis: the CFTR gene → the CFTR protein, a channel in cell membranes that lets chloride ions out of cells → a faulty channel causes thick, sticky mucus in the lungs and pancreas, leading to lung infections and poor digestion. (How the mutation arises belongs to "DNA Replication, Protein Synthesis and Mutations".)
- [ ] E3 Haemoglobin: the haemoglobin genes → haemoglobin, the protein in red blood cells that carries oxygen → normal oxygen transport. A changed form of one of these genes gives sickle-cell haemoglobin, which makes red blood cells sickle-shaped when oxygen is low (sickle-cell anaemia; detail in "DNA Replication, Protein Synthesis and Mutations").
- [ ] E4 Seed shape in peas (Mendel's trait): the gene for starch-branching enzyme → the enzyme helps convert sugar into starch in the developing seed → seeds with two non-working copies (rr) make less starch and keep more sugar, take up more water while developing, and wrinkle as they dry out; seeds with a working copy are round.

The chain is a simplification (one block)
- [ ] L1 Many traits are controlled by several genes acting together, e.g. height and skin colour in humans, so there is no single "gene for" the trait.
- [ ] L2 The environment also affects many traits: a person's height depends on diet as well as genes; a plant's height depends on light and nutrients. The trait (phenotype) results from genes and environment together. Refer to "Genetic Inheritance" for genotype, phenotype and the limits of Mendelian genetics; one bullet each here.

## 3. Traps

- Codons, anticodons, triplets and start and stop codons are Higher Level and belong to "The genetic code". Don't mention them in this subsection: P1 says "sequence of bases", not "triplets".
- Transcription, translation, RNA polymerase and tRNA belong to "DNA Replication, Protein Synthesis and Mutations"; P3 names the steps in one bullet.
- How mutations arise (substitution, deletion, etc.) belongs to "DNA Replication, Protein Synthesis and Mutations"; E2 and E3 give the gene–protein–trait chain only.
- Alleles, dominance, genotype, phenotype, crosses, chromosome structure, coding and non-coding DNA, gene expression and epigenetics belong to "Genetic Inheritance"; T2, T3, L1 and L2 use the terms in one sentence each.
- Don't say "one gene, one trait" as a rule; L1 and L2 qualify it.
- Don't say albinism is caused by "a lack of the melanin gene"; the gene codes for an enzyme, and the pigment is not a protein.
- A gene codes for a protein; it is not itself a protein, and the trait is not the protein.
- No diagram cues are needed beyond one flow diagram: Diagram: DNA (gene) → mRNA → protein (amino acid chain folded into shape) → trait, with E1 written under it as an example.

## 4. What exams ask (topics only)

- Explain how a change in a gene can change a trait, using an example.
- Outline why the shape of a protein matters to its function.
