# Package: bio2-s3 — Objectivity and data quality

- **loId:** `bio2-s3` · **chapterId:** `bio2` · **syllabusRef:** U2.3–U2.4
- **Syllabus:** students learn about "objectivity: identifying potential sources of random and
  systematic error; evaluating data in terms of repeatability and reproducibility." It supports
  outcome 3 ("explain how reliability, accuracy, precision, error … have been considered") and
  outcome 4 (analysing data). Ordinary and Higher level (no Higher Level only blocks).
- **Prepared by:** a higher model, 2026-10-22. Error, accuracy, precision and reliability are
  defined in "Designing and conducting investigations"; use them here without redefining.

## 1. Must cover

Objectivity (one block)
- [ ] O1 Objectivity: results and conclusions depend on the evidence, not on the investigator's opinions, expectations or wishes. A subjective judgement depends on the person making it.
- [ ] O2 Investigators can unconsciously influence results, e.g. by reading a scale generously, deciding later which results "count", or judging a colour change sooner when they expect it. This is experimenter (observer) bias.
- [ ] O3 Ways to increase objectivity: measure with instruments and record numbers rather than judging by eye (e.g. a colorimeter instead of deciding when a blue-black starch–iodine colour has gone); decide the method, the end-point and how results will be analysed before collecting data; record all results; allocate samples or participants to groups at random; sample at random (e.g. random coordinates for quadrats; method in "Surveys"); use blinding.
- [ ] O4 Random allocation and random sampling mean every individual has an equal chance of being chosen or placed in a group, so the investigator's choices can't make one group different from another.
- [ ] O5 Blind trial (single-blind): the participants don't know whether they are in the treatment group or the control group. Double-blind trial: neither the participants nor the researchers who give the treatment and record the results know who is in which group until the results are analysed. Double-blinding removes bias from both participants' expectations and researchers' expectations.
- [ ] O6 Placebo: a dummy treatment with no active ingredient that looks the same as the real one (e.g. a sugar pill), given to the control group in a trial on people. People given a placebo sometimes improve because they expect to (the placebo effect), so the real treatment must do better than the placebo to show it works. Placebos are a form of the control described in "Designing and conducting investigations".

Random error (one block)
- [ ] E1 Random error: unpredictable variation that makes repeated measurements scatter on both sides of the true value, by different amounts each time.
- [ ] E2 Sources in biology investigations: human reaction time when starting and stopping a stopwatch; judging an end-point colour by eye; reading a scale between divisions; small fluctuations in water-bath temperature; natural variation between biological samples (cylinders cut from different parts of a potato, leaves of different ages, individuals of different fitness).
- [ ] E3 Effect: random error reduces precision (repeat readings spread out). It can't be removed completely.
- [ ] E4 Reducing its effect: take repeats and replicates and calculate a mean (random errors partly cancel out); increase the sample size; use more precise instruments or automatic measurement (data logger, colorimeter); control the variables more tightly.

Systematic error (one block)
- [ ] S1 Systematic error: a consistent error that shifts every measurement in the same direction by the same amount or proportion, so results are all too high or all too low.
- [ ] S2 Sources: an instrument that is not calibrated or has a zero error (a balance that reads 0.05 g with nothing on it; a thermometer that reads 2 °C too high); reading a scale from the same wrong angle every time (parallax); a flaw in the method repeated every time, e.g. never blotting potato cylinders dry so all masses include surface solution, or a leaking delivery tube so some gas is never collected.
- [ ] S3 Effect: systematic error reduces accuracy but not precision: repeats can agree closely and still all be wrong.
- [ ] S4 Repeating does not reduce systematic error, because the same error occurs every time. It is reduced by calibrating instruments against a known standard, zeroing (taring) the balance before use, reading scales at eye level, and improving the method. Comparing results with another method or with published values can reveal it.
- [ ] S5 Exam distinction table (one comparison): random error, unpredictable, both directions, affects precision, reduced by repeats and means; systematic error, consistent, one direction, affects accuracy, not reduced by repeats, reduced by calibration and better method.
- [ ] S6 Mistakes (spilling a sample, misreading a number, using the wrong solution) are not random or systematic error; the result is discarded and the measurement repeated. Avoid the vague phrase "human error" in answers: name the specific source and its effect.
- [ ] S7 Diagram: a number line or target showing a true value with (a) readings scattered both sides (random error) and (b) readings clustered together but all displaced to one side (systematic error).

Repeatability and reproducibility (one block)
- [ ] P1 Repeatable: the same investigator, using the same method and equipment under the same conditions, repeats the investigation and gets the same or very similar results.
- [ ] P2 Reproducible: the investigation is repeated by a different person or group, or with different equipment or a different method, and similar results are obtained.
- [ ] P3 Evaluating data for repeatability: compare the repeats or replicates for each value of the independent variable; a small range (spread) means the data are repeatable and precise; a large range shows high random error or an uncontrolled variable. (How to calculate a range is in "Producing and analysing data".)
- [ ] P4 Evaluating data for reproducibility: compare the class's results with other groups' results, or with secondary data from a textbook or published source; agreement increases confidence that the results are reliable and not due to one person's error or equipment.
- [ ] P5 Results that are repeatable but not reproducible point to a systematic error or a feature of one set-up (e.g. one group's balance or one batch of potatoes).
- [ ] P6 Repeatable and reproducible data increase confidence in a conclusion, but don't make it certain.

## 3. Traps

- Random vs systematic (S5) is the key exam distinction. Repeats reduce random error only.
- Precision vs accuracy: definitions are in "Designing and conducting investigations"; here use them only to state which type of error affects which.
- Repeatability vs reproducibility: same person, same method vs different person or method. Students often swap them.
- Bias in secondary sources and how to evaluate a source belong to "Recognising bias and evaluating sources"; bias here means bias in carrying out an investigation only. Evaluating evidence in the media belongs to "Evaluating evidence".
- Reproducibility as a principle of science as a global enterprise and peer review belong to "Science as a global enterprise"; don't discuss them.
- Anomalous results and the mean are in "Producing and analysing data".
- Diagram cues: only S7. No other diagram cues.

## 4. What exams ask (topics only)

- How can scientists avoid bias in experiments? (large sample, replicates, random sampling, double-blind testing, a placebo, a control.)
- What is a double-blind trial; why use a placebo?
- New to this specification, expect: identify a random or a systematic error in a described method and say how to reduce it; decide whether given data are repeatable or reproducible and justify.

## 5. Practice questions to write

1. 6 marks: "Distinguish between random error and systematic error. Give a biological example of each." Model answer: E1 and S1, one source from E2 and one from S2, and which one repeats reduce.
2. 9 marks: "Explain what is meant by a double-blind trial and why a placebo is used when testing a new medicine." Model answer: O5 and O6, with why each removes bias; mustn't say the placebo is the treatment.
3. 12 marks: "Two groups measured the change in mass of potato cylinders in the same sucrose solutions. Group A's three replicates were very close to each other but all higher than Group B's and the textbook values. Evaluate Group A's data in terms of repeatability and reproducibility and suggest a cause." Model answer: repeatable (P1, P3) but not reproducible (P2, P4, P5); systematic error such as not blotting the cylinders or a balance zero error (S2); fix by calibration or method change, not repeats (S4).
