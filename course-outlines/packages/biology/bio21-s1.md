  system, musculoskeletal system, immune system, various anatomical and chemical adaptations

  short overview, because each has its own chapter.
- **Prepared by:** a higher model, 2026-10-20.

## 1. Must cover

Stimulus and response (assumed)
- [ ] A1 (assumed) Response is a characteristic of all living things: organisms detect changes (stimuli) in their internal and external environment and react to them, which helps them survive.
- [ ] A2 (assumed) The general pathway is stimulus → receptor (detects it) → coordinator (processes the information) → effector (a muscle or gland) → response.

Systems for response in humans (one bullet group per system, each ending with where it is covered in full)
- [ ] H1 Nervous system: receptors in sense organs detect stimuli; neurons carry fast electrical impulses to the brain and spinal cord, which send impulses to muscles and glands. Fast, short-lived responses (The Nervous System).
- [ ] H2 Hormonal (endocrine) system: glands release hormones into the blood, which travel to target organs. Slower, longer-lasting responses, e.g. adrenaline in danger, insulin after a meal (The Endocrine System).
- [ ] H3 Musculoskeletal system: the effector of most nervous responses. Muscles contract and pull on bones at joints to move the body, e.g. withdrawing a hand from heat (The Musculoskeletal System).
- [ ] H4 Immune system: responds to pathogens and other foreign antigens, e.g. white blood cells engulfing bacteria or producing antibodies (Immunity).
- [ ] H5 The systems work together: e.g. in danger the nervous system stimulates the adrenal glands, adrenaline prepares the muscles, and the musculoskeletal system carries out the movement.

Responses in plants
- [ ] P1 Plants have no nervous system or muscles; they respond more slowly, mainly through chemicals and through their structure.
- [ ] P2 Anatomical (structural) adaptations that protect plants: thorns, spines and prickles deter grazing animals (e.g. holly leaves, bramble prickles, cactus spines); a thick waxy cuticle and bark form barriers to pathogens and water loss; stinging hairs inject irritants (nettles); hairy leaves deter insects.
- [ ] P3 Chemical adaptations that protect plants: toxic or bad-tasting chemicals deter herbivores, e.g. nicotine in tobacco, caffeine in tea and coffee plants, tannins in oak leaves; the stinging hairs of nettles contain histamine and formic acid; some plants produce antimicrobial chemicals when infected that limit the spread of a pathogen.
- [ ] P4 Responses to changing conditions: guard cells close stomata when water is short, reducing water loss (detail in Transport in Plants); the sensitive plant (Mimosa pudica) folds its leaves rapidly when touched.
- [ ] P5 A short comparison of human and plant responses: speed, mechanism (nerves and hormones vs chemicals and structures), and the fact that plants can't move away from danger, so protective adaptations matter more.
