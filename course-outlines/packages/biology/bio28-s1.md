## 1. Must cover

Why we need gas exchange (one short block, or the lead of the first block)
- [ ] G1 (assumed) Every cell respires aerobically, using oxygen and producing carbon dioxide (see "Respiration as a catabolic process"). The breathing system exchanges these gases between the external environment (the air) and the internal environment (the blood and tissue fluid): oxygen is taken into the blood and carbon dioxide (and some water vapour) is removed. Breathing (ventilation) is the movement of air into and out of the lungs; it is not respiration (distinction in "Characteristics of living things"; use, don't restate).
- [ ] G2 A large animal such as a human can't get enough oxygen by diffusion through its skin: its surface area is too small for its volume and the distances to the inner cells are too great. It needs a specialised exchange surface (the alveoli) with a transport system (the blood) to carry gases to and from every cell. One bullet (surface area to volume ratio is in "Levels of organisation"; use, don't recalculate).

The breathing tract: structure and function (one structure-and-function block; one bullet per part, in the order air passes)
- [ ] T1 Nose (nasal cavity): air is filtered by hairs and sticky mucus that trap dust and microorganisms; it is warmed by a rich blood supply in the lining and moistened by the mucus, so it does not dry out or chill the delicate alveoli. Breathing through the mouth skips this conditioning.
- [ ] T2 Pharynx (throat): the shared passage for air and food behind the nose and mouth.
- [ ] T3 Epiglottis: a flap of cartilage that closes over the entrance to the larynx and trachea during swallowing, so food and drink go down the oesophagus instead of the airway; choking happens when food enters the trachea.
- [ ] T4 Larynx (voice box): at the top of the trachea; contains the vocal cords, which vibrate as air passes over them to produce sound.
- [ ] T5 Trachea (windpipe): a tube held open by C-shaped rings of cartilage, so it does not collapse when the pressure inside falls during inhalation. The rings are incomplete at the back, where the trachea lies against the oesophagus, so the oesophagus can expand as food is swallowed.
- [ ] T6 Lining of the trachea and bronchi: ciliated epithelium with mucus-secreting (goblet) cells. Mucus traps dust and microorganisms; the cilia beat in waves that sweep the mucus up to the throat, where it is swallowed or coughed out. This is part of the body's first line of defence (see "Innate and adaptive immunity").
- [ ] T7 Bronchi: the trachea divides into two bronchi (singular bronchus), one to each lung; they are also supported by cartilage.
- [ ] T8 Bronchioles: each bronchus branches repeatedly into thousands of narrower bronchioles. They have no cartilage rings; their walls contain smooth muscle, which can narrow or widen them and so change air flow. The finest bronchioles end in clusters of alveoli.
- [ ] T9 Alveoli (air sacs): tiny thin-walled sacs, each surrounded by a network of capillaries; the site of gas exchange (detail in the alveoli block).
- [ ] T10 Lungs, pleural membranes and thorax: the two lungs are spongy, elastic organs in the thorax (chest cavity), protected by the rib cage (ribs, sternum and vertebrae; see "The axial skeleton") and separated from the abdomen by the diaphragm. Each lung is enclosed by two pleural membranes with a thin layer of pleural fluid between them; the fluid reduces friction as the lungs move and makes the lungs cling to the chest wall, so they expand when the thorax expands.
- [ ] T11 Diaphragm: a dome-shaped sheet of muscle and tendon forming the floor of the thorax. Intercostal muscles: two sets (external and internal) between the ribs, which move the rib cage.
- [ ] T12 Diagram: the breathing system in a front view of the chest: nasal cavity, pharynx, epiglottis, larynx, trachea with cartilage rings, two bronchi, bronchioles, lungs, pleural membranes, ribs, intercostal muscles, diaphragm, heart (outline only). With an enlarged inset of a cluster of alveoli at the end of a bronchiole, wrapped in capillaries.

The breathing mechanism (one process block: inhalation, then exhalation, as numbered steps)
- [ ] M1 Principle: air always moves from a region of higher pressure to one of lower pressure. Breathing works by changing the volume of the thorax: when the volume increases, the pressure inside the lungs falls below atmospheric pressure and air flows in; when the volume decreases, the pressure rises above atmospheric pressure and air flows out. The lungs have no muscle of their own; they follow the chest wall (T10).
- [ ] M2 Inhalation (inspiration), an active process: 1. the brain sends nerve impulses to the breathing muscles; 2. the diaphragm contracts and flattens, moving downwards; 3. the external intercostal muscles contract, pulling the ribs (and sternum) upwards and outwards; 4. the volume of the thorax increases; 5. the lungs expand with it, so the pressure inside them falls below atmospheric pressure; 6. air rushes in through the nose and trachea until the pressures are equal. Muscle contraction uses ATP, so inhalation is active.
- [ ] M3 Exhalation (expiration) at rest, a passive process: 1. impulses stop and the diaphragm and external intercostal muscles relax; 2. the diaphragm returns to its dome shape (pushed up by the abdominal organs), and the ribs move down and in under their own weight; 3. the stretched, elastic lung tissue recoils; 4. the volume of the thorax decreases and the pressure in the lungs rises above atmospheric pressure; 5. air is forced out. No muscle contracts, so no energy is used: exhalation at rest is passive.
- [ ] M4 Forced exhalation (during exercise, coughing, blowing, speaking loudly): the internal intercostal muscles contract, pulling the ribs down and in, and the abdominal muscles contract, pushing the diaphragm up, so air is expelled faster and more completely. This makes exhalation active.
- [ ] M5 Comparison (paired points): inhalation vs exhalation at rest: diaphragm contracts and flattens vs relaxes and domes upwards; external intercostals contract vs relax; ribs up and out vs down and in; thorax volume increases vs decreases; lung pressure falls below vs rises above atmospheric; air in vs out; active vs passive.
- [ ] M6 Model (one bullet): the bell-jar model shows the volume–pressure principle when the rubber sheet (diaphragm) is pulled down and the balloons (lungs) inflate. Its parts and limitations are taught in "Models"; refer to it, don't restate.
- [ ] M7 Diagram: two side-view sketches of the thorax, at inhalation and exhalation, showing the position of the diaphragm (flat vs domed) and ribs (raised vs lowered), with arrows for rib movement and air flow; label diaphragm, ribs, intercostal muscles, lungs, trachea.

The brain and CO₂ levels (one short block, Ordinary and Higher)
- [ ] B1 Breathing is controlled automatically by the breathing (respiratory) centre in the medulla oblongata, at the base of the brain (the medulla is described in "CNS and PNS"). It sends rhythmic nerve impulses to the diaphragm and intercostal muscles, so breathing continues without thought, even during sleep.
- [ ] B2 We can also change breathing voluntarily for a short time, using the cerebrum (e.g. holding our breath, speaking, singing), but the automatic control takes over again.
- [ ] B3 The main factor that sets the rate and depth of breathing is the level of carbon dioxide in the blood. When CO₂ rises (e.g. during exercise, when muscles respire faster), the breathing centre increases the rate and depth of breathing, so more CO₂ is removed and more oxygen taken in. When CO₂ falls, breathing slows. How the rise is detected is in "CO₂ as a controlling factor in breathing" (Higher Level); one bullet here.
- [ ] B4 Typical figures (assumed): an adult at rest breathes about 12 to 20 times per minute, moving about 500 cm³ (half a litre) of air in each breath (the tidal volume). During hard exercise both the rate and the depth (volume per breath) increase greatly.

Gas exchange at the alveoli and capillaries (one process block, then one structure-and-function block for the adaptations)
- [ ] E1 Gases cross between the air in an alveolus and the blood in its capillaries by diffusion, from where they are more concentrated to where they are less concentrated. No energy is used.
- [ ] E2 Oxygen: the air in the alveoli has a higher concentration of oxygen than the blood arriving from the body (deoxygenated blood, brought by the pulmonary artery). Oxygen dissolves in the moisture lining the alveolus, diffuses through the alveolus wall and the capillary wall into the plasma, and enters the red blood cells, where it combines with haemoglobin to form oxyhaemoglobin (detail in "Blood").
- [ ] E3 Carbon dioxide: the blood arriving at the lungs has a higher concentration of CO₂ (carried mostly in the plasma) than the air in the alveoli, so CO₂ diffuses out of the blood, into the alveolus, and is breathed out. The blood leaving the lungs (oxygenated, in the pulmonary veins) returns to the heart (route in "Circulation").
- [ ] E4 The concentration gradients are kept steep in two ways: breathing constantly brings in fresh air with a high oxygen and low CO₂ content and removes stale air; and blood flow constantly carries oxygenated blood away and brings new deoxygenated blood.
- [ ] E5 Adaptations of the alveoli, each related to its function (one bullet each): (a) very large total surface area: about 300 million alveoli in the two lungs give an exchange surface of roughly 70 m², so a lot of gas can diffuse at once; (b) very thin: the alveolus wall and the capillary wall are each one cell thick, and they are pressed close together, so the diffusion distance is very short; (c) moist lining, so gases dissolve before they diffuse across; (d) dense capillary network around every alveolus, with continuous blood flow, keeping the gradient steep (E4); (e) capillaries so narrow that red blood cells pass in single file, pressed against the wall, which shortens the distance further and slows them so there is time for exchange. Elastic fibres around the alveoli let them stretch on inhalation and recoil on exhalation (M3).
- [ ] E6 Diagram: a single alveolus with a capillary wrapped around it: label alveolus wall (one cell thick), capillary wall (one cell thick), moist lining, red blood cell, plasma; arrows showing O₂ diffusing from the alveolus into the blood and CO₂ diffusing from the blood into the alveolus; arrows for blood flow from the pulmonary artery (deoxygenated) to the pulmonary vein (oxygenated).
- [ ] E7 Composition of inhaled and exhaled air (assumed; one comparison block or bullet): inhaled air contains about 21% oxygen, about 0.04% carbon dioxide and 78% nitrogen, with variable water vapour; exhaled air contains about 16% oxygen, about 4% carbon dioxide, 78% nitrogen, is saturated with water vapour and is warmer. Nitrogen is not used by the body. Exhaled air still contains much oxygen, which is why rescue breaths in CPR work. Lime water turns milky faster with exhaled air, showing it contains more CO₂.

Relating anatomy to physiology: when the structures are damaged (one short block, two or three bullets)
- [ ] D1 Smoking: tar and other chemicals in cigarette smoke paralyse and destroy the cilia and increase mucus production, so mucus and trapped microorganisms build up in the airways, causing coughing ("smoker's cough") and frequent chest infections. One bullet.
- [ ] D2 If alveolus walls break down and merge into larger air spaces (as in emphysema, mainly caused by smoking), the surface area for exchange falls and the lungs lose their elasticity, so the person becomes breathless; this shows why the large surface area (E5a) matters. Carbon monoxide in smoke combines with haemoglobin, reducing the oxygen the blood can carry (see "Blood"). One bullet each; this is a health link, not a study of a disorder.
- [ ] D3 In an asthma attack the smooth muscle in the bronchiole walls (T8) contracts and the lining swells and produces extra mucus, narrowing the airways, so less air reaches the alveoli; inhalers deliver drugs that relax the smooth muscle. One bullet.

## 3. Traps

- Breathing is not respiration; gas exchange is not respiration.
- The diaphragm flattens (moves down) when it contracts; it does not "move up to push air in". Students often reverse this.
- Air enters because the pressure in the lungs falls, which is because the volume increases. Give the order: muscles contract → volume up → pressure down → air in. Don't say the lungs "suck" air in or that the lungs expand by themselves.
- Exhalation at rest is passive; forced exhalation is active. Don't call all exhalation passive.
- Gases cross the alveolus and capillary walls by diffusion only, not active transport.
- The pulmonary artery brings deoxygenated blood to the lungs; the pulmonary vein carries oxygenated blood away. The full route is in "Circulation".
- How haemoglobin carries oxygen, and how CO₂ is carried in the plasma, belong to "Blood"; E2 and E3 name them in one phrase.
- Chemoreceptors, blood pH, carotid and aortic bodies, and negative feedback in breathing belong to "CO₂ as a controlling factor in breathing" (Higher Level only). Keep B1–B3 to the breathing centre and the effect of CO₂ level. The control of stomata by CO₂ belongs to "CO₂ as a controlling factor in plant stomata" (Transport in Plants).
- "A breathing disorder: cause, prevention and treatment" was on the previous syllabus and is not on this specification. D1–D3 are short health links that show why structure matters; don't write a disorder case study, and give no figures for smoking deaths or asthma prevalence.
- The bell-jar model's parts and limitations belong to "Models"; M6 is one bullet.
- Gas exchange in plants (stomata, lenticels) belongs to "Transport in Plants" and "Photosynthesis"; don't compare.
- The diagram cues T12, M7 and E6 are the only ones wanted. No other diagram cues.

## 4. What exams ask (topics only)

- Label a diagram of the breathing system; give the function of the epiglottis, the cartilage rings, the cilia and mucus, the pleural membranes.
- Describe the mechanism of inhalation (muscles, volume, pressure); explain why exhalation at rest is passive.
- List adaptations of the alveoli for gas exchange and explain how each helps.
- Explain how oxygen passes from the alveolus to the blood, and why the concentration gradient is maintained.
- State which part of the brain controls breathing and the effect of a rise in CO₂ on breathing rate.
- Compare inhaled and exhaled air.

## 5. Practice questions to write

1. 6 marks: "Give the function of (i) the epiglottis, (ii) the C-shaped rings of cartilage in the trachea and (iii) the cilia lining the trachea." Model answer: T3, T5 (keeps trachea open when pressure falls; gap lets the oesophagus expand), T6 (sweep mucus with trapped dust and microbes up to the throat).
2. 12 marks: "Describe the mechanism of inhalation and explain why exhalation at rest is described as passive." Model answer: M1, M2 steps in order (diaphragm contracts and flattens, external intercostals raise the ribs, volume up, pressure below atmospheric, air in) and M3 (muscles relax, elastic recoil, no energy). Must link volume to pressure. Must not say the diaphragm moves up during inhalation.
3. 9 marks: "Explain how three features of the alveoli make them efficient at gas exchange." Model answer: three of E5 (a)–(e), each feature linked to its effect on diffusion (more area, shorter distance, steep gradient, gases dissolve), with E4 for the gradient.
