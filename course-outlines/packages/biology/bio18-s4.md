## 1. Must cover

Joints (one block)
- [ ] J1 (assumed) A joint is a place where two or more bones meet. Joints are grouped by how much movement they allow.
- [ ] J2 (assumed) Fixed (immovable) joints: bones joined by fibrous tissue, allowing no movement, e.g. the sutures of the cranium. Slightly movable (cartilaginous) joints: bones joined by cartilage, allowing a little movement, e.g. between vertebrae, joined by intervertebral discs (see "The axial skeleton"). Freely movable (synovial) joints: bones separated by a fluid-filled cavity, allowing a wide range of movement, e.g. shoulder, elbow, hip and knee.
- [ ] J3 (assumed) Types of synovial joint by the movement they allow: ball-and-socket joints (shoulder, hip): a rounded head fits into a cup, allowing movement in all directions and rotation. Hinge joints (elbow, knee): movement in one plane only, bending and straightening, like a door. Pivot joints: one bone rotates around another, e.g. the atlas turning on the axis to turn the head, and the radius turning against the ulna to rotate the forearm. Gliding joints: flat surfaces slide over each other, e.g. between the small wrist and ankle bones. One bullet per type.

Structure and function of a synovial joint (one structure-and-function block; one bullet per part)
- [ ] S1 Articular cartilage (hyaline cartilage): a smooth, slippery, slightly compressible layer covering the ends of the bones inside the joint. Functions: reduces friction so the bones glide over each other; absorbs shock, cushioning the bones against impact; prevents the bone ends from wearing each other away. Cartilage has no blood vessels, so it gets its nutrients from the synovial fluid and heals slowly if damaged.
- [ ] S2 Synovial membrane: a thin membrane lining the inside of the joint capsule (but not covering the cartilage). It secretes synovial fluid into the joint cavity.
- [ ] S3 Synovial fluid: a thick, slippery fluid filling the joint cavity. Functions: lubricates the joint, reducing friction and wear; supplies oxygen and nutrients to the cartilage and removes its wastes; helps absorb shock.
- [ ] S4 Joint capsule: a tough, fibrous sleeve that encloses the joint, holds the synovial fluid in, and helps hold the bones together. It is strengthened by ligaments.
- [ ] S5 Ligaments: tough, strong bands of fibrous connective tissue (mainly collagen with some elastic fibres) that join bone to bone across the joint. Functions: hold the bones of the joint together and keep them in line; being slightly elastic, they allow the joint to move in its normal directions while limiting movement in others, preventing dislocation. Some lie outside the capsule and some inside the joint (e.g. inside the knee).
- [ ] S6 Tendons: tough, strong, inelastic (non-stretchy) cords of fibrous connective tissue (mainly collagen) that join muscle to bone. Function: transmit the pull of a contracting muscle to the bone, so the bone moves at the joint. Because they don't stretch, all of the muscle's pull goes into moving the bone. Example: the Achilles tendon joins the calf muscle to the heel bone.
- [ ] S7 Pads of fibrocartilage inside some joints (in the knee, the menisci) deepen the joint surfaces, spread the load and absorb shock. One bullet.
- [ ] S8 How the parts work together at the knee (one bullet): articular cartilage on the femur, tibia and back of the patella and the synovial fluid let the bones glide; ligaments inside and outside the joint keep the femur and tibia aligned and stop the knee bending sideways or backwards; the quadriceps tendon, containing the patella, passes the pull of the thigh muscle to the tibia to straighten the leg.
- [ ] S9 Diagram: a generalised synovial joint (or the knee or hip) in section, labelling the two bones, articular cartilage, joint cavity with synovial fluid, synovial membrane, joint capsule, ligament, and a tendon attaching a muscle to one bone.

Ligaments, tendons and cartilage compared (one comparison block, paired bullets)
- [ ] C1 Joins: ligament, bone to bone; tendon, muscle to bone; cartilage covers bone ends (articular) or joins bones (discs).
- [ ] C2 Elasticity: ligament slightly elastic, allowing some movement; tendon inelastic, transmitting force; cartilage firm but compressible, absorbing shock.
- [ ] C3 Main function: ligament holds the joint together and prevents dislocation; tendon moves the bone when the muscle contracts; cartilage reduces friction and absorbs shock.

Antagonistic muscle pairs (one block)
- [ ] A1 Because a muscle can only contract and pull, never push, each movement at a joint needs a second muscle to pull the bone back. An antagonistic pair is two muscles that produce opposite movements at the same joint: when one contracts, the other relaxes and is stretched.
- [ ] A2 The flexor is the muscle that bends a joint (flexion); the extensor straightens it (extension).
- [ ] A3 Each muscle is attached by tendons at both ends: the origin is the attachment to the bone that stays still; the insertion is the attachment to the bone that moves. When the muscle contracts it shortens (and becomes fatter), pulling the insertion towards the origin.
- [ ] A4 The biceps and triceps at the elbow: the biceps lies at the front of the upper arm, with its origin on the scapula and its insertion on the radius; the triceps lies at the back of the upper arm, with its origin on the scapula and humerus and its insertion on the ulna.
- [ ] A5 Bending the arm (flexion): the biceps contracts and shortens, pulling the radius and the forearm up; the triceps relaxes and is stretched. The biceps is the flexor.
- [ ] A6 Straightening the arm (extension): the triceps contracts, pulling the ulna and the forearm down; the biceps relaxes and is stretched. The triceps is the extensor.
- [ ] A7 A second example: at the knee, the quadriceps (front of the thigh) contracts to straighten the leg (extensor), and the hamstrings (back of the thigh) contract to bend it (flexor).
- [ ] A8 Lever action: the forearm acts as a lever with the elbow joint as the pivot. The biceps is inserted close to the elbow, so a small shortening of the muscle moves the hand through a large distance quickly, at the cost of needing a large muscle force.
- [ ] A9 Coordination: the nervous system sends impulses to one muscle of the pair while the other is allowed to relax, so movements are smooth; both muscles of a pair contracting slightly together hold a joint steady in one position (e.g. holding a weight still).
- [ ] A10 Diagram: two drawings of the arm (bent and straight) showing scapula, humerus, radius, ulna, elbow joint, biceps and triceps with their tendons, and labelled "contracted" and "relaxed" for each muscle in each position, with arrows showing the direction of pull.

Modelling an antagonistic pair (one block)
- [ ] M1 A simple physical model: two strips of stiff card or wood represent the upper arm (humerus) and forearm (radius and ulna), joined at one end by a split pin, which represents the elbow hinge joint. Two pieces of string (or elastic bands or long balloons) represent the biceps and triceps; each is fixed to the "upper arm" at one end (origin) and to the "forearm" at the other (insertion), one on the front and one on the back, with their ends standing for tendons.
- [ ] M2 Using it: pulling (shortening) the front string raises the forearm, and the back string slackens: this shows the biceps contracting and the triceps relaxing in flexion; pulling the back string lowers the forearm, showing extension. Pushing on a string does nothing, showing that a muscle can only pull.
- [ ] M3 What the model shows well: muscles work in pairs with opposite effects; a muscle must be attached on both sides of a joint to move it; the position of the insertion close to the joint gives a large movement of the forearm end (A8).
- [ ] M4 Limitations of the model: real muscles shorten actively by contracting and get fatter, while the model's strings are pulled by hand; the model has no articular cartilage, synovial fluid or ligaments; a split pin allows movement only in one plane and doesn't show the rotation of the radius; the model has no nervous control or energy supply (ATP); real tendons are inelastic, while elastic bands stretch.

## 3. Traps

- Ligament joins bone to bone; tendon joins muscle to bone. Students often reverse them.
- Ligaments are slightly elastic; tendons are inelastic. Don't call tendons elastic.
- The synovial membrane lines the capsule; it does not cover the articular cartilage. It secretes the fluid; the cartilage doesn't.
- A relaxed muscle doesn't push or lengthen itself; it is stretched by the contraction of its antagonist.
- In flexion of the elbow the biceps contracts and the triceps relaxes; don't say both contract or that the triceps "pushes".
- The biceps inserts on the radius, the triceps on the ulna; don't attach both to the humerus at the moving end.
- How a muscle fibre contracts (actin, myosin, calcium, ATP, neuromuscular junction) belongs to "The musculoskeletal system as a system for response"; don't repeat it.
- Joint injuries and disorders (arthritis, sprains, torn ligaments or cartilage, dislocations as injuries) aren't on this specification. Don't teach them, their causes or treatments.
- The knee-jerk reflex belongs to "The reflex action"; don't repeat it.
- The diagram cues in S9 and A10 are the only ones wanted. No other diagram cues.

- Distinguish between a ligament and a tendon.
- Draw and label a synovial joint; give the function of the synovial fluid, the synovial membrane, cartilage and ligaments.
- Name a ball-and-socket joint and a hinge joint and where each is found; name a fixed and a slightly movable joint.

- Explain why muscles work in antagonistic pairs.
