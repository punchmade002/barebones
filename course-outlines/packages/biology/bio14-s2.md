## 1. Must cover

Rate and limiting factors (one block, the theory)
- [ ] T1 The rate of photosynthesis is the amount of product made (or reactant used) per unit time. It is usually measured as the rate of oxygen production (e.g. bubbles per minute, or cm³ of gas per minute); it can also be measured as the rate of CO₂ uptake. The oxygen comes from the splitting of water in the light-dependent stage (see "Photosynthesis as an anabolic process").
- [ ] T2 Light intensity: light supplies the energy for the light-dependent stage, so more light means more ATP and hydrogen are made per minute and the rate rises, until something else holds it back.
- [ ] T3 Carbon dioxide concentration: CO₂ is the raw material of the light-independent stage, so more CO₂ raises the rate, until something else holds it back.
- [ ] T4 Temperature: the light-independent stage is controlled by enzymes. Raising the temperature increases the rate (molecules move faster and collide more often with the enzymes' active sites) up to an optimum; above the optimum the rate falls quickly as the enzymes are denatured. (Enzyme action and denaturation are taught in "Enzymes as catalysts" and "Investigation: rate of enzyme activity".)
- [ ] T5 A limiting factor is the factor in shortest supply, which holds back the rate of a process even when the other factors are increased. At any moment the rate of photosynthesis is limited by whichever of light intensity, CO₂ concentration or temperature is furthest from its best value. Raising a limiting factor increases the rate; raising a factor that is not limiting has no effect.
- [ ] T6 Net (apparent) rate: a plant respires all the time, using some of the oxygen it makes. The oxygen given off is therefore photosynthesis minus respiration. In very dim light the two are equal and no oxygen is given off (the compensation point); in darkness the plant takes in oxygen.

Planning (one block)
- [ ] A1 Aim: to investigate the effect of light intensity (or CO₂ concentration, or temperature) on the rate of photosynthesis of pondweed. Write one investigation per factor, changing only that factor.
- [ ] A2 Hypotheses (examples to write): "As light intensity increases, the rate of photosynthesis increases, until it levels off"; "As the concentration of CO₂ increases, the rate increases, until it levels off"; "The rate increases with temperature up to an optimum, then decreases." Each rests on T2–T4.
- [ ] A3 Variables for the light-intensity investigation: independent variable is light intensity (changed by the distance of the lamp from the plant, or measured with a light meter in lux); dependent variable is the rate of oxygen production (bubbles per minute or cm³ of gas per minute); controlled variables are temperature (water bath), CO₂ concentration (same concentration of sodium hydrogencarbonate solution), the same piece of pondweed (same species, length and number of leaves), the same lamp and bulb, time allowed to adjust, and length of counting period. For the other factors, swap the independent variable and keep light intensity constant.
- [ ] A4 Control: an identical set-up kept in darkness (lamp off, tube covered with foil), or a tube with no plant, gives no bubbles. This shows the bubbles come from photosynthesis by the plant in the light, not from gas leaving the solution.
- [ ] A5 Why pondweed: it is aquatic, so the oxygen it gives off appears as visible bubbles from the cut stem, which can be counted or collected; it is easy to obtain, grows quickly and photosynthesises rapidly.
- [ ] A6 Equipment and materials: fresh Elodea (Canadian pondweed), scalpel or scissors, boiling tube or test tube, paper clip (to weigh the pondweed down), beaker of water as a water bath (or an electric water bath), thermometer, bench lamp (an LED bulb gives out less heat), metre stick or ruler, light meter (optional), stopwatch, sodium hydrogencarbonate (NaHCO₃) powder or solutions of known concentrations, distilled or tap water that has been boiled and cooled, and for measuring volume a funnel and graduated tube (or a capillary tube attached to a syringe, called a photosynthometer). Optionally a glowing splint to test the gas.

The basic set-up and measurement (one block, method)
- [ ] M1 1. Fill a boiling tube with water containing a set concentration of sodium hydrogencarbonate, which dissolves to supply CO₂ to the plant, so that CO₂ is not limiting (except in the CO₂ investigation). 2. Cut a healthy shoot of pondweed about 5–8 cm long, cutting the stem cleanly and slantwise under water. 3. Place it in the tube upside down, with the cut end at the top, weighed down with a paper clip. 4. Stand the tube in a beaker of water at a constant temperature (e.g. 25 °C), checked with a thermometer, to keep the temperature steady. 5. Carry out the investigation in a darkened room so the lamp is the only light source.
- [ ] M2 Counting: let the plant adjust to each new condition for about 5 minutes, then count the bubbles leaving the cut end in one minute. Repeat the count three times at each setting and calculate the mean.
- [ ] M3 Collecting (a more accurate alternative): place an inverted funnel over the pondweed in a beaker, with a water-filled graduated tube over the stem of the funnel, and record the volume of gas collected in a set time (e.g. 5 minutes); or draw the gas up a capillary tube with a syringe and measure the length of the bubble. Volume per unit time is more accurate than counting, because bubbles vary in size.
- [ ] M4 Testing the gas (optional): collected gas relights a glowing splint, showing it is rich in oxygen. It is not pure oxygen: some nitrogen and carbon dioxide dissolved in the water also come out.

Method: light intensity (one block)
- [ ] L1 1. Set up as in M1. 2. Place the lamp 10 cm from the pondweed, measured with a metre stick from the bulb to the plant. 3. Allow 5 minutes to adjust, then count bubbles per minute three times (M2). 4. Move the lamp to 20, 30, 40 and 50 cm and repeat. 5. Check the water temperature at each distance and keep it constant (add cold or warm water to the bath).
- [ ] L2 Keeping temperature constant: a lamp close to the plant also heats it, which would change two variables at once. Use an LED bulb, or place a beaker or flat-sided tank of water between the lamp and the plant as a heat shield, and check the thermometer.
- [ ] L3 Light intensity and distance: moving the lamp further away lowers the light intensity, not in simple proportion but as the inverse square of the distance (light intensity ∝ 1/d²): doubling the distance gives a quarter of the light intensity. A plot of rate against 1/d² therefore shows the relationship with light intensity; alternatively measure the intensity at each position with a light meter.

Method: carbon dioxide concentration (one block)
- [ ] K1 1. Make up a series of sodium hydrogencarbonate solutions of increasing concentration (e.g. 0%, 0.25%, 0.5%, 0.75% and 1%) using water that has been boiled and cooled (boiling removes dissolved CO₂, so the 0% tube has very little). 2. Keep the lamp at a fixed distance close to the plant (e.g. 10 cm) and the temperature constant. 3. Place the same pondweed in each solution in turn, allow 5 minutes to adjust, and count bubbles per minute three times. 4. Calculate the mean for each concentration.

Method: temperature (one block)
- [ ] W1 1. Set up as in M1 with a fixed lamp distance and a fixed sodium hydrogencarbonate concentration. 2. Place the tube in a water bath at a range of temperatures (e.g. 10, 20, 30, 40 and 50 °C), using ice or warm water or electric water baths, and check each with a thermometer. 3. Let the tube reach the bath temperature (about 5 minutes) before counting bubbles per minute three times. 4. Use a fresh piece of pondweed for the higher temperatures if the first one stops producing bubbles, and note it.
- [ ] W2 Warm water holds less dissolved gas, so some bubbles at high temperatures can come from gas leaving the solution; the darkness control (A4) at each temperature checks this.

Safety (one block)
- [ ] H1 Electricity and water: keep the lamp, its cable and plug away from the water bath; dry hands before touching switches; don't move a lamp while it is on and hot.
- [ ] H2 Lamp bulbs (except LEDs) get very hot and can burn; let them cool before moving them.
- [ ] H3 Take care with the scalpel: cut on a tile, away from the body. Take care with hot water baths and glassware.
- [ ] H4 Wash hands after handling pondweed and pond water. Elodea canadensis is a non-native invasive species in Ireland: never put leftover pondweed or its water into ponds, lakes, rivers or drains; dispose of it in the compost or general waste.

Results, graphs and conclusion (one block)
- [ ] D1 Record primary data in a table: the independent variable (distance in cm and 1/d², or concentration in %, or temperature in °C), the three counts and the mean bubbles per minute. Plot the mean rate (y-axis) against the independent variable (x-axis), with units, and join the points with a smooth curve or straight lines.
- [ ] D2 Light intensity: as the lamp moves closer (light intensity increases), the rate increases; at high intensity the curve levels off (a plateau), because another factor, CO₂ concentration or temperature, has become limiting. A plot of rate against distance falls as distance increases.
- [ ] D3 CO₂ concentration: the rate increases with CO₂ concentration and then levels off, because light intensity or temperature becomes limiting. At 0% (boiled water) there are few or no bubbles.
- [ ] D4 Temperature: the rate increases as temperature rises up to an optimum, then falls steeply above it as the enzymes of the light-independent stage are denatured; at the highest temperatures bubbling may stop. The curve is not symmetrical: the fall is steeper than the rise.
- [ ] D5 Conclusion: state the relationship shown by the primary data and say whether it supports the hypothesis; on the levelling-off part of the graph, name the factor that is now limiting.
- [ ] D6 Diagram: the set-up: a lamp and metre stick, a heat shield, a beaker water bath with thermometer, and a boiling tube containing sodium hydrogencarbonate solution with an upside-down shoot of pondweed giving off bubbles from the cut end (and, as an alternative, the funnel and graduated collecting tube); label each part.
- [ ] D7 Diagram: two sketch graphs. (a) Rate against light intensity, rising then levelling off, with a second, higher curve labelled "higher CO₂ concentration" that levels off at a higher rate; label "light limiting" on the rising part and "CO₂ or temperature limiting" on the plateau. (b) Rate against temperature rising to an optimum and falling steeply.

Using secondary data (one block)
- [ ] E1 Secondary data are results collected by others: other groups' class results, textbook or published graphs, or data-logger results from an oxygen or CO₂ sensor. Pooling class results increases the sample size and checks reproducibility.
- [ ] E2 Interpreting a graph of rate against light intensity at two CO₂ concentrations (or two temperatures): where the two curves overlap, light is the limiting factor (raising CO₂ makes no difference); where they separate and each levels off, CO₂ (or temperature) is limiting, because raising it raises the plateau.
- [ ] E3 Applying limiting factors (technology and sustainability link): commercial glasshouse growers raise the yield of crops such as tomatoes and lettuce by supplying extra light (artificial lighting), heat and CO₂ (e.g. burning paraffin or propane, which also gives heat, or adding CO₂ gas), keeping each factor just high enough that it isn't limiting. Raising a factor that is not limiting wastes fuel and money and adds to emissions.

Sources of error and limitations (one block)
- [ ] R1 Bubbles vary in size and can be missed or merge when the rate is fast, so counting is imprecise; collecting the volume of gas is more accurate (M3).
- [ ] R2 Other light in the room (daylight, other groups' lamps) changes the light intensity; work in a darkened room.
- [ ] R3 Temperature can drift, especially with a hot bulb close to the plant (L2); check and adjust it.
- [ ] R4 The gas is not pure oxygen (M4), and some oxygen dissolves in the water instead of forming bubbles, so the bubbles underestimate the true rate. The measured rate is also the net rate (T6).
- [ ] R5 The CO₂ from sodium hydrogencarbonate is used up over time, and the plant's response may change during a long investigation; use fresh solution, a fresh plant for each run if needed, and repeat the whole series.
- [ ] R6 Allowing too little time to adjust after each change gives rates that still reflect the previous setting.
- [ ] R7 Repeat each reading at least three times and calculate a mean; compare with other groups (E1).

## 3. Traps

- Change only one factor at a time. When light intensity is the independent variable, temperature must be kept constant (L2); students often forget the heating effect of the lamp.
- Light intensity doesn't fall in proportion to distance; use 1/d² or a light meter (L3). Don't plot rate against distance and call it "light intensity".
- The plateau means another factor is limiting, not that "the plant is full" or "the enzymes are denatured". Denaturation explains only the fall at high temperature.
- At low and moderate temperatures, a low rate is because enzymes and molecules move slowly, not because the enzymes are denatured; only high temperatures denature.
- The bubbles are mostly oxygen from photosynthesis, not carbon dioxide; the plant takes in CO₂.
- Sodium hydrogencarbonate is the source of CO₂ (via hydrogencarbonate ions), not a nutrient or an oxygen source.
- Previous syllabus wording asked for light intensity or CO₂ concentration only; this specification also names temperature, so all three methods are needed.
- The leaf-disc flotation method is a valid alternative but not needed; don't add it.
- How light and CO₂ act within the light-dependent and light-independent stages at Higher Level belongs to "The light-dependent reactions" and "The light-independent reactions"; T2–T4 stay at the level given.
- CO₂ as a controlling factor in stomata belongs to "Transport in Plants"; don't add it.
- No other diagram cues beyond D6 and D7.

## 4. What exams ask (topics only)

- Name the plant used and give a reason for using it.
- State how the rate was measured, how light intensity (or CO₂ concentration) was varied, and how temperature was kept constant.
- Name the independent, dependent and controlled variables, and a control.
- Sketch and label a graph of rate against light intensity or CO₂ concentration over a wide range, and explain its shape (rises, then levels off as another factor becomes limiting).
- Explain the term limiting factor; apply it to greenhouse growing.
