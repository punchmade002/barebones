## 1. Must cover

What linkage is (one block)
- [ ] K1 Building on the definition in "Key concepts of information transfer" (don't restate it): each chromosome carries many genes in a fixed order along one DNA molecule, and a chromosome passes into a gamete as one unit, so any two genes on it travel together.
- [ ] K2 All the genes on one chromosome form a linkage group; there are as many linkage groups as there are chromosome pairs.
- [ ] K3 Writing linked genotypes: show the alleles on each chromosome together, with a line or slash for the pair of chromosomes, e.g. AB/ab means one chromosome carries A and B and its homologue carries a and b. Diagram: two vertical bars (a homologous pair), one labelled with A above B, the other with a above b, at matching positions; label "homologous chromosomes", "linked genes", "locus".

How linkage affects independent assortment (one block, the core explanation)
- [ ] E1 The Law of Independent Assortment holds only for genes on different chromosomes. Linked genes do not assort independently: the alleles on one chromosome move together into the same gamete when the homologous chromosomes separate in meiosis.
- [ ] E2 So a double heterozygote with linked genes, AB/ab, produces only two types of gamete, AB and ab, in equal numbers, instead of the four types (AB, Ab, aB, ab) produced by an unlinked double heterozygote. The gametes Ab and aB (new combinations) are not formed.
- [ ] E3 Results: offspring show only (or mostly) the parental combinations of traits; the new combinations of traits seen with unlinked genes are missing (or rare); there are fewer types of offspring and less variation; and the ratios differ from 9 : 3 : 3 : 1 and 1 : 1 : 1 : 1.
- [ ] E4 In real organisms, linked genes are occasionally separated, so a small number of offspring with new combinations can appear. The reason (crossing over) is not on the course: name it only as the reason and do not describe it. Genes are more likely to stay together the closer they lie on the chromosome; one clause at most.
- [ ] E5 Diagram: side by side, (a) unlinked genes: two separate chromosome pairs (A/a on one, B/b on the other) giving four gamete types AB, Ab, aB, ab; (b) linked genes: one chromosome pair, A and B on one homologue, a and b on the other, giving two gamete types AB and ab.

Worked example 1: test cross with linked genes (one block, standard layout)
- [ ] W1 Key: A = dominant allele of gene 1, a = recessive; B = dominant allele of gene 2, b = recessive; A and B on the same chromosome. Parents' genotypes: AB/ab × ab/ab. Gametes: AB, ab × ab. Offspring genotypes: AB/ab (AaBb) and ab/ab (aabb), 1 : 1. Phenotypes: 1 showing both dominant traits : 1 showing both recessive traits. If the genes were unlinked the same cross (AaBb × aabb) would give four phenotypes, 1 : 1 : 1 : 1.
- [ ] W2 The arrangement matters: if the double heterozygote has the dominant allele of one gene with the recessive allele of the other on each chromosome (Ab/aB), its gametes are Ab and aB, and the test cross gives 1 Aabb : 1 aaBb, i.e. only the two mixed phenotypes. Always read which alleles are on the same chromosome.

Worked example 2: F1 × F1 with linked genes (one block)
- [ ] W3 AB/ab × AB/ab. Gametes: AB, ab × AB, ab. Punnett square: AB/AB, AB/ab / AB/ab, ab/ab. Offspring genotypes: 1 AABB : 2 AaBb : 1 aabb. Phenotypes: 3 both dominant : 1 both recessive (3 : 1), compared with 9 : 3 : 3 : 1 if the genes were unlinked.

A real example (one bullet)
- [ ] R1 In the fruit fly Drosophila melanogaster, the genes for body colour (grey dominant to black) and wing shape (normal wings dominant to vestigial wings) are on the same chromosome. A test cross of a fly heterozygous for both (with grey and normal on one chromosome) with a black, vestigial fly gives mostly grey normal-winged and black vestigial-winged offspring, the parental combinations, and only a few grey vestigial or black normal flies, not the 1 : 1 : 1 : 1 expected for unlinked genes. Don't give numbers.

Recognising linkage from results (one bullet)
- [ ] D1 A dihybrid cross whose offspring show mainly the parental combinations, and fewer phenotype classes than expected or a ratio far from 9 : 3 : 3 : 1 (F1 × F1) or 1 : 1 : 1 : 1 (test cross), suggests the two genes are linked.

## 3. Traps

- Every block is Higher Level only; mark every heading.
- Crossing over, chiasmata, recombination frequency and gene mapping are not required. E4 names crossing over once only as the reason linked genes occasionally separate; no description, no diagram.
- Linkage (genes on the same chromosome) is not sex-linkage (genes on a sex chromosome). Sex-linked genes are one kind of linked genes, but their crosses belong to "Sex-linked traits".
- Linked genes are not "joined" to each other; they are on the same DNA molecule.
- Don't say linkage disproves Mendel's laws: it is an exception to the Law of Independent Assortment only; the Law of Segregation still applies to each gene.
- The unlinked crosses and ratios are in "Dihybrid crosses"; quote the ratios only for comparison.
- Diagram cues: only K3 and E5. No other diagram cues.

## 4. What exams ask (topics only)

- Explain what is meant by linkage.
- Explain how the results of a dihybrid cross would differ if the genes were linked (mostly parental phenotypes, few or no new combinations, less variation, not the expected ratio).
- Explain why linked genes do not obey the Law of Independent Assortment.
