## 1. Must cover

Overview (one short block)
- [ ] O1 The female reproductive system produces eggs (ova; singular ovum, the female gametes) and the hormones oestrogen and progesterone; receives sperm during intercourse; is the site of fertilisation; and holds, nourishes and protects the developing baby until birth. Its organs lie in the lower abdomen (pelvis), protected by the pelvic bones, except the external parts (vulva).
- [ ] O2 Route of an egg: ovary → fallopian tube (where it may be fertilised) → uterus. Route of sperm after intercourse: vagina → cervix → uterus → fallopian tube.

External parts: the vulva (one structure-and-function block)
- [ ] V1 Vulva: the collective name for the external female genitals. It includes the labia, the clitoris and the openings of the urethra and the vagina.
- [ ] V2 Labia: two pairs of folds of skin around the openings. The outer labia (labia majora) are fleshier and covered with pubic hair after puberty; the inner labia (labia minora) are thinner. They protect the openings of the vagina and urethra from infection and damage.
- [ ] V3 Clitoris: a small, very sensitive organ at the front of the vulva where the inner labia meet, with many nerve endings and erectile tissue that fills with blood during arousal. Its function is sexual pleasure. It develops in the embryo from the same tissue as the penis.
- [ ] V4 In females the urethra (from the bladder) opens separately, in front of the vaginal opening, and carries urine only; it is not part of the reproductive system. One bullet; this is the main contrast with the male urethra (see "The male reproductive system").

Internal parts (one structure-and-function block for vagina and cervix; one for uterus and endometrium; one for fallopian tubes and ovaries)
- [ ] I1 Vagina: a muscular, elastic tube about 7–10 cm long leading from the vulva to the cervix. It receives the penis during intercourse, and semen is deposited at its top, near the cervix. It is the birth canal, stretching greatly during birth, and menstrual blood leaves the body through it. Its lining is moist and kept acidic by harmless bacteria (lactobacilli), which helps prevent infection by pathogens (an innate defence; see "Innate and adaptive immunity").
- [ ] I2 Cervix: the narrow neck at the lower end of the uterus, opening into the top of the vagina through a small canal. Glands in it make mucus: for most of the cycle the mucus is thick and blocks sperm and pathogens; around ovulation it becomes thin, clear and slippery, letting sperm pass (detail in "The menstrual cycle"). During pregnancy a thick mucus plug seals it; in labour it widens (dilates) to about 10 cm to let the baby out (see "Pregnancy"). Cells from the cervix are sampled in cervical screening (see "Causes, prevention and treatment of cancer").
- [ ] I3 Uterus (womb): a hollow, pear-shaped organ, about the size of a fist when not pregnant, with a very thick wall of smooth muscle. The embryo implants in it and the foetus develops there. Its muscular wall stretches enormously during pregnancy and contracts powerfully to push the baby out in labour.
- [ ] I4 Endometrium: the inner lining of the uterus, rich in blood vessels and glands. Each month it thickens under the influence of ovarian hormones, ready to receive an embryo; if no embryo implants, its outer layer breaks down and is shed with blood in menstruation (see "The menstrual cycle"). If an embryo implants, it stays and helps form the mother's part of the placenta (see "Pregnancy").
- [ ] I5 Fallopian tubes (oviducts): two narrow tubes, about 10 cm long, one from each side of the top of the uterus towards an ovary. The open end near the ovary is funnel-shaped with finger-like projections that sweep the released egg into the tube. Cilia lining the tube beat, and waves of muscle contraction (peristalsis) move the egg (or the early embryo) towards the uterus, because the egg can't swim. Fertilisation normally happens in the fallopian tube.
- [ ] I6 Ovaries: two almond-sized organs, one on each side of the uterus, close to (but not joined to) the open end of each fallopian tube. They contain follicles, each holding an immature egg; usually one follicle matures and releases its egg each month from puberty to menopause (ovulation). They also secrete oestrogen and progesterone (see "Glands, locations and hormones"). Because the ovary isn't attached to the tube, the egg must be swept in by the funnel (I5).
- [ ] I7 (assumed) If an embryo implants in the fallopian tube instead of the uterus, this is an ectopic pregnancy: the tube can't stretch to hold it and may rupture, a medical emergency. One bullet; a damaged tube (e.g. scarred by infection) raises the risk (see "Fertility control and infertility").

Summary (one comparison block)
- [ ] S1 One bullet per part, "part – where it is – function – how its structure suits it", for the nine syllabus parts, in order from outside to inside (vulva, labia, clitoris, vagina, cervix, uterus, endometrium, fallopian tube, ovary).

The details of gamete production are in "Gametes and fertilisation"; the comparison names them only.

## 3. Traps

- The vulva is the external genitals; the vagina is the internal tube. Don't use "vagina" for the whole external area.
- The cervix is part of the uterus (its neck), not part of the vagina.
- The endometrium is the lining; the muscular wall is the bulk of the uterus. Only the endometrium is shed in menstruation, not the muscle.
- Eggs are not made in the fallopian tubes or the uterus; fertilisation takes place in the fallopian tube, not in the uterus or the vagina.
- The ovary is not joined to the fallopian tube.
- Follicles, the Graafian follicle and the corpus luteum, and the monthly hormone changes, belong to "The menstrual cycle". Name "follicles" only in I6.
- The female urethra is not a reproductive organ (V4).
- Menstrual disorders (e.g. endometriosis, fibroids) were on the previous syllabus as a topic and aren't on this specification; don't teach them here. Endometriosis appears once as a cause of infertility in "Fertility control and infertility".
- Don't describe intercourse beyond the functional points in I1; keep the language clinical.
- Diagram cues DG1 and DG2 only. No other diagram cues.

## 4. What exams ask (topics only)

- Draw and label the female reproductive system; identify parts on a diagram.
- Give the function of the endometrium, fallopian tube, cervix, ovary.
- State where fertilisation and implantation take place.
- Compare the male and female systems.

## 5. Practice questions to write

1. 6 marks: "Give the function of each of the following: (i) fallopian tube, (ii) endometrium, (iii) cervix." Model answer: I5 (carries egg to uterus by cilia and peristalsis; site of fertilisation), I4 (thickens for implantation; shed in menstruation; part of placenta), I2 (mucus controls entry of sperm; dilates in birth).
2. 12 marks: "Draw a labelled diagram of the female reproductive system showing the ovary, fallopian tube, uterus, endometrium, cervix and vagina. Indicate on it where fertilisation and implantation normally occur." Model answer: DG1 with labels; fertilisation marked in the fallopian tube (I5), implantation in the endometrium of the uterus (I4).
3. 9 marks: "Describe how the structure of (i) the fallopian tube and (ii) the uterus suits each to its function." Model answer: I5 (funnel with finger-like projections, cilia, muscle for peristalsis); I3 (thick smooth muscle that stretches and contracts in labour; endometrium rich in blood vessels).
