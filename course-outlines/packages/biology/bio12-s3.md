## 1. Must cover

Fossil records (one block)
- [ ] F1 A fossil is the preserved remains or traces of an organism that lived long ago, e.g. bones, shells, teeth, footprints, or impressions of leaves. Most form when an organism is quickly buried in sediment (mud, sand) in water; the soft parts decay, and minerals gradually replace the hard parts as the sediment becomes sedimentary rock. Whole organisms are occasionally preserved in amber, ice or peat.
- [ ] F2 Dating: in undisturbed layers of sedimentary rock, deeper layers are older; radiometric dating, which measures the decay of radioactive elements in rocks, gives ages in years. The Earth is about 4.6 billion years old; the oldest widely accepted fossils, of simple bacteria-like cells, are about 3.5 billion years old.
- [ ] F3 What the record shows: the oldest rocks contain only simple, single-celled life; more complex organisms appear in younger rocks in a consistent order (e.g. fish before amphibians, reptiles before birds and mammals); most species that ever lived are extinct; and in many groups a series of fossils shows gradual change over time.
- [ ] F4 Transitional fossils show features of two groups. Archaeopteryx, found in Germany in 1861 (two years after On the Origin of Species) and about 150 million years old, had feathers and wings like a bird but teeth, a long bony tail and clawed fingers like a reptile (a dinosaur).
- [ ] F5 Tiktaalik, found in 2004 on Ellesmere Island in the Canadian Arctic, about 375 million years old, was a fish with scales, fins and gills, but also a flat head, a neck, and fin bones like the bones of a limb, intermediate between fish and the first four-limbed land animals. The team searched rocks of that age because the theory predicted such an animal should have lived then: a prediction confirmed (its significance is in "The importance of the theory").
- [ ] F6 The horse: a series of fossils over more than 50 million years runs from Hyracotherium, a small animal about the size of a dog, with four toes on its front feet, to the modern horse, which is much larger and stands on a single toe (the hoof), with teeth suited to grazing, matching a change from forest to open grassland.
- [ ] F7 Limitations: the record is incomplete, because fossils form only in rare conditions, soft-bodied organisms seldom fossilise, many fossils have been destroyed by erosion or movements of the Earth, and many have not been found. The gaps don't contradict evolution, and every new fossil found has fitted the expected order.
- [ ] F8 Diagram: a cross-section of rock layers with fossils, the deepest labelled "oldest", the top "youngest", showing simpler forms in lower layers; optional: three horse feet from Hyracotherium to the modern horse showing toe reduction.

Comparative anatomy (one block)
- [ ] H1 Homologous structures have the same basic structure, because they were inherited from a common ancestor, but are adapted for different functions. Example: the pentadactyl (five-digit) limb: the human arm (grasping), bat wing (flying), whale flipper (swimming) and horse leg (running) all have one upper bone (humerus), two lower bones (radius and ulna), wrist bones (carpals), palm bones (metacarpals) and finger bones (phalanges), differently shaped and sized. This is divergent evolution: one ancestral form adapted in different directions.
- [ ] H2 Analogous structures have the same function but different structures and origins, e.g. the wing of a bird and the wing of an insect (bones and feathers vs a thin membrane of chitin, with no bones). They are not evidence of common ancestry; they show that similar environments select for similar solutions (convergent evolution).
- [ ] H3 Vestigial structures are reduced structures with little or no function in the organism, which were functional in its ancestors: the human coccyx (the remains of a tail), the human appendix (a much-reduced part of the gut, larger in plant-eating mammals), the small pelvic and leg bones inside the bodies of some whales, and the tiny wings of flightless birds such as the kiwi. They make sense only if the organism descended from ancestors that used them.
- [ ] H4 Diagram: the forelimbs of a human, a bat and a whale side by side, each bone group shaded the same way in all three; label humerus, radius, ulna, carpals, metacarpals, phalanges.

Embryology (one block)
- [ ] M1 Comparative embryology compares the development of embryos. The early embryos of all vertebrates (fish, amphibians, reptiles, birds, mammals) look very similar: all have a tail and a series of pouches in the throat region (pharyngeal pouches).
- [ ] M2 In fish these pouches develop into gills; in humans they develop into parts of the ear, jaw and throat, and the tail is reduced to the coccyx. The shared early stages suggest that all vertebrates inherited the same basic plan of development from a common ancestor, which was then modified in each group.
- [ ] M3 Limitations: the similarity is in early development, not adult structures; embryos do not pass through the adult forms of their ancestors. In the 19th century Ernst Haeckel published drawings that exaggerated the similarity of embryos; modern photographs confirm the shared features, but not the exaggeration.
- [ ] M4 Diagram: early embryos of a fish, a chicken and a human side by side, labelled tail and pharyngeal pouches.

Phylogeny and molecular evidence (one block)
- [ ] P1 Phylogeny is the evolutionary history of a group; phylogenetic trees show which species share the most recent common ancestors (how to read them is in "Classification"). Evolution predicts that species that are closely related by anatomy and fossils should also have the most similar DNA and proteins, because they have had less time to accumulate differences since their common ancestor.
- [ ] P2 All living things use DNA, the same four bases and almost the same genetic code (see "The genetic code"), and the same ATP: evidence that all life shares one origin.
- [ ] P3 DNA comparisons: human and chimpanzee DNA is about 98–99% identical in comparable sequences; human DNA is less similar to that of a mouse and less again to that of a fish.
- [ ] P4 Protein comparisons: cytochrome c, a protein used in aerobic respiration in nearly all eukaryotes, has an identical amino-acid sequence in humans and chimpanzees, differs by one amino acid between humans and rhesus monkeys, and differs more in more distantly related organisms such as yeast.
- [ ] P5 The trees built from DNA and proteins largely agree with those built from anatomy and fossils. Independent lines of evidence giving the same pattern is strong support for evolution. Molecular evidence has also corrected some classifications based on appearance (e.g. the domains of life, in "Domains of life").

Antibiotic resistance (one process block)
- [ ] A1 An antibiotic is a drug that kills or stops the growth of bacteria (antibiotics have no effect on viruses). Antibiotic resistance is the ability of bacteria to survive an antibiotic that would normally kill them.
- [ ] A2 Mechanism (natural selection, step by step): 1. a bacterial population varies; a few bacteria carry a mutation (or a plasmid gene) that makes them resistant, e.g. by producing an enzyme that breaks down the antibiotic; 2. the antibiotic kills the non-resistant bacteria; 3. the resistant bacteria survive and, with less competition, reproduce rapidly by binary fission, as often as every 20 minutes in ideal conditions; 4. they pass the resistance gene to their offspring, and can pass plasmids carrying it to other bacteria, even of other species; 5. the population is soon mostly resistant, and the antibiotic no longer works.
- [ ] A3 The resistance mutations are present before the antibiotic is used; the antibiotic selects them but does not cause them. In 1952 Joshua and Esther Lederberg showed this by growing bacteria that had never met the antibiotic and finding resistant colonies already among them.
- [ ] A4 Example: MRSA (methicillin-resistant Staphylococcus aureus), resistant to many common antibiotics, causes infections that are hard to treat, especially in hospitals.
- [ ] A5 Why it is evidence: evolution by natural selection is observed directly, within years, because bacteria have huge populations and very short generation times. Overuse of antibiotics (e.g. prescribing them for viral infections, or in farm animals) speeds it up by applying selection more often. (Its health and sustainability impacts are in "Science, scientists and society" and "The cross-cutting themes"; refer, don't repeat.)
- [ ] A6 Diagram: four panels of a bacterial population: mostly non-resistant cells with a few resistant ones (shaded) → antibiotic added, non-resistant cells die → resistant cells multiply → population mostly resistant.

Natural selection observed in the wild (one block; the specification's "etc.")
- [ ] O1 Peter and Rosemary Grant studied the medium ground finch on the Galápagos island of Daphne Major. In a drought in 1977, small soft seeds became scarce and mainly large, hard seeds were left. Most finches died, but birds with deeper (larger) beaks, which could crack the hard seeds, survived more, and their offspring had, on average, deeper beaks than the generation before. Natural selection was measured happening in one generation.
- [ ] O2 Biogeography (the distribution of species): species on oceanic islands resemble those on the nearest mainland rather than those on islands with similar conditions elsewhere, e.g. the Galápagos species resemble South American species. This is explained by colonisation from the mainland followed by evolution, not by separate creation for each environment.

Speciation (one block)
- [ ] S1 Speciation is the formation of new species. A species is a group that can interbreed to produce fertile offspring (defined in "Key concepts of information transfer"), so a new species has formed when two populations can no longer interbreed: they are reproductively isolated.
- [ ] S2 Speciation by geographical isolation, step by step: 1. a population is split by a barrier, such as a sea, a mountain range or a river, so the two groups no longer interbreed; 2. different mutations arise in each; 3. the environments differ, so natural selection favours different characteristics in each; 4. over many generations the two populations become so different that they could not interbreed even if they met; they are now separate species.
- [ ] S3 Example: the Galápagos finches. More than a dozen species of finch on the Galápagos are descended from a common ancestor that arrived from the South American mainland. Populations on different islands, eating different foods, evolved beaks of different shapes, e.g. large, deep beaks for cracking seeds and thin, pointed beaks for catching insects.
- [ ] S4 Speciation without geographical isolation: in plants, a new species can form in one or a few generations when a change in chromosome number (polyploidy, in "Variation") makes offspring unable to breed with the parent species. Example: the cordgrass Spartina anglica arose in southern England in the late 1800s from a cross between a native European cordgrass and an introduced American one, followed by doubling of the chromosomes, which made the hybrid fertile. It is now a separate, widespread species, including on Irish coasts.
- [ ] S5 Why it is evidence: new species have been seen to form, and the pattern of species (groups of similar species on neighbouring islands) is what the theory predicts.
- [ ] S6 Diagram: a flow diagram of geographical speciation: one population → barrier divides it → different mutations and selection on each side → two species that can't interbreed.

Weighing the evidence (one block)
- [ ] W1 Each source has limitations (incomplete fossil record; embryos similar only early on; analogous structures can mislead), but they are independent of each other and all point to the same conclusion: common descent with change over time, driven largely by natural selection. Antibiotic resistance, the finch beaks, the peppered moth (in "The theory of evolution by natural selection") and new species such as Spartina anglica show evolution happening now.

## 3. Traps

- Homologous vs analogous: only homologous structures show common ancestry. Don't list bird and insect wings as homologous.
- Don't say humans evolved from chimpanzees or monkeys: they share a common ancestor.
- Don't say "embryos pass through fish stages" or quote "ontogeny recapitulates phylogeny" as true (M3).
- Antibiotics don't cause resistance mutations (A3); bacteria, not people, become resistant; antibiotics don't affect viruses. Don't use "finish the course" as a scientific fact; the cause is selection by antibiotic use.
- Antibiotic resistance as a factor in the emergence and spread of disease, and its control, is in "Emergence and spread of infectious disease"; here it is evidence for evolution. How antibiotics work, and resistance mechanisms beyond the one example in A2, are not required; the health and sustainability impacts of resistance are in "Science, scientists and society" and "The cross-cutting themes" (refer, don't repeat).
- Reading phylogenetic trees and the lion/tiger/cat example belong to "Classification"; the universal genetic code is in "The genetic code"; the domains of life in "Domains of life". Refer by title.
- Mechanism of natural selection and the peppered moth belong to "The theory of evolution by natural selection"; polyploidy and mutation to "Variation". Refer, don't repeat.
- Why evolution is "only a theory" is a misunderstanding answered in "The importance of the theory"; F5 and W1 refer there.
