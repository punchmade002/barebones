## 1. Must cover

Why nutrients cycle (one block)
- [ ] W1 A biogeochemical cycle (nutrient cycle) is the continuous movement of a chemical element between living things (bio) and the non-living environment (geo: air, water, rocks and soil). Matter is not created or destroyed: the same atoms are used again and again, whereas energy flows through an ecosystem once and is lost as heat (energy flow is in "Energy and nutrient transfer"; one phrase only).
- [ ] W2 (assumed) Why carbon matters: carbon is the element at the centre of every biomolecule (carbohydrates, lipids, proteins and nucleic acids; see "Biomolecules and metabolism"), so every organism needs a constant supply. Producers get it as CO₂; consumers get it already built into food.
- [ ] W3 A store (reservoir) is a place where carbon is held: the atmosphere (as CO₂, and a little methane, CH₄); living organisms (biomass); dead organic matter in soil; the oceans (dissolved CO₂, hydrogencarbonate and carbonate); rocks such as limestone and chalk (calcium carbonate); and fossil fuels. A process (flux) moves carbon from one store to another.
- [ ] W4 CO₂ makes up only about 0.04% of the air (over 420 parts per million), yet it is the source of carbon for almost all life on Earth.

Processes that remove carbon from the atmosphere (one block)
- [ ] R1 Photosynthesis by plants, algae and phytoplankton fixes CO₂ into glucose and then into the rest of their biomass (carbon sink: see "Photosynthesis as an anabolic process"; don't redefine). This is the only major route by which carbon enters living things.
- [ ] R2 Dissolving: CO₂ dissolves in the surface of the oceans, lakes and rivers; aquatic producers take it up for photosynthesis.
- [ ] R3 Shell and skeleton building: many marine organisms (e.g. corals, molluscs and some phytoplankton) combine dissolved carbonate with calcium to make calcium carbonate shells and skeletons. When they die these settle on the sea floor and, over millions of years, form limestone and chalk, a very long-term store.

Processes that move carbon through living things (one block)
- [ ] F1 Feeding: carbon in the producer's biomass passes to herbivores and then carnivores when they eat and digest it; at each step most of it is respired away rather than built into the consumer's body (trophic levels are in "Energy and nutrient transfer").
- [ ] F2 Egestion and excretion: carbon leaves animals in faeces and in wastes such as urea, and enters the dead-organic-matter store, as do shed leaves and dead organisms.

Processes that return carbon to the atmosphere (one block)
- [ ] B1 Respiration by plants, animals and microorganisms breaks down glucose and releases CO₂ (detail in "Respiration as a catabolic process"; one bullet). Plants respire all the time, day and night; over a growing season photosynthesis exceeds respiration, so the plant gains carbon.
- [ ] B2 Decomposers: bacteria and fungi feed saprophytically on dead organisms, faeces and fallen leaves. They secrete enzymes that digest the organic matter outside their cells, absorb the products, and respire them, releasing CO₂ (and minerals). Without decomposers, carbon would stay locked in dead matter and the CO₂ supply for photosynthesis would run out. (Structure and nutrition of bacteria and fungi are in "Bacteria and fungi"; soil microbiomes in "The microbiome"; use the terms only.)
- [ ] B3 Conditions for decomposition: decomposers work fastest when it is warm, moist and well aerated (they respire aerobically). In cold, waterlogged, acidic or oxygen-poor places decomposition is very slow, so dead matter builds up (e.g. peat in bogs).
- [ ] B4 Anaerobic decomposition: where there is no oxygen (waterlogged soils, bogs, rice paddies, landfill, slurry tanks and the stomachs of cattle and sheep), some microorganisms break organic matter down to methane (CH₄) instead of CO₂. These methane-producing microorganisms are archaea (see "Domains of life"; no species names). Methane in the air is slowly converted to CO₂.
- [ ] B5 Combustion: burning biomass (wood, peat, forest fires) or fossil fuels combines the carbon with oxygen and releases it as CO₂ very quickly.
- [ ] B6 Natural geological release: volcanoes release CO₂ from rocks, and the weathering of limestone releases some; small compared with the biological fluxes and with fossil-fuel burning.

Fossil fuels (one block)
- [ ] FF1 Fossil fuels (coal, oil and natural gas) are the remains of organisms that lived millions of years ago and were buried before they could decompose fully: coal mainly from land plants in ancient swamps; oil and natural gas mainly from tiny marine organisms (plankton) buried in sea-floor mud. Heat and pressure over millions of years changed them into fuels.
- [ ] FF2 The carbon in fossil fuels was fixed by photosynthesis long ago, so they store ancient solar energy and carbon. Peat is an early stage in the same process, which is why it is sometimes counted with them.
- [ ] FF3 In the natural cycle almost no carbon leaves this store. Burning fossil fuels since the Industrial Revolution moves carbon locked away for millions of years into the atmosphere in a few centuries, far faster than photosynthesis and the oceans can take it back, so atmospheric CO₂ rises (consequences in "Climate change and biological strategies").

Carbon sinks and sources in the cycle (one block; builds on, does not repeat, "Photosynthesis as an anabolic process")
- [ ] S1 Using the cycle, a store acts as a sink when its inputs exceed its outputs (it gains carbon) and as a source when its outputs exceed its inputs. The same store can switch: a growing forest is a sink; a mature forest roughly balances; a burning or felled forest is a source; a healthy wet bog is a sink; a drained or burning bog is a source.
- [ ] S2 The main natural sinks shown on a carbon-cycle diagram: land plants (forests and grassland, with the soil carbon under them); algae and phytoplankton in the oceans (with dissolving CO₂ and shells sinking to the sea floor); bogs and other wetlands; limestone and fossil fuels as very long-term stores. Their comparison and the strategies that use them are in "Climate change and biological strategies".
- [ ] S3 Balance: before large-scale fossil-fuel burning, the CO₂ taken up by photosynthesis and the oceans each year was roughly balanced by the CO₂ released by respiration, decomposition and natural fires, so atmospheric CO₂ stayed nearly constant (about 280 parts per million before the Industrial Revolution). Deforestation and fossil-fuel burning have tipped the balance.
- [ ] S4 Seasonal pattern: atmospheric CO₂ falls each northern summer (photosynthesis exceeds respiration in the large land area of the northern hemisphere) and rises each winter (respiration and decomposition exceed photosynthesis), on top of the long-term rise. This is a good example of reading the model.

Modelling the carbon cycle (one block, diagram and use of the model)
- [ ] M1 Diagram: the carbon cycle drawn as boxes for the stores (CO₂ in the air; green plants and algae; animals; dead organisms and wastes; decomposers (bacteria and fungi); fossil fuels; oceans) joined by labelled arrows: photosynthesis (air → plants); feeding (plants → animals); death and excretion (plants and animals → dead matter); decomposition (dead matter → decomposers); respiration (from plants, animals and decomposers → air); fossilisation (dead matter → fossil fuels, over millions of years); combustion (fossil fuels and wood → air); dissolving (air ↔ oceans).
- [ ] M2 Using the model to predict, worked examples (one bullet each): (a) cutting down and burning a forest removes photosynthesis and adds combustion and decomposition of the remains, so atmospheric CO₂ rises; (b) draining a bog lets air in, aerobic decomposers break down the peat, so CO₂ is released; (c) if decomposers were killed (e.g. soil made very acidic or polluted), dead matter would accumulate, minerals would not be returned and plant growth would eventually fall.
- [ ] M3 Limitations of the model (one bullet each): a diagram shows the processes but not how much carbon moves or how fast; it treats each store as one box, although the oceans, soils and forests each hold carbon for very different lengths of time; it simplifies feedbacks (e.g. warming speeds up decomposition, releasing more CO₂); it is a snapshot that hides seasonal and long-term change. (General model limitations are in "Models".)

## 3. Traps

- Photosynthesis as a carbon sink (definition, peat and wood holding carbon for centuries or millennia) is already in "Photosynthesis as an anabolic process": use "carbon sink" without redefining it and don't repeat its bullets. S1 adds the sink/source distinction only.
- Respiration equations and stages belong to "Respiration as a catabolic process"; B1 is one bullet.
- Don't name any decomposer species (the specification says names of microorganisms are not required). "Bacteria and fungi" and "archaea" only.
- Decomposers release CO₂ by respiring, not by "breaking down into CO₂" directly; say they digest the matter and respire the products.
- Plants respire as well as photosynthesise; don't draw the respiration arrow from animals only.
- Fossil fuels are not formed from dinosaurs; coal is from plants, oil and gas from marine plankton.
- Don't say CO₂ is the most abundant greenhouse gas or that it is "harmful pollution" here: the greenhouse effect and climate change belong to "Climate change and biological strategies". FF3 and S3 state only that atmospheric CO₂ rises.
- Human impacts and their ethics (deforestation, peat extraction, justice) belong to "Ethics and sustainability of nutrient cycling"; M2 gives them only as predictions from the model.
- Energy flow and pyramids belong to "Energy and nutrient transfer".
- Ocean acidification is in "Climate change and biological strategies", not here.
- Diagram cue M1 only. No other diagram cues.
