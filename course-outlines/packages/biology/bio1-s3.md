# Package: bio1-s3 — Recognising bias and evaluating sources

- **loId:** `bio1-s3` · **chapterId:** `bio1` · **syllabusRef:** U1.2
- **Syllabus:** "recognising bias." Outcome U1.2: "conduct research relevant to a scientific
  issue, evaluate different sources of information including secondary data, understanding that
  a source may lack detail or show bias." Ordinary and Higher level. This subsection is about
  sources (who produced the information, and how far to trust it). Weighing the strength of the
  evidence inside a claim, and media arguments, are in "Evaluating evidence".
- **Prepared by:** a higher model, 2026-10-22.

## 1. Must cover

Key terms (one block)
- [ ] T1 A source is anything that provides information: a journal article, a textbook, a website, a news report, a government or agency report, a company leaflet, a video, a social media post.
- [ ] T2 A primary source is the original report of research by the people who did it, e.g. a research paper in a peer-reviewed journal. A secondary source summarises or interprets primary sources, e.g. a review article, a textbook, a news report or a documentary. A tertiary source compiles secondary sources, e.g. an encyclopaedia.
- [ ] T3 Primary data are data collected by yourself; secondary data are data collected by somebody else (the specification's definitions), e.g. a table of national vaccination rates, a population survey, or the results in a published paper. A research paper is a primary source, but its data are secondary data to a student who uses them.
- [ ] T4 Bias is a one-sided influence that pushes information or its interpretation away from the truth in a consistent direction. A biased source is not necessarily false, but it selects, presents or interprets information to favour one view.

Conducting research on a scientific issue (one block, numbered steps; this is the research the Biology in Practice Investigation requires, see "The Biology in Practice Investigation")
- [ ] RS1 1. Define the issue and a focused research question (e.g. "Does eating ultra-processed food raise the risk of type 2 diabetes?" rather than "Is food bad for you?").
- [ ] RS2 2. Choose keywords, including scientific terms and synonyms, and search suitable places: the school or public library, scientific search engines and databases (e.g. Google Scholar, PubMed for medical research), the websites of scientific and public bodies, and textbooks.
- [ ] RS3 3. Gather several different types of source, including some primary sources and some secondary data, and sources that take different views.
- [ ] RS4 4. Record each source as you use it: author or organisation, date, title, where it was published, web address and the date you accessed it, so it can be referenced and checked.
- [ ] RS5 5. Evaluate each source with the criteria below; note what it lacks and any bias.
- [ ] RS6 6. Cross-check (corroborate) important claims in independent sources; a claim found in several independent reliable sources is more trustworthy than one found in a single source.
- [ ] RS7 7. Separate facts, evidence and opinions; summarise in your own words; reference every source; copying another's words or ideas without acknowledgement is plagiarism.

Criteria for evaluating a source (one block; each criterion with the question to ask and a good vs poor sign)
- [ ] E1 Authority: who wrote it, and are they qualified and named? Good: named scientists at a university, research institute or public health body. Poor: anonymous author, or a celebrity or influencer with no expertise.
- [ ] E2 Peer review and publisher: has it been checked by experts before publication? A peer-reviewed journal article is more reliable than a blog or press release (see "Science as a global enterprise").
- [ ] E3 Currency: when was it published or updated? Biology moves quickly, so older sources may be out of date (e.g. a source describing a five-kingdom classification). Older sources are still useful for historical background.
- [ ] E4 Purpose: why was it written: to inform, to teach, to persuade, to sell or to entertain? A source written to sell or persuade is more likely to be biased.
- [ ] E5 Evidence and references: does it give data, describe how they were collected, and reference its sources so they can be checked? Claims such as "studies show" or "scientists say" with no reference are a warning sign.
- [ ] E6 Relevance: does it address the research question, for the right organism, population or place? (Relevance of evidence is developed in "Evaluating evidence".)
- [ ] E7 Accuracy and consistency: does it agree with other reliable sources and with established science? Are there factual errors, or emotive or exaggerated language?
- [ ] E8 A memory aid used in schools and libraries is the CRAAP test: Currency, Relevance, Authority, Accuracy, Purpose.

A source may lack detail (one block: what missing detail looks like and why it matters)
- [ ] D1 Missing method: without knowing how data were collected, a reader can't judge whether they are valid or repeat the work.
- [ ] D2 Missing sample size, or a very small sample: a result from a handful of people or plants may be due to chance.
- [ ] D3 Missing units, dates, location or definitions (e.g. what counts as "obese" or "a heavy drinker"), so data can't be compared with other data.
- [ ] D4 Only averages or percentages, without the range or the raw data, can hide great variation or very small numbers (e.g. "50% improved" could mean 1 of 2 patients).
- [ ] D5 No control or comparison group, no information on who funded the work, and no limitations stated.
- [ ] D6 Simplified summaries (news reports, infographics, social media) often leave out the uncertainty, the limitations and the conditions under which a result applies.

Evaluating secondary data (one block)
- [ ] S1 Advantages: secondary data can cover far larger samples, longer periods and more places than a student could collect (e.g. national health statistics, long-term climate records, genome databases); they are quick and cheap to obtain; and they allow results to be compared with others.
- [ ] S2 Limitations: they were collected for someone else's purpose, so may not exactly fit your question; you don't control how they were collected or how carefully; methods, definitions or units may be unclear or may change over time; they may be out of date, incomplete or already processed (averaged or selected); and the collector may be biased.
- [ ] S3 Questions to ask of secondary data: who collected them and why; how, when and where; how big the sample was; whether the method is described; whether the data are raw or processed; and whether other sources give similar data.

Types of bias a source may show (one block, each type with a biological example)
- [ ] B1 Funding or commercial bias, and conflict of interest: a source paid for by a company (or individual) that gains from a particular conclusion may favour it, e.g. research on sugary drinks funded by a drinks manufacturer, or a supplement company's website reporting only its own favourable trials. A conflict of interest is a situation in which a person's financial or personal interests could influence their judgement; reliable journals require authors to declare them.
- [ ] B2 Selection bias in the data reported (cherry-picking): choosing only the data or studies that support a view and ignoring the rest, e.g. quoting the one cold winter as evidence against climate warming.
- [ ] B3 Publication bias: studies with positive or exciting results are more likely to be published than those finding no effect, so the published literature can overstate how well a treatment works.
- [ ] B4 Confirmation bias: the tendency of authors (and readers) to look for, interpret and remember information that confirms what they already believe.
- [ ] B5 Sampling bias in the data a source relies on: a sample that is not representative of the population, e.g. a survey of diet carried out only among gym members, or a drug trialled only on young men and then recommended for everyone.
- [ ] B6 Political, ideological or cultural bias: a source from a campaign group, political party or lobby group presents the evidence that suits its cause (this can apply to groups on either side of an issue, e.g. both for and against genetically modified crops).
- [ ] B7 Sensationalism: exaggerating a result to attract readers or clicks (developed in "Evaluating evidence").
- [ ] B8 Recognising bias: check who produced and paid for the source; look for one-sided presentation, emotive language, no mention of limitations or opposing evidence, and claims not supported by references. A biased source can still contain accurate facts, so check them against independent sources rather than simply dismissing it.

Comparison block: which sources are usually most and least reliable
- [ ] Q1 Usually most reliable: peer-reviewed research papers and systematic reviews; reports of national and international scientific and public health bodies (e.g. the World Health Organization, the Health Service Executive (HSE), the Environmental Protection Agency (EPA)); up-to-date textbooks. Usually least reliable: anonymous websites, social media posts, advertisements and personal blogs. Each source must still be judged on the criteria, not just its type: a reliable body can make mistakes and a blog can be accurate.

## 3. Traps

- Primary vs secondary source is not the same as primary vs secondary data (T2 vs T3). State this.
- Experimental bias in one's own investigation (observer bias, the placebo effect, blinding, randomisation) and random vs systematic error belong to "Objectivity and data quality" and "Designing and conducting investigations". Don't teach them here.
- Analysing graphs and tables of data (trends, anomalies) belongs to "Producing and analysing data".
- Weighing the strength of evidence (anecdote vs controlled trial, correlation vs causation, single study vs consensus) and media arguments, false balance and misinformation belong to "Evaluating evidence". Here B7 is one sentence.
- Peer review is taught in "Science as a global enterprise"; E2 cross-refers.
- Don't say biased sources are always wrong (B8), or that a peer-reviewed source is always right.
- No diagram cues.

## 4. What exams ask (topics only)

No legacy questions; this outcome is new. Expect:
- Distinguish primary and secondary data (or sources), with examples.
- Give advantages and limitations of using secondary data.
- Given a short source (an advert, a news extract, a company website), identify possible bias and missing detail, and say how to check the claim.
- Describe how to research a scientific issue.

## 5. Practice questions to write

1. 6 marks: "Distinguish between primary data and secondary data. Give one advantage and one limitation of secondary data." Model answer: T3 definitions; one from S1 and one from S2.
2. 9 marks: "A website selling a herbal supplement claims that 'studies show it boosts the immune system'. Identify three reasons to doubt this source and say how you would check the claim." Model answer: commercial purpose and conflict of interest (B1, E4); no reference or method given (E5, D1); likely selective reporting (B2 or B3); check by searching for peer-reviewed studies in PubMed or Google Scholar and the advice of a public health body (RS2, RS6, Q1).
3. 12 marks: "Describe how you would research a scientific issue and evaluate the sources you find." Model answer: RS1–RS7 in order, with at least four criteria from E1–E7.
