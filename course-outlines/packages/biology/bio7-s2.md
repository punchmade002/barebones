## 1. Must cover

Key terms (one block)
- [ ] T1 (assumed) Magnification: how many times larger the image is than the real object.
- [ ] T2 (assumed) Resolution (resolving power): the ability to show two points that are close together as separate; it decides how much detail can be seen. Magnifying an image beyond the resolution limit only makes it bigger and blurrier, not more detailed.
- [ ] T3 (assumed) Total magnification of a light microscope = eyepiece lens magnification × objective lens magnification. Worked example: a ×10 eyepiece with a ×40 objective gives ×400.

The light (optical) microscope: parts and functions (structure-and-function block)
- [ ] L1 Eyepiece (ocular) lens: the lens you look through; magnifies the image from the objective, usually ×10.
- [ ] L2 Objective lenses: on a revolving nosepiece, usually ×4 (scanning), ×10 (low power) and ×40 (high power), sometimes ×100 (oil immersion); produce the first magnified image.
- [ ] L3 Stage with clips: holds the slide over the hole through which light passes.
- [ ] L4 Coarse focus knob: moves the stage (or tube) a long way quickly, for focusing at low power only.
- [ ] L5 Fine focus knob: moves it a small distance, for sharp focus, and the only focus used at high power.
- [ ] L6 Light source: a built-in lamp or a mirror reflecting light up through the specimen.
- [ ] L7 Condenser: a lens under the stage that focuses light onto the specimen.
- [ ] L8 Diaphragm (iris): adjusts the amount of light reaching the specimen; less light often improves contrast for pale, unstained cells.
- [ ] L9 Arm and base: support the microscope; the arm is for carrying it.
- [ ] L10 Diagram: a light microscope labelled eyepiece, body tube, revolving nosepiece, objective lenses, stage, clips, condenser, diaphragm, light source (or mirror), coarse focus knob, fine focus knob, arm and base.

Using the light microscope (skills block, numbered)
- [ ] U1 1. Carry the microscope with one hand on the arm and the other under the base. 2. Switch on the light and turn the lowest-power objective into place. 3. Clip the slide on the stage with the specimen over the hole. 4. Looking from the side, use the coarse focus to bring the objective close to the slide without touching it. 5. Looking through the eyepiece, focus by moving the objective and slide apart until the image is clear. 6. Use the fine focus to sharpen it and the diaphragm to adjust the light. 7. Move the part to be studied to the centre of the field of view. 8. Turn the next objective into place and use only the fine focus. 9. Record the total magnification with every drawing.
- [ ] U2 The image is inverted (upside down and reversed left to right): moving the slide to the left moves the image to the right.
- [ ] U3 At higher magnification the field of view is smaller and the image dimmer, so more light is needed; at high power the objective is very close to the slide, which is why the coarse focus mustn't be used.
- [ ] U4 Never use a mirror to reflect direct sunlight into the microscope: it can damage the eye.

Preparing and mounting a slide (skills block)
- [ ] P1 A wet mount: the specimen is placed in a drop of water or stain on a clean glass slide and covered with a coverslip.
- [ ] P2 The specimen must be very thin, ideally one layer of cells thick, so light can pass through it and cells don't overlap.
- [ ] P3 Stains add contrast to transparent structures by colouring some parts more than others: iodine solution is used for plant cells (stains the nucleus yellow-brown; turns starch blue-black); methylene blue is used for animal cells (stains the nucleus dark blue). The full procedures are in "Investigation: plant and animal cells".
- [ ] P4 Lowering the coverslip: touch one edge of the coverslip to the liquid at an angle of about 45° and lower it slowly with a mounted needle. This pushes air out ahead of it and avoids air bubbles, which appear as black-edged circles and can hide cells.
- [ ] P5 The coverslip keeps the specimen flat, stops it drying out, and protects the objective lens from the liquid. Excess liquid is removed by touching filter paper or a paper towel to the edge.
- [ ] P6 Stain can also be added after the coverslip is on: put a drop at one edge and draw it under with filter paper held at the opposite edge (irrigation).

Electron microscopes (one block)
- [ ] E1 An electron microscope uses a beam of electrons instead of light, focused by electromagnets instead of glass lenses. Electrons have a much shorter wavelength than light, so resolution is far better.
- [ ] E2 The specimen is in a vacuum (air would scatter the electrons), so it must be dead; the image is viewed on a screen and recorded digitally, and is black and white (colour is added afterwards: false colour).
- [ ] E3 Transmission electron microscope (TEM): electrons pass through an extremely thin, stained section of a specimen. Gives a flat (2D) image of internal structure at the highest magnifications, e.g. mitochondria, ribosomes, nuclear pores, viruses.
- [ ] E4 Scanning electron microscope (SEM): a beam of electrons scans across the surface of a specimen coated with a very thin layer of metal (e.g. gold), and electrons bouncing off it form an image. Gives a 3D image of surfaces, e.g. the surface of cells, pollen grains, insects; lower resolution than TEM and shows no internal structure unless the specimen is broken open.
- [ ] E5 The first electron microscope was built in Germany in 1931 by Max Knoll and Ernst Ruska.

Comparing light and electron microscopes (comparison block, paired points)
- [ ] C1 Radiation: light vs electrons. Lenses: glass vs electromagnets.
- [ ] C2 Maximum useful magnification: about ×1000–1500 for the light microscope vs hundreds of thousands of times or more for electron microscopes.
- [ ] C3 Resolution: about 0.2 µm (200 nm) for the light microscope vs less than 1 nm for the TEM, roughly a thousand times better. So the light microscope shows the nucleus, cell wall, vacuole and chloroplasts, but ribosomes, ER, Golgi, nuclear pores and viruses need an electron microscope.
- [ ] C4 Specimens: living or dead, and colour visible, with the light microscope vs dead only, in a vacuum, black and white, with the electron microscope.
- [ ] C5 Preparation and cost: simple, quick, cheap and portable vs complex preparation (thin sectioning, metal coating) that can create artefacts (structures that aren't really in the living cell), very expensive, large, and needing trained operators.

Existing and emerging fields (one block)
- [ ] F1 Existing history: Robert Hooke described and named "cells" from thin slices of cork in his book Micrographia (1665); Antonie van Leeuwenhoek, using his own single-lens microscopes, was the first to see and describe bacteria and other single-celled organisms in the 1670s.
- [ ] F2 Fluorescence microscopy: specific molecules are tagged with fluorescent dyes or fluorescent proteins that glow when lit with a particular colour of light, showing exactly where a protein is in a living cell. Green fluorescent protein (GFP), from a jellyfish, earned the 2008 Nobel Prize in Chemistry (Osamu Shimomura, Martin Chalfie, Roger Tsien).
- [ ] F3 Confocal laser scanning microscopy: a laser scans one thin plane of the specimen at a time, giving sharp images that a computer combines into a 3D model.
- [ ] F4 Super-resolution fluorescence microscopy overcomes the light microscope's normal resolution limit (Nobel Prize in Chemistry 2014: Eric Betzig, Stefan Hell, William Moerner).
- [ ] F5 Cryo-electron microscopy: samples are flash-frozen in a thin layer of ice instead of being dried and stained, so molecules keep their natural shape; it shows the structure of proteins and viruses in near-atomic detail (Nobel Prize in Chemistry 2017: Jacques Dubochet, Joachim Frank, Richard Henderson). It was used to show the structure of the SARS-CoV-2 spike protein, helping vaccine design.
- [ ] F6 Uses (health and technology links): examining blood smears and biopsies to diagnose disease, including spotting cancer cells; identifying pathogens; research into how cells and viruses work; forensic science (e.g. comparing hairs and fibres). Digital cameras and computer image analysis now allow images to be measured, shared and analysed automatically.

## 3. Traps

- Don't confuse magnification with resolution; the light microscope's limit is resolution, not magnification.
- Don't say the SEM shows organelles inside the cell; that is the TEM.
- Only the fine focus at high power; never say "use the coarse focus to focus at ×400".
- The specification has no formula for actual size from an image; the size calculation with a scale bar is taught (as assumed) in "Investigation: plant and animal cells", not here.
- The onion and cheek-cell methods belong to "Investigation: plant and animal cells"; P1–P6 give the general technique once, and that subsection refers back to it.
- What each organelle does belongs to "Structures and organelles of animal and plant cells"; C3 only names what each microscope can show.
- Diagram cues: only L10. No other diagram cues.
