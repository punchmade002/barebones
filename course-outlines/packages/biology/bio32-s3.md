## 1. Must cover

Measuring abiotic factors: general rules (one block)
- [ ] G1 The specification groups abiotic factors as air (climatic) factors, ground (topographic) factors, aquatic (water) factors and edaphic (soil) factors. It names water, oxygen, space, temperature, soil, aspect, wind speed and slope.
- [ ] G2 Measure abiotic factors at the same places as the quadrats or transect points and at the same time as the survey, so each measurement can be linked to the species found there.
- [ ] G3 Many factors (light, temperature, wind, humidity) change during the day and with the weather, so take all readings within a short period under similar conditions, record the time and weather, and repeat each reading (e.g. three times) and use the mean.
- [ ] G4 Electronic probes connected to a data logger can record factors such as temperature, light intensity, humidity, pH and dissolved oxygen continuously over hours or days, and at several points at once; this removes reading errors and shows changes over time. Calibrate meters before use (e.g. a pH meter with buffer solutions of known pH).

Air (climatic) factors (one block, one item per factor)
- [ ] A1 Light intensity: the energy for photosynthesis, so it affects plant growth and which plants can grow in shade; it also affects animal activity. Measured with a light meter (lux meter), in lux. Hold the sensor horizontally, facing up, at the same height each time (e.g. at ground level or at the top of the vegetation), keep your shadow off it, and take readings quickly, as light changes with cloud cover.
- [ ] A2 Air temperature: controls the rate of enzyme reactions, so the activity and growth of plants and of cold-blooded animals. Measured with a thermometer or temperature probe, in °C, held in the shade at a set height above the ground (e.g. 1 m) so that direct sunlight doesn't heat it. A maximum–minimum thermometer left on site records the highest and lowest temperatures over a period, e.g. 24 hours.
- [ ] A3 Wind speed: increases water loss by evaporation and transpiration, cools organisms, can damage tall plants, and makes flying difficult for insects. Measured with an anemometer (a cup anemometer or a hand-held digital one), in metres per second (m/s) or km/h, held at a set height facing into the wind; take the mean of several readings, as wind gusts. Wind direction is found with a wind vane or a compass.
- [ ] A4 Humidity (water in the air): affects water loss from plants and from animals with moist skins (e.g. slugs and woodlice need humid places). Measured with a hygrometer or humidity probe, as relative humidity (%).
- [ ] A5 Rainfall (water supply): measured with a rain gauge (a funnel draining into a measuring cylinder, set in an open place), in mm, read at the same time each day.

Ground (topographic) factors and space (one block)
- [ ] T1 Slope: affects drainage (water runs off steep slopes, so they are drier and soils thinner; hollows at the bottom collect water) and the amount of sunlight received. Measured with a clinometer, in degrees: two people of the same eye height (or two poles marked at the same height) stand up and down the slope a set distance apart; one sights the other's eye level (or the mark) through the clinometer and reads the angle.
- [ ] T2 Aspect: the compass direction a slope faces. Measured with a compass. In Ireland (northern hemisphere) a south-facing slope receives more direct sunlight than a north-facing one, so it is warmer and drier and plants there start growing earlier in spring.
- [ ] T3 Space: the room available for organisms to live, grow, find shelter, nest or attach. Measure the area of the habitat (length × width with tapes or a trundle wheel), and the percentage of bare ground in each quadrat (squares of a gridded quadrat with no plants), which shows space available for new seedlings. Density from "Surveys" (individuals per m²) shows how crowded a species is. Shortage of space is a cause of competition (see "Biotic factors and relationships").

Edaphic (soil) factors: temperature, pH and water content (one block)
- [ ] S1 Soil temperature: affects root growth, germination and the activity of soil organisms and decomposers. Push a soil thermometer or temperature probe into the soil to a set depth (e.g. 10 cm), leave it for a minute or two to settle, and read it in °C.
- [ ] S2 Soil pH: affects which plants grow (by changing which minerals are available) and the soil organisms (e.g. earthworms are scarce in acid soils). Method: 1. take a small soil sample from a set depth; 2. put about 1 cm depth of soil in a test tube, add about the same amount of barium sulfate (which makes the clay particles settle, so the liquid clears), then distilled water and a few drops of universal indicator; 3. stopper and shake, then leave to settle; 4. compare the colour of the liquid with the universal indicator colour chart to read the pH. Alternatively, mix the soil with distilled water and use a calibrated pH meter, rinsing the probe in distilled water between samples. Use distilled water, as tap water has its own pH.
- [ ] S3 Soil water content: affects which plants grow (rushes in wet soil) and soil animals (earthworms need moist soil); waterlogged soil lacks air. Method: 1. weigh an empty evaporating dish; 2. add a soil sample and weigh again (fresh mass of soil by subtraction); 3. heat in an oven at about 100–105 °C (hot enough to evaporate water, not to burn humus) for 24 hours; 4. cool in a desiccator (so the soil doesn't reabsorb water from the air) and reweigh; 5. repeat the heating, cooling and weighing until the mass stays the same (constant mass), showing all the water has gone. Percentage water = (fresh mass − dry mass) ÷ fresh mass × 100.
- [ ] S4 Worked example: fresh soil 50.0 g; dry soil 38.0 g at constant mass. Water = 12.0 g. Percentage water = 12.0 ÷ 50.0 × 100 = 24%.
- [ ] S5 A soil-moisture probe (meter) gives a quick reading in the field, but less accurately.

Edaphic (soil) factors: air content and humus (one block)
- [ ] S6 Soil air content: roots and soil animals need oxygen for respiration; air spaces are larger in sandy soils and smaller in clay and waterlogged soils. Method: 1. find the volume of an empty can by filling it with water from a graduated cylinder; 2. push the can, open end down, into the soil until full, dig it out and level the soil with the rim; 3. put a volume of water equal to the can's volume in a large graduated cylinder; 4. add the soil, and stir with a rod to release the trapped air; 5. read the new level. Volume of air = (volume of water + volume of soil) − final reading. Percentage air = volume of air ÷ volume of soil × 100.
- [ ] S7 Worked example: can volume 100 cm³; 100 cm³ water; reading after adding soil and stirring 185 cm³. Air = 200 − 185 = 15 cm³; percentage air = 15 ÷ 100 × 100 = 15%.
- [ ] S8 Humus (organic matter) content: humus is the dark, partly decomposed remains of dead organisms in soil; it holds water and minerals and feeds decomposers. Method: 1. weigh a crucible; 2. add dry soil from the water-content method and weigh it; 3. heat it strongly over a Bunsen burner (lid partly on) until the humus burns away (about 15 minutes); 4. cool in a desiccator and reweigh; 5. repeat until constant mass. Percentage humus = loss in mass ÷ mass of dry soil × 100.
- [ ] S9 Worked example: dry soil 38.0 g; after burning, 34.2 g. Loss = 3.8 g; percentage humus = 3.8 ÷ 38.0 × 100 = 10% of the dry soil.
- [ ] S10 Soil texture (one bullet): shake soil with water in a graduated cylinder and leave to settle; the particles settle in layers, largest at the bottom: stones and gravel, then sand, silt and clay, with humus floating on top. The proportions show the soil type.

Aquatic factors (one block)
- [ ] W1 Water temperature: thermometer or probe in °C, at a set depth; affects how much oxygen water holds (cold water holds more) and the activity of aquatic organisms.
- [ ] W2 Dissolved oxygen: oxygen meter (probe), in mg per litre, or a chemical test kit. Animals such as stonefly larvae need high oxygen; low oxygen is a sign of organic pollution, because decomposers use up the oxygen.
- [ ] W3 Water pH: pH meter or universal indicator paper, as for soil.
- [ ] W4 Rate of flow (current speed): mark two points a set distance apart along a straight stretch of stream (e.g. 10 m); time a float (e.g. an orange or a dog biscuit) from one to the other; repeat three times and take the mean time; speed = distance ÷ time. Worked example: mean time 25 s over 10 m: 10 ÷ 25 = 0.4 m/s. Fast flow carries away animals that can't cling on and keeps the water well oxygenated.
- [ ] W5 Turbidity (cloudiness): a Secchi disc (a black-and-white disc on a marked rope) is lowered until it can no longer be seen; the depth is recorded. Cloudy water lets less light reach water plants.
- [ ] W6 Salinity (seawater, rock pools, estuaries): a conductivity or salinity meter; rock pools become saltier as water evaporates in sunshine.

Quantitative investigation: an abiotic factor and the distribution and abundance of a species — plan (one block)
- [ ] I1 Aim: to investigate quantitatively how light intensity affects the distribution and abundance of the daisy in a grassland, along a belt transect from under a hedge or tree into the open field.
- [ ] I2 Hypothesis: "The number of daisies per quadrat increases as light intensity increases." Reason: the daisy is a low-growing rosette plant that needs plenty of light for photosynthesis and is shaded out where light is low.
- [ ] I3 Variables: independent variable, light intensity (it varies naturally along the transect, so it is measured, not set by the student); dependent variable, the abundance of daisies (number per quadrat, or percentage cover); controlled variables: quadrat size, the interval between quadrats, the counting rule, the height and direction of the light sensor, the time of day (all readings within a short period) and the weather.
- [ ] I4 No true control is possible: other factors also change along the transect (soil water content, leaf litter, trampling, competition from taller plants near the hedge). Measure these too where possible, so their effect can be considered. This is why a field investigation shows a correlation, not proof of cause.
- [ ] I5 Equipment: two long tape measures (one for the transect), a gridded quadrat, a light meter, pegs, a recording sheet, and a soil-moisture probe or soil-sampling equipment for the second factor.
- [ ] I6 Other suitable pairs of factor and species (one bullet): soil water content and rushes (more in wet ground); trampling or soil compaction across a path and plantain or grass cover; soil pH and a named plant; height above low tide on a rocky shore and the cover of each seaweed (X1).

Quantitative investigation — method and safety (one block)
- [ ] M1 1. Stretch a tape measure from the base of the hedge (0 m) straight out into the open field, e.g. for 10 m.
- [ ] M2 2. Place a gridded quadrat at 0 m and then at every 2 m along the tape (interrupted belt transect; transects in "Surveys").
- [ ] M3 3. In each quadrat, count the daisy rosettes (or the squares in which daisy covers more than half, for percentage cover).
- [ ] M4 4. At each quadrat, hold the light meter at ground level, facing up, and take three readings in quick succession; record the mean in lux. Do all the light readings within a few minutes, ideally under even cloud.
- [ ] M5 5. Measure the second factor (e.g. soil water content) at each quadrat.
- [ ] M6 6. Repeat the whole transect at least twice more, parallel to the first and a few metres away, and calculate the mean count at each distance.
- [ ] M7 7. Record the results in a table and plot a scatter graph of the number of daisies (y-axis) against light intensity (x-axis), or plot both against distance from the hedge.
- [ ] M8 Safety: take care at hedges with thorns, nettles and brambles; wear gloves when handling soil; wash hands afterwards; avoid trampling the quadrat sites before they are sampled.
- [ ] M9 Diagram: a belt transect running from a hedge into open grassland with quadrats every 2 m and a light meter at one quadrat, with distances labelled.

Quantitative investigation — results, conclusion and errors (one block)
- [ ] R1 Worked example (illustrative data, one bullet as a set of pairs): distance 0 m: light 800 lux, 0 daisies; 2 m: 2500 lux, 1 daisy; 4 m: 6000 lux, 4 daisies; 6 m: 9500 lux, 9 daisies; 8 m: 12 000 lux, 12 daisies; 10 m: 12 500 lux, 11 daisies.
- [ ] R2 Expected result and interpretation: daisies are absent or few in deep shade close to the hedge and become more abundant as light intensity increases into the open field, where they level off once light no longer limits them (other factors, such as space or competition with grass, then limit them). The scatter graph shows a positive correlation.
- [ ] R3 Conclusion: the results support the hypothesis that daisy abundance increases with light intensity along this transect; but since soil water and leaf litter also changed, the result shows a correlation, not proof that light alone is the cause. A laboratory experiment growing daisies at different light intensities, with other factors controlled, could test the cause.
- [ ] R4 Sources of error: light intensity changes rapidly with cloud cover, so readings at different quadrats may not be comparable (take readings quickly, or use data loggers at several points at once); a single transect may not be representative (use several); counting errors and inconsistent edge rules; misidentification of small daisy rosettes; other factors varying along the transect (I4); the survey is a snapshot of one day and season.
- [ ] R5 Using primary data: the student's own counts and measurements are the primary data; they can be compared with secondary data (e.g. published descriptions of where daisies grow) when drawing conclusions.

Abiotic factors in other habitats (one block)
- [ ] X1 Rocky shore: the main factor is the time spent out of water at low tide (exposure), which rises with height up the shore and brings drying, temperature change and, in rock pools, salinity change. This produces zonation, bands of species at different heights. Brown seaweeds (wracks) on Irish shores are zoned from the top down: channelled wrack (highest, survives longest out of water), spiral wrack, bladder wrack and knotted wrack (middle shore), serrated wrack (lower shore), and kelps (at and below the low-tide mark). Measured with a belt transect from low to high water, with height measured using a ranging pole and level.
- [ ] X2 Woodland: light intensity under the canopy (lowest in summer when trees are in leaf), humidity, leaf-litter depth, soil humus and pH are the key factors; many woodland floor plants (e.g. bluebells) grow and flower in spring before the canopy closes.
- [ ] X3 Freshwater: dissolved oxygen, temperature, rate of flow and pH are key. Fast, cold streams are rich in oxygen and support stonefly and mayfly larvae; still or polluted water low in oxygen supports fewer species.

## 3. Traps

- Light is measured in lux with a light meter, not a "photometer reading in %"; wind speed with an anemometer, not a wind vane (which shows direction only); slope with a clinometer; aspect with a compass.
- Percentage water is calculated from the fresh mass; percentage humus from the dry mass. Say which.
- Heat to constant mass, and cool in a desiccator, or the results are too low (water not all removed) or wrong (water reabsorbed).
- A field transect shows correlation, not causation; the student does not control the independent variable.
- "Edaphic" means soil; "topographic" means the shape of the land (slope, aspect, altitude).
- Distribution (where) and abundance (how many) are defined in "Surveys"; use the terms without redefining them.
- How limiting factors set carrying capacity, and S- and J-shaped population curves, belong to "Carrying capacity and limiting factors" and "Population curves". Predation, competition and symbiosis belong to "Biotic factors and relationships".
- How light, temperature and CO₂ limit photosynthesis belongs to "Investigation: rate of photosynthesis"; A1 gives one phrase. Transpiration belongs to "Investigation: rate of transpiration"; A3 and A4 give one phrase each.
- Don't teach chemical tests for nitrate or phosphate, or the Winkler method for oxygen.
- The diagram cue in M9 is the only one wanted. No other diagram cues.

Past papers (previous syllabus) asked these, and they remain on the specification:
- Name two abiotic factors investigated in a named ecosystem and describe how each was measured (light meter, soil pH, temperature).
- Name a plant from the ecosystem studied and two abiotic conditions that favour its presence.
New on this specification, expect: the instrument and units for each named factor (aspect, slope, wind speed, oxygen, water); soil water and humus calculations; a quantitative investigation linking an abiotic factor to a species' distribution and abundance, with variables, method and limitations.

## 5. Practice questions to write

1. 6 marks: "Name the instrument used to measure (i) light intensity, (ii) wind speed and (iii) slope, and give the unit for each." Model answer: A1 light meter, lux; A3 anemometer, m/s; T1 clinometer, degrees.
2. 9 marks: "Describe how you would find the percentage water content of a soil sample. A 40.0 g sample had a mass of 31.0 g after drying to constant mass. Calculate its percentage water content." Model answer: S3 steps (oven at about 100–105 °C, desiccator, constant mass) and the formula; (40.0 − 31.0) ÷ 40.0 × 100 = 22.5% (checked).
3. 12 marks: "Describe how you would investigate quantitatively the effect of one named abiotic factor on the distribution and abundance of a named plant in a grassland. Include the variables, a limitation, and the conclusion you would expect." Model answer: I1–I3 (or another I6 pair), M1–M7, one limitation from I4 or R4, and R2–R3 (positive correlation; correlation not proof). Must name the instrument and unit for the factor.
