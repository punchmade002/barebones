## 1. Must cover

Overview (one block lead or one bullet)
- [ ] O1 The steps are: 1. collect a sample; 2. extract the DNA; 3. amplify (copy) the chosen repeat regions by PCR; 4. separate the copies by size by gel electrophoresis; 5. make the bands visible and analyse (compare) the pattern. In the earlier method, step 3 was replaced by cutting the DNA with restriction enzymes (C1).
- [ ] O2 Diagram: a flow diagram of the five steps, with the key reagent or equipment at each: sample (swab, blood) → extraction (detergent, salt, protease, cold ethanol) → PCR (primers, nucleotides, Taq polymerase, thermal cycler) → gel electrophoresis (agarose gel, wells, electric current) → stained bands compared.

Sample collection and DNA extraction (one block)
- [ ] E1 Sources: any cells with a nucleus, e.g. white blood cells (mature red blood cells have no nucleus), cheek cells from a mouth swab, skin cells, hair roots (not the cut hair shaft), saliva and semen. Samples are collected with gloves and stored sealed and cold to avoid contamination and breakdown.
- [ ] E2 Principle of extraction: 1. break open cells and nuclei (lysis): a detergent breaks down the phospholipid membranes (cell membrane and nuclear envelope), releasing the DNA; 2. remove proteins: a protease enzyme digests proteins, including the histones bound to the DNA (see "Chromosome structure and genes"); 3. separate the DNA: DNA is insoluble in cold ethanol, so it comes out of solution (precipitates) and can be collected; salt helps the DNA strands clump together.
- [ ] E3 School extraction from plant tissue (e.g. onion; kiwi or strawberry work too), method: 1. Chop the onion finely, to break cell walls and give a large surface area. 2. Add it to a beaker containing a solution of washing-up liquid (detergent) and table salt. 3. Stand the beaker in a water bath at 60 °C for 15 minutes: the heat helps the detergent work and denatures enzymes that would break down the DNA; longer heating would start to break the DNA itself. 4. Cool the beaker in an ice-water bath for 5 minutes, slowing any enzyme breakdown of the DNA. 5. Blend in a food processor for no more than a few seconds (longer would break the long DNA strands). 6. Filter through coffee filter paper (or several layers of muslin) to remove cell debris, collecting the liquid filtrate in a test tube. 7. Add a few drops of protease to digest proteins. 8. Pour ice-cold ethanol gently down the side of the tilted test tube so it forms a layer on top of the filtrate. 9. DNA appears at the boundary as white, stringy threads that can be wound onto a glass rod.
- [ ] E4 Safety for E3: ethanol is highly flammable (no flames nearby); care with blender blades and hot water; wear safety glasses.
- [ ] E5 Forensic laboratories use the same principles (break cells, remove protein, purify DNA) with purified kits, because crime-scene samples are tiny; one bullet.

PCR: the polymerase chain reaction (one block, plus one block for the cycle)
- [ ] P1 PCR makes millions to billions of copies of a chosen short region of DNA (here, the STR regions) from a tiny sample, e.g. a single hair root or a spot of blood, so that there is enough DNA to see on a gel. It is DNA replication carried out in a tube.
- [ ] P2 The reaction mixture: the extracted DNA (the template); primers, which are short single-stranded pieces of DNA made to be complementary to the sequences on either side of the region to be copied, so only that region is copied (DNA polymerase can only add nucleotides onto an existing strand, so a primer is needed to start each new strand); a supply of the four DNA nucleotides (A, T, C, G); a heat-stable DNA polymerase (Taq polymerase); and a buffer solution. The tubes are placed in a thermal cycler, a machine that changes the temperature automatically.
- [ ] P3 Taq polymerase comes from Thermus aquaticus, a bacterium found in hot springs (first found in Yellowstone National Park, USA). Its structure holds its shape at high temperatures (see "Enzyme structure and specificity"), so it is not denatured at 95 °C and works best at about 72 °C. A human DNA polymerase would be denatured in the first heating step, and fresh enzyme would have to be added every cycle.
- [ ] P4 The cycle, step 1, denaturation: heat to about 95 °C. The hydrogen bonds between the complementary bases break and the two strands of each DNA molecule separate. (Heat does here what helicase does in cells.)
- [ ] P5 Step 2, annealing: cool to about 55 °C (usually between 50 and 65 °C, depending on the primers). The primers bind (anneal) by complementary base pairing to their matching sequences on each single strand.
- [ ] P6 Step 3, extension: heat to about 72 °C, the optimum temperature of Taq polymerase. Taq polymerase adds nucleotides to the end of each primer, complementary to the template strand, building a new strand. Each double-stranded region becomes two.
- [ ] P7 Each cycle takes a few minutes and doubles the number of copies of the target region, so after n cycles one molecule gives 2ⁿ copies: 10 cycles give 1,024 copies; 30 cycles give 2³⁰, about 1 billion (1,073,741,824). A run is usually 25–35 cycles and takes a few hours.
- [ ] P8 History (one bullet): Kary Mullis invented PCR in 1983 and shared the Nobel Prize in Chemistry in 1993. Using Taq polymerase, an enzyme discovered in basic research on bacteria of hot springs, made PCR quick and automatic.
- [ ] P9 Diagram: the PCR cycle as three stages in a circle or a row: double-stranded DNA at 95 °C → separated strands; primers binding at 55 °C; Taq polymerase extending the primers at 72 °C → two double-stranded copies; with "cycle repeats, doubling the DNA each time" (1 → 2 → 4 → 8). Label template strand, primer, nucleotides, Taq polymerase, new strand, and the three temperatures.
- [ ] P10 Other uses of PCR (one bullet): diagnosing infections by detecting a pathogen's DNA or RNA (e.g. PCR tests for COVID-19), detecting alleles that cause genetic diseases, and preparing DNA for sequencing ("DNA sequencing").

The earlier method: cutting with restriction enzymes (one block, short)
- [ ] C1 In Alec Jeffreys' original method (see "DNA profiling"), the extracted DNA was cut with restriction enzymes (see "Genetic engineering"), which cut at specific recognition sequences on either side of the repeat regions. Because people have different numbers of repeats between the cutting sites, the fragments differed in length between people, and these were separated by gel electrophoresis. This needed much more DNA than PCR-based profiling, which has replaced it.

Gel electrophoresis (one block)
- [ ] G1 Gel electrophoresis separates DNA fragments by size using an electric current. The gel is usually agarose, a jelly made from a polysaccharide extracted from seaweed; it is full of tiny pores that act like a sieve.
- [ ] G2 Setting up: the gel is made with a row of small holes (wells) near one end and placed in a tank of buffer solution, which conducts electricity, with the wells at the negative electrode (cathode) end. Each DNA sample is mixed with a loading dye (which is dense, so the sample sinks into the well, and coloured, to show how far the run has gone) and placed in its own well with a micropipette. One well holds a DNA ladder (size marker): a mixture of fragments of known lengths.
- [ ] G3 Running: when the current is switched on, the negatively charged DNA (its phosphate groups) moves through the gel towards the positive electrode (anode). Small fragments pass through the pores more easily and move faster, so after a set time fragments of different lengths are separated into bands, the smallest furthest from the wells. The current is switched off before the loading dye reaches the end of the gel.
- [ ] G4 Making the bands visible: DNA is colourless, so the gel is stained. In schools a safe stain such as methylene blue is used, showing blue bands under white light. Research laboratories use fluorescent stains that glow under ultraviolet light; the older stain ethidium bromide is a mutagen and must be handled with gloves. Modern forensic profiling uses primers labelled with fluorescent dyes and separates the fragments in a thin capillary tube in a machine, which reads the result as peaks (see "DNA profiling").
- [ ] G5 Diagram: an electrophoresis tank seen from the side and from above: gel in buffer, wells at the negative (black) electrode end, positive (red) electrode at the other end, power pack, lanes with ladder and samples, bands; arrow showing the direction DNA moves. Label agarose gel, well, buffer solution, cathode (−), anode (+), DNA ladder, bands, "smaller fragments travel further".

Analysis (one bullet; detail in the investigation)
- [ ] A1 The band positions (or peak sizes) of the samples are compared with each other and with the ladder; matching at every locus indicates the same person, and inheritance of one band per locus from each parent is used in paternity cases. Worked examples are in "Investigation: DNA-profile patterns".

## 3. Traps

- Every block is Higher Level only; mark every heading.
- PCR copies a chosen region, not the whole genome; the primers decide which region.
- Primers are short DNA pieces here. RNA primers in cell replication are not on the specification (see the traps of "Modelling DNA replication and information flow"); don't mention them.
- Temperature order is hot–cool–warm: 95 °C (separate), 55 °C (primers bind), 72 °C (extend). Students often put annealing last or swap the temperatures.
- Taq polymerase is not denatured at 95 °C; don't say it "works best at 95 °C" (its optimum is about 72 °C).
- DNA moves to the positive electrode; samples are loaded at the negative end. If the gel is put in the wrong way round, the DNA runs out of the gel into the buffer.
- Modern profiling uses PCR, not restriction enzymes; the restriction-enzyme method is the earlier one (C1). Don't teach Southern blotting, radioactive probes or X-ray film.
- In E3, detergent breaks membranes (not cell walls; chopping and blending break cell walls); salt clumps the DNA; protease removes proteins; cold ethanol precipitates the DNA. Don't swap these roles.
- What a profile is, STRs and reading bands belong to "DNA profiling"; band-matching worked examples, the hands-on gel practical and its safety belong to "Investigation: DNA-profile patterns". The chain-termination use of gels belongs to "DNA sequencing".
- Diagram cues: O2, P9 and G5 only. No other diagram cues.

## 4. What exams ask (topics only)

- Name the stages of producing a DNA profile, in order, and explain each.
- Explain why PCR is used; name the enzyme and explain why it must be heat-stable; give the three steps of a PCR cycle with their approximate temperatures.
- Calculate the number of copies after a given number of PCR cycles.
- Explain how gel electrophoresis separates DNA fragments (charge, size, pores, direction).
- Describe the extraction of DNA from plant tissue and give the role of the detergent, salt, protease and ethanol (the old mandatory experiment, still relevant to "DNA extraction").
