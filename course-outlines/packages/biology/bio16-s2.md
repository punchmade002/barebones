## 1. Must cover

Mitosis: genetic information stays the same (one block)
- [ ] M1 Mitosis is nuclear division that produces two nuclei, each with the same number of chromosomes as the parent nucleus and genetically identical to it. A diploid cell gives two diploid cells (2n → 2n); a haploid cell (in organisms that have haploid stages) gives haploid cells (n → n).
- [ ] M2 Why the information stays the same: before mitosis every chromosome is copied exactly; during mitosis the two identical copies of each chromosome are separated so that each new nucleus receives one copy of every chromosome. Homologous chromosomes do not pair up. One division, two daughter cells. (How the copying and separation relate is in "DNA replication and mitosis in the cell cycle".)
- [ ] M3 Cells produced by mitosis from one parent cell form a clone: a group of genetically identical cells or organisms. Mitosis on its own produces no genetic variation; the only source of difference is a rare mistake in copying (a mutation).

Meiosis: genetic information is varied (one block)
- [ ] E1 Meiosis is nuclear division that halves the chromosome number, producing four haploid nuclei from one diploid nucleus (2n → n). It involves two divisions, one after the other, after the DNA is copied once.
- [ ] E2 In the first division, homologous chromosomes pair up and then separate, one of each pair going to each new cell, so each cell receives one complete set; in the second division the two copies of each chromosome separate. The stages are not required.
- [ ] E3 Why the four cells are genetically different from each other and from the parent: the homologous pairs line up and separate independently of one another (random, or independent, assortment), so each gamete receives a random mixture of maternal and paternal chromosomes. With 23 pairs, one person can make 2²³ (more than 8 million) different combinations of chromosomes in their gametes. This is the basis of Mendel's Law of Independent Assortment (see "Mendel's Laws").
- [ ] E4 During the first division, homologous chromosomes can also exchange matching segments (crossing over), mixing alleles further. One sentence: the specification says knowledge of crossing over is not required.
- [ ] E5 Random fertilisation adds further variation: any one of a huge number of different sperm may fuse with any one of a huge number of different eggs. So offspring of sexual reproduction differ from their parents and from each other (except identical twins). Variation from sexual reproduction and its role in evolution are in "Variation" (Evolution).
- [ ] E6 Meiosis keeps the chromosome number constant across generations: gametes are haploid, so the zygote formed at fertilisation is diploid (n + n = 2n). Without meiosis the number would double every generation.

Roles in unicellular organisms (one block)
- [ ] U1 In unicellular eukaryotes (e.g. Amoeba, yeast), mitosis followed by division of the cell is asexual reproduction: one organism becomes two genetically identical organisms (in yeast, by budding). It is fast, needs only one parent and passes on a successful set of genes unchanged, so a population can grow quickly when conditions are good; but all the offspring are alike, so a change in conditions (e.g. a new disease) can affect them all.
- [ ] U2 Some unicellular eukaryotes also reproduce sexually, using meiosis to produce varied haploid cells, often when conditions become poor; e.g. starved diploid yeast cells undergo meiosis to form four haploid spores, which can survive harsh conditions. The variation makes it more likely that some offspring survive a changed environment.
- [ ] U3 Prokaryotes (bacteria) have neither mitosis nor meiosis: they copy their single circular chromosome and divide by binary fission (see "Prokaryotic and eukaryotic cells"). One bullet.

Roles in multicellular organisms (one block)
- [ ] P1 Mitosis for growth: a multicellular organism develops from a single cell, the zygote, by repeated mitosis, so every body cell has the same genetic information as the zygote. In plants, growth by mitosis is concentrated at the tips of roots and shoots (meristems).
- [ ] P2 Mitosis for repair and replacement: replacing cells that wear out or are damaged, e.g. healing a cut, replacing skin cells and the cells lining the gut, and making new blood cells in the bone marrow (stem cells there divide by mitosis).
- [ ] P3 Mitosis for asexual reproduction: some multicellular organisms, including many plants, also reproduce asexually by mitosis, producing clones of the parent. One bullet; methods of asexual reproduction in plants are not on the specification.
- [ ] P4 Meiosis for sexual reproduction: meiosis takes place only in the reproductive organs. In humans it happens in the testes (forming sperm) and ovaries (forming eggs) (see "Gametes and fertilisation"); in flowering plants it happens in the anthers and the ovules, leading to the pollen grains and the egg cell.
- [ ] P5 Because body cells are made by mitosis and gametes by meiosis, a change to the DNA of a body cell (e.g. a skin cell) is not passed to offspring, but a change in a cell that gives rise to gametes can be. (Mutations: "Mutations", Higher Level.)

Comparison of mitosis and meiosis (one comparison block: paired points, then a table)
- [ ] C1 Similarities: both are nuclear divisions of eukaryotic cells; both follow DNA replication in interphase; both use a spindle to separate chromosomes; both are followed by cytokinesis.
- [ ] C2 Number of divisions: mitosis one; meiosis two.
- [ ] C3 Number of cells produced: mitosis two; meiosis four.
- [ ] C4 Chromosome number: mitosis keeps it the same (2n → 2n); meiosis halves it (2n → n).
- [ ] C5 Genetic make-up of the cells produced: mitosis gives cells identical to each other and to the parent; meiosis gives cells that differ from each other and from the parent.
- [ ] C6 Homologous chromosomes: they don't pair in mitosis; they pair and separate in meiosis.
- [ ] C7 Where: mitosis in body cells throughout the organism; meiosis only in the reproductive organs (testes, ovaries, anthers, ovules).
- [ ] C8 Role: mitosis for growth, repair, replacement and asexual reproduction; meiosis for producing gametes (or spores) for sexual reproduction, giving variation and keeping the chromosome number constant across generations.
- [ ] C9 Comparison table with rows: number of divisions; cells produced; chromosome number in the cells produced; genetically identical or varied; homologous pairs; where it happens; role in unicellular organisms; role in multicellular organisms.
- [ ] C10 Diagram: two simple flow diagrams side by side for a cell with 2n = 4 (one long and one short homologous pair, the two parents' chromosomes shaded differently): mitosis → two cells each with 2n = 4 and identical shading to the parent; meiosis → four cells each with n = 2, showing different combinations of shaded chromosomes. Label diploid, haploid, homologous pair.

## 3. Traps

- Don't teach the stages of meiosis (meiosis I and II, prophase I, chiasmata, etc.); they are not on the specification. E2 gives the outcome of each division in one bullet. E4 gives crossing over one sentence only.
- Meiosis produces four cells, not two; the cells are haploid, not "half a chromosome".
- Homologous chromosomes separate in meiosis; in mitosis copies of each chromosome separate. Don't say homologous chromosomes separate in mitosis.
- Bacteria don't do mitosis; don't give binary fission as an example of mitosis.
- Mitosis does not produce variation (except by mutation); don't say it "causes some variation".
- Meiosis in humans happens only in the testes and ovaries, not in "the reproductive system" in general, and never in body cells.
- Gamete formation in the testes and ovaries, fertilisation and the zygote's development belong to "Gametes and fertilisation" and "Pregnancy" (Human Reproduction). Plant gamete formation, pollination and fertilisation belong to Plant Reproduction. P4 names the organs only.
- Independent assortment as a law, and crosses, belong to "Mendel's Laws" and "Dihybrid crosses"; E3 gives the cell-level reason only. Variation and evolution belong to "Variation", which also explains independent assortment and random fertilisation as sources of variation: keep E3 and E5 to one bullet each here and cross-refer, without a second worked explanation.
- How replication and mitosis work together belongs to "DNA replication and mitosis in the cell cycle"; definitions of haploid, diploid and homologous chromosomes belong to "The cell cycle".
- Diagram cues: C10 only. No other diagram cues.
