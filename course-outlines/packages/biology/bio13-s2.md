## 1. Must cover

How 3D structure creates the active site (one block)
- [ ] T1 An enzyme's three-dimensional shape is set by the sequence of amino acids in its polypeptide chain (its primary structure), which is set by its gene. The chain coils and folds (secondary structure) and then folds further into a precise globular shape (tertiary structure); some enzymes are made of more than one polypeptide (quaternary structure). One bullet; the levels are defined in "Structures of biomolecules".
- [ ] T2 The active site is formed by folding: it is made of a small number of amino acids, often far apart in the chain, that folding brings together into a pocket or cleft. Most of the rest of the enzyme holds these amino acids in exactly the right positions.
- [ ] T3 The R groups (side groups) of the active-site amino acids give the site its shape and its pattern of charges and of water-attracting and water-repelling regions. They bind the substrate by weak attractions (hydrogen bonds and ionic attractions between opposite charges), and some take part directly in the reaction.
- [ ] T4 So specificity depends on two things: the shape of the active site (only a substrate of complementary shape fits) and its chemistry (only a substrate with matching charges and groups is held in the right position). A molecule of similar shape but the wrong charges is not catalysed.
- [ ] T5 What holds the 3D shape: hydrogen bonds, ionic bonds between charged R groups, and attractions between water-repelling R groups, which are weak; and disulfide (sulfur) bridges between cysteine units, which are strong covalent bonds. Because most of these bonds are weak, the shape is easily changed.
- [ ] T6 Diagram: a folded polypeptide chain drawn as a looping ribbon, with three or four amino acids (circles) from distant parts of the chain meeting to form a labelled active site; label polypeptide chain, R groups of active-site amino acids, active site, a hydrogen bond and a disulfide bridge.

Why 3D structure matters for activity (one block)
- [ ] V1 Activity depends on the active site keeping its shape. Heat makes the enzyme molecule vibrate until weak bonds (T5) break; a change in pH changes the charges on R groups (by adding or removing hydrogen ions), breaking ionic and hydrogen bonds. Either way the active site loses its shape, the substrate no longer fits, and the enzyme is denatured. The effects on rate, and the investigations, are in "Investigation: rate of enzyme activity".
- [ ] V2 A change in a single amino acid can change the shape or charge of the active site, so a mutation in the gene can make an enzyme work less well or not at all; e.g. in phenylketonuria the enzyme that converts phenylalanine is faulty (see "Enzymes as catalysts" and "Mutations").
- [ ] V3 Some enzymes need a cofactor, such as a metal ion, held in or near the active site to complete its shape or chemistry; without it the enzyme is inactive (see "Minerals").
- [ ] V4 Degrees of specificity: some enzymes act on only one substrate (e.g. urease acts only on urea, breaking it into ammonia and carbon dioxide); others act on a group of substrates that share the same type of bond (e.g. lipase acts on many different triglycerides; proteases such as pepsin break peptide bonds in many different proteins). A rigid lock-and-key active site does not explain the second kind well; induced fit does (I8).
- [ ] V5 Enzymes that work in extreme conditions have structures that hold their shape there, e.g. enzymes of bacteria from hot springs, such as Taq polymerase from Thermus aquaticus, which stays active at temperatures that denature human enzymes (its use is in "Generating a DNA profile"). Pepsin keeps its shape in the strong acid of the stomach, where most enzymes are denatured. One bullet.

The induced-fit model (one process block, illustrated)
- [ ] I1 The induced-fit model says the active site is flexible, not rigid. Its shape is close to, but not exactly, complementary to the substrate before binding; binding of the substrate induces (causes) a change in the enzyme's shape that produces a close fit.
- [ ] I2 Step 1: the substrate collides with the enzyme and starts to bind to the active site through weak attractions to its R groups.
- [ ] I3 Step 2: binding causes the enzyme to change shape: the active site moulds itself around the substrate, like a glove taking the shape of a hand, forming a tightly fitting enzyme–substrate complex.
- [ ] I4 Step 3: the change in shape puts strain on particular bonds in the substrate, or brings reacting groups of two substrates close together in the correct orientation, and the R groups of the active site may lend or take hydrogen ions or electrons. This lowers the activation energy, and the substrate is converted to products.
- [ ] I5 Step 4: the products no longer fit the active site closely and are released.
- [ ] I6 Step 5: the enzyme returns to its original shape, unchanged and ready to bind another substrate molecule.
- [ ] I7 Example: the enzyme hexokinase (used in the first stage of respiration) closes around its substrate glucose like a clamp when glucose binds; structural studies of enzymes showed this change of shape.
- [ ] I8 How induced fit explains observations that lock and key cannot: (a) why some enzymes act on several similar substrates (a flexible site can mould around each); (b) how binding itself helps catalysis (the shape change strains the substrate's bonds, lowering the activation energy); (c) why molecules of similar shape may bind but not react (they don't induce the correct change of shape). Lock and key remains a useful simple model of specificity.
- [ ] I9 Diagram: induced fit in four labelled stages: (1) enzyme with an active site not quite matching the approaching substrate; (2) substrate binding and the active site changing shape around it (enzyme–substrate complex, with arrows showing the change); (3) products formed in the active site; (4) products released and the enzyme back in its original shape. Label enzyme, active site, substrate, change in shape, enzyme–substrate complex, products. For contrast, a one-line sketch of lock and key with a rigid active site that already fits exactly.

Comparison (one comparison block, paired bullets)
- [ ] K1 Active site: rigid with a fixed shape that exactly matches the substrate (lock and key) vs flexible, changing shape when the substrate binds (induced fit).
- [ ] K2 Fit: exact fit before binding vs close fit only after binding.
- [ ] K3 Role of binding: holds the substrate only vs the change of shape itself helps the reaction by straining bonds.
- [ ] K4 Specificity explained: absolute specificity only vs both absolute and group specificity.

## 3. Traps

- The induced-fit change of shape is temporary and reversible; it is not denaturation. Don't say the enzyme "changes permanently" or "is used up".
- It is the enzyme's active site that changes shape in induced fit, not (mainly) the substrate.
- Don't say the active site is made of "the substrate's" amino acids, or that the whole enzyme is the active site.
- Hydrogen bonds and ionic bonds are weak; disulfide bridges are covalent. Don't call peptide bonds the bonds that break on denaturation: denaturation leaves the amino-acid sequence intact.
- Denaturation's effect on rate, optimum temperature and pH, and the graphs belong to "Investigation: rate of enzyme activity"; V1 gives the structural reason only.
- Allosteric regulation and named types of inhibition are not on the specification. Don't teach them.
- The diagram cues in T6 and I9 are the only ones wanted. No other diagram cues.
