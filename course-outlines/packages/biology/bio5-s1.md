# Package: bio5-s1 — Characteristics of living things

## 1. Must cover

What makes something alive (one block)
- [ ] L1 Biology is the study of living things (organisms). The specification lists eleven characteristics that together distinguish living from non-living things: organisation, response, respiration, nutrition, excretion, reproduction, growth, metabolism, homeostasis, heredity and cellular basis.
- [ ] L2 An organism shows all eleven at some stage of its life, though not necessarily all at once (e.g. a dormant seed or a hibernating animal shows very little response or growth, but is still alive).
- [ ] L3 Some non-living things show one or two characteristics: a crystal grows, but only by adding material to its outside, not by making new material inside cells; a fire "uses oxygen", "releases energy" and "spreads", but has no cells, no heredity and no control of its reactions. Something is alive only if it shows the full set. Viruses are the main borderline case (detail in "Viruses"; one sentence here).

Structure (one block: organisation and cellular basis)
- [ ] O1 Organisation: living things have a highly ordered structure. In multicellular organisms, cells are grouped into tissues, tissues into organs and organs into organ systems, each level carrying out particular jobs; even a single cell has organised parts (organelles). Detail of the levels is in "Levels of organisation".
- [ ] C1 Cellular basis: all living things are made of one or more cells; the cell is the smallest unit that can carry out all the processes of life (the basic unit of structure and function).
- [ ] C2 All cells arise only from pre-existing cells, by cell division.
- [ ] C3 All cells share basic features: a cell membrane, cytoplasm, ribosomes and DNA as their genetic material. This shared basis is evidence that all life has a common origin.
- [ ] C4 These points form the cell theory, developed in the 19th century: Matthias Schleiden concluded that all plants are made of cells (1838); Theodor Schwann extended this to animals (1839); Rudolf Virchow stated that all cells come from pre-existing cells (1855).
- [ ] C5 Unicellular organisms (e.g. bacteria, yeast, Amoeba) are one cell that carries out every life process; multicellular organisms (e.g. humans, oak trees) have many specialised cells.

Energy and materials (one block: nutrition, respiration, metabolism)
- [ ] N1 Nutrition: the way an organism obtains food, which supplies the energy and the raw materials (e.g. carbon, nitrogen, minerals) it needs.
- [ ] N2 Autotrophic nutrition: the organism makes its own food from simple inorganic substances. Photosynthetic autotrophs (plants, algae, some bacteria) use light energy to make glucose from carbon dioxide and water; a few bacteria are chemosynthetic, using energy from chemical reactions instead of light.
- [ ] N3 Heterotrophic nutrition: the organism takes in ready-made food made by other organisms, e.g. animals eat plants or other animals; fungi and many bacteria absorb food from dead matter (saprophytes) or from a living host (parasites). Detail of saprophytes and parasites is in "Microorganisms"; one sentence here.
- [ ] R1 Respiration: the controlled release of energy from food (mainly glucose) inside every living cell. The energy is trapped in ATP, which powers the cell's activities.
- [ ] R2 Aerobic respiration uses oxygen: glucose + oxygen → carbon dioxide + water + energy (C₆H₁₂O₆ + 6O₂ → 6CO₂ + 6H₂O + energy). It releases much more energy per glucose than anaerobic respiration.
- [ ] R3 Anaerobic respiration happens without oxygen and releases less energy: in human muscle it produces lactic acid; in yeast and plant cells it produces ethanol and carbon dioxide.
- [ ] R4 Respiration is not the same as breathing: breathing is the movement of air into and out of the lungs (gas exchange); respiration is a chemical process in cells. Plants respire all the time, day and night. Stages are in "Respiration".
- [ ] M1 Metabolism: the sum of all the chemical reactions in an organism. Every reaction is controlled by an enzyme.
- [ ] M2 Anabolic reactions build large molecules from smaller ones and need energy, e.g. photosynthesis (glucose from carbon dioxide and water), protein synthesis from amino acids.
- [ ] M3 Catabolic reactions break large molecules into smaller ones and release energy, e.g. respiration, digestion of starch to maltose. The roles of biomolecules in these reactions are in "Biomolecules — The Chemicals of Life".

Waste and stability (one block: excretion and homeostasis)
- [ ] E1 Excretion: the removal of the waste products of metabolism (substances made in cells that are harmful or not needed). If they built up they would poison cells or upset conditions such as pH.
- [ ] E2 Human examples: carbon dioxide from respiration is excreted by the lungs; urea (made in the liver from excess amino acids) is excreted by the kidneys in urine; excess water and salts are excreted by the kidneys and in sweat.
- [ ] E3 Plant examples: oxygen from photosynthesis and carbon dioxide from respiration diffuse out through the stomata; some waste is stored in old leaves and lost when they fall.
- [ ] E4 Excretion is not egestion: egestion is the removal of undigested food (faeces), which was never inside body cells and so is not a product of metabolism.
- [ ] H1 Homeostasis: the maintenance of a relatively constant internal environment (e.g. body temperature, blood glucose concentration, water and salt balance, pH) despite changes outside or inside the organism.
- [ ] H2 It matters because enzymes work only within narrow ranges of temperature and pH. Example: human core body temperature is kept at about 37 °C; insulin lowers blood glucose after a meal. Mechanism (negative feedback) is in "Homeostasis".

Reacting and continuing (one block: response, reproduction, growth, heredity)
- [ ] S1 Response: the ability to detect a change in the internal or external environment (a stimulus) and react to it, which helps the organism survive.
- [ ] S2 Animals respond quickly using the nervous system (e.g. pulling a hand away from a hot object) and more slowly with hormones; plants respond more slowly, mainly through chemicals and their structure (e.g. guard cells close the stomata when water is short). Detail in "Structures and systems for response".
- [ ] P1 Reproduction: the production of new individuals of the same species. Every organism eventually dies, so reproduction is needed for the species to continue.
- [ ] P2 Asexual reproduction: one parent, no gametes; the offspring are genetically identical to the parent (clones), e.g. binary fission in bacteria, budding in yeast.
- [ ] P3 Sexual reproduction: two gametes (sex cells) fuse at fertilisation to form a zygote; the offspring are genetically different from the parents and from each other, which gives variation, e.g. humans, flowering plants.
- [ ] G1 Growth: a permanent increase in size and mass. It happens from within, by making new materials (anabolism), by cell division (mitosis) increasing the number of cells, and by cells enlarging and becoming specialised.
- [ ] G2 Plants keep growing throughout life at regions of dividing cells at the root and shoot tips; most animals grow to a fixed adult size.
- [ ] I1 Heredity: the passing of genetic information (genes, made of DNA) from parents to offspring, so offspring inherit their parents' characteristics. "Like produces like", e.g. a cat's offspring are always kittens.
- [ ] I2 Genes code for proteins, which control an organism's traits. Variation arises from sexual reproduction and from mutations (changes in DNA). Detail in "Genetic Inheritance".

Summary (one block)
- [ ] T1 A summary list: one line per characteristic (eleven lines), each a definition of a few words, to use for revision. This block is the only place the eleven are restated.

## 3. Traps

- The previous syllabus listed only five characteristics (organisation, nutrition, excretion, response, reproduction) with a "continuity of life" idea. This specification lists eleven; teach all eleven and never say "seven" or "five characteristics".
- Tropisms (phototropism, geotropism) and auxins are not on this specification. Don't use them as the plant response example; use S2's stomata example.
- Don't teach glucagon: the specification names insulin only.
- No ATP yields (no "38 ATP", no "2 ATP"); just "much more energy" and "less energy".
- Respiration vs breathing and excretion vs egestion are the commonest errors; R4 and E4 must state the distinction.
- Don't say plants "only photosynthesise by day and respire at night"; they respire all the time.
- Each characteristic is one short bullet group. Full treatment belongs elsewhere: levels of organisation to "Levels of organisation"; viruses as living or non-living to "Viruses"; cell structure to "The Unit of Life — The Cell"; respiration stages to "Respiration"; photosynthesis to "Photosynthesis"; feedback to "Homeostasis"; response systems to "Structures and systems for response"; mitosis to "Cell Division and Cancer"; genes and inheritance to "Genetic Inheritance"; enzymes to "Enzymes".
- No diagram cues in this subsection.

## 4. What exams ask (topics only)

Past papers (previous syllabus) asked these, and they remain on the specification:
- Name or list characteristics of living things; define one or two (e.g. metabolism, excretion, heredity).
- Distinguish autotrophic and heterotrophic nutrition; anabolic and catabolic reactions.
- Define heredity.
Expect also: explain why a given non-living thing (crystal, fire) or a virus is not counted as living.

## 5. Practice questions to write

2. 9 marks: "Distinguish between (i) autotrophic and heterotrophic nutrition, (ii) respiration and breathing, (iii) excretion and egestion." Model answer: N2 vs N3; R1 vs R4; E1 vs E4, each with an example.
3. 12 marks: "A crystal grows in a saturated salt solution. Using the characteristics of living things, explain why the crystal is not alive." Model answer: L2–L3; growth from outside vs within (G1); no cells (C1), no respiration, nutrition, excretion, response, reproduction or heredity; must name at least four characteristics it lacks.
