## 1. Must cover

Carbohydrates: composition (one block, or the lead of the first carbohydrate block)
- [ ] C1 Carbohydrates contain carbon, hydrogen and oxygen only. Their general formula is Cx(H₂O)y: hydrogen and oxygen are in the ratio 2 : 1, as in water. Example: glucose, C₆H₁₂O₆. The name means "hydrated carbon"; the molecule does not actually contain water molecules.
- [ ] C2 There are three groups by size: monosaccharides (one sugar unit), disaccharides (two units) and polysaccharides (many units). Mono- and disaccharides are sugars: sweet, soluble in water and crystalline. Polysaccharides are not sweet and are insoluble or only slightly soluble, which suits them to storage and structure.

Monosaccharides (one block)
- [ ] C3 Monosaccharides are single sugar units, the monomers of all other carbohydrates. Glucose (C₆H₁₂O₆) is the main respiratory substrate and the product of photosynthesis. Fructose (fruit sugar, found in fruit and honey) has the same formula, C₆H₁₂O₆, but a different arrangement of atoms. Galactose (also C₆H₁₂O₆) is a unit of lactose. All three are six-carbon sugars (hexoses).
- [ ] C4 Ribose and deoxyribose are five-carbon monosaccharides (pentoses) found in RNA and DNA nucleotides. Name them only; detail in "DNA, RNA and the Genetic Code".
- [ ] C5 Diagram: glucose drawn as a simple six-sided ring (hexagon) labelled "glucose, C₆H₁₂O₆". Atom-by-atom structural formulas are not needed.

Disaccharides (one block)
- [ ] C6 A disaccharide is formed when two monosaccharides join by condensation, releasing one molecule of water. The link between sugar units is a glycosidic bond. Disaccharides have the formula C₁₂H₂₂O₁₁ (2 × C₆H₁₂O₆ minus H₂O). Hydrolysis splits them back into monosaccharides.
- [ ] C7 Maltose (malt sugar) = glucose + glucose. Formed when starch is digested by amylase; found in germinating grain.
- [ ] C8 Sucrose (table sugar, cane or beet sugar) = glucose + fructose. The form in which plants transport sugar in the phloem.
- [ ] C9 Lactose (milk sugar) = glucose + galactose. Found in milk.
- [ ] C10 Diagram: two hexagons joined, with H₂O removed by condensation and returned by hydrolysis; label "maltose = glucose + glucose".

Polysaccharides (one block)
- [ ] C11 A polysaccharide is a polymer of many monosaccharide units (often thousands) joined by condensation. Starch, glycogen and cellulose are all polymers of glucose; they differ in how the glucose units are linked and whether the chains branch.
- [ ] C12 Starch: the storage carbohydrate of plants, held as starch grains in cells of potato tubers, seeds and cereal grains. It is a mixture of amylose (an unbranched chain that coils into a helix) and amylopectin (a branched chain). Being insoluble, it does not draw water into cells by osmosis, and its compact shape stores much glucose in a small space.
- [ ] C13 Glycogen: the storage carbohydrate of animals (and fungi), stored mainly in the liver and muscles. It is more highly branched than amylopectin, so it has many chain ends from which glucose can be released quickly when energy is needed.
- [ ] C14 Cellulose: the structural carbohydrate of plants, forming the cell wall. Its glucose units are linked in a different orientation from starch, giving long, straight, unbranched chains that lie side by side, held together by many hydrogen bonds, forming strong fibres. Humans have no enzyme to digest cellulose; it passes through the gut as dietary fibre (roughage).
- [ ] C15 Chitin, a polysaccharide that contains nitrogen, forms the cell walls of fungi and the exoskeletons of insects and other arthropods. One bullet.
- [ ] C16 Diagram: a chain of hexagons for starch (unbranched amylose and branched amylopectin), a more branched chain for glycogen, and parallel straight chains linked by cross-links for cellulose fibres.

Lipids (one block on triglycerides, one on fats vs oils, one on phospholipids)
- [ ] L1 Lipids are fats, oils and related compounds such as phospholipids. They contain carbon, hydrogen and oxygen, with much less oxygen in proportion to carbon and hydrogen than carbohydrates (they are not of the form Cx(H₂O)y). Phospholipids also contain phosphorus. Lipids are insoluble in water but dissolve in organic solvents such as ethanol.
- [ ] L2 The triglyceride unit: one glycerol molecule joined to three fatty acids. Glycerol is a small molecule with three carbons, each carrying an –OH group, so it can bond to three fatty acids. A fatty acid is a long chain of carbon and hydrogen atoms (a hydrocarbon chain) with an acid (carboxyl, –COOH) group at one end.
- [ ] L3 Each fatty acid joins to glycerol by condensation, so forming one triglyceride releases three water molecules. The bond formed is an ester bond. Hydrolysis by lipase reverses this, giving glycerol and fatty acids (detail in "The Digestive System").
- [ ] L4 Diagram: a triglyceride as an "E" shape: a vertical glycerol bar with three horizontal fatty acid tails, labelled glycerol, fatty acid, and "3 H₂O removed".
- [ ] L5 Different combinations: there are many different fatty acids, varying in chain length and in whether they contain carbon–carbon double bonds, so glycerol combines with different fatty acids to give many different fats and oils.
- [ ] L6 Saturated fatty acids have no C=C double bonds (the chain holds the maximum hydrogen); unsaturated fatty acids have one or more C=C double bonds, which put kinks in the chain. Saturated chains pack closely, so fats rich in them are solid at room temperature (e.g. butter, lard, the fat on meat; mostly animal fats). Kinked unsaturated chains pack loosely, so oils are liquid at room temperature (e.g. olive oil, sunflower oil, fish oils; mostly from plants and fish).
- [ ] L7 Phospholipid: glycerol joined to two fatty acids and a phosphate-containing group in place of the third fatty acid. The phosphate "head" is hydrophilic (attracted to water); the two fatty acid "tails" are hydrophobic (repelled by water).
- [ ] L8 In water, phospholipids arrange themselves in a double layer (bilayer) with the heads facing the watery fluid on each side and the tails pointing inwards. This bilayer is the basis of every cell membrane (membrane detail in "Transport Across Membranes" and "The Unit of Life — The Cell").
- [ ] L9 Diagram: a phospholipid as a circle (head) with two wavy tails, labelled hydrophilic phosphate head and hydrophobic fatty acid tails; and a section of a bilayer.

Proteins (one block on amino acids and peptide bonds, one on folding and shape)
- [ ] P1 Proteins contain carbon, hydrogen, oxygen and nitrogen, and sometimes sulfur, phosphorus or other elements. Examples: sulfur is in some amino acids (e.g. cysteine and methionine); phosphorus is in casein, the main protein of milk; iron is in haemoglobin.
- [ ] P2 Proteins are made of amino acids. There are 20 different amino acids in the proteins of living things. Each has a central carbon atom bonded to an amino group (–NH₂), an acid (carboxyl) group (–COOH), a hydrogen atom and a variable side group (R group). Only the R group differs between amino acids. Humans cannot make some amino acids (essential amino acids) and must take them in from food.
- [ ] P3 Diagram: the general structure of an amino acid: a central C with –NH₂, –COOH, –H and –R attached; and two amino acids joined by a peptide bond with H₂O released.
- [ ] P4 Amino acids join by condensation between the –COOH group of one and the –NH₂ group of the next, releasing water. The bond formed is a peptide bond. Two amino acids form a dipeptide; a short chain is a peptide; a long chain is a polypeptide. A protein is one or more polypeptides folded into a particular shape.
- [ ] P5 The sequence of amino acids in a polypeptide (its primary structure) is set by a gene. Because 20 amino acids can be arranged in any order and length, a vast number of different proteins is possible.
- [ ] P6 Folding into shapes: the chain coils into a helix or folds into a pleated sheet, held by hydrogen bonds (secondary structure); this folds further into a precise three-dimensional shape (tertiary structure), held by bonds between R groups, including sulfur bridges between cysteine units; some proteins consist of several polypeptides fitted together (quaternary structure), e.g. haemoglobin has four. One bullet per level at most; no further bond chemistry.
- [ ] P7 The shape determines the function: e.g. an enzyme's active site or an antibody's binding site fits only particular molecules. Heat, extremes of pH and some chemicals break the bonds holding the shape, so the protein loses its shape and function: denaturation, usually permanent (e.g. egg white turning solid and white when cooked). Enzyme denaturation detail is in "Enzymes".
- [ ] P8 Fibrous proteins are long strands, insoluble and structural, e.g. keratin (hair, nails) and collagen (skin, tendons). Globular proteins are folded into compact, roughly spherical shapes and are usually soluble, e.g. enzymes, antibodies, haemoglobin and insulin.

Nucleic acids (one bullet)
- [ ] N1 Nucleic acids (DNA and RNA) are polymers of nucleotides. Each nucleotide contains a sugar, a phosphate group and a nitrogenous base, so nucleic acids contain C, H, O, N and P. One bullet; all structure detail is in "DNA, RNA and the Genetic Code".

Summary (one comparison block)
- [ ] S1 A summary table in bullet form, one bullet per biomolecule: elements; unit(s); bond joining the units; examples. Carbohydrate: C, H, O; monosaccharides; glycosidic bond; glucose, sucrose, starch. Lipid: C, H, O (+ P in phospholipids); glycerol and fatty acids; ester bond; triglycerides, phospholipids. Protein: C, H, O, N (sometimes S, P, others); amino acids; peptide bond; enzymes, keratin. Nucleic acid: C, H, O, N, P; nucleotides; DNA, RNA.

## 3. Traps

- Glucose and fructose have the same formula; don't say fructose is C₅ or C₁₂.
- Sucrose is glucose + fructose, not glucose + glucose (that is maltose). Lactose contains galactose.
- Glycogen is not stored in plants; starch is not stored in animals. Cellulose is not a storage carbohydrate.
- A triglyceride is not a polymer; don't call fatty acids its monomers.
- "Fats" and "oils" differ in melting point (solid vs liquid at room temperature), not in their basic unit.
- Peptide bond (proteins), glycosidic bond (carbohydrates) and ester bond (lipids) are easily confused.
- Don't teach steroids or cholesterol structure, or alpha/beta glucose ring diagrams; the specification doesn't ask for them.
- Nucleotide structure, base pairing and the double helix belong to "DNA, RNA and the Genetic Code". ATP belongs to "Transfer Molecules".
- Roles and food sources of each biomolecule belong to "Metabolic roles and nutritional sources"; here give structure, plus only the one-line roles that explain a structure (C12–C14, L8, P7–P8).
- How proteins are made (transcription, translation) belongs to "DNA Replication, Protein Synthesis and Mutations".
- The diagram cues listed (C5, C10, C16, L4, L9, P3) are all that are wanted. No other diagram cues.
