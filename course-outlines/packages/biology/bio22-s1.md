## 1. Must cover

What a pathogen is (one block)
- [ ] P1 A pathogen is an organism or agent that causes disease in its host. An infectious (communicable) disease is one caused by a pathogen that can be passed from one host to another. The host is the organism the pathogen lives in or on. Most microorganisms are not pathogens: many are harmless or useful (see "The microbiome").
- [ ] P2 Pathogens cause harm in three main ways: (1) they invade and destroy host cells or tissues (e.g. viruses bursting the cells they reproduce in; the malaria parasite bursting red blood cells); (2) they release toxins, poisonous chemicals that damage cells or disturb how they work (e.g. the tetanus toxin made by bacteria in a wound causes muscle spasms); (3) they take nutrients from the host. In some diseases the host's own immune response causes much of the damage (e.g. inflammation of the lungs in pneumonia).
- [ ] P3 Each pathogen is adapted to its host: it must enter the host, survive its defences, reproduce, and leave to infect a new host. Many need a vector, an organism that carries a pathogen from one host to another without itself being the disease (e.g. the mosquito for malaria, aphids for many plant viruses). Routes of transmission are in "Emergence and spread of infectious disease"; one sentence here.

Prions (one block)
- [ ] PR1 A prion is an infectious protein particle. It contains no nucleic acid (no DNA or RNA), so it is not a cell and not a virus. The name comes from "proteinaceous infectious particle", coined by Stanley Prusiner in 1982 (Nobel Prize 1997).
- [ ] PR2 How it causes disease: a prion is a misfolded form of a normal protein (prion protein) found on nerve cells, especially in the brain. When a prion meets a normal prion protein, it causes the normal one to fold into the abnormal shape too, so the misfolded protein builds up in a chain reaction. Clumps of misfolded protein kill nerve cells, leaving tiny holes so the brain looks like a sponge under the microscope (spongiform encephalopathy). The diseases are slow (years to appear), always fatal, and have no cure.
- [ ] PR3 Examples: bovine spongiform encephalopathy (BSE, "mad cow disease") in cattle, spread when cattle were fed meat-and-bone meal made from infected animals; variant Creutzfeldt–Jakob disease (vCJD) in humans, linked to eating BSE-infected beef, first described in the UK in 1996; scrapie in sheep; kuru in the Fore people of Papua New Guinea, spread by eating the brains of dead relatives at funeral rites.
- [ ] PR4 Prions are not destroyed by normal cooking, by the usual sterilisation temperatures or by many disinfectants, and the immune system does not attack them because they are the body's own protein in a different shape. Prevention is therefore by keeping them out of the food chain: banning the feeding of meat-and-bone meal to farm animals, and removing the brain and spinal cord of cattle from food (specified risk material).

Viruses (one block; structure assumed)
- [ ] V1 (assumed) A virus is a non-cellular particle of nucleic acid (DNA or RNA) in a protein coat, an obligate intracellular parasite: it reproduces only inside a living host cell (structure in "Viruses"; use without restating).
- [ ] V2 Viruses cause disease by destroying the cells they reproduce in, or by changing how those cells work. Each virus attaches to particular receptor proteins, so it infects only certain hosts and tissues (e.g. HIV infects helper T cells; influenza viruses infect cells lining the airways). How they replicate is in "Viruses: replication".
- [ ] V3 Examples. Humans: influenza, the common cold, measles, chickenpox, COVID-19 (SARS-CoV-2), AIDS (HIV). Animals: foot-and-mouth disease (cattle, sheep, pigs), avian influenza (birds). Plants: tobacco mosaic virus (tobacco, tomatoes). Antibiotics don't affect viruses.

Bacteria (one block)
- [ ] B1 (assumed) Bacteria are single-celled prokaryotes (no nucleus or membrane-bound organelles), about 1–10 µm long (structure in "Bacteria and fungi"; one sentence here). Pathogenic bacteria reproduce rapidly by binary fission in host tissues; most cause harm by releasing toxins, some by invading and destroying tissue.
- [ ] B2 Examples (disease, bacterium, how spread): tuberculosis (TB), Mycobacterium tuberculosis, airborne droplets, mainly affecting the lungs; food poisoning, Salmonella, contaminated food such as undercooked chicken or eggs; cholera, Vibrio cholerae, water contaminated with faeces, causing severe diarrhoea; tetanus, Clostridium tetani, spores in soil entering a wound, its toxin causing muscle spasms ("lockjaw"); Lyme disease, Borrelia burgdorferi, passed by tick bites. In plants: fire blight of apple and pear trees, caused by Erwinia amylovora. In cattle: bovine TB, Mycobacterium bovis.
- [ ] B3 Bacterial diseases can usually be treated with antibiotics, because antibiotics target structures bacteria have and human cells lack (detail in "Acquired immunity, prevention and treatment"), but antibiotic-resistant strains are increasing (e.g. MRSA; see "Emergence and spread of infectious disease").

Fungi (one block)
- [ ] F1 (assumed) Fungi are eukaryotes with cell walls of chitin; most are made of thread-like hyphae, some (yeasts) are single cells (structure in "Bacteria and fungi"). Parasitic fungi grow hyphae into or over host tissue and secrete enzymes that digest it, absorbing the products; they spread by spores, often carried by wind, water or contact.
- [ ] F2 Human examples: athlete's foot and ringworm, skin infections caused by fungi that feed on keratin (ringworm is a fungus, not a worm); thrush, caused by the yeast Candida albicans, in the mouth or vagina, often after antibiotics have killed the bacteria that normally keep it in check. Fungal infections are most dangerous in people with weakened immune systems.
- [ ] F3 Plant and animal examples: ash dieback, caused by the fungus Hymenoscyphus fraxineus, killing ash trees across Europe; Dutch elm disease, caused by the fungus Ophiostoma novo-ulmi and spread by elm bark beetles; rusts of cereals such as wheat (Puccinia species). In amphibians, chytrid fungus (Batrachochytrium dendrobatidis) infects the skin of frogs and other amphibians (details of these as emerging diseases are in "Emergence and spread of infectious disease").
- [ ] F4 Antibiotics don't work on fungi, because fungi are eukaryotic and have no peptidoglycan cell wall or 70S ribosomes; antifungal drugs are used instead.

Protists (one block)
- [ ] PT1 (assumed) Protists are eukaryotes, mostly single-celled, that are not animals, plants or fungi (kingdom Protista; see "Classification"). Parasitic protists often have complex life cycles with more than one host.
- [ ] PT2 Malaria: caused by Plasmodium, passed to humans by the bite of an infected female Anopheles mosquito (the vector). The parasite reproduces in the liver and then in red blood cells, which burst together, causing repeated fevers and anaemia. Control: insecticide-treated bed nets, insecticides, draining standing water where mosquitoes breed, and antimalarial drugs (e.g. artemisinin; see "Science, scientists and society").
- [ ] PT3 Other examples: amoebic dysentery (Entamoeba histolytica), spread in water or food contaminated with faeces; African sleeping sickness (Trypanosoma brucei), spread by tsetse flies.
- [ ] PT4 Potato late blight is caused by Phytophthora infestans, a water mould (oomycete). It looks like a fungus (thread-like and spread by spores) but is classed with the protists, not the fungi. Spores spread on wind and rain splash in mild, wet weather; it rots leaves and tubers. It caused the Irish Great Famine (from 1845; see "Emergence and spread of infectious disease").

Parasitic animals (one block)
- [ ] PA1 A parasite lives in or on another organism (its host), feeding on it and harming it. Endoparasites live inside the host; ectoparasites live on the outside. Parasitic animals are multicellular and much larger than microorganisms; most are worms or arthropods.
- [ ] PA2 Endoparasites: tapeworms (e.g. Taenia) live in the small intestine, absorbing digested food through their body surface and attaching by hooks and suckers; humans are infected by eating undercooked infected pork or beef. Liver fluke (Fasciola hepatica), a flatworm of sheep and cattle, damages the liver; it needs a freshwater mud snail as a second host, so it is common on wet pasture in Ireland. Onchocerca volvulus, a roundworm spread by blackflies, causes river blindness (see "Science, scientists and society").
- [ ] PA3 Ectoparasites: head lice, which feed on blood from the scalp; ticks, which feed on blood and can also act as vectors, carrying the bacterium that causes Lyme disease.
- [ ] PA4 Treatment: drugs that kill parasitic worms (anthelmintics, e.g. ivermectin); prevention by cooking meat thoroughly, sanitation, and controlling the second host or vector.

Summary comparison (one block, one bullet per pathogen type)
- [ ] S1 One bullet each, giving: cellular or not; genetic material; size; treated by. Prion: not cellular; no nucleic acid; a single protein molecule; no treatment. Virus: not cellular; DNA or RNA; about 20–300 nm; antivirals (not antibiotics). Bacterium: prokaryotic cell; DNA; about 1–10 µm; antibiotics. Fungus: eukaryotic; DNA; single cells to large networks of hyphae; antifungals. Protist: eukaryotic, mostly single cells; DNA; antiprotozoal drugs (e.g. antimalarials). Parasitic animal: multicellular eukaryote; DNA; millimetres to metres; anthelmintics.

## 3. Traps

- A prion is not a virus and contains no nucleic acid. Don't say prions "reproduce" or "replicate"; they convert normal protein.
- Phytophthora infestans (potato blight) is not a fungus; call it a water mould (oomycete) placed with the protists. Ringworm is a fungus, not a worm.
- Malaria is caused by Plasmodium, a protist; the mosquito is the vector, not the cause. Lyme disease is caused by a bacterium; the tick is the vector.
- Not every microorganism is a pathogen; don't call bacteria or fungi "germs" as a group.
- Bacterial and fungal cell structure, nutrition types and Rhizopus belong to "Bacteria and fungi" (Microorganisms); virus structure and the living/non-living debate belong to "Viruses". One assumed sentence each here.
- Virus replication belongs to "Viruses: replication"; immune responses to "Innate and adaptive immunity"; vaccines, drugs and hygiene in detail to "Acquired immunity, prevention and treatment"; transmission routes, vectors' spread and antibiotic resistance as a factor to "Emergence and spread of infectious disease". Here each gets one phrase at most.
- Cancer-causing viruses (HPV) belong to "Causes, prevention and treatment of cancer".
- Don't mention lysosomes (not on the specification).

2. 9 marks: "Distinguish between a prion and a virus, and explain why prion diseases are difficult to prevent and impossible to treat." Must not say prions contain DNA or RNA.
3. 12 marks: "Pathogens include viruses, bacteria, fungi, protists and parasitic animals. For any three of these, name one example, state the disease it causes and explain how it harms the host." Model answer: three of V2–V3, B1–B2, F1–F3, PT2–PT3, PA2, each with the mechanism of harm from P2 (destroying cells, toxins, taking nutrients).
