# Package: geo-option-culture-identity-s6 — A European regional case study of identity

- **loId:** `geo-option-culture-identity-s6` · **chapterId:** `geo-option-culture-identity` · **syllabusRef:** 8.3
- **Syllabus:** Statement: "Identity as a concept entails a variety of cultural factors
  including nationality, language, race, and religion." Students should study "a case study
  of a European region to draw together the issues of race, nationality, and identity already
  discussed. This should include an examination of – historical developments: physical and
  political boundaries – ethnicity and race – religion – music, art, festivals etc – the role
  of migration – new boundaries." International setting: "An appropriate case study e.g.
  Celtic regions, The Balkans or Switzerland."
- **Choice:** students study one region. Write each option out in full, under the same six
  syllabus headings, so a student can use any one: **A. Switzerland** (SimpleStudy's case
  study and the most common answer), **B. The Balkans (former Yugoslavia)**, **C. Celtic
  regions (Wales as the main region, Brittany as a second)**, **D. Belgium** (a common
  textbook alternative; its facts match `geo-region-concept-s3` and `geo-region-european-s3`/
  `-s4`). Each option must be able to carry a full 80-mark essay (about four aspects, about
  25–30 SRPs) on its own.
- **Exam form:** Higher Level only. The case-study essay has been set in five of the last ten
  years ("Identity as a concept entails a variety of cultural factors…"; "Cultural identity is
  defined by many diverse factors…"), always "with reference to a European region".
- **Prepared by:** a higher model, 2026-10-20. The writer works only from this package and

## 1. Must cover (one bullet or block for every item; tick each off)

General
- [ ] Z1 What a regional identity case study shows: identity is layered (local, regional, national, European) and made of several factors (language, religion, ethnicity, nationality, culture, shared history); the six syllabus headings; how to structure an essay (choose about four factors as aspects, each developed with the region's facts).

A. Switzerland
- [ ] A1 Historical developments, physical boundaries: the Alps (most of the area), the Jura and the Swiss Plateau where most people live; mountains isolated valleys, preserving local dialects and Romansh; landlocked; lakes and rivers as borders (Lake Geneva, Lake Constance, the Rhine).
- [ ] A2 Historical developments, political boundaries: the 1291 alliance of Uri, Schwyz and Unterwalden; growth by cantons joining; independence recognised in 1648; neutrality and borders fixed in 1815; the Sonderbund War (1847) and the 1848 federal constitution; 26 cantons; direct democracy; the Federal Council; neutrality today (not in the EU; UN only in 2002; Schengen). Switzerland as a "nation by will" (Willensnation) rather than a nation of one language, religion or ethnicity.
- [ ] A3 Ethnicity and language: four national languages and their regions and shares; Swiss German dialects alongside written Standard German; the bilingual and trilingual cantons; the territoriality principle; Romansh's status; the Röstigraben (cultural divide between German- and French-speaking Swiss, visible in referendum results); separate public broadcasters in each language; how Swiss identity overrides language (no language group wants to join its neighbouring country).
- [ ] A4 Religion: the Reformation (Zwingli in Zurich; Calvin in Geneva); Catholic central cantons, Fribourg, Valais, Ticino, Jura; the Sonderbund War as a religious-political conflict; religion cross-cuts language (there are Protestant and Catholic German speakers, and Protestant and Catholic French speakers), which reduces conflict; today's mix (Catholic, Reformed, no religion, Muslim); the 2009 minaret ban and the 2021 face-covering ban.
- [ ] A5 Music, art, festivals etc: yodelling and the alphorn; Schwingen (Swiss wrestling); the national day (1 August); Basel Fasnacht; Zurich's Sechseläuten; the Fête des Vignerons, Vevey; Montreux Jazz Festival; Locarno Film Festival; Art Basel; food (fondue, raclette, rösti, chocolate, cheese); the Red Cross and international Geneva as part of Swiss identity.
- [ ] A6 Role of migration: a quarter of residents are foreign nationals; Italian guest workers after 1945 and the seasonal-worker system; later migrants from Spain, Portugal, Turkey and former Yugoslavia (Kosovo, Bosnia), who brought Islam; free movement with the EU since 2002; cross-border commuters; referendums against immigration (Schwarzenbach 1970, rejected; "against mass immigration" 2014, passed); emigration historically (Swiss mercenaries; emigrants to the Americas).
- [ ] A7 New boundaries: the creation of the canton of Jura (1979) after a separatist campaign by French-speaking Catholics in the north of the canton of Bern; Moutier's move to Jura (2026); relations with the EU (the 1992 rejection of the EEA; bilateral agreements) as a boundary of identity; women's suffrage (1971) as a change in who belongs politically.
- [ ] A8 Evaluation: why Switzerland is stable despite diversity: federalism, direct democracy, neutrality, cross-cutting language and religion, wealth, consensus government.

B. The Balkans (former Yugoslavia)
- [ ] B1 Historical developments, physical boundaries: the Dinaric Alps and rugged relief fragmenting settlement; the Danube and Sava; the Adriatic coast; "Balkanisation" as a term for break-up into small hostile states.
- [ ] B2 Historical developments, political boundaries: the Great Schism line and the Ottoman–Habsburg frontier running through the region; Ottoman rule in the south and east (from the 14th–15th centuries) and Austro-Hungarian rule in the north and west; Sarajevo 1914; the Kingdom of Serbs, Croats and Slovenes (1918), Yugoslavia (1929); the Second World War (Ustaše, Chetniks, Tito's Partisans); Tito's federation of six republics and two autonomous provinces; "brotherhood and unity"; Tito's death (1980); rising nationalism (Milošević, Kosovo's autonomy removed 1989).
- [ ] B3 Ethnicity and race: South Slav peoples (Slovenes, Croats, Serbs, Bosniaks, Montenegrins, Macedonians) who are of similar ancestry and largely speak one language (Serbo-Croatian, now standardised as Serbian, Croatian, Bosnian and Montenegrin) but differ in religion, script and history; Albanians (non-Slav) in Kosovo; Hungarians in Vojvodina; Bosnia's 1991 mix; ethnicity defined largely by religion rather than race.
- [ ] B4 Religion: Catholic Slovenes and Croats; Orthodox Serbs, Montenegrins and Macedonians; Muslim Bosniaks and most Albanians (from conversion under the Ottomans); Sarajevo's mosques, churches and synagogue; religious buildings as war targets (Ferhadija Mosque, Banja Luka); Medjugorje pilgrimage.
- [ ] B5 The wars of the 1990s as identity conflict: Slovenia (1991), Croatia (1991–95: Vukovar, Operation Storm), Bosnia (1992–95: ethnic cleansing, siege of Sarajevo, Srebrenica, Mostar bridge), Kosovo (1998–99, NATO bombing); the ICTY; deaths and displacement.
- [ ] B6 Music, art, festivals etc: EXIT festival (Novi Sad); the Guča trumpet festival (Serbia); Sarajevo Film Festival (founded during the siege); sevdalinka (Bosnian traditional song); kolo dance; Mostar's Old Bridge and bridge diving; football and nationalism (the 1990 Maksimir riot); Eurovision.
- [ ] B7 Role of migration: guest workers to Germany, Austria and Switzerland from the 1960s; refugee flows and ethnic cleansing in the 1990s that made areas ethnically uniform; the 2015 Western Balkans refugee route; emigration of young people today.
- [ ] B8 New boundaries: independence of Slovenia, Croatia, Macedonia (1991), Bosnia (1992); Serbia and Montenegro (2003) and Montenegro's independence (2006); Kosovo (2008) and its partial recognition; Bosnia's Dayton structure (two entities plus Brčko); North Macedonia's name change (2019); EU membership of Slovenia and Croatia; candidate status of the others.
- [ ] B9 Evaluation: boundaries drawn along old republic lines left large minorities; the Dayton structure ended war but fixed ethnic divisions; EU accession as a hoped-for softening of borders.

C. Celtic regions (Wales main, Brittany second)
- [ ] C1 The Celtic regions and languages: the six regions usually counted (Ireland, Scotland, the Isle of Man, Wales, Cornwall, Brittany) on Europe's Atlantic fringe; Goidelic and Brythonic branches; Celtic peoples pushed west by Romans and Anglo-Saxons; Britons migrating to Brittany (5th–6th centuries); peripheral, mountainous or coastal locations helped the languages survive.
- [ ] C2 Wales, historical developments and boundaries: Offa's Dyke as an early boundary; conquest by Edward I (1282–83) and his castles; the Laws in Wales Acts (1535, 1542); industrialisation of the south; devolution (1997 referendum, 1999 Assembly, Senedd 2020, enlarged 2026); Wales's border with England unchanged but its political powers growing.
- [ ] C3 Wales, ethnicity and language: the Welsh as a nation within the UK; Welsh as the core of identity (Census 2021 figures, the north-west heartland "Y Fro Gymraeg", the English-speaking south-east); language policy (facts in s2: use, don't repeat at length).
- [ ] C4 Wales, religion: Nonconformist chapels (Methodist, Baptist, Independent) as the centre of 19th-century Welsh-speaking life; the 1851 religious census; the Church in Wales disestablished (1920); decline of chapel-going today.
- [ ] C5 Wales, music, art, festivals etc: the National Eisteddfod and the Gorsedd; the Urdd Eisteddfod; male voice choirs; the harp; rugby as a national sport (WRU 1881, Principality Stadium); St David's Day (1 March); the anthem "Hen Wlad Fy Nhadau"; symbols (daffodil, leek, red dragon); food (cawl, Welsh cakes, bara brith).
- [ ] C6 Wales, role of migration: English and Irish in-migration to the coalfield in the 19th century; Welsh emigration (Patagonia 1865; to England and America); modern in-migration of English retirees and second-home owners to rural Welsh-speaking areas and out-migration of young Welsh speakers; responses (2023 second-homes premium; earlier arson campaign); newer immigrant communities in Cardiff.
- [ ] C7 Brittany: union with France (1532); centralisation after 1789 and the decline of Breton; the 1941 removal of Loire-Atlantique (Nantes) from the region and the campaign to reunite it; Catholic identity (pardons); music and festivals (bagadoù, biniou and bombarde, fest-noz, Lorient, Alan Stivell); costume (Bigouden coiffe); food (crêpes, galettes, cider); the Gwenn-ha-Du flag; language policy contrast (Diwan; France's constitution) from s2.
- [ ] C8 Links among Celtic regions: the Festival Interceltique de Lorient; shared music; Irish and Welsh cooperation on language; EU minority-language support.

D. Belgium
- [ ] D1 Historical developments and boundaries: low, open physical landscape with no barrier between language groups (the language border is cultural, not physical); independence 1830; French dominance; the language border fixed 1962–63; federalisation (1970 reform; federal state 1993; regions and communities).
- [ ] D2 Ethnicity and language: Flemings (Dutch-speaking) in Flanders, Walloons (French-speaking) in Wallonia, bilingual Brussels, the German-speaking Community; population shares; the Leuven split (1968); separate parties and media; 541 days without a government; the N-VA and Flemish nationalism.
- [ ] D3 Religion: traditionally Catholic across both language groups (religion unites rather than divides); historic Catholic–liberal conflicts over schools; secularisation; Islam through Moroccan and Turkish migration.
- [ ] D4 Music, art, festivals etc: Flemish art (van Eyck's Ghent Altarpiece, Bruegel, Rubens); comics (Hergé's Tintin); the Carnival of Binche; Tomorrowland (Boom); 11 July (Flemish Community holiday, Battle of the Golden Spurs, 1302); food and drink (beer culture on UNESCO's list, chocolate, frites, waffles).
- [ ] D5 Role of migration: Italian miners after 1946 and the Marcinelle disaster (1956); Moroccan and Turkish workers (1964 agreements); Brussels as one of Europe's most diverse cities and seat of the EU; tensions (the 2016 Brussels bombings).
- [ ] D6 Economic reversal as part of identity: Wallonia's coal and steel decline vs Flanders' growth (Antwerp), shifting power and feeding Flemish nationalism.
- [ ] D7 New boundaries: the language border, federal regions, and debate over further devolution or separation; Brussels as an enclave.

### A. Switzerland
| Item | Example(s) |
|---|---|
| A1 | About 60% of Switzerland is in the Alps; the Jura mountains lie in the north-west; most people and cities are on the Swiss Plateau (Mittelland) between them. The Rhine and Rhône rise in the Swiss Alps. Lake Geneva (shared with France) and Lake Constance (shared with Germany and Austria) form parts of its borders. Population about 9 million (2024). Main cities: Zurich, Geneva, Basel, Lausanne, Bern (the federal city/capital). |
| A2 | Federal Charter of 1 August 1291: alliance of Uri, Schwyz and Unterwalden (1 August is the national day). Independence from the Holy Roman Empire recognised in the Peace of Westphalia, 1648. Congress of Vienna, 1815: recognised Switzerland's permanent neutrality; Geneva, Valais and Neuchâtel joined the Confederation as cantons. Sonderbund War, November 1847: a short civil war in which a league of Catholic, conservative cantons was defeated. Federal constitution 1848 created the modern federal state; revised in 1874 and fully revised in 1999. 26 cantons with wide powers (schools, police, taxes, churches). Direct democracy: citizens vote in referendums several times a year; a popular initiative needs 100,000 signatures. The Federal Council: seven members from the main parties governing collectively, with language regions represented by custom. Switzerland is not in the EU; it joined the UN only after a referendum in 2002; it joined the Schengen area in 2008. "Willensnation": a nation formed by the will of its people rather than by a common language or ethnicity. |
| A3 | Main languages (Federal Statistical Office, early 2020s; people may give more than one): German about 62%, French about 23%, Italian about 8%, Romansh about 0.5%. 17 cantons are German-speaking; 4 French-speaking (Geneva, Vaud, Neuchâtel, Jura); 1 Italian-speaking (Ticino); 3 bilingual German–French (Bern, Fribourg, Valais); 1 trilingual (Graubünden: German, Romansh, Italian). German-speaking Swiss speak Swiss German dialects (Schweizerdeutsch) in daily life but write Standard German. Territoriality principle: each canton or commune has its official language(s), used in its schools and administration. Romansh became a national language by referendum in 1938 and a partly official language in 1996; Rumantsch Grischun (a written standard) was created in 1982. The Röstigraben (the "rösti ditch"): the cultural divide between German- and French-speaking Switzerland, often seen in referendum results; it runs roughly along the River Sarine (Saane) in Fribourg. The public broadcaster SRG SSR runs separate services in German (SRF), French (RTS), Italian (RSI) and Romansh (RTR). Children learn a second national language at school. |
| A4 | The Reformation was led by Ulrich Zwingli in Zurich (from 1519) and John Calvin in Geneva (from the 1530s–40s); Geneva became known as the "Protestant Rome". Historically Protestant: Zurich, Bern, Basel, Geneva, Vaud, Neuchâtel. Historically Catholic: the central cantons (Lucerne, Uri, Schwyz, Zug, Unterwalden), Fribourg, Valais, Ticino and Jura. Religion therefore cuts across language: both German- and French-speaking Switzerland contain Protestant and Catholic cantons. Religion today (2022): Roman Catholic about a third, Reformed (Protestant) about a fifth, no religion about a third, Muslim about 6%. 29 November 2009: a popular initiative banning the building of new minarets passed with about 57.5%. March 2021: a ban on face coverings in public passed with about 51%; in force from 1 January 2025. |
| A5 | Yodelling and the alphorn are traditional Alpine music. Schwingen: Swiss wrestling in sawdust rings, held at Alpine festivals. 1 August: national day, with bonfires and fireworks. Basel Fasnacht: carnival beginning at 4 a.m. on the Monday after Ash Wednesday (the "Morgestraich"); on UNESCO's intangible heritage list since 2017. Sechseläuten, Zurich (April): the guilds parade and a snowman figure, the Böögg, is burned to mark the end of winter. Fête des Vignerons, Vevey: winegrowers' festival held only about four or five times a century, most recently in 2019; on UNESCO's list since 2016. Montreux Jazz Festival (since 1967), Locarno Film Festival (since 1946), Art Basel (since 1970). Foods: fondue, raclette, rösti, chocolate, Gruyère and Emmental cheese. The International Committee of the Red Cross was founded in Geneva in 1863 (Henri Dunant); Geneva hosts the UN's European office and many international organisations. |
| A6 | Foreign nationals are about a quarter of the permanent resident population (about 27%, 2023); the largest groups are Italians, Germans, Portuguese and French, plus large communities from Kosovo and elsewhere in former Yugoslavia, Spain and Turkey. Most Swiss Muslims have roots in former Yugoslavia (Kosovo, Bosnia, North Macedonia) or Turkey. After 1945 many Italian guest workers came, often on a seasonal-worker permit that restricted family reunification (abolished 2002). Schwarzenbach Initiative (1970) to cap the foreign population was rejected by about 54%. Free movement of persons with the EU since 2002 (bilateral agreements). "Against mass immigration" initiative, 9 February 2014: passed with about 50.3%. Almost 400,000 cross-border commuters (from France, Italy and Germany) work in Switzerland each day (2020s), many in Geneva, Basel and Ticino. Historically, Swiss men served abroad as mercenaries (the Pope's Swiss Guard, since 1506, is a survivor of this). |
| A7 | Canton of Jura created on 1 January 1979 after referendums from 1974 in which the French-speaking, mainly Catholic northern districts voted to leave the German-speaking, mainly Protestant canton of Bern; the southern French-speaking districts (mainly Protestant) chose to stay in Bern. The town of Moutier voted in 2021 to move from Bern to Jura and transferred on 1 January 2026. 6 December 1992: Swiss voters rejected joining the European Economic Area (about 50.3% No); relations with the EU since rest on bilateral agreements. Women gained the federal vote only in 1971; the last canton, Appenzell Innerrhoden, was forced by a federal court ruling to allow women to vote in cantonal matters in 1990. |

Medjugorje, Bosnia and Herzegovina: Catholic pilgrimage site since reported apparitions in 1981. |

Siege of Sarajevo by Bosnian Serb forces: April 1992 to February 1996, the longest siege of a capital city in modern warfare. Mostar's Old Bridge (Stari Most, 1566) was destroyed by Croat forces on 9 November 1993 and rebuilt in 2004 (UNESCO World Heritage Site 2005). Kosovo war 1998–99: NATO bombed Serbia for 78 days (March–June 1999); about 800,000 Kosovo Albanians fled or were expelled. Large-scale emigration of young people is causing population decline in Serbia, Bosnia and Croatia. |

## 3. Traps

- Switzerland's religion: Geneva, Vaud and Neuchâtel are historically **Protestant** (Calvin's Geneva). Past-paper model answers wrongly say French-speaking regions are Catholic. The key point is that religion cuts across language.
- Switzerland's language shares: use only the rounded figures in A3; don't say "63%" or "<1%" variations from past papers, and don't present them as summing to 100% (people can give several languages).
- Don't claim that all seven Federal Council members are German speakers (a past-paper answer does); language balance is customary.
- Moutier: voted in 2021 and moved on 1 January 2026. An earlier 2017 vote was annulled; don't cite it as the decision.
- The Sonderbund War was short; give no death toll.
- Balkans: give only the Bosnia death toll (about 100,000) and Srebrenica (about 8,000); don't give a total for all the Yugoslav wars (consistent with `geo-region-european-s4`). Don't use "1.4 million Serbs outside Serbia" or "two million displaced" (unchecked past-paper figures).
- Bosniaks were counted as "Muslims" (a nationality) in the 1991 census; the term Bosniak came into general use during the war.
- Serbo-Croatian: the groups mostly understand each other; differences are of script, religion and history, not race. Don't describe Balkan groups as different races.
- Wales: the 1997 referendum passed by about 50.3%, very narrowly; don't confuse it with the 1979 referendum (not checked: don't use). The Senedd's 2026 election results are not checked: don't state them.
- Brittany: don't give Breton speaker numbers (none checked). Nantes is outside the official Brittany region.
- Belgium: Brussels is inside Flanders geographically but is its own region; it is bilingual officially, mostly French-speaking in practice. Don't use "85% French-speaking".
- Overlaps: s2 owns Welsh and Breton language policy (refer to it, give only the key figures here); `geo-region-european-s3` owns Belgium's present-day politics and the EU, and `-s4` owns Belgium's language-border law and Yugoslav boundary changes as boundary topics; use identical facts. Here everything is organised under the six identity headings.
- Don't write a hybrid essay: an 80-mark answer uses one region only.

## 4. What exams ask (topics only)

- 80 marks (set five times in ten years): identity entails a variety of cultural factors / cultural identity is defined by many diverse factors, with reference to one European region. Expected: four aspects such as historical development and boundaries, language/ethnicity, religion, and culture (music, art, festivals) or migration, each developed with dated facts for that one region, with an evaluation of how the factors combine.
