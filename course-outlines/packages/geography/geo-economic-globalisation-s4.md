## 1. Must cover (one bullet or block for every item; tick each off)

Basic processing units are widely spread
- [ ] B1 Meaning: a basic processing unit is a factory carrying out one stage of production (processing a raw material, making a component or assembling a product). MNCs place each stage in a different country (link to the Intel chain in s1; refer to it, don't repeat it).
- [ ] B2 Why they are spread: cheap labour for labour-intensive assembly; nearness to raw materials or to markets; getting inside trade blocs; tax breaks and grants; export processing zones; falling transport and communication costs; spreading risk so one disaster or strike does not stop all production.
- [ ] B3 Nike block (about 6 SRPs): headquarters, design and marketing in Oregon, USA; owns almost none of the factories that make its shoes; contracts production to independent factories in Asia, Vietnam being the largest; sells worldwide; criticism of sweatshop conditions in the 1990s led to factory monitoring and published supplier lists.
- [ ] B4 Export processing zones (EPZs) / Special Economic Zones: areas where firms import components and export finished goods free of customs duty, with tax breaks. Examples: Shannon Free Zone (Ireland's own pioneer), Shenzhen (China), maquiladoras along Mexico's border with the USA.
- [ ] B5 Disadvantages of a spread-out system: long supply chains are vulnerable to disruption (examples in s3 checked facts: COVID-19 chip shortage, Ever Given, Red Sea); branch plants close when costs change; low wages and poor conditions in some plants; environmental cost of transport. Response: reshoring and "friend-shoring" (one bullet).

Core and peripheral regions at global scale
- [ ] C1 Core: wealthy, densely developed areas with capital, MNC headquarters, R&D, skilled labour, good infrastructure and political power (the triad: north-east USA and California, western Europe's "Blue Banana", Japan). Periphery: poorer areas supplying raw materials, food and cheap labour, with weak infrastructure and out-migration (most of sub-Saharan Africa, parts of Latin America and South Asia). Semi-periphery: NICs moving towards the core (South Korea, earlier; China, India, Brazil, Mexico now).
- [ ] C2 How the core developed: early industrialisation (Britain first, then western Europe and the USA); colonial empires supplied raw materials and markets; profits reinvested; cumulative causation (success attracts more investment, people and services); backwash effects drain capital and skilled people from the periphery.
- [ ] C3 How the periphery developed (or failed to): colonial economies built around exporting one or two raw materials; railways ran from mines and plantations to ports, not between regions; borders drawn by colonisers; after independence, dependence on falling commodity prices, debt and conflict.
- [ ] C4 The pattern can change: spread effects and investment can raise peripheral regions; the Asian Tigers and China moved from periphery to semi-periphery or core in two generations; Ireland moved from European periphery to a core-like economy through MNC investment (one bullet; link to `geo-region-ireland`).
- [ ] C5 North–South (Brandt Line) as a simple model of the same pattern: one bullet only; criticism of the idea belongs to the Global Interdependence option.

Regions excluded from world manufacturing
- [ ] X1 Sub-Saharan Africa is the main excluded region: it has a very small share of world manufacturing and exports mostly unprocessed raw materials (examples in section 2).
- [ ] X2 Reasons, each a developed point: poor infrastructure (roads, ports, unreliable electricity); many landlocked countries with high transport costs; political instability, conflict and corruption that scare off investment; low levels of education and skills, and poor health; small, poor home markets; heavy debt; colonial legacy of raw-material economies; tariff escalation (rich countries charge higher tariffs on processed goods than on raw materials, so processing at home doesn't pay); competition from established Asian manufacturers; climate hazards such as drought.
- [ ] X3 A named example of exclusion: Sudan (civil war since 2023, dependence on oil and farm exports; links to `geo-economic-development` case study, so facts only from section 2); the Democratic Republic of the Congo (cobalt exported raw despite huge reserves, conflict).
- [ ] X4 Attempts to reduce exclusion: debt relief (HIPC), the African Continental Free Trade Area, Chinese and other foreign investment in infrastructure, fair trade. One block.

The impact of globalisation on world trade
- [ ] W1 Definition of globalisation: the growing integration of the world's economies through trade, investment, MNCs, technology and the movement of people and ideas.
- [ ] W2 Effects on world trade, each one point: more trade (grown faster than output); MNCs control most of it, much as trade between parts of the same company or its suppliers (intra-firm trade, global value chains); trade in components rather than finished goods; rapid growth in trade in services and digital trade; new trading powers (China); the WTO and trade blocs lowering barriers; containerisation and huge ports; e-commerce bringing small sellers into world markets.
- [ ] W3 Winners and losers: consumers gain cheaper goods; NICs and China gain jobs and growth; manufacturing regions in the core lose jobs; excluded regions fall further behind; workers in low-wage assembly can face poor conditions (Rana Plaza); environmental costs of transport.
- [ ] W4 Recent reaction against globalisation: the US–China trade war (2018), US tariffs (2025), Brexit, reshoring of strategic industries such as chips, supply-chain worries after COVID-19. One block; facts already in s3, refer to them.

Impact of globalisation on one developing economy (asked at Higher)
- [ ] E1 India, full block (about 15 SRPs): before 1991 (protection, Licence Raj); the 1991 liberalisation; positive impacts (rapid growth, IT and business-process outsourcing, foreign investment by MNCs, car and pharmaceutical exports, a growing middle class, Make in India and iPhone assembly, hundreds of millions moved out of extreme poverty); negative impacts (inequality between cities and rural areas and between states, small farmers exposed to world prices, the 2020–21 farmers' protests, most workers still in the informal sector, pollution and MNC accidents such as Bhopal).
- [ ] E2 China, short block: the 1978 reforms, SEZs, WTO 2001, huge growth in exports, about 800 million people lifted out of extreme poverty, but inequality between the coast and the interior, pollution and dependence on exports.
- [ ] E3 Bangladesh, short block: garment exports dominate; jobs, especially for women; Rana Plaza disaster and the Accord on building safety that followed.

## 3. Traps

- Don't give Africa's percentage share of world trade or manufacturing: say "very small".
- Sudan and South Sudan are separate countries since 2011. Don't call the 2023 war a war with South Sudan.
- Shannon Free Zone was 1959; the duty-free shop was 1947. Don't swap them.
- The Rana Plaza toll is "over 1,100": don't give a different figure. It was a building collapse, not a fire.
- Nike is a "manufacturer without factories": don't say Nike owns its Asian factories.
- Don't present globalisation as wholly good or wholly bad; exams reward both sides.
- Don't copy the past-paper model answers' India figures (growth rates, job counts, suicide totals): they are invented.
- The full India regional study (monsoon, agriculture, the Golden Quadrilateral, urban case studies) is in `geo-region-continental-s1`; India's colonial history and its effects belong to `geo-economic-development-s3`. Here, use only India's globalisation story.
- The trading triad, the division of labour and trade vocabulary belong to `geo-economic-globalisation-s3`; branch-plant mobility and the product life cycle to `-s2`; the Intel and Apple supply chains to `-s1`. Refer, don't repeat.
- Measures of development (GNP, HDI) belong to `geo-economic-development-s1`; North–South criticism and fair trade in depth to `geo-option-global-interdependence`.

## 4. What exams ask (topics only)

- Higher, 30 marks: explain the impact of globalisation on one developing economy. Expected: India (or another studied economy) with both positive and negative impacts, named examples and dates.

- Short: define globalisation; name a region excluded from manufacturing and give a reason.
