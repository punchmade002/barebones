# Package: geo-mapwork-s1 — Map interpretation (Ordnance Survey mapwork)

- **loId:** `geo-mapwork-s1` · **chapterId:** `geo-mapwork` · **syllabusRef:** 3 (Skills)
- **Syllabus:** Higher Level, "1. Map and aerial photograph interpretation. Students should use
  and apply the spatial concepts of co-ordinate systems (latitude and longitude); grid
  references; scale, distance, and direction; altitude and slope; sketch maps; symbol
  recognition; cross-sections; pattern recognition; statistical mapping; absolute and relative
  location using maps and aerial photographs." Ordinary Level lists "co-ordinate systems
  (latitude and longitude); grid references; scale, distance and direction; altitude and slope;
  sketch maps; symbol recognition; pattern recognition" (no cross-sections, statistical mapping
  or absolute/relative location). The general aims list "maps of various scales including
  Ordnance Survey maps" as an information-gathering skill, and the assessment section says all
  exam questions "will contain stimulus material and a geographical skills element, where
  appropriate".
- **Scope decision:** `geo-aerial-photographs-s1` hands every map-only skill to this package. So
  this package covers in full: grid references, scale, distance and area, direction, height and
  slope (including gradient and aspect), cross-sections and elevation profiles, sketch maps from
  OS maps, symbols, latitude and longitude, drainage patterns and landforms read from the map,
  and settlement and historical interpretation on OS maps (rural settlement patterns, settlement
  distribution, historic settlement, why a town developed, town functions), plus the map-evidence
  method for site-suitability and economic or tourism questions. Many of those longer questions
  are tagged to other chapters (`geo-urban`, `geo-population-migration`, economic chapters);
  the map-reading method lives here, the theory lives there.
- **This is a skills subsection.** It teaches methods and worked examples, not places. Every
  worked example uses the **invented "Rivertown" map** in section 2, with invented values
  labelled "illustrative" in the notes. Never use a real exam map, its town, features, grid
  references, road numbers or measurements.
- **Prepared by:** a higher model, 2026-10-20. The writer works only from this package and
  `course-outlines/NOTES_STANDARD.md`.

## 1. Must cover (one bullet or block for every item; tick each off)

The Ordnance Survey map
- [ ] O1 The exam map is a 1:50 000 Ordnance Survey (Discovery Series) extract with a legend; questions say "examine the map and legend". All symbol, colour and road-class answers come from the legend.
- [ ] O2 Scale types: ratio or representative fraction (1:50 000 means 1 unit on the map = 50 000 of the same units on the ground); linear scale (scale bar); statement ("2 cm to 1 km"). On 1:50 000: 1 cm = 500 m (0.5 km); 2 cm = 1 km; each grid square is 2 cm × 2 cm = 1 km × 1 km = 1 km².
- [ ] O3 Large scale vs small scale: a large-scale map (e.g. 1:1 000) shows a small area in great detail; a small-scale map (e.g. 1:1 000 000) shows a large area with little detail. 1:50 000 is a medium scale suited to towns and their surroundings.

Grid references and location
- [ ] G1 The Irish Grid: Ireland is divided into 25 sub-zones, each 100 km × 100 km, lettered A–Z with I omitted. Every grid reference starts with the sub-zone letter, shown on the map.
- [ ] G2 Eastings (vertical lines, numbered along the top and bottom, values increase eastward) are read first; northings (horizontal lines, numbered up the sides, values increase northward) second. Memory aid: "along the corridor, then up the stairs".
- [ ] G3 Four-figure grid reference: identifies a whole 1 km square, using the easting and northing of the line at its bottom-left (south-west) corner. Used for areas (a lake, a forest, a slope).
- [ ] G4 Six-figure grid reference: identifies a point to the nearest 100 m; divide the square into tenths mentally, estimate tenths east of the easting line and tenths north of the northing line. Used for point features (a building symbol, a road junction, a spot height, where a road exits the map). Give the reference for the centre of the symbol.
- [ ] G5 Reading a grid reference given in a question: find the sub-zone, the easting, then the northing, and name what is there exactly as the map or legend labels it (e.g. a named antiquity, a public service such as a Garda station or post office, a tourist feature). Common Ordinary task: name the feature at three or four grid references.
- [ ] G6 Absolute location: a precise position given by a grid reference or by latitude and longitude. Relative location: position described relative to other features or directions ("north-west of the town, beside the river", "in the north-east of the map"). Exams ask which area of the map (north-west, south-east, etc.) a named feature is in.

Latitude and longitude (co-ordinate systems)
- [ ] L1 Latitude: angular distance north or south of the Equator (0°), measured along meridians, up to 90°N and 90°S at the poles. Lines of latitude (parallels) run east–west and are parallel.
- [ ] L2 Longitude: angular distance east or west of the Prime Meridian (0°) through Greenwich, London, up to 180°. Lines of longitude (meridians) run north–south and meet at the poles.
- [ ] L3 Degrees are divided into 60 minutes (′); a position is written latitude first with N/S, then longitude with E/W (e.g. 53°20′N, 6°15′W).
- [ ] L4 Key lines: Equator 0°; Tropics of Cancer and Capricorn at about 23.5°N and S; Arctic and Antarctic Circles at about 66.5°N and S; Prime Meridian 0°; the International Date Line roughly follows 180°.
- [ ] L5 One degree of latitude ≈ 111 km everywhere; a degree of longitude is about 111 km at the Equator and shrinks to zero at the poles.
- [ ] L6 Time and longitude: the earth turns 360° in 24 hours, so 15° of longitude = 1 hour of time; places east are ahead. One bullet.
- [ ] L7 Ireland's position: in the northern and western hemispheres, between about 51.5°N and 55.5°N and about 5.5°W and 10.5°W. Grid references are used on OS maps because they are quicker and more precise at local scale; latitude and longitude are used for global location.

Scale, distance and area
- [ ] D1 Straight-line distance: measure with a ruler in cm and multiply by 0.5 to get km; or lay a paper edge between the points and read it against the scale bar. Answer in km, to one decimal place.
- [ ] D2 Straight-line distance from two grid references (alternative): difference in eastings and in northings in km; distance = √(ΔE² + ΔN²). One bullet with the worked example W2.
- [ ] D3 Curved distance (a road, railway, river or walk): mark the start on the straight edge of a strip of paper; pivot the strip at each bend, marking as you go (or lay a piece of string along the route); straighten and measure against the scale bar. Start and stop exactly at the points named (e.g. where the road enters the map, a junction, a roundabout).
- [ ] D4 Area of a rectangular part of the map: count kilometres east–west × kilometres north–south (e.g. the whole map, or the part north of a named northing or west of a named easting). 1 square = 1 km².
- [ ] D5 Area of an irregular feature (a lake, a bay, land above a contour): count the full squares, then count squares more than half covered as full and ignore those less than half covered (or add part-squares as estimated fractions). Answer in km², "approximately".
- [ ] D6 Area of part of a sub-zone or of the land on one side of a road or railway: count squares on that side; a partly covered square is estimated. Exclude sea where the question says "land".

Direction
- [ ] R1 North is the top of the OS map (grid north). Eight compass points: N, NE, E, SE, S, SW, W, NW; sixteen add NNE, ENE, ESE, SSE, SSW, WSW, WNW, NNW.
- [ ] R2 Method: draw a line from the start point to the destination; imagine a compass centred on the start; read the direction of travel. "From A to B" means the direction you travel, measured at A. A quick check: is B above or below A (north/south), left or right (west/east)?
- [ ] R3 Direction along a route (a walk, a road, a river's flow): give the overall direction from its start to its end. A river flows from high ground to low ground, from source to mouth; use spot heights and contours to tell.
- [ ] R4 Bearings (optional for Higher): angle measured clockwise from north, 0°–360°; E = 090°, S = 180°, W = 270°. One bullet.

Height and slope (altitude and slope)
- [ ] H1 How height is shown: contours (lines joining places of equal height above sea level; on the Discovery Series the vertical interval is 10 m; some contours are numbered with their height); spot heights (a dot with a height in metres); triangulation pillars (a survey point with its height); layer colouring (colour bands for height ranges, shown in the legend). Height in metres.
- [ ] H2 Finding the height of a point: read the spot height or pillar beside it, or the nearest numbered contour, and count contours up or down at 10 m each. The highest point on the map is the highest spot height or pillar (check the uplands).
- [ ] H3 Slope from contour spacing: contours close together = steep; far apart = gentle; no contours over a wide area = flat land (e.g. floodplains, coastal lowland).
- [ ] H4 Slope types by contour pattern: even (constant) slope, contours evenly spaced; concave slope, contours close together near the top and widely spaced near the bottom (steep above, gentle below, so you can see the whole slope from the top); convex slope, contours widely spaced near the top and close together near the bottom (gentle above, steep below, so the foot cannot be seen from the top); stepped slope, alternating bands of close and wide spacing.
- [ ] H5 Relief features from contours: valley (contours form V or U shapes pointing upstream, towards higher ground, with a river along the floor); spur (V shapes pointing downhill, towards lower ground); plateau (high, flat or gently sloping top with steep edges; contours close around the edge, few on top); ridge; col or saddle (a low pass between two summits); escarpment (steep slope on one side, gentle on the other); knoll or isolated hill (closed ring of contours).
- [ ] H6 Gradient: the steepness of a slope = vertical interval (VI, the difference in height) ÷ horizontal equivalent (HE, the ground distance), both in the same units, expressed as "1 in x" (or as a ratio 1:x). Method: find the height of each point; subtract for the VI; measure the distance on the map and convert to metres for the HE; divide HE by VI. A larger x means a gentler slope. It is an average gradient between the two points.
- [ ] H7 Aspect: the direction a slope faces, i.e. the direction you look when facing downhill (a slope that falls towards the south is south-facing, with a southerly aspect). In Ireland south-facing slopes receive more sunshine and are warmer.
- [ ] H8 Intervisibility (Higher, one bullet): whether one point can be seen from another; a convex slope or intervening high ground blocks the view; check with a cross-section.

Cross-sections and elevation profiles
- [ ] X1 Drawing a cross-section: (1) join the two points with a line on the map; (2) lay a straight paper edge along it and mark every contour crossing with its height, plus rivers, roads and other features; (3) draw a horizontal axis the same length as the line (horizontal scale = map scale) and a vertical axis with a chosen height scale; (4) plot each height above its mark and join the points with a smooth curve; (5) label rivers, roads, the summit, slope types, the ends (X and Y with grid references) and give a title and both scales.
- [ ] X2 Vertical exaggeration: the vertical scale is stretched so relief is visible; vertical exaggeration = horizontal scale distance ÷ vertical scale distance for the same map length (worked example W6). Mention it in the title or key.
- [ ] X3 Reading an elevation profile set in the exam (a printed profile along a named easting or northing between points X and Y, with features labelled A–E): match each labelled point to the map by its distance along the line and its height; then identify (a) a river crossing (a dip, with a blue line on the map at that spot), (b) a road (read its number from the map), (c) the height at a point (read from the vertical axis or the map), (d) the slope type between two points (concave, convex, even, steep, gentle, flat), (e) the land use or forestry type (from the map symbol there), (f) a named walk, cycle route or tourist attraction crossing the line, (g) the townland or place name at a point. True/false versions of these are common.

Symbols (symbol recognition)
- [ ] S1 Learn the legend groups: roads (motorway M, national N, regional R, third-class and other roads); railways and stations; water features; height; vegetation; antiquities; tourist and leisure information; public services; boundaries. The legend is provided in the exam, so drawing a symbol means copying it accurately from the legend, in proportion, with its colour if coloured pencils are available.
- [ ] S2 Road letters: M = motorway; N = national road (national primary and national secondary routes); R = regional road. Exams ask what N or R stands for.
- [ ] S3 Vegetation: coniferous plantation (pointed, conifer-shaped tree symbols), deciduous woodland (rounded tree symbols), mixed woodland (both); exams ask the type of forestry at a point.
- [ ] S4 Symbols exams have asked students to draw or name: viewpoint, picnic site, parking, boating activities, triangulation pillar, ringfort, county boundary, wind farm, post office, Garda station, youth or independent holiday hostel, golf course, camping site, electricity transmission line, waymarked walk, cycle route. List these as "learn from the legend"; do not describe exact shapes or colours beyond S3 and those in Checked facts.
- [ ] S5 Antiquities: the legend groups them; name an antiquity exactly as the map labels it (e.g. "Castle (in ruins)", "Ringfort", "Standing Stone", "Megalithic Tomb", "Church (in ruins)", "Holy Well", "Motte"). Antiquity names are in the historic-settlement table (A1).
- [ ] S6 Cultural areas: some maps mark a Gaeltacht (Irish-speaking area) boundary; the legend identifies it. Irish-language place names on the map are further evidence.

Sketch maps from the OS map
- [ ] K1 Higher asks for a sketch "to half scale" (20 marks): half the length and half the breadth of the map extract. Measure the extract (or count squares: each 2 cm square becomes 1 cm) and draw a frame with four ruled sides. Ordinary asks for "a sketch map of the area shown" (30 marks); a frame of a similar shape, about half size, is safest.
- [ ] K2 Draw light pencil grid lines on the frame at the same eastings and northings (every 1 cm at half scale, or every second line) to place features accurately; label the edge numbers.
- [ ] K3 Show only the features asked for, in the right position, shape and proportion: coastline as a firm line; rivers (the course between named grid references, or the entire course) as a line, broad rivers or lakes as outlined shapes; roads and railways as lines in their entire route where asked; areas (land above a contour, plantation, built-up area, a lake, a golf course) as closed outlined shapes, shaded; point features (post office, triangulation pillar, viewpoint, camping site) as a symbol or dot.
- [ ] K4 "Land above 300 m": follow the 300 m contour accurately around every part of the area asked for; "entire area" means all of it; "at" or "in the south-west of the map" means only that part.
- [ ] K5 Label every feature (on it or with a key); a feature not labelled earns nothing. Add a title, a north arrow and, for completeness, the scale or "half scale".
- [ ] K6 Never trace. Don't add features not asked for. Position matters: a feature placed in the wrong part of the frame loses marks.

Pattern recognition: drainage and physical landscape on the map
- [ ] P1 Drainage patterns as seen on a map: dendritic (tree-like, tributaries joining at acute angles, on rock of uniform resistance); trellised (tributaries joining the main river at right angles, on alternating bands of hard and soft rock); radial (streams flowing outward in all directions from a central hill or mountain); centripetal (streams flowing inward to a central lake or lowland); deranged (no clear pattern, many small lakes and marshes, on glacial deposits). Physical causes are taught in `geo-rivers-s1`; here, how to recognise them on the map. Exams ask to name two patterns or match a pattern to a grid reference.
- [ ] P2 Locating landforms on the map (exam: match features to grid references, or "name and locate with six-figure grid references two features formed by glacial, coastal or fluvial processes"). Map clues:
  - Fluvial: V-shaped valley (sharp contour Vs pointing upstream, close contours, river on the floor, upland); meander (river swinging in loops); oxbow lake (crescent-shaped lake beside a meandering river); floodplain (wide, flat, no contours, along a lowland river, often marshy); levees (embankments along the channel, where shown); braiding (river splitting into several channels around islands); confluence (where a tributary joins); delta (deposits where a river enters a lake or sea); waterfall (shown by symbol or name, contours crowded across the river).
  - Glacial: corrie (armchair-shaped hollow, contours close on three sides, open downhill); tarn (small round lake in a corrie, often high up); arête (narrow ridge, very close contours on both sides); U-shaped valley (broad flat floor, steep sides, contours close on the valley sides); ribbon lake (long, narrow lake on a U-valley floor); drumlins (many small oval closed contour rings, often with small lakes between); esker (a long, narrow, winding ridge on lowland).
  - Coastal: beach (sand or shingle shading along the shore); cliff (cliff symbol or contours crowded at the coast); headland (land projecting into the sea, often high); bay (curved inlet); sand spit, tombolo, lagoon (shapes and names); sand dunes (dune shading or name).
  - Karst: swallow hole (a stream ending abruptly where it disappears underground); few surface streams on limestone.
  - Lake types: glacial (tarn, ribbon lake), fluvial (oxbow), coastal (lagoon), artificial (reservoir: straight dam edge, dam symbol or "Reservoir" label).
  Formation of each landform belongs to its chapter (`geo-rivers`, `geo-glaciation`, `geo-sea`, `geo-rocks-weathering`); here one map-clue bullet each.
- [ ] P3 Steep and gentle slopes for mass-movement questions: locate a very steep area (crowded contours, cliffs) and a very gentle area (widely spaced contours) with grid references; mass movement itself is taught in `geo-rocks-weathering`.

Pattern recognition: settlement on the OS map
- [ ] N1 Rural settlement patterns: nucleated (clustered: buildings grouped at a crossroads, bridge, church or village); linear or ribbon (buildings strung along a road, sometimes a coast road or valley floor); dispersed (individual farmhouses scattered across farmland, each on its own land). For each: what it looks like on the map, a grid reference, and why it formed there (a route focus or service; access to a road and services; farming and land consolidation). Exams ask to name three patterns with grid references and to describe and explain them.
- [ ] N2 Ribbon development on approach roads to a town: linear housing spreading out along main roads; reasons (cheaper rural sites, road access, services along the road); one bullet each.
- [ ] N3 Absence of settlement: give a four-figure grid reference and explain with map evidence: high land (above about 200–300 m: cold, wet, exposed, thin soils), steep slopes (contours crowded), poorly drained land (marsh or bog symbol, floodplain liable to flooding), forestry plantations, lakes. Each reason with its map evidence.
- [ ] N4 High density of settlement: a four-figure grid reference for lowland, gently sloping, well-drained land near roads, a town or the coast. Explain each factor.
- [ ] N5 Variations in settlement distribution (Higher 30 marks; often two contrasting parts of the map, e.g. north vs south of a named northing): compare relief and height, drainage, soils implied by land use, communications (roads, rail), and the pull of a town or the coast; each factor stated, evidenced with a grid reference and explained, for both areas.

Historic settlement on the OS map
- [ ] A1 Phases of historic settlement and the map evidence for each (name, give a six-figure grid reference, describe):
  - Prehistoric (Stone Age to Iron Age): megalithic tombs (court, portal or dolmen, passage, wedge tombs), standing stones, stone circles, cists, fulachta fia (Bronze Age cooking sites), promontory forts, crannógs (lake dwellings). Evidence of early farming communities and burial or ritual sites.
  - Early Christian (early medieval): ringforts (also named rath, lios, dún, caher or cashel on the map), souterrains, ecclesiastical sites, churches and graveyards, holy wells, round towers, high crosses. Ringforts were the farmsteads of the period, enclosed by an earth bank (stone in a cashel or caher).
  - Norman (medieval): mottes and motte-and-bailey sites, castles, town walls, abbeys and friaries, towns at bridging points.
  - Later medieval to Plantation: tower houses (fortified stone houses), planned towns with a square or wide main street, demesnes and large houses with estate woodland.
  - 19th and 20th centuries: canals, railways and stations, mills, coastguard stations, later housing and industrial estates.
- [ ] A2 What each phase tells us (2–3 SRPs per phase is enough for a 30-mark answer): who lived there, why they chose the site (defence, fertile land, a river crossing, a lake), and what it shows about the economy and religion of the time.
- [ ] A3 Place names as evidence (Irish elements): baile/bally (a townland or settlement), cill/kil (church), ráth/rath and lios/lis (ringfort), dún/dun (fort), cathair/caher (stone fort), áth/ath (ford), droichead/drogheda (bridge), cnoc/knock (hill), sliabh/slieve (mountain), ard (height), drom/drum (ridge), gleann/glen (valley), loch/lough (lake), inis/inch (island), cluain/clon (meadow), doire/derry (oak wood), carraig/carrick (rock), mullach/mullagh (summit), beag/beg (small), mór/more (big). One bullet; useful for site and history questions.
- [ ] A4 Townlands: the smallest land division in Ireland; townland names are printed on OS maps and exams ask which townland a point is in.

Urban settlement on the OS map
- [ ] U1 Reasons a town developed at its location (30-mark question: three reasons, each named, evidenced from the map with grid references, and explained): bridging point (the town grew where a river could be crossed; bridge and roads converge); route focus (several N and R roads and a railway meet, so it became a market and service centre); defence (castle, town walls, a hill or river bend); river or coastal site (water supply, transport, power for mills, a harbour or pier for trade and fishing); flat or gently sloping lowland (easy to build on, contours widely spaced); fertile surrounding farmland (dispersed farms, no marsh: market town for its hinterland); ecclesiastical origin (an abbey or early church site); tourism (coast, beaches, lakes, uplands, walks, hotels and caravan sites).
- [ ] U2 Functions of a town from map evidence: name each function, give the symbol or label and its grid reference, explain. Residential (built-up area, housing estates); market and retail (route focus, central square); services (hospital, Garda station, post office, schools, churches, courthouse where labelled); transport (railway station, bus routes, motorway junction, harbour); industrial (industrial estate or factory labels); tourist and recreational (tourist information office, golf course, hostels, camping and caravan sites, marinas, viewpoints, walks); port or fishing (pier, harbour, slipway); ecclesiastical (churches, abbeys); defensive in the past (castle).
- [ ] U3 Town growth and change on the map: old core (narrow streets, square, castle or abbey near the bridge) vs newer edge (housing estates, industrial estates, bypass or motorway with junctions, ribbon development). Planned towns show a regular street plan with a square.

Using map evidence for other questions (method only; theory belongs to the chapter named)
- [ ] E1 Site suitability for a proposed development (a factory, hotel, wind farm, shopping centre, fish-processing plant): choose a site with a six-figure grid reference; give 3 reasons, each with map evidence: flat land (contours), access (an N road, motorway junction, railway, port), near a town for labour, services and markets, space to expand, not on a floodplain, away from protected or scenic areas; wind farms on exposed high ground away from houses; hotels near tourist attractions. State a disadvantage when asked (e.g. visual impact, traffic, loss of farmland, conflict with residents). Economic location theory is in `geo-economic-activities`.
- [ ] E2 Economic activity on the map: primary (forestry plantations, farming implied by dispersed farms and field land, quarries, fishing piers, wind farms), secondary (industrial estates, factories), tertiary (services, tourism, transport). Each with a map symbol and grid reference.
- [ ] E3 Tourism potential from the map (accessibility; services such as hostels, hotels, camping sites, tourist information; attractions such as antiquities, viewpoints, walks, cycle routes, beaches, lakes, golf courses): each factor evidenced with grid references and explained.

Statistical mapping (Higher)
- [ ] M1 One bullet only: statistical maps (choropleth, dot, isoline, proportional-symbol and flow-line maps) are taught in full in `geo-graph-skills-s1`. Here, note that contours are an isoline and that the OS map itself is read with its key (legend) in the same way. Don't re-teach the map types.

### The invented Rivertown map (all values illustrative; reuse in every worked example)

The same invented town as `geo-aerial-photographs-s1`. Sub-zone **R**. The extract runs from
easting 20 to easting 34 and from northing 40 to northing 48 (14 km × 8 km; printed size
28 cm × 16 cm). The writer describes it once:

- Rivertown's town centre and square around R 270 440; a stone bridge over the invented
  **River Dallow** at R 268 441; a castle (in ruins) at R 269 439 beside the bridge; the railway
  station at R 272 445; a GAA pitch north of the bridge at R 268 452.
- The River Dallow enters the map in the south-west at R 200 402 and flows north-east, leaving
  the map at R 340 470; it meanders across a flat floodplain (no contours) east of the town.
- The invented national road **N99** runs south-west to north-east through the town; the
  invented regional road **R990** runs north from the town to a village at a crossroads.
- **Slievenagh** (invented), a mountain in the south, summit triangulation pillar 512 m at
  R 240 405; coniferous plantation on its northern slopes; no houses above 300 m; small streams
  radiate from the summit.
- Antiquities: megalithic tomb R 225 412; standing stone R 231 418; ringfort R 255 430;
  church (in ruins) and graveyard R 262 446; motte R 280 436; castle (in ruins) as above.
- Settlement: nucleated (Rivertown; the crossroads village on the R990); linear (houses along the
  R990 north of the town); dispersed (farmhouses on the lowland in the north-west).
- An industrial estate on the N99 at the north-eastern edge of the town; housing estates on the
  northern edge; a golf course in the north-east.

### Worked-example specs (the writer must say "illustrative" each time)

- **W1 Grid references (G2–G5).** The bridge: six-figure R 268 441. The square containing the
  ringfort: four-figure R 25 43. Reading R 280 436 → motte. Show the easting-then-northing order.
- **W2 Straight-line distance (D1–D2).** Bridge R 268 441 to pillar R 240 405: on the map 9.1 cm
  → 4.6 km (rounded). Check by grid: ΔE = 2.8 km, ΔN = 3.6 km; √(2.8² + 3.6²) = √20.8 ≈ 4.6 km.
- **W3 Curved distance (D3).** The R990 from the town to the crossroads village measures 9.0 cm
  along its bends → 4.5 km.
- **W4 Area (D4–D6).** Whole extract 14 × 8 = 112 km². Land north of northing 44: 14 × 4 = 56 km².
  An invented lake covering 3 full squares and 4 squares more than half covered → about 7 km².
- **W5 Direction (R2).** From the bridge to the summit pillar: the pillar is 2.8 km west and
  3.6 km south, so the direction of travel is south-west (eight-point). From the pillar to the
  bridge is north-east. The River Dallow flows towards the north-east.
- **W6 Gradient and cross-section (H6, X1–X2).** Summit 512 m to the bridge at 52 m: VI = 460 m;
  HE = 4.6 km = 4 600 m; gradient = 4 600 ÷ 460 = 1 in 10. Cross-section from the summit north
  to the river: horizontal scale 1:50 000; vertical scale 1 cm = 50 m (1:5 000); vertical
  exaggeration = 50 000 ÷ 5 000 = ×10. The slope from the summit is concave (contours crowded
  near the top, widely spaced near the river).
- **W7 Sketch map half scale (K1–K6).** Extract 28 cm × 16 cm → frame 14 cm × 8 cm, grid lines

## 3. Traps

- **Never use real exam maps.** Do not name Dundalk, Clonmel, Macroom, Sligo, Arklow, Bantry,
  Oughterard, Foxford, Templemore, Cavan or any other town, river, road, lough, walk or grid
  reference from a past paper. Do not copy past-paper measurements, areas or tick-box options.
- Eastings first, then northings. Always include the sub-zone letter.
- A four-figure reference uses the **bottom-left (south-west)** corner of the square.
- 1 cm on a 1:50 000 map is **0.5 km**, not 5 km or 50 m. Give distances in km and areas in km².
- Concave = steep at the top, gentle at the bottom (contours close together near the top);
  convex is the reverse. Students often swap them.
- Valley contour Vs point **upstream** (uphill); spur Vs point **downhill**.
- Aspect is the direction the slope **faces** (downhill direction), not the uphill direction.
- Direction "from A to B" is the direction of travel, read at A.
- N stands for **National**, R for **Regional** (not "route" or "rural").
- Coniferous **plantation**, deciduous **woodland**, mixed **woodland**: use the legend's terms.
- Ringforts are early Christian (early medieval), not Norman or prehistoric. Mottes are Norman.
  Fulachta fia are Bronze Age.
- A sketch map is not a tracing; draw a ruled frame; label every feature; include only what is
  asked; "half scale" halves each side.
- A raised beach, ria or fjord is formed by isostatic or eustatic change (`geo-glaciation-s2`);
  name such features on a map but don't explain them here.
- Landform formation, drainage-pattern causes, mass-movement processes, urban and rural
  settlement theory and economic location theory belong to their own chapters; here give map
  clues and the answer method only.
- Aerial photograph skills (oblique vs vertical, nine-area notation, camera direction, photo
  sketch maps, photo-based functions and traffic) are in `geo-aerial-photographs-s1`. Mention
  only that photo features are located on the map by grid reference.
- Graph drawing and statistical maps (choropleth, dot, isoline, flow-line, proportional symbols)
  are in `geo-graph-skills-s1`; weather-map isobars are in `geo-weather-climate`.
- Historical maps and textual sources are in `geo-mapwork-s2`.

## 4. What exams ask (topics only)

- Short questions (Higher 2 marks each; Ordinary 3–5 marks, often tick-box): six-figure grid
  reference of a named building or a road junction or exit point; naming the antiquity, public
  service or feature at a grid reference; height of a spot height, pillar or highest point;
  length of a road, motorway or railway between two points; straight-line distance between two
  points; area of the whole map, of a lake or bay, of land above a contour, of land east/west
  or north/south of a grid line or a railway, or of part of a sub-zone; direction of travel
  between two places or along a walk; which part of the map (NW, SE etc.) a feature is in; type
  of forestry; what N or R stands for; drawing a legend symbol; identifying a cultural area
  (Gaeltacht).
- Matching features to grid references: V-shaped valley, even, concave and convex slopes, flat
  land, plateau surface, meander, braiding, oxbow lake, confluence, levees, drumlin, tarn,
  headland, beach, swallow hole, trellised and dendritic drainage. Naming two drainage patterns.
  Type of lake at a grid reference (glacial ribbon lake or tarn).
- Aspect of a slope at a grid reference.
- Higher elevation-profile questions and true/false profile statements: river, road, forestry
  type, land use, height, slope type, walk or cycle route, townland, attraction at labelled
  points.
- Sketch maps (Higher 20 marks at half scale; Ordinary 30 marks): 4–5 features such as the
  coastline, a river's course between two points, lakes, islands, land above a contour, a
  plantation in a named part of the map, the built-up area, N/R roads and motorways, the
  railway, golf course, post office, triangulation pillar, viewpoint, camping site, cycle
  route, transmission line, a feature of coastal deposition, a confluence marked X, clustered
  and linear settlement.
- 15-mark map-and-process questions (Higher): name and locate two landforms with six-figure
  grid references and explain one; locate a very steep and a very gentle slope.
- 30-mark map-evidence questions (often tagged to other chapters): three reasons why a town
  developed at its location; three rural settlement patterns; variations in settlement
  distribution; absence and high density of settlement; three phases of historic settlement;
  site suitability for a factory, hotel or wind farm with a six-figure grid reference;
  economic activity and tourism potential evidenced from the map. Expected answers: each point
  named, evidenced with a grid reference and explained in several SRPs.
