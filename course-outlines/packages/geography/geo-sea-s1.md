## 1. Must cover (one bullet or block for every item; tick each off)

Waves and the sea's energy
- [ ] W1 How waves form: wind blowing over the sea surface transfers energy to the water. Wave size depends on wind speed, how long the wind blows, and **fetch** (the distance of open water the wind blows over). Water particles move in circles; the water itself does not travel forward until the wave breaks.
- [ ] W2 Wave terms: crest, trough, wave height, wavelength. A wave breaks when it reaches shallow water: friction with the sea bed slows its base, the crest topples forward. **Swash** is the water rushing up the beach; **backwash** is the water draining back down it under gravity.
- [ ] W3 Constructive waves: low, gentle, long wavelength, low frequency; spill onto the beach; swash is stronger than backwash, so they carry material up the beach and build it up. Typical of calm weather.
- [ ] W4 Destructive waves: high, steep, short wavelength, high frequency; plunge down onto the beach; backwash is stronger than swash, so they drag material out to sea and erode the beach. Typical of storms. (Exams test "destructive backwash > swash" and "constructive swash > backwash" as true/false.)
- [ ] W5 Wave refraction: waves approaching an indented coast slow down in shallow water off headlands first, so the wave fronts bend. Energy converges on headlands (more erosion) and spreads out in bays (less energy, so deposition). Wave fronts end up roughly parallel to the shore.
- [ ] W6 Tides: the twice-daily rise and fall of sea level caused mainly by the pull of the moon (and sun). Tidal range sets the vertical band the waves work on. Storm surges raise sea level and let waves reach higher up cliffs and beaches. One bullet each; no tidal figures.

Coastal erosion
- [ ] E1 Hydraulic action: the force of breaking waves; air and water are forced into joints and cracks, compressed, then released as the wave retreats, which weakens and shatters the rock.
- [ ] E2 Abrasion (corrasion): waves hurl sand, shingle and boulders against the cliff, grinding and undercutting it. The most effective process at the cliff base.
- [ ] E3 Attrition: rocks carried by the waves collide with each other and are broken into smaller, rounder pieces (this wears down the load, not the cliff).
- [ ] E4 Solution (corrosion): sea water dissolves soluble rock such as limestone.
- [ ] E5 Sub-aerial processes on the cliff face above the waves: weathering (e.g. freeze-thaw, salt crystallisation) and mass movement (rockfalls, slumping on soft clay cliffs) also wear cliffs back. Waves then remove the fallen debris.
- [ ] E6 Factors controlling the rate of erosion: rock type and resistance; rock structure (joints, faults, bedding); wave energy (fetch, storms, refraction); whether a beach protects the cliff foot; human activity (defences, removal of beach material). Soft glacial deposits (boulder clay) erode fastest.

Coastal patterns
- [ ] P1 Spatial variation in Ireland: the west coast faces the long Atlantic fetch and prevailing south-westerly winds, so high-energy waves give rugged, cliffed, erosional coasts. The east coast faces the narrow Irish Sea (short fetch), with lower-energy waves and soft glacial deposits, so depositional features (beaches, spits, lagoons, dunes) are common. Temporal variation: storms do most erosion in short episodes.
- [ ] P2 Headlands and bays by differential erosion: where bands of hard and soft rock meet the coast at right angles (a discordant coast), the soft rock is eroded back into bays and the hard rock is left as headlands. A coast where the rock bands run parallel to the shore (a concordant coast) stays straighter. Then refraction (W5) concentrates further erosion on the headlands.

Landforms of coastal erosion (each: what it is, formation in numbered stages, example from section 2, diagram cue)
- [ ] L1 Cliff and wave-cut notch: waves erode a notch between high and low tide by hydraulic action and abrasion; the overhang above collapses; the cliff retreats inland, staying steep.
- [ ] L2 Wave-cut platform: the gently sloping rock surface left at the foot of a retreating cliff, exposed at low tide; it widens as the cliff retreats until waves lose most of their energy crossing it, slowing erosion.
- [ ] L3 Sea cave: waves exploit a line of weakness (joint or fault) at the base of a cliff or headland, enlarging it by hydraulic action and abrasion.
- [ ] L4 Sea arch: caves on opposite sides of a headland meet, or one cave cuts right through.
- [ ] L5 Sea stack and stump: the arch roof is weakened from below by wave erosion and from above by weathering, and collapses, leaving an isolated pillar (stack); the stack is undercut at its base and collapses into a low stump covered at high tide. Teach L3–L5 as one sequence block: cave → arch → stack → stump.
- [ ] L6 Blowhole: a cave is enlarged upward along a vertical joint by hydraulic action (compressed air) until it breaks through to the cliff top; spray is forced up through the hole in storms. If the roof between blowhole and sea collapses, a narrow inlet (a geo) forms. Exams classify blowholes as erosional.
- [ ] L7 Headland and bay: one bullet linking to P2; the formation is in P2, don't repeat it.

Coastal transport
- [ ] T1 Load carried in the same four ways as rivers: traction, saltation, suspension, solution. One bullet.
- [ ] T2 Longshore drift: waves approach at an angle set by the prevailing wind; swash carries material up the beach at that angle; backwash drags it straight back down the slope under gravity; repeated, this moves sand and shingle along the coast in a zigzag. **Longshore drift is the transport process behind spits, bars, lagoons and tombolos** (exams ask for it by name).

Coastal deposition
- [ ] D1 Why deposition happens: waves lose energy (constructive waves, sheltered bays, shallow water, after refraction); a coast changes direction (e.g. at a river mouth or bay); large supply of sediment from rivers, cliff erosion or glacial deposits offshore.
- [ ] D2 Beach: sand and shingle deposited between low tide and the highest storm waves; bay-head beaches in sheltered bays; finer sand lower down, coarser shingle higher up; storm beach (ridge of large shingle thrown above normal high tide by storm waves); berms (ridges marking successive high-tide levels).
- [ ] D3 Sand spit: longshore drift carries material past a point where the coast changes direction (e.g. a bay or river mouth); it is deposited in the calmer, deeper water, building a ridge out from the land, attached at one end; wave refraction or a change in wind direction curves the tip (recurved end); sheltered water behind it fills with silt to form salt marsh or mudflat.
- [ ] D4 Sand bar (barrier beach): a spit that grows right across the mouth of a bay, joining two headlands.
- [ ] D5 Lagoon: the body of water trapped behind a sand bar; it slowly fills with sediment and vegetation and may become marsh. Exams classify lagoons as depositional.
- [ ] D6 Tombolo: a ridge of sand or shingle joining an island to the mainland, built by longshore drift and by waves refracting around the island and meeting in its shelter.
- [ ] D7 Sand dunes: wind blows dry sand from a wide beach at low tide inland; it piles up against obstacles (driftwood, seaweed, plants) above high tide; marram grass colonises it, its long roots binding the sand and its growth keeping pace with burial; embryo dunes grow into fore dunes, then grey (fixed) dunes further inland as soil develops and other plants arrive; dune slacks are damp hollows between dune ridges. Damage to marram can lead to wind blowouts (link only; management is in `geo-sea-s2`).

Links and skills
- [ ] K1 One bullet only: coasts also change through changes in land and sea level; raised beaches (emergent coasts) and rias, fjords and drowned drumlins (submergent coasts) are covered in `geo-glaciation-s2` (Isostasy). Name them; don't explain their formation.
- [ ] K2 Skill: average annual rate of coastal erosion (or cliff retreat) = total distance retreated ÷ number of years. Show a worked example with invented round numbers labelled illustrative. Count years by subtracting the earlier year from the later one.
- [ ] K3 Classifying features: a short list sorting coastal features into erosion (cliff, notch, wave-cut platform, cave, arch, stack, stump, blowhole, headland) and deposition (beach, storm beach, spit, sand bar, lagoon, tombolo, sand dunes, salt marsh). Exams test this by ticking boxes.

## 3. Traps (known errors)

- The transport process behind spits, lagoons and tombolos is **longshore drift**. A stored answer in the question bank wrongly gives "groynes"; groynes are a defence (`geo-sea-s2`), not a process.
- Destructive waves: backwash stronger than swash. Constructive waves: swash stronger than backwash. Never swap them.
- Refraction concentrates energy on **headlands**, not bays.
- Attrition wears down the load, not the cliff; don't present it as the main cliff-eroding process.
- Spit = attached to land at one end only. Sand bar = joins two headlands across a bay. Tombolo = joins an island to the mainland. Lagoon = the water behind a bar, a depositional feature.
- Sequence order is cave → arch → stack → stump. A stump is lower than a stack and is covered at high tide.
- The Cliffs of Moher are shale and sandstone. The Burren limestone is nearby, but don't call the cliffs limestone.
- Don't use Chesil Beach (its classification as tombolo or barrier beach is disputed), Old Harry's Wife, or any blowhole, cave or wave-cut platform name: none is checked.
- Don't give erosion rates for Holderness or anywhere else; use invented illustrative numbers for the rate skill and never the past-paper distances or years.
- Raised beaches, rias, fjords and drowned drumlins belong to `geo-glaciation-s2`: name them in K1 only.
- Antrim coastal landslides are mass movement (`geo-rocks-weathering`); the Giant's Causeway is a volcanic (basalt) feature (`geo-volcanoes-earthquakes`). Leave both out.
- Coastal defences, recreation, dune damage and management belong to `geo-sea-s2`. Here, mention marram grass only as part of dune formation.
- North Bull Island's origin (it grew after the North Bull Wall was built) is a human-impact example and is taught in `geo-sea-s2`; here name it only as a dune example.
