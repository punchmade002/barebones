## 1. Must cover (one bullet or block for every item; tick each off)

Solar radiation
- [ ] R1 The sun's energy arrives as short-wave radiation (mostly visible light, plus ultraviolet and near-infrared); the earth, being much cooler, gives off long-wave (infrared, heat) radiation. This difference is the basis of the greenhouse effect.
- [ ] R2 Insolation (incoming solar radiation): the energy reaching the top of the atmosphere is the solar constant (section 2); far less reaches the surface.
- [ ] R3 Energy transformation (the syllabus word "transformed"), one bullet each: radiant energy absorbed by surfaces becomes heat (sensible heat); energy used to evaporate water is stored as latent heat in water vapour and released when it condenses, warming the air and powering storms; heat differences create pressure differences, which become the kinetic energy of wind and, through wind drag, of waves and currents; plants convert a small fraction into chemical energy by photosynthesis.

Energy flows and the heat budget of the earth (use the rounded "100 units" model in section 2; diagram cue: a labelled flow diagram with incoming short-wave arrows split into reflected, absorbed by atmosphere and absorbed by surface, and outgoing long-wave, latent and sensible heat arrows)
- [ ] B1 What happens to incoming radiation: reflected back to space by clouds, the surface and air (together the planet's albedo); scattered by gas molecules and dust (scattering of blue light makes the sky blue); absorbed by ozone, water vapour, dust and clouds; absorbed by the land and ocean surface (about half).
- [ ] B2 Albedo: the proportion of radiation a surface reflects. High for fresh snow and ice and thick cloud; low for oceans and forests (values in section 2). Change the surface and you change the heat absorbed (ice–albedo feedback, s5; deforestation and urban surfaces).
- [ ] B3 How the surface loses energy, one bullet each: long-wave radiation; latent heat transfer (evaporation, the largest non-radiative loss, especially from oceans); sensible heat transfer (conduction to the air touching the ground, then convection).
- [ ] B4 The natural greenhouse effect: water vapour, CO2, methane and other gases are largely transparent to incoming short-wave radiation but absorb outgoing long-wave radiation and re-radiate much of it back down, keeping the surface far warmer than it would otherwise be (figures in section 2). The enhanced greenhouse effect is one bullet linking to s5 and `geo-economic-environment-s3`.
- [ ] B5 The heat budget balances for the earth as a whole over a year: energy in = energy out, so the average temperature stays stable. If more energy is retained than lost (more greenhouse gases), the earth warms until a new balance is reached.
- [ ] B6 The heat budget does **not** balance by latitude: between the equator and about 35–40° N and S more energy is received than radiated away (surplus); poleward of that more is lost than received (deficit). Diagram cue: a graph of incoming and outgoing radiation against latitude, with the surplus and deficit areas shaded. Without a poleward transfer, the tropics would keep heating and the poles keep cooling; they don't, so energy must be moved.

Why insolation varies (causes of the uneven distribution), each a separate bullet with a diagram cue where useful
- [ ] U1 Angle of incidence (latitude and the earth's curvature): at the equator the sun's rays strike nearly vertically and the energy is concentrated on a small area; towards the poles the same beam strikes at a low angle and is spread over a larger area. Diagram cue: two equal beams hitting the curved earth.
- [ ] U2 Thickness of atmosphere: low-angle rays pass through more atmosphere, so more energy is absorbed, scattered and reflected before reaching the ground.
- [ ] U3 Earth's tilt and orbit: the axis is tilted about 23.5°, so the overhead sun migrates between the Tropics of Cancer and Capricorn through the year, giving seasons and changing day length. The poles have months of continuous daylight in summer and continuous darkness in winter.
- [ ] U4 Day length: long summer days at high latitudes partly offset the low sun angle; the equator has about 12 hours of daylight all year.
- [ ] U5 Cloud cover: cloudy regions (the equatorial belt, mid-latitude west coasts) reflect much insolation; clear-skied subtropical deserts receive the most at the surface. Clouds also keep nights warmer by trapping long-wave radiation.
- [ ] U6 Surface albedo (B2), e.g. snow-covered polar regions reflect most of what little they receive.

The geographical distribution of temperature (each factor explained with an example; diagram cue: a world map of January or July isotherms)
- [ ] T1 Latitude: the main control: temperatures generally fall from the equator to the poles; isotherms run broadly east–west.
- [ ] T2 Land and sea (continentality): land heats and cools quickly; the sea slowly (high specific heat, mixing, transparency, evaporation). So continental interiors have hot summers, cold winters and a large annual range; coasts and islands have a small range (Ireland's range about 10 °C against more than 60 °C in north-east Siberia, section 2). Isotherms bend where they cross coasts.
- [ ] T3 Ocean currents: warm currents raise coastal temperatures (the North Atlantic Drift keeps Ireland, Britain and Norway's coast mild, so January isotherms bend sharply northward over the north-east Atlantic); cold currents lower them (e.g. along the coasts of Peru and south-west Africa). Named currents in section 2; mechanics in s4.
- [ ] T4 Altitude: temperature falls by about 6.5 °C per 1,000 m, so mountains and plateaus are cooler than lowlands at the same latitude (snow-capped peaks near the equator, section 2).
- [ ] T5 Prevailing winds: onshore westerlies carry ocean warmth (in winter) and coolness (in summer) over west coasts of the mid-latitudes; winds from cold continental interiors (e.g. polar continental air reaching Ireland, s4) bring cold spells.
- [ ] T6 Aspect and local factors: south-facing slopes in the northern hemisphere are warmer (vineyards on south-facing slopes); urban heat islands; snow cover.
- [ ] T7 The thermal equator: the line of highest mean temperature; it lies mostly north of the geographical equator because the northern hemisphere has more land, and it moves north and south with the seasons, further over land than over sea.
- [ ] T8 Extremes and the pattern summarised: hottest places are subtropical deserts (not the equator, where cloud and rain limit heating); the coldest are the interior of Antarctica and, in winter, north-east Siberia. Figures in section 2. Annual range is smallest at the equator and over oceans, largest in high-latitude continental interiors.

Redistribution of energy by circulation (the energy-transfer view; mechanics in s4)
- [ ] D1 Transfer happens by two linked systems: the atmosphere (winds, depressions, tropical cyclones) and the oceans (surface currents and the deep thermohaline circulation). Together they move heat from the surplus belt to the deficit belt; the atmosphere carries most of it at mid and high latitudes, the oceans a large share in the tropics.
- [ ] D2 Hadley cell (0–30°): intense heating at the equator makes air rise (low pressure, the ITCZ), cool and release latent heat as convectional rain; at height it flows poleward, sinks at about 30° (subtropical high pressure, deserts) and returns to the equator as the trade winds. It carries heat poleward at height. Diagram cue: the three-cell model, both hemispheres.
- [ ] D3 Ferrel cell (30–60°) and Polar cell (60–90°): surface westerlies carry warm air poleward to meet cold polar air at the polar front (about 60°); polar air sinks at the poles (polar high) and flows out as polar easterlies. Most mid-latitude heat transfer is done by depressions and anticyclones forming along the polar front and by the meandering jet stream (s4).
- [ ] D4 Latent heat as a transfer: water evaporated over warm tropical oceans carries energy; it is released when the vapour condenses far away, e.g. in a depression over Ireland.
- [ ] D5 Tropical cyclones (hurricanes) as heat transfer: they draw huge quantities of heat and moisture from tropical oceans and carry them poleward; some, like ex-hurricane Ophelia (2017, from `geo-weather-climate-s1`), reach the mid-latitudes. One bullet; formation in s4.
- [ ] D6 Ocean currents: warm currents carry heat poleward along the western sides of the oceans (Gulf Stream, Kuroshio, Brazil, East Australian); cold currents return cooled water towards the equator along the eastern sides (Canary, Benguela, Humboldt, California). The Gulf Stream and North Atlantic Drift carry tropical heat to north-west Europe (effects in section 2).
- [ ] D7 The thermohaline circulation (global conveyor belt): cold, salty, dense water sinks in the North Atlantic and around Antarctica and spreads through the deep ocean, while warm surface water flows in to replace it; it carries heat slowly round the globe over centuries. One block; detail in s4.
- [ ] D8 Evidence that redistribution works: the equator is not continually heating up and the poles cooling down; west coasts of Europe are far milder than places at the same latitude elsewhere (section 2).
- [ ] D9 Changes to redistribution (one bullet, links s5): melting Arctic ice lowers albedo; a weaker Atlantic circulation could change Europe's climate; El Niño shifts heat across the Pacific.

## 3. Traps

- Short-wave radiation comes **in** from the sun; long-wave radiation goes **out** from the earth. Greenhouse gases trap the outgoing long-wave radiation; they don't block sunlight.
- The hottest places on earth are subtropical deserts, **not** the equator. The equator's cloud and rain keep maximum temperatures around 30–32 °C.
- The heat budget balances for the **whole earth**, but not at each latitude. Don't say the tropics "have no balance": they have a surplus that is exported.
- Latitude 35–40° is the approximate crossover; don't give a precise single number.
- Warm currents flow on the **western** sides of ocean basins (eastern coasts of continents) and cold currents on the **eastern** sides (western coasts), in both hemispheres. Ireland is the exception that proves the rule: the North Atlantic Drift reaches the eastern side of the Atlantic at high latitude.
- The Gulf Stream does not reach Ireland by name: off Europe it is the North Atlantic Drift (Current).
- The ozone hole does not let in more heat; it lets in more ultraviolet. Keep it out of the greenhouse explanation.
- Don't repeat s4's forces (Coriolis, pressure gradient), pressure belts and jet streams in depth: one clause each.
- Don't give heat-transfer percentages between atmosphere and ocean, or heat budget figures beyond the rounded model in section 2.
- Global warming as a case study (evidence, effects, responses) belongs to s5 and `geo-economic-environment-s3`; here one bullet on the enhanced greenhouse effect.
