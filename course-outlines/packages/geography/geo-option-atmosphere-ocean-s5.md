## 1. Must cover (one bullet or block for every item; tick each off)

Climatic environments
- [ ] E1 Weather vs climate in one clause (defined in `geo-weather-climate-s1`); a climatic environment (climate region) is a large area with a similar pattern of temperature, precipitation and seasons, usually matched by similar vegetation and soils.
- [ ] E2 Classification: the Köppen system divides the world mainly by temperature and precipitation thresholds that match vegetation; the broad groups are tropical, dry, warm temperate, cool temperate (continental), and polar. Climates are arranged in belts by latitude, modified by distance from the sea, ocean currents, relief and position on the east or west of a continent. Diagram cue: a world map of climate regions.
- [ ] E3 Controls of climate in one block (each a clause, pointing to the subsection that explains it): latitude and insolation (s2); pressure belts and planetary winds and their seasonal shift (s4); air masses (s4); ocean currents (s4); continentality (s2); altitude and relief (s2, s3).

Climate option 1: equatorial (tropical rainforest) climate
- [ ] Q1 Location: about 0–10° N and S: the Amazon Basin, the Congo Basin, South-East Asia (section 2).
- [ ] Q2 Temperature: high all year (about 26–28 °C monthly means), tiny annual range (about 2–3 °C); daily range larger than annual (hot afternoons, warm nights). Reasons: the sun is always high (overhead at the equator at the equinoxes, never far from overhead); day length about 12 hours all year; cloud cover stops maxima rising much above the low 30s °C.
- [ ] Q3 Precipitation: over 2,000 mm a year, rain in every month, often with two wetter peaks after the equinoxes when the overhead sun and the ITCZ pass; convectional, falling as heavy afternoon thunderstorms. Reasons: intense heating, very moist air from warm seas and forest transpiration, convergence of the trade winds at the ITCZ.
- [ ] Q4 Daily weather cycle (one bullet): clear, calm, humid morning; cumulus building by late morning; towering cumulonimbus and torrential rain with thunder in the afternoon; clearing in the evening; humid nights.
- [ ] Q5 Pressure and winds: permanent low pressure (equatorial trough, ITCZ, doldrums); light, variable winds; very high humidity (often over 80%). No temperate-style seasons.
- [ ] Q6 Vegetation and human response (one bullet): tropical rainforest, latosols; detail in `geo-option-geoecology-s4`.

Climate option 2: tropical monsoon climate (India)
- [ ] M1 Location: South and South-East Asia, especially India, Bangladesh and Myanmar; northern Australia and West Africa have weaker monsoon climates.
- [ ] M2 Definition: a climate with a seasonal reversal of wind direction, bringing a very wet summer and a dry winter.
- [ ] M3 Three seasons with temperature: the hot season (March–May, often over 40 °C on the northern plains before the rains); the wet south-west monsoon (June–September, slightly cooler because of cloud and rain); the cool dry season (October–February, pleasant, cool nights in the north).
- [ ] M4 Summer monsoon cause, in steps: intense heating of the land and Tibetan Plateau creates low pressure over north-west India; the ITCZ moves north; south-east trade winds cross the equator and are deflected by the Coriolis force into south-west winds, gathering moisture over the Indian Ocean; the subtropical jet stream shifts north of the Himalayas, letting the monsoon "burst" suddenly (reuse `geo-region-continental-s1`).
- [ ] M5 Rainfall pattern: relief rain on the Western Ghats and the north-east hills; rain shadow on the Deccan; rain decreasing north-westwards along the Ganges plain to the Thar Desert (reuse; give no totals).
- [ ] M6 Winter monsoon: the land cools, high pressure over Central Asia, dry north-east winds blow off the land; the Himalayas block cold Central Asian air; the Tamil Nadu coast gets winter rain from the north-east monsoon after it crosses the Bay of Bengal.
- [ ] M7 Variability and hazards: late, weak or broken monsoons bring drought; very heavy ones bring floods; cyclones in the Bay of Bengal (reuse). El Niño years often bring a weaker Indian monsoon (link C-EN).

Climate option 3: mid-latitude west coast (cool temperate oceanic) climate
- [ ] W1 Location: west coasts between about 40° and 60° in the path of the westerlies: north-west Europe including Ireland, British Columbia and the Pacific north-west of the USA, southern Chile, New Zealand and Tasmania.
- [ ] W2 Temperature: mild winters, cool summers, small annual range (Ireland about 10 °C; Irish figures in section 2); frost and snow infrequent on low ground.
- [ ] W3 Precipitation: rain in all months, maximum in autumn and winter, many wet days; mostly frontal (cyclonic) with relief rain on west-facing mountains; amounts fall from west to east.
- [ ] W4 Reasons, one bullet each: prevailing south-westerly winds off the Atlantic (maritime air); the North Atlantic Drift warming winter air and seas; the sea's slow heating and cooling; frequent depressions on the polar front, steered by the jet stream; occasional anticyclones (Azores High ridges in summer, blocking highs) giving dry spells; air masses (tropical and polar maritime most often).
- [ ] W5 Changeable weather: the day-to-day variety as depressions and ridges alternate; the NAO's effect on winter (s4).
- [ ] W6 Transition eastward into continental Europe (reuse `geo-region-concept-s1` C7) and vegetation and farming (deciduous forest, brown earths, grass) in one bullet.

Climate option 4: Mediterranean (warm temperate oceanic, west coast) climate
- [ ] D1 Location: west sides of continents between about 30° and 40° (some places to about 45°): the lands around the Mediterranean Sea (e.g. southern Italy, southern Spain, Greece), central and southern California, central Chile, the south-western tip of South Africa around Cape Town, and south-western and southern Australia (around Perth and Adelaide).
- [ ] D2 Temperature: hot summers (lowland July means about 25 °C or more), mild winters (coastal January about 9–12 °C; reuse `geo-region-european-s2` figures); frost is rare on coasts; inland and upland areas have hotter summers and colder winters.
- [ ] D3 Precipitation: moderate (commonly about 400–900 mm), concentrated in winter (most rain October–March); summers dry with drought for several months; rain often falls in intense downpours, causing flash floods and soil erosion.
- [ ] D4 Reasons (seasonal shift of pressure belts, the key explanation): in summer the subtropical high (the Azores High in the Mediterranean, the North Pacific High in California) moves poleward over the region: sinking, warming air, clear skies, no rain; in winter the belts shift towards the equator and the westerlies and their depressions bring rain. The warm Mediterranean Sea also spawns its own winter depressions. Cold currents (California, Humboldt, Benguela, Canary) keep summers cooler and drier on some coasts.
- [ ] D5 Local winds: the sirocco (hot, dry, dusty, from the Sahara; reuse), the mistral and the bora (cold, s4).
- [ ] D6 Hazards and human response (one bullet each): summer drought and water shortage; wildfires in hot, dry, windy summers (section 2); drought-tolerant vegetation (olives, vines, citrus with irrigation; maquis scrub; cork oak); tourism (s6).

Climate option 5: continental (cool temperate continental) climate
- [ ] N1 Location: interiors of the large northern-hemisphere continents between about 40° and 60° N, far from the sea: central and eastern Europe into Russia and Ukraine, Central Asia, the Canadian Prairies and the US Midwest and Great Plains. There is no large equivalent in the southern hemisphere (too little land at those latitudes).
- [ ] N2 Temperature: very cold winters (monthly means well below 0 °C for several months), warm to hot summers, a large annual range that increases with distance from the sea (Moscow and Winnipeg figures in section 2; Verkhoyansk as the extreme, reuse s2); short spring and autumn.
- [ ] N3 Precipitation: low to moderate (roughly 250–750 mm), decreasing into the interior; summer maximum from convectional thunderstorms when the land heats; winter precipitation falls as snow, which lies for months.
- [ ] N4 Reasons, one bullet each: continentality (land heats and cools fast, no sea to moderate); distance from the oceans' moisture (westerlies have lost most of their moisture crossing Europe or the Rockies block Pacific air); the winter Siberian High (thermal anticyclone, cold, dry, clear) and the North American equivalent; summer low pressure over the heated land draws in some moist air.
- [ ] N5 Extremes and hazards: blizzards; cold waves of arctic air; summer heatwaves, drought and dust storms (the Dust Bowl, reuse `geo-rocks-weathering-s7`); spring snowmelt floods.
- [ ] N6 Vegetation and human response (one bullet): temperate grassland (steppe, prairie) and chernozems (reuse `geo-option-geoecology-s2`): wheat and maize belts; boreal forest further north.

Climate option 6 (commonly studied; compact block): hot desert climate
- [ ] H1 Location about 15–30° N and S on the west sides of continents: Sahara, Arabian, Namib, Atacama, Sonoran and Mojave, Australian (reuse `geo-option-geoecology-s4`).
- [ ] H2 Characteristics with reasons, one bullet each: very low, unreliable rainfall (under about 250 mm) because of sinking air in the subtropical highs, offshore trade winds, cold currents and rain shadows; the highest air temperatures on earth (56.7 °C record, reuse) because of clear skies and high sun; very large daily range (about 20–30 °C) because dry, cloudless air lets heat escape at night; fog on cold-current coasts.

Climate change over space (the "space" half of the recurring question; one block)
- [ ] S1 Climate changes across space because of the controls in E3: from equator to pole (latitude), from coast to interior (continentality: Ireland's range about 10 °C vs Verkhoyansk's over 60 °C), from west to east coasts (cold vs warm currents), with altitude (vertical zonation, e.g. Kilimanjaro's snow near the equator, reuse s2), and across a mountain range (windward vs rain shadow). One bullet each, using examples already in this package and s2–s4.
- [ ] S2 Climate boundaries are transition zones that shift: e.g. the Sahel lies between the desert and the savanna and moves with the ITCZ from year to year.

Climate change over time: causes (one block)
- [ ] K1 Timescales: long-term (millions of years: plate movement, mountain building), glacial cycles (tens to hundreds of thousands of years), centuries (Little Ice Age), years (El Niño, volcanic eruptions), and recent human-caused warming.
- [ ] K2 Natural causes, one bullet each: Milankovitch orbital cycles; changes in solar output; volcanic eruptions (sulphate aerosols reflect sunlight and cool the earth for a year or two); plate tectonics (moving continents and opening or closing seaways change ocean currents); changes in ocean circulation; feedbacks (ice–albedo; CO2 and methane released or absorbed by oceans and permafrost).
- [ ] K3 Human cause: the enhanced greenhouse effect from burning fossil fuels, deforestation and agriculture; also land-use change and aerosols. One bullet; sources of each gas are in `geo-economic-environment-s3`.
- [ ] K4 How past climates are known, one bullet each: ice cores (bubbles of ancient air give past CO2; oxygen isotopes give past temperature); ocean-floor sediments; tree rings; pollen preserved in peat bogs and lake muds (Irish bogs record changes in vegetation and therefore climate since the Ice Age); glacial landforms (reuse `geo-glaciation-s1`); historical records (harvest dates, frost fairs, ships' logs); instrument records since the 19th century.

Climate change example 1: the Ice Ages (full case study)
- [ ] I1 The Quaternary glaciation: began about 2.6 million years ago; since then the earth has swung between long glacial periods and shorter, warm interglacials; over the last 800,000 years the cycle has been about 100,000 years long. We now live in an interglacial.
- [ ] I2 The Last Glacial Maximum, about 20,000 years ago: ice sheets covered much of North America, Scandinavia, Britain and Ireland; global sea level was about 120 m lower than today, exposing land bridges (e.g. between Britain and continental Europe); global average temperature was several degrees colder; the climate belts were pushed towards the equator.
- [ ] I3 Ireland: two main cold stages (Munsterian and Midlandian); the Midlandian ice had gone by about 10,000 years ago; the landscape of drumlins, eskers, corries and U-shaped valleys is the evidence (reuse `geo-glaciation-s1`; name the landforms only); isostatic rebound and raised beaches as after-effects (one clause, `geo-glaciation-s2`).
- [ ] I4 Causes: Milankovitch cycles (section 2: eccentricity, obliquity and precession change how much sunlight reaches high northern latitudes in summer; when northern summers are cool, winter snow survives and ice sheets grow); feedbacks amplify the small orbital change: more ice raises albedo; colder oceans absorb more CO2, lowering greenhouse gases (ice cores show CO2 near 180 ppm in glacials and about 280 ppm in interglacials); changes in the Atlantic circulation. The position of the continents (land near the North Pole) allows ice sheets to build.
- [ ] I5 Effects beyond the ice: drier, windier climates; deserts expanded; loess was blown from outwash plains; sea level fell; plants and animals migrated towards the equator and recolonised afterwards.
- [ ] I6 Shorter cold and warm periods in historical times, one bullet each: the Medieval Warm Period (Norse settlement of Greenland; vines grown further north in England); the Little Ice Age (Alpine glaciers advanced; the River Thames froze, with frost fairs; the Great Frost in Ireland; the Maunder Minimum of low sunspot activity; volcanic eruptions such as Tambora); facts in section 2.

Climate change example 2: 20th-century (and present) global warming (full case study, climate-system focus)
- [ ] G1 Evidence with checked figures: rising CO2 at Mauna Loa; global temperature rise; record warm years; shrinking Arctic sea ice; retreating glaciers; rising sea level; Ireland's warming (section 2; reuse `geo-economic-environment-s3`).
- [ ] G2 Mechanism: the enhanced greenhouse effect: more CO2, methane and nitrous oxide absorb more outgoing long-wave radiation, so the heat budget (s2) is out of balance and the earth warms until a new balance is reached; most of the extra heat goes into the ocean (s1).
- [ ] G3 Feedbacks, one bullet each: ice–albedo (melting ice exposes dark ocean, absorbing more heat; why the Arctic is warming fastest); water vapour (warmer air holds more vapour, itself a greenhouse gas); permafrost thaw releasing methane and CO2; reduced ocean CO2 uptake as the water warms.
- [ ] G4 Effects on climate patterns (the "space" link): a more intense hydrological cycle (heavier downpours, more severe droughts, s3); more intense heatwaves (section 2); poleward shift of climate belts and storm tracks; expansion of the subtropical dry zones; possible weakening of the AMOC (s4); stronger tropical cyclones; sea-level rise from thermal expansion and melting land ice.
- [ ] G5 Effects on Ireland: warmer temperatures; wetter winters and more flooding; risk of summer drought (2018, s6); stronger storms and coastal erosion. One bullet; reuse `geo-economic-environment-s3`.
- [ ] G6 Scientific consensus and responses: the IPCC assesses the evidence (it stated in 2021 that it is unequivocal that human influence has warmed the climate); the Paris Agreement aims (reuse). One bullet; policy detail stays in `geo-economic-environment-s3`.

Climate change example 3: El Niño (and La Niña) (full case study). Diagram cues: two cross-sections of the equatorial Pacific, normal year and El Niño year, showing the trade winds, warm pool, thermocline, rising and sinking air and rain.
- [ ] C-N Normal conditions (Walker circulation): strong south-east trade winds push warm surface water westward across the Pacific, piling up a warm pool near Indonesia and northern Australia (sea level is higher there); warm, moist air rises over the warm pool (low pressure, heavy rain); air returns east at height and sinks over the cool eastern Pacific (high pressure, dry coastal Peru and northern Chile); off Peru cold, nutrient-rich water upwells (Humboldt Current), supporting the anchoveta fishery.
- [ ] C-EN El Niño: every 2–7 years the trade winds weaken or even reverse; the warm water flows back east towards South America; the thermocline deepens in the east and upwelling stops; sea-surface temperatures in the central and eastern Pacific rise well above normal; the zone of rising air and rain shifts east. It usually begins around the end of the year and lasts about 9–12 months, sometimes longer. Named by Peruvian fishermen after the Christ child because the warm water appeared around Christmas. Together with the Southern Oscillation (the see-saw of air pressure across the Pacific, studied by Gilbert Walker) it is called ENSO.
- [ ] C-EF Effects of El Niño, one bullet each: heavy rain and floods in coastal Peru and Ecuador (normally desert); collapse of the anchoveta catch and the fishmeal industry, seabird deaths; drought in Indonesia, the Philippines and eastern Australia (crop failure, bushfires, forest fires and haze); often a weaker Indian monsoon and drought in southern Africa; fewer Atlantic hurricanes, more in the central and eastern Pacific; global average temperature rises temporarily (strong El Niño years set records). Effects on Ireland and Europe are weak and uncertain (Trap).
- [ ] C-ES Named events, one bullet each, facts in section 2: 1972–73 (anchoveta collapse); 1982–83; 1997–98; 2015–16; 2023–24.
- [ ] C-LN La Niña: the opposite phase: stronger trade winds, cooler than normal eastern Pacific, stronger upwelling, heavier rain in Indonesia and Australia (floods), drought in Peru and the south-western USA, more Atlantic hurricanes. Example in section 2.
- [ ] C-LK Why El Niño matters to the syllabus: it shows the atmosphere and ocean acting as one linked system (s1) and how a change in ocean circulation changes weather around the world (teleconnections). Monitoring by the equatorial Pacific buoy array and satellites gives months of warning (s1).

## 3. Traps

- "Monsoon" means a **seasonal reversal of winds**, not just heavy rain.
- Mediterranean summers are dry because of the **subtropical high moving poleward**, not because of the Sahara or cold currents alone. Winter rain comes from **westerly depressions**.
- The continental climate's precipitation maximum is in **summer** (convection), unlike Ireland's autumn–winter maximum.
- Equatorial: the **daily** range exceeds the **annual** range. Maximum temperatures are lower than in deserts.
- The hottest temperatures are in deserts; the largest annual ranges are in continental interiors; the smallest at the equator and on oceanic coasts.
- El Niño is **warming** of the central and eastern Pacific (off Peru), not the western Pacific. It causes **drought in Indonesia and Australia** and **floods in Peru**; La Niña is the reverse. Don't claim El Niño causes Irish weather events: its effect on Ireland is weak and unproven.
- El Niño is a natural climate **variation**; don't present it as caused by global warming (scientists are still researching whether warming changes it).
- Milankovitch cycles don't change the total sunlight much; they change its **distribution** by latitude and season. Feedbacks do the rest.
- Ice Age sea level was **lower** (water locked in ice), not higher.
- Keep the Little Ice Age and Medieval Warm Period as regional (mainly North Atlantic and Europe) and approximate in date; don't give global temperature figures for them.
- Don't give 2100 temperature or sea-level projections, Irish emissions percentages or policy detail (those belong to `geo-economic-environment-s3`). Don't give rainfall totals for Indian stations.
- Only one climate is answered in the exam: each practice model answer covers exactly one climate.
- The Sahel's desertification and the economic effects of climate (farming, water, tourism) are s6 and `geo-option-geoecology-s3`; here one clause on the Sahel as a shifting transition zone.

## 4. What exams ask (topics only)

- 80 marks (asked in most years): describe and account for the characteristics of one global climate type; one variant names aspects (temperature, precipitation, location, pressure belts and air circulation). Expected: location with a map cue; temperature pattern with reasons; precipitation amount, season and type with reasons; pressure and winds with the seasonal shift; a named example place; often vegetation briefly.
- 80 marks (asked repeatedly): climate characteristics change over time and space. Expected: spatial variation (latitude, continentality, currents, altitude) and temporal change (Ice Ages and their causes; Little Ice Age; global warming with evidence and effects; El Niño).
