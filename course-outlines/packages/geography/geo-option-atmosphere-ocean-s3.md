## 1. Must cover (one bullet or block for every item; tick each off)

The hydrological cycle (diagram cue: a labelled cycle from ocean to atmosphere to land and back, with stores as boxes and flows as arrows)
- [ ] H1 A closed system: the total amount of water on earth stays the same; it moves between stores driven by solar energy (evaporation) and gravity (precipitation, runoff).
- [ ] H2 Stores with rounded shares (section 2): oceans, ice sheets and glaciers, groundwater, lakes and rivers, soil moisture, the atmosphere, living things. Only a tiny fraction is fresh liquid surface water.
- [ ] H3 Flows (processes), one bullet each: evaporation (liquid to vapour, mainly from the ocean); transpiration (from plant leaves); evapotranspiration (the two combined over land); sublimation (ice to vapour); advection (vapour carried by wind from sea to land); condensation (vapour to droplets: cloud, fog, dew); precipitation; interception, infiltration, surface runoff, throughflow and groundwater flow returning water to the sea (one bullet; detail in `geo-rivers`).
- [ ] H4 The ocean–atmosphere exchange in numbers: most evaporation is from the oceans and most precipitation falls back on them; the surplus evaporated over the oceans is carried to the land by wind and returns by rivers and groundwater (section 2).
- [ ] H5 Residence time: water stays in the atmosphere only about 9 days on average, but in the ocean for thousands of years and in the Antarctic ice sheet for far longer. So the atmosphere's water is quickly recycled.
- [ ] H6 The exchange varies over the earth: evaporation exceeds precipitation over the subtropical oceans (high salinity, link s1) and precipitation exceeds evaporation in the equatorial belt and the mid-latitude westerly belt. Human activity changes the cycle (deforestation reduces evapotranspiration and rainfall recycling in the Amazon; global warming intensifies it, s5).

Humidity
- [ ] U1 Humidity: the amount of water vapour in the air. Absolute humidity: the mass of water vapour in a volume of air (g/m³). Relative humidity: the amount of water vapour present as a percentage of the most the air could hold at that temperature. Saturated air: 100% relative humidity.
- [ ] U2 Warm air can hold much more water vapour than cold air (about 7% more for each 1 °C rise). So cooling air raises its relative humidity without adding water; warming it lowers it. This is why relative humidity is usually highest at dawn and lowest in mid-afternoon.
- [ ] U3 Dew point: the temperature at which air becomes saturated as it cools; further cooling causes condensation. Measuring humidity is in s1 (one clause).
- [ ] U4 Humidity's effects: high humidity in the tropics makes heat oppressive because sweat evaporates slowly; low humidity in deserts allows rapid evaporation and large daily temperature ranges.

Evaporation
- [ ] E1 Evaporation: water molecules gain enough energy to escape as vapour. It needs energy (latent heat of vaporisation) taken from the surface, so it cools the surface; this energy is released again on condensation (link s2).
- [ ] E2 Factors increasing the rate, one bullet each: higher temperature (more energy); drier air (low relative humidity); wind (removes moist air and replaces it with drier air); a large supply of water (oceans, lakes, wet ground); strong sunshine; low air pressure.
- [ ] E3 Where evaporation is greatest: the subtropical oceans under clear skies and steady trade winds, and warm currents such as the Gulf Stream; least over cold polar seas. Potential evapotranspiration far exceeds rainfall in hot deserts (drought, s6).

Condensation
- [ ] C1 Condensation: water vapour turning into liquid droplets when air cools to its dew point. It needs condensation nuclei: tiny particles of dust, sea salt, smoke or pollen around which droplets form.
- [ ] C2 Ways air is cooled to dew point, one bullet each: adiabatic cooling of rising air (the main cause of cloud); contact cooling (radiation) when air touches ground that has lost heat at night; advection cooling when warm, moist air moves over a cold surface; mixing of air masses of different temperature.
- [ ] C3 Adiabatic temperature change: rising air expands because pressure falls with height, and cools without losing heat to its surroundings; sinking air is compressed and warms. Unsaturated air cools at the dry adiabatic lapse rate (about 10 °C per 1,000 m); after condensation begins, latent heat released slows the cooling to the saturated adiabatic lapse rate (about 5–7 °C per 1,000 m, varying with temperature). The height where condensation begins is the condensation level (cloud base).
- [ ] C4 Stability and instability: if rising air stays warmer than its surroundings (it cools more slowly than the environmental lapse rate of about 6.5 °C per 1,000 m), it keeps rising (unstable): towering cumulus, showers, thunderstorms. If it becomes cooler than its surroundings, it sinks back (stable): layer cloud or clear skies. A temperature inversion (warmer air above cooler air) is very stable and traps fog and pollution. Diagram cue: temperature-height graph comparing the environmental and adiabatic lapse rates.
- [ ] C5 Forms of condensation near the ground, one bullet each: dew (on surfaces cooled below dew point on clear, calm nights); hoar frost (when dew point is below 0 °C, vapour turns straight to ice crystals); radiation fog (clear, calm nights, usually under anticyclones in autumn and winter; forms in valleys and hollows; "burns off" in the morning sun); advection fog (warm moist air over cold sea or land, e.g. sea fog; section 2); hill fog (cloud on high ground); smog (fog mixed with smoke).

Cloud formation and classification
- [ ] K1 Clouds are visible masses of tiny water droplets or ice crystals formed when rising air cools below its dew point. Five causes of uplift, one bullet each: convection (surface heating), orographic (relief) uplift, frontal uplift (warm air rising over cold air at a front), convergence (air streams meeting, e.g. the ITCZ), and turbulence.
- [ ] K2 Classification by shape (from Luke Howard, section 2): cirrus (wispy, fibrous, "curl of hair"), cumulus (heaped, puffy, "heap"), stratus (layered sheet, "layer"); "nimbus" or "nimbo-" means rain-bearing; "alto-" means middle level.
- [ ] K3 Classification by height into the ten main types (WMO), each with appearance and weather, one bullet each:
  - High (above about 6,000 m, ice crystals): cirrus (thin wisps, "mares' tails"; fair weather but often the first sign of an approaching warm front); cirrostratus (thin milky veil producing a halo round the sun or moon; rain likely within hours); cirrocumulus (small ripples, "mackerel sky").
  - Middle (about 2,000–6,000 m): altocumulus (grey or white patches in rolls); altostratus (grey sheet through which the sun shows as if through frosted glass; thickening ahead of a warm front).
  - Low (below about 2,000 m): stratus (grey, featureless layer; drizzle, hill fog); stratocumulus (lumpy grey layer, the commonest cloud over Ireland; usually dry or light drizzle); nimbostratus (thick, dark rain cloud; long periods of steady rain or snow at warm fronts).
  - Vertical development (base low, top may reach the tropopause): cumulus (fair-weather heaps with flat bases on sunny days; can grow into showers); cumulonimbus (towering cloud with an anvil-shaped top spreading at the tropopause; heavy showers, hail, thunder and lightning, squalls).
- [ ] K4 Diagram cue: a chart of the ten cloud types arranged by height. Cloud sequence ahead of a warm front (cirrus → cirrostratus → altostratus → nimbostratus) and cumulonimbus at a cold front: one bullet; the full sequence is in s4.
- [ ] K5 Clouds and the energy balance (one bullet): low thick clouds cool the surface by reflecting sunlight; high thin clouds warm it by trapping long-wave radiation; cloudy nights are milder.

Precipitation
- [ ] P1 Cloud droplets are far too small to fall; they must grow about a million times in volume to form a raindrop.
- [ ] P2 Ice-crystal (Bergeron–Findeisen) process: in clouds colder than 0 °C, supercooled droplets and ice crystals exist together; vapour is deposited on the ice crystals, which grow at the droplets' expense, become heavy and fall as snow, melting into rain below the freezing level. The main process in mid-latitude clouds, including Ireland's.
- [ ] P3 Collision–coalescence process: in warm clouds (above 0 °C throughout, common in the tropics) larger droplets fall faster, collide with and absorb smaller ones until heavy enough to fall.
- [ ] P4 Forms, one bullet each: rain; drizzle (very small drops from stratus); snow (ice crystals clumped as flakes, falling when the air is near or below freezing to the ground); sleet (melting snow, or rain and snow mixed); hail (layered ice balls formed when strong updrafts in cumulonimbus carry frozen drops up and down repeatedly, adding layers, until too heavy); freezing rain and glaze.
- [ ] P5 Types of rainfall by cause, each in numbered stages with a diagram cue:
  - Convectional: the ground is heated strongly; air above it warms, expands, rises and cools adiabatically; it reaches dew point and cumulus then cumulonimbus clouds form; heavy showers and often thunder follow, usually in the afternoon. Typical of the equatorial belt every day and of Irish summer afternoons occasionally, especially inland.
  - Relief (orographic): moist air from the sea is forced to rise over a mountain barrier; it cools, condenses and gives heavy rain on the windward slope; on the leeward side the air descends, is compressed and warms, so clouds evaporate and a rain shadow results; a warm, dry föhn-type wind may blow down the lee side (s4). Examples: Ireland's west coast mountains, the Western Ghats and the Khasi Hills of India (section 2).
  - Frontal (cyclonic): in a depression, warm, moist tropical air is forced to rise over cold polar air at the warm front (gentle ascent, long steady rain) and is undercut at the cold front (sharp ascent, short heavy showers). Ireland's main source of rain. Mechanism of depressions in s4; one or two bullets here.
- [ ] P6 Convergence: where air streams meet they are forced to rise (the ITCZ, where the trade winds converge), giving heavy rain. One bullet.

Precipitation and its distribution patterns ("global patterns and variations")
- [ ] D1 World pattern by latitude, each explained by the pressure belt (diagram cue: a pole-to-pole profile of rainfall under the three-cell model, links s2 and s4):
  - Equatorial belt (about 0–10°): heavy rain all year (over 2,000 mm): low pressure, rising air, convergence at the ITCZ, convection, high evaporation from warm seas and forests.
  - Tropics with a wet and dry season (about 10–20°, savanna and monsoon lands): rain in summer when the ITCZ moves over them, dry in winter.
  - Subtropical high-pressure belts (about 20–30°): very low rainfall, the world's great hot deserts: sinking, warming air, stable conditions and clear skies; worst on west coasts beside cold currents.
  - Mid-latitudes (about 40–60°): moderate to heavy rain from depressions in the westerlies, wettest on west coasts facing onshore winds (Ireland, Norway, British Columbia, southern Chile); decreasing into continental interiors.
  - Polar regions: very low precipitation (cold air holds little vapour; sinking air under polar high pressure); Antarctica is the driest continent, a cold desert.
- [ ] D2 Modifying factors, one bullet each with an example: relief and rain shadow; distance from the sea (continental interiors are dry, e.g. Central Asia); prevailing winds onshore or offshore; ocean currents (warm currents add moisture and instability; cold currents chill the air, making it stable, so rain is rare though fog is common, e.g. the Atacama and Namib, reuse `geo-option-geoecology-s4`); seasonal migration of the pressure belts with the overhead sun.
- [ ] D3 Seasonal regimes, one bullet each: equatorial (rain all year, often peaks after the equinoxes); monsoon (very wet summer, dry winter: India); Mediterranean (wet winter, dry summer); continental interiors (summer maximum from convection); Irish/oceanic (rain all year, maximum in autumn and winter). Full climates in s5.
- [ ] D4 Ireland's pattern: west wetter than east, mountains wettest, more wet days in the west; caused by prevailing south-westerlies, Atlantic depressions arriving first in the west, relief uplift, and the rain shadow in the east. Reuse figures in section 2 only.
- [ ] D5 Variability: rainfall is most unreliable where totals are lowest (semi-arid lands such as the Sahel), so drought risk is greatest there (s6). One bullet.
- [ ] D6 "Distinctive weather and climate regimes" (the syllabus statement): one block tying exchanges to regimes, one bullet each: the equatorial daily cycle (morning heating, afternoon storms); the monsoon's seasonal reversal over India (moist sea air in summer, dry land air in winter); the fog deserts of cold-current coasts; the cool, cloudy, wet maritime regime of Ireland fed by moisture from the Atlantic and the North Atlantic Drift; snowy lake-effect and continental winters; the intensification of the water cycle with warming (heavier downpours, more evaporation, s5).

## 3. Traps

- Relative humidity is a **percentage of what the air could hold at that temperature**, not the amount of water in the air (that is absolute humidity). Cooling air raises relative humidity without adding any water.
- Rising air cools because it **expands**, not because it is "higher and closer to space" or because it meets cold air.
- The leeward side of mountains gets **less** rain (rain shadow) because descending air warms; don't say it is drier only "because the rain has all fallen".
- Radiation fog forms on **clear, calm** nights (cloud would stop the ground losing heat; wind would mix the air). Advection fog can form in wind.
- Cumulonimbus, not nimbostratus, gives thunder and hail; nimbostratus gives steady frontal rain. Stratus gives drizzle.
- Hail forms in strong **updrafts** inside cumulonimbus, mostly in warm seasons; it is not frozen rain (that is sleet or ice pellets).
- Don't call the ice-crystal process "the Bergeron process only" without explaining it; name it Bergeron–Findeisen.
- Don't give rainfall totals for Mawsynram, Cherrapunji or desert stations, or any global water-volume figures beyond section 2.
- Weather of a depression's fronts in sequence, anticyclones, land and sea breezes, föhn and katabatic winds are s4. Here, frontal rain in one or two bullets and föhn as one clause.
- Full climate descriptions (equatorial, monsoon, Mediterranean, etc.) are s5. Here, regimes as one-line bullets only.
- Irish chart-reading (isohyets, reading rainfall maps) is `geo-weather-climate-s1`; don't re-teach it.
