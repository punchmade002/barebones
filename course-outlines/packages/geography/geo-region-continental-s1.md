## 1. Must cover (one bullet or block for every item; tick each off)

The region and the sketch map
- [ ] O1 Location and size: South Asia; a subcontinent bounded by the Himalayas to the north, the Arabian Sea to the west and the Bay of Bengal to the east; the Tropic of Cancer crosses it; area and rank from section 2.
- [ ] O2 Why India counts as a continental/subcontinental region: its size, its physical unity behind the Himalayan wall, and its own cultural and economic identity.
- [ ] O3 Sketch-map skill: draw the outline (triangular peninsula, north-eastern lobe around Bangladesh); show and name one relief feature (as an outlined area), one river (its full course from source to mouth), urban centres (dots on the correct river or coast) and an agricultural or industrial region (as an outlined area). Give the recommended set from section 2 and the placing cues for each. Remind students that names may go on the map or in a key.

Physical processes: climate (including the monsoon)
- [ ] C1 India's climate type: tropical monsoon. "Monsoon" means a seasonal reversal of wind direction, not just rain.
- [ ] C2 The three seasons: the hot season (March–May), the wet south-west monsoon (June–September), the cool dry season (October–February), with the retreating monsoon in October–November.
- [ ] C3 Cause of the south-west monsoon: intense heating of the land in early summer creates low pressure over north-west India and the Tibetan Plateau; the ITCZ shifts north; south-east trade winds cross the equator and are deflected by the Coriolis force into south-west winds; they pick up moisture over the Indian Ocean.
- [ ] C4 The jet streams: the subtropical westerly jet stream moves north of the Himalayas in early summer, allowing the monsoon to "burst" suddenly; it returns south of the Himalayas in winter.
- [ ] C5 The two branches: the Arabian Sea branch (hits the Western Ghats) and the Bay of Bengal branch (hits the north-east hills, then is turned west along the Ganges plain by the Himalayas).
- [ ] C6 Onset and timing: arrives in Kerala around 1 June and covers the country by mid-July; it brings about three-quarters of India's annual rainfall.
- [ ] C7 Relief (orographic) rainfall and rain shadow: very wet windward slopes of the Western Ghats and the Khasi Hills; drier Deccan Plateau in the rain shadow of the Western Ghats.
- [ ] C8 Rainfall decreases westwards along the Ganges plain (wetter east, drier north-west), and the Thar Desert is dry because the Arabian Sea branch passes parallel to the Aravalli Range with no barrier to force uplift.
- [ ] C9 The cool dry season: high pressure over Central Asia gives north-east winds blowing off the land, so most of India is dry; the Himalayas shelter India from cold Central Asian air.
- [ ] C10 Exceptions in winter: the Tamil Nadu (Coromandel) coast gets most of its rain from the north-east monsoon (winds pick up moisture over the Bay of Bengal); western disturbances (depressions from the west) bring light winter rain to the north-west, important for wheat.
- [ ] C11 Temperature: the hot season often exceeds 40°C on the northern plains, with hot dry "loo" winds; the south stays hot all year; the Himalayas are cold with altitude.
- [ ] C12 Unreliability and hazards: late, weak or broken monsoons cause drought; heavy monsoons cause floods; tropical cyclones strike the east coast (example in section 2). Climate change is making the monsoon more erratic and heatwaves more severe.
- [ ] C13 Importance: the monsoon's timing decides the farming calendar (kharif and rabi; see A3), refills reservoirs and groundwater, and affects GDP.

Physical processes: soils
- [ ] S1 Alluvial soils: the most extensive and most fertile; on the Indo-Gangetic Plain and the eastern deltas; deposited by Himalayan rivers; newer khadar (floodplain) and older bhangar (higher terraces).
- [ ] S2 Black soils (regur, black cotton soil): weathered from the basalt lava of the Deccan Traps (north-west Deccan, Maharashtra); clay-rich, hold moisture, crack deeply when dry ("self-ploughing"); ideal for cotton.
- [ ] S3 Red soils: weathered from ancient crystalline (granite and gneiss) rocks of the south and east Deccan; red from iron oxide; low in nitrogen and humus; millets, groundnuts and pulses, better with fertiliser and irrigation.
- [ ] S4 Laterite soils: formed under high temperatures and heavy rainfall, which leach out silica and nutrients, leaving iron and aluminium oxides; on the Western Ghats and in Kerala; infertile but used for tea, coffee and cashew with fertiliser; hardens when exposed and is cut into building bricks.
- [ ] S5 Desert soils (Thar, Rajasthan): sandy, low in humus, sometimes saline; productive where canal irrigation reaches them (section 2).
- [ ] S6 Mountain soils: thin and immature on the Himalayan slopes; tea and fruit on terraces.
- [ ] S7 Soil problems: erosion by monsoon downpours and deforestation (gullies and ravines); salinisation and waterlogging under canal irrigation in Punjab and Haryana; nutrient exhaustion from intensive Green Revolution farming.
- [ ] S8 Link soils to the rock cycle and weathering, briefly: basalt → black soil; laterite as a product of intense chemical weathering. Do not re-teach weathering.

Physical processes: relief
- [ ] R1 Three main relief divisions: the Himalayas (north), the Northern Plains (Indo-Gangetic Plain), the Peninsular Plateau (the Deccan). Add the Thar Desert, the coastal plains and the islands.
- [ ] R2 The Himalayas: young fold mountains formed by the collision of the Indian (Indo-Australian) plate with the Eurasian plate; three parallel ranges (Greater Himalaya, Lesser Himalaya, Shiwaliks); India's highest peak. Point to `geo-plate-tectonics-s3` for the collision; one or two sentences here only.
- [ ] R3 Effects of the Himalayas: a barrier to cold air, a trigger for monsoon rain, the source of perennial snow-fed rivers, hydroelectric potential, a barrier to transport, earthquakes, landslides, sparse population.
- [ ] R4 The Northern Plains: vast, flat and low, built from alluvium deposited by the Indus, Ganges and Brahmaputra systems in a trough in front of the Himalayas; the agricultural and population heartland.
- [ ] R5 The Deccan Plateau: very old, hard crystalline rocks, once part of Gondwanaland; a stable shield; tilts from west to east, so most of its rivers flow east; rich in minerals (especially the Chota Nagpur Plateau in the north-east).
- [ ] R6 The Deccan Traps: thick layers of basalt from huge fissure eruptions, covering much of the north-west Deccan (link to S2).
- [ ] R7 The Western Ghats: a steep, continuous escarpment along the west coast, rich in biodiversity; the Eastern Ghats: lower and broken into separate hills by east-flowing rivers.
- [ ] R8 Other features: the Vindhya and Satpura ranges separating the plains from the Deccan, with the Narmada flowing west between them; the Aravalli Range, among the oldest fold mountains on earth; the Thar Desert west of it.
- [ ] R9 Coastal plains: the narrow western plain (Konkan in the north, Malabar in Kerala, with backwaters/lagoons) and the wider eastern plain (Coromandel) with large river deltas.
- [ ] R10 Relief and human activity: plains = dense population, intensive farming, easy transport; plateau = minerals and industry; mountains = tourism, hydroelectricity, sparse settlement.

Physical processes: drainage
- [ ] D1 Two river systems: Himalayan rivers (perennial: fed by snow and glacier melt plus monsoon rain; large, with high flows in summer) vs Peninsular rivers (seasonal: rain-fed, low in the dry season; shorter; in older, harder rock with fixed valleys).
- [ ] D2 The Ganges (Ganga): source, course, cities on it, tributaries, delta and length (section 2); the largest basin in India.
- [ ] D3 The Brahmaputra: rises in Tibet, enters India in the north-east, flows west through Assam, joins the Ganges in Bangladesh; braided, carries huge loads of silt; floods Assam most years.
- [ ] D4 The Indus: rises in Tibet, flows through Ladakh and then mostly through Pakistan; its five Punjab tributaries (section 2) irrigate India's wheat belt; the Indus Waters Treaty divides its waters.
- [ ] D5 East-flowing peninsular rivers: Mahanadi, Godavari, Krishna, Kaveri; they build deltas on the Bay of Bengal coast (fertile rice lands).
- [ ] D6 West-flowing peninsular rivers: Narmada and Tapi; they flow through rift valleys and end in estuaries, not deltas.
- [ ] D7 Floods: monsoon floods on the Ganges plain, in Bihar and Assam; silt deposition renews soils but floods destroy crops and homes; rivers shift course on the flat plains (example in section 2).
- [ ] D8 River management: multipurpose dams for irrigation, hydroelectricity and flood control (examples and the controversy in section 2); canal irrigation; water disputes between states and with neighbours.
- [ ] D9 Pollution of the Ganges by sewage, industrial waste and cremations, and the clean-up programmes (section 2).
- [ ] D10 Religious and economic importance of the rivers: the Ganges is sacred to Hindus; rivers provide water, irrigation, transport and fertile alluvium.

Economic processes: primary activities — agriculture
- [ ] A1 Importance: agriculture employs the largest share of workers but produces a much smaller share of GDP (figures in section 2), showing low productivity.
- [ ] A2 Types of agriculture (exams ask students to name two): subsistence farming on small plots; intensive wet-rice farming (paddy) in the wet east, south and deltas; commercial irrigated wheat farming in the north-west (Punjab, Haryana); plantation farming of tea (Assam, Darjeeling) and coffee (Karnataka); cash crops (cotton on black soils, sugar cane, jute in West Bengal); dairy farming; pastoralism in dry areas.
- [ ] A3 The farming calendar: kharif crops sown with the monsoon (June–July) and harvested in autumn (rice, cotton, maize, millets); rabi crops sown in October–November and harvested in March–April (wheat, mustard, gram), using irrigation and western-disturbance rain.
- [ ] A4 Physical factors (advantages): tropical temperatures allow two or three crops a year; monsoon rain; fertile alluvium on the plains and black soil on the Deccan; flat land; perennial Himalayan rivers for irrigation.
- [ ] A5 Physical factors (problems): monsoon unreliability (drought and floods); poor laterite and desert soils; steep slopes; soil erosion and salinisation; falling water tables.
- [ ] A6 Irrigation: canals (Punjab, the Indira Gandhi Canal in Rajasthan) and tubewells; its role in the Green Revolution; overuse of groundwater in Punjab.
- [ ] A7 The Green Revolution: from the mid-1960s, high-yielding varieties of wheat and rice, chemical fertilisers, pesticides, irrigation and machinery; food-grain output rose many times over (figures in section 2) and India became self-sufficient; benefits concentrated in Punjab, Haryana and western Uttar Pradesh and among larger farmers; costs: groundwater depletion, soil degradation, chemical pollution, debt.
- [ ] A8 The White Revolution: Operation Flood and co-operative dairying (section 2) made India the world's largest milk producer; religion supports dairying (the cow is sacred to Hindus, so cattle are kept for milk and draught, not beef).
- [ ] A9 Human and economic factors: very small and fragmented holdings (figures in section 2), subdivided at each inheritance; landless labourers; lack of capital and credit; farmer debt; middlemen; poor storage and roads causing post-harvest losses.
- [ ] A10 Markets and government: a huge domestic market of over 1.4 billion people; minimum support prices and state purchase of grain; income support for farmers (section 2); India is a large food exporter (section 2).
- [ ] A11 Challenges and conflict: drought, farmer distress and suicides, stubble burning in Punjab and Haryana after the rice harvest worsening Delhi's winter smog, the 2020–21 farmers' protests (section 2).
- [ ] A12 Production facts to support answers: India's rank in rice, wheat, milk, tea and jute (section 2).

Economic processes: primary activities — forestry, fishing, mining and energy
- [ ] P1 Forestry: forests cover about one-fifth of India; tropical monsoon (deciduous) forests, wet evergreen forest on the Western Ghats and in the north-east, mangroves in the Sundarbans; fuelwood use; deforestation; afforestation and protected areas.
- [ ] P2 Fishing: a long coastline and large rivers; marine fishing (Kerala, Gujarat, West Bengal) and fast-growing inland aquaculture (fish and prawn ponds); India is one of the world's largest fish producers.
- [ ] P3 Mining: the Chota Nagpur Plateau (Jharkhand, Odisha, Chhattisgarh, West Bengal) holds most of India's coal and iron ore, the basis of the steel industry; iron ore also in Karnataka and Goa.
- [ ] P4 Coal: India is the world's second-largest coal producer; coal generates about 70% of its electricity (early 2020s); environmental cost (air pollution, displacement of tribal people).
- [ ] P5 Oil and gas: offshore Mumbai High field; India imports most of its crude oil.
- [ ] P6 Hydroelectricity from Himalayan rivers and large dams; renewable energy expansion, especially solar in Rajasthan and wind in Tamil Nadu and Gujarat; the 2030 non-fossil target (section 2).

Economic processes: secondary activities (patterns in manufacturing)
- [ ] M1 Colonial legacy (brief; detail is in `geo-economic-development-s3`): Britain used India as a source of raw materials and a market; early industry was cotton textiles in Bombay (Mumbai) and jute in Calcutta (Kolkata), and the first steel works at Jamshedpur (section 2).
- [ ] M2 1947–1991: state-led industrialisation; five-year plans (from 1951); state-owned steel plants on the Chota Nagpur minerals (section 2); protection from imports and the "Licence Raj" (permits needed to start or expand a factory), which made industry inefficient.
- [ ] M3 1991 economic liberalisation: after a balance-of-payments crisis, India removed most licences, cut tariffs and opened to foreign investment; manufacturing and services grew rapidly.
- [ ] M4 Recent policy: Make in India (2014), Special Economic Zones, production-linked incentives (subsidies per unit produced) for electronics, cars and pharmaceuticals; industrial corridors such as the Delhi–Mumbai Industrial Corridor.
- [ ] M5 Factor — raw materials: coal, iron ore, limestone and manganese of the Chota Nagpur Plateau → iron and steel (Jamshedpur and the state plants); cotton on black soils → textiles (Mumbai, Ahmedabad); jute in the Ganges delta → jute mills in Kolkata.
- [ ] M6 Factor — labour: a huge, young, low-cost labour force; also a large pool of engineers and graduates from the IITs; most workers are in the informal sector.
- [ ] M7 Factor — markets: a domestic market of over 1.4 billion with a growing middle class (cars, phones, consumer goods); export markets after 1991.
- [ ] M8 Factor — infrastructure: ports (Mumbai, Chennai, Kolkata), railways, the Golden Quadrilateral; but weak in many areas (power cuts, congestion), raising costs.
- [ ] M9 Factor — government policy: as in M2–M4.
- [ ] M10 The manufacturing pattern (named industrial regions): Mumbai–Pune; Ahmedabad–Vadodara (Gujarat); the Hooghly belt around Kolkata; the Chota Nagpur/Damodar valley; the Delhi National Capital Region (Gurugram, Noida); the southern cluster of Bengaluru, Chennai and Coimbatore. Industry is concentrated in the west and south; the north and east (Bihar, Uttar Pradesh, Odisha) are less industrialised.
- [ ] M11 Key industries with examples: iron and steel; cotton textiles and garments; cars (Maruti Suzuki in Haryana; Chennai's car cluster); pharmaceuticals and vaccines (Hyderabad, Pune); electronics assembly (smartphones near Chennai); aerospace and defence in Bengaluru.
- [ ] M12 Challenges for manufacturing: poor infrastructure and power supply; land acquisition conflicts (Singur); bureaucracy and complex regulation; competition from China; pollution and industrial accidents (Bhopal); decline of old industries (Mumbai's textile mills); manufacturing's share of the economy has stayed low compared with China.

Economic processes: tertiary activities
- [ ] T1 Services are now the largest sector, producing over half of India's GDP.
- [ ] T2 IT and business-process outsourcing: Bengaluru as "India's Silicon Valley"; also Hyderabad, Pune, Chennai, Gurugram, Noida; Indian firms and multinationals (section 2).
- [ ] T3 Reasons for IT growth: large English-speaking graduate workforce; IITs; low wages; time difference with the USA allowing round-the-clock work; 1991 liberalisation and software technology parks; Bengaluru's early lead in public-sector science and engineering.
- [ ] T4 Tourism attractions (exams ask for two named attractions): the Taj Mahal, Agra; the Golden Triangle (Delhi–Agra–Jaipur); Rajasthan's forts and palaces; Goa's beaches; Kerala's backwaters; Varanasi and the Ganges; the Himalayas and hill stations; wildlife reserves (section 2).
- [ ] T5 Reasons tourists come: culture and history (Mughal and Hindu monuments, religion, festivals); varied physical landscapes; warm, dry winter (October–March) matching the European winter; low costs; spiritual, yoga and Ayurveda tourism; medical tourism.
- [ ] T6 Government and access: "Incredible India" marketing (from 2002); e-visas (from 2014); airport expansion and low-cost airlines; foreign arrivals (section 2); domestic tourism is far larger than foreign tourism.
- [ ] T7 Positive impacts of tourism: jobs (hotels, guides, crafts, transport), foreign currency, infrastructure, preservation of heritage and wildlife.
- [ ] T8 Negative impacts and challenges: overcrowding and pollution at sites (air pollution discolouring the Taj Mahal's marble), water shortages and waste in Goa, cultural erosion, seasonality, safety and sanitation concerns, poor infrastructure.
- [ ] T9 Transport — rail: Indian Railways, begun under British rule, is one of the world's largest networks (section 2); carries huge numbers of passengers and freight (coal, grain); modernisation (dedicated freight corridors, Vande Bharat trains, the Mumbai–Ahmedabad high-speed line under construction).
- [ ] T10 Transport — road: the Golden Quadrilateral linking Delhi, Mumbai, Chennai and Kolkata; rural roads programme; congestion and very high road-death rates.
- [ ] T11 Transport — air, sea and urban: fast growth of domestic aviation and low-cost airlines; major container ports; metro systems (Delhi Metro, section 2).
- [ ] T12 Importance of transport: links farms to markets, raw materials to factories, workers to cities; integrates a huge country; enables tourism. Exams ask for two named types of transport.

Human processes: population dynamics and distribution
- [ ] Y1 Size and growth: Census 2011 total; growth since 1951; India overtook China as the world's most populous country in 2023 (UN estimate). The census due in 2021 was postponed; Census 2011 remains the latest census.
- [ ] Y2 Demographic transition: death rates fell first (medicine, vaccination, food supply from the Green Revolution) while birth rates stayed high, giving rapid growth; fertility has now fallen to about replacement level nationally (section 2). India is in late stage 3 of the model.
- [ ] Y3 Regional contrast in fertility: low in the south (Kerala) and high in the north (Bihar, Uttar Pradesh) (section 2), linked to female literacy, women's status, income and health care.
- [ ] Y4 Population policy: the world's first national family-planning programme (1952); forced sterilisations during the Emergency (1975–77) caused a backlash; today the focus is on education, health and voluntary family planning.
- [ ] Y5 Sex ratio: fewer females than males (Census 2011 figures in section 2) due to son preference and sex-selective abortion; the ban on prenatal sex determination and the "Beti Bachao Beti Padhao" campaign.
- [ ] Y6 Population structure: a young population (a broad-based pyramid); the "demographic dividend" if young workers find jobs; pressure on schools and jobs.
- [ ] Y7 Literacy: Census 2011 figures, with the male–female gap and the Kerala–Bihar contrast.
- [ ] Y8 Population distribution (a frequent 30-mark question): dense on the Ganges plain (fertile alluvium, water, flat land, long history of settlement), in the deltas and on the coastal plains (Kerala, West Bengal), and around the large cities; sparse in the Thar Desert (aridity), the Himalayas (cold, steep, thin soils), the forested hills of the north-east and the central plateaus. Explain with physical (climate, relief, soils, water) and human (history, farming, industry, transport, government) factors.
- [ ] Y9 Density figures: national density and the most and least densely populated states (Census 2011, section 2); Uttar Pradesh as the most populous state.
- [ ] Y10 Migration: internal rural-to-urban migration from Bihar and Uttar Pradesh to Mumbai, Delhi and other cities; the migrant workers' exodus during the 2020 COVID-19 lockdown; emigration to the Gulf (especially from Kerala) and to Western countries; India receives the world's largest remittances.

Human processes: language
- [ ] L1 Linguistic diversity: the Constitution's Eighth Schedule recognises 22 languages; Census 2011 found 121 languages spoken by 10,000 or more people.
- [ ] L2 Two main language families: Indo-Aryan in the north (Hindi, Bengali, Marathi, Gujarati, Punjabi) and Dravidian in the south (Tamil, Telugu, Kannada, Malayalam).
- [ ] L3 Hindi: the most widely spoken (Census 2011 share in section 2), concentrated in the northern "Hindi belt"; Hindi in Devanagari script is the official language of the Union, with English also used for official purposes.
- [ ] L4 Linguistic states: state boundaries were redrawn along language lines (States Reorganisation Act 1956), so most states are language regions.
- [ ] L5 Language conflict: resistance in the south, especially Tamil Nadu, to Hindi being imposed (anti-Hindi agitation of 1965); the three-language formula in schools.
- [ ] L6 English: a colonial legacy and a link language between states; an economic advantage for IT, outsourcing and global business (link to T3).

Human processes: religion
- [ ] G1 Religious composition (Census 2011 figures in section 2): Hindu majority; a very large Muslim minority; Christians, Sikhs, Buddhists, Jains.
- [ ] G2 Distribution: Sikhs concentrated in Punjab; Christian majorities in the north-eastern states of Nagaland, Mizoram and Meghalaya; Muslims spread throughout, with a majority in Jammu and Kashmir; Hindus everywhere.
- [ ] G3 India as birthplace of four religions (Hinduism, Buddhism, Jainism, Sikhism) and its holy places (Varanasi, Bodh Gaya, the Golden Temple at Amritsar).
- [ ] G4 Partition (1947): British India was divided into India and Muslim-majority Pakistan; mass migration and communal violence; East Pakistan became Bangladesh in 1971.
- [ ] G5 India is constitutionally a secular state; communal tension has persisted (Ayodhya 1992; Kashmir).
- [ ] G6 The caste system: hereditary social groups linked to Hinduism; Dalits (formerly "untouchables"); untouchability abolished by the Constitution (1950); reserved places (quotas) in education, government jobs and parliament for Scheduled Castes and Scheduled Tribes (Census 2011 shares in section 2); caste still affects rural life, marriage and politics.
- [ ] G7 Religion's effect on economic life: sacred cattle and dairying; vegetarian diets and demand for pulses and milk; pilgrimage tourism (Varanasi, the Kumbh Mela); festivals (Diwali) boosting consumer spending.

Human processes: urban development
- [ ] U1 Urbanisation: Census 2011 urban share and its rise since 1951; number of million-plus cities; India is still mostly rural but urbanising fast.
- [ ] U2 The megacities with Census 2011 urban-agglomeration populations: Mumbai, Delhi, Kolkata (section 2).
- [ ] U3 Case study of one urban area (exams ask "factors influencing the development of one urban area" and "reasons for the growth of an urban area"): **Mumbai** in full (site, port, colonial trade, cotton textiles, financial capital, Bollywood, migration, problems, planning). Kolkata and Bengaluru as shorter alternatives. Write each as its own block so a student can choose one.
- [ ] U4 Mumbai: factors and growth (section 2). Problems: slums (Dharavi), overcrowding, housing costs, congestion and overcrowded suburban trains, flooding in the monsoon (2005), water supply and sanitation. Responses: Navi Mumbai planned twin city, slum redevelopment, metro lines.
- [ ] U5 Kolkata (short): founded by the British East India Company, capital of British India until 1911, port on the Hooghly, jute and engineering; decline after Partition cut it off from its jute-growing hinterland; congestion and slums.
- [ ] U6 Bengaluru (short): growth as the IT capital (link to T2–T3); public-sector science and engineering base; pleasant climate on the Deccan; problems of traffic congestion, water shortages and loss of lakes and green space.
- [ ] U7 Delhi and New Delhi: the national capital (from 1911); planned New Delhi; growth of the National Capital Region (Gurugram, Noida); severe winter air pollution.
- [ ] U8 Planned cities and policy: Chandigarh (designed by Le Corbusier); the Smart Cities Mission (2015); metro systems.
- [ ] U9 Urban problems in general: slums (Census 2011 slum population), informal employment, inadequate water, sanitation and waste disposal, air pollution, traffic. Keep developing-world city theory for `geo-urban`.

Human processes: rural development
- [ ] V1 Most Indians are rural (Census 2011 figure and the number of villages in section 2).
- [ ] V2 Rural problems: poverty, small holdings and landlessness, debt, dependence on the monsoon, under-employment, poor services (water, sanitation, health, schools), lower literacy (especially of women), caste discrimination, out-migration of young men.
- [ ] V3 Rural development policies: Panchayati Raj (elected village councils, one-third of seats reserved for women); MGNREGA (100 days of guaranteed paid work); rural roads programme; Swachh Bharat Mission (toilets); rural electrification; PM-KISAN income support; self-help groups and microcredit for women.
- [ ] V4 Contrast in outcomes: Kerala (high literacy, low fertility, good health, remittances) vs Bihar (low literacy, high fertility, out-migration). Use this to show human processes interacting with economic ones.

Interaction of processes (the syllabus statement)
- [ ] I1 Regional inequality within India: the more developed west and south (Maharashtra, Gujarat, Tamil Nadu, Karnataka, Kerala) vs the poorer north and east (Bihar, Uttar Pradesh, Madhya Pradesh, Rajasthan, the "BIMARU" states); a core–periphery pattern within the subcontinent.
- [ ] I2 Chains of interaction to use in conclusions: monsoon → farming calendar → rural incomes → migration → urban slums; Chota Nagpur minerals → steel → industrial region; English (colonial legacy) → IT services → Bengaluru's growth; religion → dairying and pilgrimage tourism.

## 3. Traps (known errors)

- **Everest is not in India.** It is on the Nepal–China border. India's highest peak is Kangchenjunga. Don't put volcanoes in the Himalayas (collision boundary).
- **For a sketch map, prefer the Ganges.** The Indus flows mostly through Pakistan, and the lower Ganges and Brahmaputra and most of their delta are in Bangladesh. Show the Ganges within India, ending at the delta.
- **The region is the Republic of India.** Don't treat Pakistan or Bangladesh as part of it, and don't call the Sundarbans wholly Indian (they span India and Bangladesh).
- **Monsoon** means a seasonal reversal of the winds. The north-east (winter) monsoon is dry for most of India but wet on the Tamil Nadu coast. Don't say it rains everywhere all summer: the monsoon has breaks and is unreliable.
- **Kharif vs rabi:** kharif = monsoon (summer) crops such as rice; rabi = winter crops such as wheat. Don't swap them.
- **Soils:** black soil comes from basalt (Deccan Traps); red soil from older granite and gneiss; laterite from intense leaching. Don't call laterite fertile.
- **Hindi is the official language of the Union, not a "national language".** The Constitution names no national language.
- **Caste is a social system, not a religion.** Don't say caste was abolished; untouchability was.
- **Population figures:** use Census 2011 or the labelled survey/UN figures only. Never give a 2021 census figure (there was none), and don't state today's total as a census figure. Don't use the old figure of 2.1 children per woman as India's current fertility.
- **Capitals:** Kolkata was the British capital until 1911; Delhi/New Delhi since. Mumbai is the financial capital, not the political one. Bollywood is in Mumbai, not Bengaluru.
- **Names:** give the current name and, where useful, the old one once: Mumbai (Bombay), Kolkata (Calcutta), Chennai (Madras), Bengaluru (Bangalore), Prayagraj (Allahabad), Gurugram (Gurgaon). Then use the current name.
- **Don't give casualty figures for Bhopal**: sources disagree widely; say "thousands".
- **Don't claim Himalayan deforestation causes Bangladesh's floods** (disputed; same rule as `geo-rocks-weathering-s7`).
- **Don't copy past-paper model answers' figures.** Several in the question bank are unreliable (e.g. a "60% landless" rural figure and an all-India poverty-line percentage). Use only section 2's figures.
- **Neighbouring chapters:**
  - Colonialism's impact on India's economy and India as a developing economy are `geo-economic-development-s3`. Here give only the one-paragraph colonial background in M1.
  - The monsoon as a "distinctive global climate" is an Optional Unit topic (`geo-option-atmosphere-ocean`, [9.5]). Here teach the monsoon only as it affects India.
  - The Himalayan collision is in `geo-plate-tectonics-s3`; Gondwanaland is in `geo-plate-tectonics-s2`. One or two sentences only, consistent with their figures.
  - Developing-world city theory and urban problems in general are `geo-urban`; demographic transition theory and overpopulation in general are `geo-population-migration`. Use them only as applied to India.
  - Defining climatic, language and religious regions in general is `geo-region-concept`.
  - The Mumbai–Ahmedabad and Ganges examples should not be reused as Irish or European examples.
- Don't add attractions, firms, dams or cities not in section 2, even well-known ones.

## 4. What exams ask (topics only)

- **Sketch/outline map** (Higher 20 marks, Ordinary 30 marks, almost every year): the outline of the region, a named relief feature (shown as an outline), the course of a named river, one or two named urban centres, and sometimes the outline of a named agricultural or industrial region. Marks for an accurate outline, for showing each feature and for naming it.
- **Agriculture / primary activity** (Higher 30): the factors influencing agriculture or primary activity (climate and the monsoon, relief and soils, irrigation, markets, the Green Revolution, holding size, government policy). Ordinary: name two types of agriculture or one primary activity, its advantages and one problem.
- **Secondary activity** (Higher 30, frequent): two factors that influenced manufacturing (raw materials, markets, infrastructure, government policy, labour, transport). Ordinary: name a secondary activity, its advantages, one challenge; the development or importance of manufacturing.
- **Tertiary activity** (Higher 30): the development of one tertiary activity, tourism or transport, or two factors influencing tourism. Ordinary: two types of transport and its importance; two named tourist attractions and why tourists come; positive and negative impacts of tourism.
- **Population distribution** (Higher 30, frequent): account for the distribution of population, with dense and sparse areas explained by physical and human factors.
- **Urban area** (Higher and Ordinary 30): factors that influenced the development of, or reasons for the growth of, one urban area in the region (Mumbai is the expected case study).
- **Human processes** (Higher 30): the impact of two of population dynamics, language, religion, urban development and rural development on the region's development.
- **Culture** (Ordinary 30, any region): the importance of religion, language and other cultural factors; India's religion and language material serves.
