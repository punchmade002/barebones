# Package: geo-mapwork-s3 — The Geographical Investigation (20%)

- **loId:** `geo-mapwork-s3` · **chapterId:** `geo-mapwork` · **syllabusRef:** 3 (Core Unit 3)
- **Syllabus (Core Unit 3 and the Syllabus Structure section):** "Students must complete a
  geographical investigation from an annual list of topics issued to schools by the State
  Examinations Commission in Leaving Certificate Year 1. A report on the completed geographical
  investigation will be submitted for assessment at the end of term one in Leaving Certificate
  Year 2. While it is recognised that class groups may work on one investigation, each candidate
  must present a full and complete report for assessment purposes. No group projects will be
  accepted. Primary and secondary sources of information should be used in the investigation to
  a ratio of 60% primary sources and 40% secondary sources. ... The geography teacher and the
  school principal will verify the authenticity of the investigation report as being that of
  each individual Leaving Certificate candidate." The report carries "an assessment weighting of
  20%" and is "assessed outside of the terminal written examination". In completing it,
  students will "devise a strategy and identify aims, objectives, and hypotheses ...; select
  methods of collection and gathering appropriate to the investigation topic; use appropriate
  methods of gathering and collection of information; prepare a report; analyse and interpret
  results and draw valid conclusions." Higher Level outcomes add: "use statistical analysis and
  information technology in the interpretation and analysis of results and conclusions" and
  "analyse and evaluate their work, and make comparisons with other studies". The five stages
  and their activities are in the table on syllabus page 18 (quoted in section 1).
- **This is a skills subsection.** It teaches the stages, what the marker looks for, and good
  practice. Every example uses the **invented river study of the River Dallow at Rivertown**
  (the invented setting of `geo-mapwork-s1`), labelled "illustrative". Name no real SEC topic
  list, school, river or past report.
- **Prepared by:** a higher model, 2026-10-20. The writer works only from this package and
  `course-outlines/NOTES_STANDARD.md`.

## 1. Must cover (one bullet or block for every item; tick each off)

The rules
- [ ] R1 What it is: an individual field-based investigation of a geographical question, written up as a report; compulsory at Higher and Ordinary level; worth 20% of the total mark, assessed outside the written exam.
- [ ] R2 Topic: chosen from the list of topics the State Examinations Commission (SEC) issues to schools in Leaving Certificate Year 1. Typical topic areas are physical (rivers, coasts, glacial or karst landscapes, weather, soils, rocks) and human (urban land use, traffic, settlement, economic activity), but only the year's own list applies.
- [ ] R3 Deadline: the syllabus sets submission at the end of term one in Year 2; the SEC sets the exact date each year, so students check it.
- [ ] R4 Individual work: a class may work on one investigation and collect data together, but each student writes a full, complete report of their own. No group projects.
- [ ] R5 Sources: about 60% primary (first-hand fieldwork data) and 40% secondary (published or previously collected information).
- [ ] R6 Authenticity: the teacher and the school principal verify that the report is the candidate's own work. Copied or shared write-ups are not acceptable.
- [ ] R7 The report is written in the official SEC report booklet, which sets the section headings, gives limited space for each, and states the marks available. Follow the booklet's headings and space limits for the year exactly.

Stage 1: Introduction (posing the problem and devising a strategy)
- [ ] I1 Syllabus activities: "the selection of a topic for investigation; a clear statement of hypothesis or aim; an outline of the objectives; identification of the types of information required".
- [ ] I2 Hypothesis: a precise, testable statement that the data can prove or disprove (e.g. a measurable claim about change with distance downstream). Aim: a statement of what the investigation sets out to find out. Use one or the other as the booklet asks.
- [ ] I3 Objectives: three to five specific, measurable tasks that together test the hypothesis (each names what will be measured and where). Each objective later gets results.
- [ ] I4 Types of information required: primary (measurements, observations, counts, questionnaire answers) and secondary (maps, statistics, texts), stated in relation to each objective.
- [ ] I5 Good practice: keep the hypothesis narrow enough to test in a day or two of fieldwork; avoid vague words ("better", "nice"); include the place studied.

Stage 2: Planning (preparing the work)
- [ ] P1 Syllabus activities: "the selection of methods for the collection and gathering of information; the design of a questionnaire or recording sheets; decisions on locations for the investigation".
- [ ] P2 Pre-fieldwork planning: study OS maps and aerial photographs of the area; choose and justify sites (e.g. evenly spaced along a river, sample points across a town); choose the date and time; gather equipment; divide tasks within the class group; arrange permission from landowners where needed.
- [ ] P3 Recording sheets: a table prepared in advance with columns for site, time, each measurement and repeat readings, so data are recorded consistently.
- [ ] P4 Questionnaire design (human topics): short; closed questions (tick boxes, yes/no, rating scales) for easy analysis plus a few open questions; neutral, unbiased wording; no unnecessary personal questions; a pilot test with a few people first; a sampling plan (who, where, when, how many).
- [ ] P5 Safety and ethics: a risk assessment; never work alone; supervision near water or traffic; suitable clothing and footwear; check the weather and river level; respect property and the environment; questionnaires are anonymous and voluntary.
- [ ] P6 Good practice: explain why each method suits its objective; describe the method clearly enough for someone else to repeat it.

Stage 3: Collection of data (gathering of information)
- [ ] C1 Syllabus activities: "the use of instruments to make measurements; records of observations made in the field; the use of questionnaires and surveys as appropriate; the use of a variety of secondary sources, e.g. documentary sources; a discussion of the problems encountered".
- [ ] C2 Instruments and how each is used (one bullet each, said once): measuring tape or ranging poles (channel width, beach width, distances); metre stick (depth at regular intervals across a channel); float and stopwatch, or a flow meter (velocity: time a float over a measured distance, repeat and average); clinometer (slope angle of a beach or valley side); callipers or ruler (pebble size) and a roundness chart (pebble shape); compass (orientation, aspect, wind direction); thermometer, rain gauge, anemometer, wind vane (weather); a camera (photographic record, dated and located); GPS or a smartphone mapping app (locating sites).
- [ ] C3 Observations in the field: field sketches with labels; land-use mapping on a base map; traffic or pedestrian counts at set times; environmental quality surveys (scored criteria).
- [ ] C4 Secondary sources: OS maps and aerial photographs, census data, weather records, local authority plans, textbooks and websites (see `geo-mapwork-s2` for evaluating them). Record full details for acknowledgement.
- [ ] C5 Accuracy: take repeat readings and average them; use the same method and the same people at every site; record units; note conditions (weather, time, river level).
- [ ] C6 Problems encountered and how they were overcome (the report must discuss them): bad weather or high water making measurement unsafe; equipment faults; a float caught in vegetation; people refusing questionnaires; restricted access to a site; time limits. For each: the problem, its effect on the data, the solution.

Stage 4: Preparation of the report (results)
- [ ] W1 Syllabus activities: "the organisation of data; the use of illustrations, graphs, maps, and tables; the use of ICT, where appropriate, to prepare and present results and conclusions".
- [ ] W2 Organise results objective by objective: for each, state the finding in words with the key figures, and support it with an illustration.
- [ ] W3 Illustrations: tables of raw and averaged data; the right graph for the data (line graph for change with distance or time, bar chart for comparison between sites, pie chart for shares, scatter graph for a relationship, channel cross-sections for river profiles); sketch maps locating sites; annotated photographs; field sketches. Every illustration titled, labelled, with units and a key, and referred to in the text. How to draw graphs is in `geo-graph-skills-s1`.
- [ ] W4 Statistical analysis (Higher outcome): averages (mean, median), range, percentages, simple calculations such as cross-sectional area (width × average depth) and discharge (cross-sectional area × velocity); spreadsheets or graphing software (ICT) to calculate and plot.
- [ ] W5 Good practice: describe trends with figures ("velocity rose from site 1 to site 5"), note anomalies and suggest causes; keep results separate from conclusions.

Stage 5: Conclusion and evaluation
- [ ] V1 Syllabus activities: "analysis and interpretation of results; the drawing of valid conclusions; the comparison of findings with established theory; the evaluation of hypotheses; the examination of the validity of the investigation and suggestions for improvements".
- [ ] V2 Conclusions: state whether the hypothesis is accepted, rejected or partly accepted, using the evidence from each objective; explain the findings geographically (why the pattern occurs).
- [ ] V3 Comparison with established theory or other studies: compare the results with the textbook model or published work (e.g. a river study compared with the Bradshaw model; an urban land-use study compared with the Burgess concentric zone model); explain any differences (local geology, human interference, a small sample).
- [ ] V4 Evaluation (validity): how reliable and accurate the data are; limits (one day's data, few sites, small questionnaire sample, measurement error, weather on the day); what went well.
- [ ] V5 Improvements: more sites, repeat visits in different seasons or conditions, better equipment, larger samples, a longer stretch of river; what further investigation the findings suggest.
- [ ] V6 Skills gained (optional, one bullet): teamwork, fieldwork skills, ICT, report writing, which the syllabus links to "working conditions similar to those likely to be encountered in the world of work".

How the report is marked
- [ ] M1 Marking is by significant relevant points (SRPs) in each section of the booklet, plus marks for illustrations; the booklet shows the marks available for each section.
- [ ] M2 What earns marks: a clear testable hypothesis or aim with matching objectives; methods described step by step with the equipment named and justified; data presented accurately with varied, well-labelled illustrations; results that refer to the actual figures; conclusions that answer the hypothesis and are supported by the results; comparison with theory; an honest evaluation with specific improvements.
- [ ] M3 What loses marks: vague or untestable hypotheses; methods listed without explanation; results without figures; illustrations that are unlabelled, irrelevant or not referred to; conclusions not linked to results; generic evaluations ("it went well"); writing outside the space provided; material copied from others or the internet.

### Worked-example specs (all invented; the writer must say "illustrative" each time)

The example investigation: **a river study of the invented River Dallow upstream of Rivertown**.

- **W1 Introduction (I1–I5).** Hypothesis: "The velocity of the River Dallow increases
  downstream." Objectives: measure channel width, average depth and velocity at five sites
  500 m apart; calculate cross-sectional area and discharge at each site; measure the size of
  ten pebbles at each site. Information required: primary measurements; secondary OS map and
  aerial photograph of the river.
- **W2 Planning (P1–P6).** Sites chosen on the OS map where the river is accessible and safe;
  a recording sheet with columns: site, width, depths at 5 points, three float times over 10 m,
  pebble lengths; equipment list; risk assessment (low river level, wellingtons, groups of four).
- **W3 Data (C2, C5, W4).** Illustrative values at site 1 and site 5: width 3.0 m and 6.0 m;
  average depth 0.20 m and 0.40 m; average float time over 10 m 25 s and 10 s, so velocity
  0.4 m/s and 1.0 m/s; cross-sectional area 0.6 m² and 2.4 m²; discharge 0.24 m³/s and
  2.4 m³/s. (Check: 3.0 × 0.20 = 0.6; 0.6 × 0.4 = 0.24; 6.0 × 0.40 = 2.4; 2.4 × 1.0 = 2.4.)
- **W4 Problem encountered (C6).** At site 3 the float snagged on reeds twice; the group
  discarded those runs and took three clean timings.
- **W5 Results and illustrations (W2–W3).** A line graph of velocity against distance downstream;
  channel cross-sections drawn to scale for sites 1 and 5; a sketch map of the five sites.
- **W6 Conclusion and evaluation (V2–V5).** Hypothesis accepted: velocity rose from 0.4 m/s to
  1.0 m/s; this matches the Bradshaw model, explained by greater depth reducing friction and
  more water from tributaries. Evaluation: one day's data after dry weather; float method
  measures surface velocity only; improvements: a flow meter, more sites, repeat visits after
  rain.

## 3. Traps

- No real SEC topic lists, real schools, real rivers or real past reports. Use only the
  invented River Dallow study.
- Do not state section mark allocations, word or page limits, or an exact submission date:
  they are set in the SEC booklet and notices for each year, and they change. Say "check the
  booklet".
- A hypothesis is a testable statement, not a question or a topic title.
- The 60:40 ratio is primary to secondary, not the reverse.
- Group data collection is allowed; a group report is not.
- Graph-drawing technique is in `geo-graph-skills-s1`; evaluating secondary sources is in
  `geo-mapwork-s2`; OS map skills for choosing sites are in `geo-mapwork-s1`. Refer, don't
  repeat.
- River landforms and processes are taught in `geo-rivers`; mention them only as background to
  the example.
- Velocity measured with a surface float overestimates average velocity; say so as a limitation,
  don't present float results as exact.
- Do not tell students the investigation appears on the written paper: it is assessed only
  through the report.

## 4. What exams ask (topics only)

- The written exam has no Geographical Investigation question. The investigation is assessed
  only through the report (20%), marked in the sections of the SEC booklet: introduction
  (hypothesis or aim, objectives), planning, gathering of information, results with
  illustrations, conclusions, and evaluation.
- Marks reward specific, relevant points in each section, accurate and varied illustrations,
  conclusions that test the hypothesis against the results and theory, and an evaluation with
  concrete improvements.

## 5. Practice questions to write

1. 10 marks: "State a hypothesis and three objectives for an investigation of a river." Model
   answer from W1 (hypothesis plus three measurable objectives), labelled illustrative.
2. 15 marks: "Describe how you would measure the velocity and discharge of a river, and name
   one problem you might encounter and how you would overcome it." Model answer: float method
   step by step (measured distance, three timings, average, velocity = distance ÷ time),
   discharge from width, average depth and velocity with the W3 site 5 calculation, and the W4
   problem and solution.
3. 15 marks: "Write a conclusion and evaluation for the River Dallow investigation using the
   results given." Model answer from W6: hypothesis decision with figures, comparison with the
   Bradshaw model, two limitations, three improvements.
