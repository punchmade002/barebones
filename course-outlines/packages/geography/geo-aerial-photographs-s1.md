# Package: geo-aerial-photographs-s1 — Aerial photograph interpretation

- **loId:** `geo-aerial-photographs-s1` · **chapterId:** `geo-aerial-photographs` · **syllabusRef:** 3 (Skills)
- **Syllabus:** Higher Level, "1. Map and aerial photograph interpretation. Students should use
  and apply the spatial concepts of co-ordinate systems (latitude and longitude); grid
  references; scale, distance, and direction; altitude and slope; sketch maps; symbol
  recognition; cross-sections; pattern recognition; statistical mapping; absolute and relative
  location using maps and aerial photographs." Ordinary Level lists the same skills without

## 1. Must cover (one bullet or block for every item; tick each off)

Types of aerial photograph
- [ ] T1 Vertical aerial photograph: taken with the camera pointing straight down; shows only roofs and the tops of features; no horizon; scale is roughly constant across the photo, so it looks like a map and can be used for measuring and mapping; features are harder to recognise because their sides and heights are hidden.
- [ ] T2 Oblique aerial photograph: taken with the camera at an angle to the ground; sides as well as roofs of buildings are visible; scale is not constant (foreground features look larger than identical features in the background); the area covered widens towards the background; features are easier to recognise; distance and area cannot be measured directly from it.
- [ ] T3 Low oblique (horizon not shown) vs high oblique (horizon or skyline shown). One bullet.
- [ ] T4 How to tell which type you have (the 2-mark question): sides of buildings visible, foreground larger than background, depth or horizon → oblique; only roofs, even scale, map-like → vertical.

Locating features (relative location)
- [ ] L1 The nine-area grid for an oblique photograph: three strips across (left, centre, right) and three strips into the picture (foreground at the bottom nearest the camera, middleground, background at the top furthest away).
- [ ] L2 Accepted notation: one term from each, across-term first, e.g. "left foreground", "centre middleground", "right background". Both halves must be right to get the marks. Spell it as the exam does: foreground, middleground, background.
- [ ] L3 Vertical photographs: foreground and background have no meaning; use top/centre/bottom with left/centre/right, or compass directions (north-east, south-west, etc.) when the orientation is known.
- [ ] L4 Method for exam tasks using the grid: draw the grid lightly on the photo in pencil (or fold mentally into thirds); find the feature; state the zone. Applies to: locating labelled features; true/false statements about what is in a zone; matching cropped sections of the photo to zones.
- [ ] L5 Matching a second source to the photo: a street map, satellite image or ground-level street-view images showing features A–E. Method: (a) the second source may be oriented differently from the photo, so first find two features common to both (the river, a bridge, the railway, a large church) and turn the map mentally to match; (b) identify each feature by distinctive clues (spire, cruciform roof, pitch markings, large flat roof and car park, bridge parapet, shopfronts); (c) give its zone in accepted notation.
- [ ] L6 Recognition clues for common features (one short clue each): church (spire or tower, cross-shaped plan, graveyard beside it); graveyard (rows of small headstones); school (large building, playground, pitches, car park); shopping centre (very large flat roof, extensive car park); factory or warehouse (large flat or sawtooth roof, no windows or gardens, yards, lorries); housing estate (rows of similar houses with gardens, cul-de-sacs); terraced, semi-detached and detached housing (by how houses are joined); apartments (large multi-storey block, communal parking); car park (marked bays, vehicles); sports pitch (rectangle with lines, goalposts); running track (oval with lanes); railway (gentle curves, no cars, crosses roads by bridges); river and lake (smooth, dark water, natural outline); bridge; harbour, pier, quay; roundabout; waste or derelict ground (rough, unused, no clear use).

Using the photograph with the OS map (absolute location and direction). Map-only skills are taught in `geo-mapwork`; use them here, don't re-teach them.
- [ ] M1 Absolute vs relative location: absolute location is a grid reference read from the OS map; relative location is the nine-area notation, or a position relative to other features ("beside the bridge, north of the river").
- [ ] M2 Direction the camera was pointing: identify a feature in the foreground and one in the background, find both on the OS map; the direction from the foreground feature to the background feature is the direction the camera was pointing. Use eight-point compass terms (e.g. north-east). Check with a third feature.
- [ ] M3 Grid reference of a feature seen on the photo: find it on the photo, use its position relative to roads, the river and junctions to find it on the map, then give a four- or six-figure grid reference as taught in `geo-mapwork`. On a 1:50 000 map individual buildings are often not drawn, so locate by the surrounding road pattern; churches are shown by map symbols.
- [ ] M4 Naming a road or river seen on the photo: trace it on the photo, find the same route on the map, read its number (N, R, M) or name from the map.
- [ ] M5 Comparing photo and map: what the photo shows that the map does not (individual buildings, land use type, crops and field use, building height and age, car parks, traffic, temporary features, recent developments); what the map shows that the photo does not (names, road numbers, height and contours, grid, antiquities labelled, boundaries, features hidden by trees or beyond the frame); the map shows the whole area at constant scale, an oblique photo shows a partial view with changing scale; the two were usually made at different dates, which reveals change.
- [ ] M6 Scale on photographs. Oblique: no single scale, so measure distances on the map instead. Vertical: scale = photo distance ÷ real ground distance, where the ground distance is found from the map (worked example W6). One bullet each; the map-scale method itself is in `geo-mapwork`.

Sketch maps
- [ ] S1 Rules: draw a frame with four ruled sides, the same shape as the photograph; the exam usually asks for "half the length and half the breadth" (each side halved, so a quarter of the area); measure the photo and calculate the sketch size; never trace.
- [ ] S2 Divide the photo and the frame into the nine-area grid lightly in pencil to place features accurately.
- [ ] S3 Show only the features asked for, in approximately the correct position, shape and proportion: rivers, roads and railways as lines (the river as a broad double line), areas (housing, industry, farmland, car parks, lakes) as closed outlined shapes, single buildings as small blocks or symbols.
- [ ] S4 Label every feature (a label on or beside it, or a key); a feature drawn but not labelled scores nothing for being shown. Give a title. A feature specified with a position (e.g. "car park in the centre foreground") must be drawn in that zone.
- [ ] S5 Common requests to practise: river, coastline, lake, bridge, railway, two connecting streets (two streets drawn meeting at a junction), car park, church, ecclesiastical land use, housing estate, residential land use, industrial building or estate, warehouse, agricultural land, recreational land use, sports ground, waste ground, a historic ruin, CBD.

Land use and urban functions
- [ ] U1 Land-use categories to recognise: residential, commercial/retail, industrial, ecclesiastical (religious), educational, recreational, transport, agricultural, open space, waste/derelict ground. Ecclesiastical land use = land used for religious purposes (church, its grounds, graveyard).
- [ ] U2 Residential types and density: older terraced housing near the centre (high density, small or no gardens); suburban housing estates of semi-detached and detached houses further out (lower density, gardens, cul-de-sacs); apartments (high density). Density usually falls outward from the centre.
- [ ] U3 Identifying the CBD (central business district): at the centre where roads converge; tallest and most densely packed buildings; no gardens; shops with frontage onto main streets; car parks and heavy traffic; often a square or main street.
- [ ] U4 Industrial land use: usually on the edge of town; large flat-roofed buildings in an industrial estate; near main roads, a bypass, railway or port; space for parking and lorries; older industry (mills) may be by the river or quays.
- [ ] U5 Agricultural land use around the town: field patterns with hedgerows; pasture (grass, livestock) vs tillage (ploughed or cropped fields); silage or hay bales.
- [ ] U6 Urban functions: a function is a purpose a town serves. Functions visible on photos: residential, commercial/retail, industrial/manufacturing, ecclesiastical/religious, educational, recreational/leisure, medical (hospital), transport (bridge, railway station, roads converging, harbour), port/fishing (piers, boats), tourism (hotels, caravan parks, marinas, beaches, historic sites), market (square, market house), administrative (courthouse, council offices, Garda station; often hard to confirm from the photo alone), defensive (historic: castle, town walls).
- [ ] U7 Answer formula for "describe and explain functions using evidence from the photograph": name the function; give the photo evidence; give its location in accepted notation; explain why the town performs it (its site, situation, size, hinterland). Several pieces of evidence per function.

Settlement and historical features
- [ ] H1 Evidence of the town's history on a photo: a castle, tower house or other ruin; an old church or abbey ruin and old graveyard; town walls or gates; a market square, fair green or market house; old narrow streets; old stone bridges; quays and warehouses on the river; mills and weirs; a railway line and station (19th-century growth); modern housing estates and industrial estates on the edge (recent growth).
- [ ] H2 Planned vs unplanned towns: wide, straight streets, a regular grid, a central square or diamond and uniform terraces indicate a planned town; narrow, winding, irregular streets indicate unplanned growth (often of medieval origin). Wide streets do not indicate an unplanned town.
- [ ] H3 Reasons for the town's location seen from the photo: bridging point, river or coastal site, routes converging, flat land, a harbour, surrounding farmland (market town), defensive site. Explaining the town's development from the OS map is in `geo-mapwork`; here use photo evidence only, linked to the map where the question allows.
- [ ] H4 Street pattern: linear growth along a main road; nucleated growth around a centre or junction; grid; streets radiating from a bridge or square. Pattern recognition is a syllabus skill.

Traffic management
- [ ] R1 Causes of traffic congestion visible on a photo: narrow streets in the old core; on-street parking narrowing roads; routes converging on one bridge or junction; through-traffic passing the town centre; delivery vehicles; limited off-street parking; schools and shopping centres generating peak traffic.
- [ ] R2 Traffic management measures visible on a photo: roundabouts; traffic lights; pedestrian crossings; one-way streets (all parked cars facing one way, arrows on the road); double yellow lines (no parking); bus lanes; cycle lanes; off-street and multi-storey car parks; pedestrianised streets; bypasses and relief roads; ring roads; bollards and traffic-calming ramps.
- [ ] R3 Answer formula: for each measure, name it, give its location (notation), explain how it reduces congestion or improves safety. For a problem, name it, locate it, explain why it causes congestion.
- [ ] R4 Suggesting a solution for a congested area on the photo: e.g. a bypass to remove through-traffic, a new car park on waste ground, a one-way system, pedestrianisation. Justify with photo evidence.

Change over time and site suitability
- [ ] C1 Land-use change: comparing the map with the photo (they are dated differently), or comparing locations on the photo with a list of changes such as "agricultural land to housing", "industrial to residential", "green space to recreation", "open space to residential", "farmland to relief road", "green space to waste ground". Method: find each location, identify the present use, infer the earlier use from the map or from surrounding clues (new estates on the edge with fields beyond, cleared sites, new roads cutting across field patterns).
- [ ] C2 Urban growth and sprawl evidence: new estates on the edge, ribbon development along approach roads, industrial estates and retail parks on the outskirts, new roundabouts and bypasses. Link only; urban sprawl as a GIS study belongs to `geo-weather-climate`.
- [ ] C3 Site suitability: choose a location on the photo for a proposed development (e.g. a shopping centre, factory, car park, playground) and justify it: available flat land, access to main roads, near the population it serves, space for parking, not on a flood-prone river bank, away from housing (for industry). Or explain why a given site is unsuitable. Give 3 reasons, each with photo evidence.

Time of year and time of day
- [ ] D1 Time of year evidence: deciduous trees in full leaf (summer) or bare (winter); crops growing or ripening, golden fields, harvested stubble (late summer to autumn); ploughed fields (autumn or spring); silage or hay bales in fields (summer); cattle grazing outdoors (spring to autumn; Irish cattle are usually housed in winter); people in light clothing; outdoor activity on beaches or pitches. Give two or three pieces of evidence.
- [ ] D2 Time of day evidence: shadows point away from the sun. In Ireland the midday sun is in the south, so midday shadows point north and are at their shortest; morning sun is in the east, so shadows point west; evening sun is in the west, so shadows point east. Long shadows = sun low (early or late in the day, or winter). Use the camera direction (M2) to turn "shadows fall to the left" into a compass direction. Other clues: busy streets and full car parks (business hours), school car parks full or empty, rush-hour traffic, street lights on.

### Worked-example specs (all invented; the writer must say "illustrative" each time)

Invented setting for every worked example: **"Rivertown"**, an invented market town on a river,
shown on an invented oblique photograph. Do not attach a real county or grid square to it.
The writer describes it in one sentence: a river flows from the left foreground to the right
background; a stone bridge in the centre middleground; a church with a spire in the left
middleground with a graveyard beside it; a main street and square in the centre middleground;
an industrial estate in the right foreground; a housing estate of semi-detached houses in the
left background; fields with hedgerows in the right background; a GAA pitch in the centre
background; waste ground in the centre foreground.

- **W1 Location table (L1–L5).** Five features with their zones from the Rivertown description:
  bridge = centre middleground; church = left middleground; industrial estate = right
  foreground; housing estate = left background; pitch = centre background. Then one true/false
  item: "There is waste ground in the centre foreground" = true.
- **W2 Camera direction (M2).** Illustrative: on the map, Rivertown's bridge (middleground) lies
  due south of the GAA pitch (background), and the waste ground (foreground) lies south of the
  bridge. Foreground → background runs south to north, so the camera was pointing north.
- **W3 Sketch map (S1–S4).** Illustrative photo size 22 cm × 16 cm → sketch frame 11 cm × 8 cm.
  Requested: the river, the bridge, a church, an industrial area in the foreground, agricultural
  land in the background. Describe how each is drawn and labelled and that the industrial area
  must sit in the foreground zone.
- **W4 Time of day (D2).** Illustrative: the camera points north (from W2), so left = west and
  right = east. Shadows of buildings fall towards the left, i.e. west, so the sun is in the
  east: morning. The shadows are long, so early morning. Add one time-of-year clue: trees in
  full leaf and silage bales in the right background fields → summer.
- **W5 Functions (U6–U7).** Three Rivertown functions written to the formula: ecclesiastical
  (church with spire and graveyard, left middleground); industrial (large flat-roofed units and
  lorry yards, right foreground, beside the main road); market/commercial (square and main
  street of shops, centre middleground, roads converging at the bridge). Each with 3–4 SRPs.
- **W6 Vertical photo scale (M6).** Illustrative: two road junctions are 5 cm apart on a vertical
  photograph and 2 cm apart on the 1:50 000 map. 2 cm on the map = 1 km = 100 000 cm on the
  ground. Photo scale = 5 : 100 000 = 1 : 20 000.
- **W7 Traffic (R1–R4).** One Rivertown problem (all routes converge on the single bridge in the
  centre middleground, with on-street parking on the main street) and two measures visible (a
  roundabout at the end of the bridge; a car park beside the square), then one suggested
  solution (a relief road crossing the river further downstream, using the open fields).
- **W8 Land-use change (C1).** Illustrative: the map shows fields where the photo shows the
  left-background housing estate → "agricultural to residential". Give one more pair.

## 3. Traps

- **Never use real exam towns or their features** (e.g. do not write about any town whose aerial
  photograph has been set on a past paper, and give no real grid references, road numbers or
  camera directions). The past questions use real towns; the notes must not.
- Don't copy past-paper marking-scheme figures, photo dimensions or answer keys. Use the invented
  values above.
- Foreground is the bottom of the photo, nearest the camera; background is the top. Students
  commonly reverse them.
- Order: across-term first ("left middleground"), not "middleground left". Use "centre", not
  "middle", for the across strip.
- The direction the camera was **pointing** is foreground → background, not the reverse.
- Oblique photos have no constant scale; don't measure distances or areas on them.
- Wide, regular streets indicate a **planned** town, not an unplanned one.
- A sketch map is not a tracing and must not include features that weren't asked for; unlabelled
  features earn nothing for being shown.
- "Ecclesiastical" means religious (churches, graveyards), not educational.
- Don't re-teach grid references, scale and distance on maps, gradient, cross-sections or OS
  symbols: they are in `geo-mapwork-s1`. Refer to them in one clause.
- Explaining why a town developed from OS map evidence alone, and settlement patterns on maps,
  belong to `geo-mapwork-s1`. Urban theory (land-use models, urban sprawl as an issue, urban
  planning case studies, traffic congestion case studies in named cities) belongs to `geo-urban`.
  Here, traffic and land use are read from the photograph only; name no city.
- Satellite imagery and the GIS studies (including aerial photographs in the study of urban
  sprawl, forestry and archaeological sites) belong to `geo-weather-climate`. Leave them out.
- Seasons and shadows: give evidence-based reasoning, not exact months or clock times.

## 4. What exams ask (topics only)

- 2 marks: is the photograph vertical or oblique (the photographs set in recent papers have been
  oblique; when a reason is asked, justify with the clues in T4).
- True/false: whether a named feature (church, bridge, car park, housing estate, graveyard,
  lake, woodland, industrial area, pitch, roundabout, CBD, bridging point) is in a given zone.
- Locating features with accepted notation: labelled features on a street map or satellite image
  with a different orientation; ground-level street-view images; cropped sections of the photo.
- Combined photo + OS map (Higher): direction the camera was pointing; six- or four-figure grid
  reference of a feature seen on the photo; the number of a road or name of a river seen on the
  photo.
- Planned vs unplanned town from the street pattern.
- Matching locations on the photo to land-use changes.
- Sketch map (Higher 20 marks, half length and half breadth; Ordinary 30 marks, about half size)
  showing 4–5 named features, some with specified zones: river, coastline, lake, bridge, railway,
  two connecting streets, church/ecclesiastical land use, car park, housing estate, residential
  land use in a zone, industrial buildings or warehouse in a zone, agricultural land in a zone,
  recreational land use, sports ground, waste ground, historic ruin, emergency services depot,
  CBD, semi-detached housing in a zone.
- Longer Higher-level skills questions in the textbook tradition: urban functions with photo
  evidence; traffic problems and management; evidence of historical development; time of year;
  site suitability for a development. Expected answers follow the formulas U7 and R3.

## 5. Practice questions to write

1. 20 marks, Higher: "Examine the (illustrative) aerial photograph of Rivertown described above.
   Draw a sketch map of it, half the length and half the breadth. On it show and label: the
   river; a bridge; a church; an industrial area in the foreground; agricultural land use in the
   background." Model answer: the method only, applied to the Rivertown description (frame size
   from W3, grid, how each feature is drawn and labelled, position rule for the two zoned
   features). Real photos cannot be supplied, so say the question uses the invented photo.
2. 30 marks, Higher: "Using evidence from the (illustrative) aerial photograph of Rivertown,
   describe and explain three functions of the town." Model answer: exactly three functions from
   W5, each named, located with accepted notation, evidenced and explained; about 15 SRPs. No
   real town.
3. 8 marks: four 2-mark parts on Rivertown: (a) vertical or oblique, with one reason; (b) the
   location of the church in accepted notation; (c) the direction the camera was pointing, using
   the W2 map information; (d) true/false "Wide, straight streets around the square suggest
   Rivertown is an unplanned town" (false, with a one-line reason).

Each model answer uses only the invented Rivertown details, labelled illustrative.
