## 1. Must cover (one bullet or block for every item; tick each off)

The immediate environment: factors of soil formation (one block; "Various soil patterns have developed because of different processes" questions use it)
- [ ] E1 Parent material: supplies mineral matter, sets texture, base content and starting pH (detail in s1; one bullet here).
- [ ] E2 Climate: the most important factor at a global scale. Temperature controls the speed of weathering and decomposition; the balance of precipitation and evapotranspiration decides whether water moves down (leaching) or up (capillary rise). This is why soil belts follow climate belts.
- [ ] E3 Relief (topography): steep slopes have thin, well-drained soils because material moves downslope; hollows and valley floors collect water and deep, wet soils. A catena is the sequence of soils down a slope from the same parent material. Altitude brings cooler, wetter conditions (more leaching, peat). Aspect affects soil temperature and moisture.
- [ ] E4 Living organisms: vegetation supplies litter and decides humus type (deciduous → mull; coniferous and heath → mor); roots bind soil and recycle nutrients; animals mix soil; humans change soils (s3).
- [ ] E5 Time: a mature soil has had long enough to develop distinct horizons; an immature (young) soil has thin or missing horizons.

Soil profile and horizons
- [ ] H1 Horizons: O (litter and humus; may be split into L, F and H layers), A (dark topsoil, humus mixed with minerals), E (eluvial: pale, washed-out layer found in leached soils such as podzols), B (illuvial: subsoil where washed-down iron, aluminium, clay or humus accumulate), C (weathered parent material), R (bedrock).
- [ ] H2 Eluviation (washing out of material from an upper horizon) and illuviation (its deposition in a lower one). Diagram cue: a labelled profile with depth and horizon letters.

Soil processes (each: conditions where it happens, step-by-step what happens, effect on soil characteristics, resulting soil type)
- [ ] P1 Weathering: physical and chemical weathering break down parent material into mineral particles and release nutrients; hydrolysis forms clay; oxidation gives red and brown colours. Its rate sets soil depth: deep soils in the hot, wet tropics where chemical weathering is fastest; thin soils in cold or dry regions. One block; processes themselves are in `geo-rocks-weathering-s3`.
- [ ] P2 Soil erosion: the removal of soil, especially topsoil, faster than it forms. By water: rain-splash, sheet wash, rills and gullies. By wind: deflation of dry, loose, bare soil. Natural (geological) erosion is slow and balanced by formation; accelerated erosion follows removal of vegetation. Effects on characteristics: a thinner profile, loss of the humus-rich A horizon and its nutrients, exposure of infertile subsoil, poorer structure and water retention; the eroded material is redeposited elsewhere (alluvium, loess). Human causes and responses are in s3.
- [ ] P3 Leaching: where precipitation exceeds evapotranspiration, gravitational water moves down through the soil and dissolves soluble minerals and nutrient bases (calcium, magnesium, potassium, sodium), carrying them to lower horizons or into groundwater. Effects: the upper soil becomes more acidic and less fertile; material may be redeposited in the B horizon. Common in Ireland's wet climate; mild leaching gives brown earths, intense leaching gives podzols.
- [ ] P4 Humification: the decomposition of litter by bacteria, fungi and soil animals into humus. Fast in warm, moist, well-aerated, base-rich conditions (mull); slow in cold, acid or waterlogged conditions, where partly decomposed matter builds up as mor or peat. Releases nutrients for plants (part of the nutrient cycle). The syllabus misprints this as "humidification"; the notes use humification.
- [ ] P5 Podzolisation: an intense form of leaching. Conditions: cool, wet climate (precipitation well above evapotranspiration), acid parent material (granite, sandstone), coniferous forest or heath vegetation giving acid mor humus. Process: (1) acid litter releases organic acids into percolating water; (2) these acids combine with iron and aluminium (chelation, cheluviation) and carry them down along with bases and humus; (3) the A/E horizon is left as a bleached, ash-grey, sandy layer of quartz; (4) iron, aluminium and humus are deposited in the B horizon, which turns dark reddish-brown; (5) a thin, hard iron pan may form in the B horizon, blocking drainage and roots, so water lies above it and peat may form on top. Result: the podzol: acidic, infertile, with sharply defined horizons.
- [ ] P6 Laterisation: in hot, wet tropical climates. (1) High temperatures and heavy rain cause intense chemical weathering to great depth; (2) silica and bases are leached out (silica is soluble in warm water under these conditions); (3) insoluble iron and aluminium oxides (sesquioxides) are left behind and concentrated, giving a deep red or yellow colour; (4) litter decomposes so quickly that little humus accumulates; (5) where the forest is removed and the soil dries out, the iron-rich layer can harden into a brick-like crust called laterite. Extreme laterisation of aluminium-rich rock forms bauxite, the ore of aluminium. Result: latosols (oxisols).
- [ ] P7 Calcification: in dry continental climates where evapotranspiration roughly equals or exceeds precipitation (temperate grasslands and semi-arid areas). (1) Rain penetrates only part of the way, so leaching is weak; (2) calcium carbonate is dissolved and carried a short distance down, then deposited in the B horizon as nodules or a lime-rich layer; (3) in dry periods capillary action draws water and calcium back up; (4) deep grass roots take up calcium and return it to the surface in litter. Result: alkaline or neutral, base-rich, fertile soil with a thick humus-rich A horizon: the chernozem.
- [ ] P8 Gleying: in waterlogged soils (heavy clay, impermeable till, high water table, hollows, or above an iron pan). Pores fill with water, air is excluded, so conditions become anaerobic; bacteria reduce iron from its red ferric form to its grey or blue-grey ferrous form; where air enters along roots or cracks, iron is re-oxidised as orange or rusty mottles. Result: gley soil: grey, mottled, sticky, cold, slow to warm; decomposition is slow.
- [ ] P9 Salinisation: in hot, dry areas, evaporation draws water up by capillary action and leaves soluble salts at or near the surface, sometimes as a white crust; poor irrigation makes it worse (s3). One bullet here, linked to desert soils.

The global pattern of soils
- [ ] G1 Classification: zonal soils are mature soils whose character is set by climate and vegetation, so they form broad belts matching the world's climate and biome belts; intrazonal soils are dominated by one local factor such as drainage (gley, peat) or parent material (rendzina on limestone); azonal soils are immature, with poorly developed horizons (alluvium, scree, sand dunes, fresh volcanic ash, recent glacial deposits).
- [ ] G2 The zonal belts, roughly from pole to equator, each with its climate, vegetation, dominant process and named regions: tundra soils (thin, gleyed, permafrost below); podzols (boreal coniferous forest); brown earths (temperate deciduous forest); chernozems and chestnut soils (temperate grassland); red Mediterranean soils (terra rossa on limestone); aridisols, desert soils (hot desert); latosols (tropical rainforest). Diagram cue: a world map of soil belts, or a pole-to-equator transect.
- [ ] G3 Mountains: soils change with altitude as climate does (vertical zonation): thinner, more leached and peatier upslope.
- [ ] G4 Ireland's soil pattern: brown earths and grey-brown podzolics on the lowlands; podzols and peaty podzols on uplands; gleys on poorly drained clay till, especially the drumlin belt; peat (blanket bog in the west and on mountains, raised bog in the Midlands) as intrazonal soil; thin rendzinas on the limestone of the Burren.

## 3. Traps (known errors)

- The syllabus says "humidification"; the process is **humification** (formation of humus). Don't explain it as anything to do with humidity.
- Podzolisation deposits iron and aluminium **in the B horizon**, and the bleached layer is the **A/E** horizon. Don't reverse them.
- Laterisation removes **silica** and leaves iron and aluminium; podzolisation removes iron and aluminium. They are opposites in that respect.
- Latosols are infertile; chernozems are among the most fertile soils on earth. Don't say rainforest soils are rich.
- A laterite crust forms when the soil is **exposed and dries out**, not under intact forest.
- Brown earths have **no** bleached E horizon: earthworms mix them.
- Calcification is not the same as calcium carbonate dissolving in karst (carbonation, `geo-rocks-weathering-s3`).
- Don't give Irish soil-type percentages, national pH maps or horizon depths beyond the rough illustrative ranges in section 2.
- Human interference with soils (overgrazing, overcropping, desertification, conservation), the Burren's farming history, the Sahel and Iceland belong to `geo-option-geoecology-s3`. Here mention soil erosion as a process only.
- The full climate, vegetation and animals of the tropical rainforest and hot desert are in `geo-option-geoecology-s4`. Here give only what explains the soil.
- The four components and the characteristics (texture, structure, colour, pH) are taught in s1; use those terms without redefining them.

## 4. What exams ask (topics only)

- How any three named soil processes affect soil characteristics (weathering, soil erosion, leaching, podzolisation, calcification and laterisation have all been named): an 80-mark essay. Expected: each process as an aspect with conditions, mechanism, effects on colour, texture, pH, fertility and horizons, and a named soil.
- The general composition and characteristics of any one soil type (asked several times): a full description of one soil: location, formation, profile, composition and characteristics. Brown earth, podzol and latosol are the usual choices.
- "Various soil patterns have developed because of different soil-forming processes": the global pattern (G1–G2) linked to the processes, or Irish soil patterns.
