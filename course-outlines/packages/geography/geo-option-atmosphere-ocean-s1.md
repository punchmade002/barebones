## 1. Must cover (one bullet or block for every item; tick each off)

Linked systems
- [ ] L1 The atmosphere and the oceans form one linked system: they exchange heat (the ocean warms or cools the air above it), water (evaporation, precipitation), gases (the ocean absorbs and releases CO2 and oxygen) and momentum (wind drives surface currents; currents shift the pressure and wind patterns). Changes in one change the other (e.g. El Niño, s5). One block.
- [ ] L2 Water's high specific heat capacity: the ocean heats up and cools down far more slowly than land and stores huge amounts of heat; this is why maritime climates have small temperature ranges. The ocean has absorbed over 90% of the extra heat trapped by global warming.

Composition of the atmosphere
- [ ] A1 The permanent gases by volume of dry air: nitrogen about 78%, oxygen about 21%, argon about 0.9%, carbon dioxide about 0.04%, plus traces of neon, helium, methane, ozone and others.
- [ ] A2 Variable components: water vapour (from almost 0% over deserts and the poles to about 4% in hot, humid tropical air); aerosols (dust, sea salt, smoke, volcanic ash, pollen) that act as condensation nuclei and scatter sunlight; ozone, concentrated in the stratosphere.
- [ ] A3 Why the small gases matter: water vapour, CO2, methane and nitrous oxide are greenhouse gases that absorb long-wave radiation (detail in s2); ozone absorbs harmful ultraviolet radiation. The amount of CO2 is rising because of human activity (figures in section 2; the global-warming case study is in s5).
- [ ] A4 Gravity holds the atmosphere: it is densest at the surface and thins rapidly with height, so pressure falls with altitude; about half the mass of the atmosphere lies below about 5.5 km.

Structure of the atmosphere (each layer: height, temperature trend and why, key features). Diagram cue: a vertical profile with height in km, the four layers, the three pauses and the temperature line.
- [ ] S1 Troposphere: from the surface up to about 8 km over the poles and about 16–18 km over the equator (thicker where warm air expands and convection is strongest). Temperature falls with height at an average of about 6.5 °C per 1,000 m (the environmental lapse rate) because the air is heated from below by the ground. Contains most of the atmosphere's mass and nearly all its water vapour, so nearly all weather and clouds occur here. Top: the tropopause, where the temperature stops falling; jet streams blow near it.
- [ ] S2 Stratosphere: from the tropopause to about 50 km. Temperature rises with height because ozone absorbs ultraviolet radiation; the ozone layer is concentrated at about 15–35 km. Very dry and stable, so few clouds; cumulonimbus anvils spread out at its base; long-haul jets cruise near the tropopause to avoid turbulence. Top: the stratopause.
- [ ] S3 Mesosphere: about 50 km to about 80–85 km. Temperature falls again with height to the coldest point in the atmosphere, about −90 °C; most meteors burn up here. Top: the mesopause.
- [ ] S4 Thermosphere: above about 85 km. Very thin air; temperature rises sharply because the few molecules absorb high-energy solar radiation (but there are too few molecules to feel "hot"). Auroras (northern lights) occur here; the International Space Station orbits within it at about 400 km. The exosphere is the outermost fringe merging into space. One bullet for the exosphere.

Composition and structure of the oceans
- [ ] O1 Extent: the oceans cover about 71% of the earth's surface and hold about 97% of its water. Five oceans: Pacific (largest and deepest), Atlantic, Indian, Southern and Arctic. Average depth about 3.7 km; deepest point in the Mariana Trench (section 2).
- [ ] O2 Salinity: the dissolved salt content, about 35 parts per thousand (35 g of salt per kg of seawater) on average; mostly sodium chloride, with magnesium, sulphate, calcium and potassium. The salts come from weathered rocks carried by rivers and from volcanic gases and vents.
- [ ] O3 Salinity varies: higher where evaporation exceeds precipitation (subtropical oceans; nearly enclosed seas in hot, dry areas, e.g. the Red Sea and the Mediterranean); lower where rainfall is heavy (the equatorial belt), where large rivers enter, and where ice melts (polar seas; the Baltic Sea). Sea ice formation leaves salt behind, so the water beneath becomes saltier and denser (link to thermohaline circulation, s4).
- [ ] O4 Temperature layers (vertical structure): a warm, well-mixed surface layer (heated by the sun, stirred by wind; about the top 100–200 m in the tropics); the thermocline, where temperature falls rapidly with depth; the deep ocean, cold (about 0–4 °C) and dark, the largest part of the ocean by volume. In polar waters there is little difference between the surface and the deep. Diagram cue: temperature against depth for a tropical ocean.
- [ ] O5 Density: seawater is denser when it is colder and saltier. Differences in density make water sink or rise and drive the deep ocean circulation (s4). Surface temperature varies with latitude from about 28–30 °C in the tropics to about −2 °C (the freezing point of seawater) at the poles; ocean currents shift these patterns (s4).
- [ ] O6 Gases in the ocean: the ocean dissolves oxygen (from the air and from phytoplankton) and CO2. It has absorbed roughly a quarter of the CO2 released by human activity, slowing global warming but making the water more acidic (ocean acidification), which harms corals and shellfish.

Measuring the atmosphere–ocean system (each instrument: what it measures, how it works, units, how readings are taken; plus sources of error). Exams ask both "how measured" and "why it matters".
- [ ] M1 Observing networks: synoptic weather stations staffed or automatic, recording at fixed times (UTC) to World Meteorological Organization (WMO) standards so that readings can be compared worldwide; plus ships, buoys, aircraft, weather balloons, radar and satellites. Met Éireann collects Irish data (reuse `geo-weather-climate-s1`).
- [ ] M2 Temperature: liquid-in-glass thermometer (mercury or alcohol expands and rises up a narrow tube as it warms); maximum–minimum thermometer (Six's thermometer; small metal indexes are pushed up to the highest and lowest point and stay there until reset with a magnet; read once a day, then reset); electronic thermometers (sensors whose electrical resistance changes with temperature) in automatic stations; a thermograph gives a continuous trace. Thermometers are kept in a Stevenson screen: white to reflect sunlight, louvred to let air circulate, on legs above grass, door facing away from direct sun (north in Ireland), so they measure true shade air temperature. A grass minimum thermometer, laid just above short grass, measures ground frost. Derived values: daily mean, daily and annual range (calculation method in `geo-weather-climate-s1`).
- [ ] M3 Pressure: mercury barometer (the weight of the atmosphere supports a column of mercury in a glass tube; the column rises as pressure rises; invented by Torricelli, section 2); aneroid barometer (a sealed, partly emptied metal capsule that is squeezed in as pressure rises and expands as it falls; levers move a needle on a dial; portable, so used in homes and ships); barograph (an aneroid linked to a pen drawing a line on a rotating drum, giving a continuous record of rising or falling pressure: the trend matters most in forecasting); electronic pressure sensors in automatic stations. Readings are corrected to mean sea level so stations at different heights can be compared and isobars drawn. Units hPa (= mb); average about 1013 hPa.
- [ ] M4 Wind speed: cup anemometer (three or four cups on arms spin faster as wind speed rises; the turns are counted electrically and converted to speed); sonic anemometers (no moving parts) on some modern stations. Standard height about 10 m above open ground, away from buildings and trees. Units: km/h, knots or m/s; gusts are recorded separately from mean speed. Estimated by the Beaufort scale without instruments.
- [ ] M5 Wind direction: wind vane (the large tail is pushed downwind, so the pointer faces the direction the wind blows from), read against compass points or degrees; a windsock at airports. Wind is named from the direction it comes from.
- [ ] M6 Humidity: wet-and-dry bulb hygrometer (psychrometer): two thermometers side by side; the wet bulb is wrapped in muslin kept wet from a water reservoir; evaporation from the muslin cools it, so it reads lower than the dry bulb; the drier the air, the faster the evaporation and the bigger the difference ("wet bulb depression"); relative humidity is read from tables using the dry bulb reading and the difference. If both read the same, the air is saturated (100%). Hair hygrometer: human hair lengthens in humid air and shortens in dry air, moving a pointer. Electronic sensors (capacitance) in automatic stations. Relative humidity is given as a percentage (definition in s3; give it here as one clause).
- [ ] M7 Other ground instruments, one bullet each: rain gauge (a funnel of standard size, set in open ground with the rim about 30 cm above the ground to avoid splash, collecting into a measuring cylinder read in mm each day; tipping-bucket gauges record automatically); sunshine recorder (Campbell–Stokes: a glass sphere focuses the sun's rays and burns a trace on a card; the length of the burn gives hours of bright sunshine); evaporation pan; cloud cover estimated in oktas by observers or ceilometers.
- [ ] M8 Upper air: weather balloons carry radiosondes (small instrument packs measuring temperature, humidity and pressure, radioing them back; tracking the balloon gives upper winds), launched twice a day from stations worldwide including Valentia Observatory. Aircraft also report temperature and wind automatically.
- [ ] M9 Remote sensing: weather satellites (geostationary and polar-orbiting) measure cloud, sea-surface temperature, water vapour and ice; rainfall radar detects precipitation by the echo from raindrops. One bullet; satellite images in `geo-weather-climate-s2`.
- [ ] M10 Measuring the oceans: moored weather buoys (e.g. the Irish marine weather buoy network) record air pressure, air and sea temperature, wind, wave height and humidity, transmitting hourly; drifting buoys follow currents; voluntary observing ships report weather; Argo floats (section 2) drift at depth and rise to the surface every 10 days or so, measuring temperature and salinity, then transmit by satellite; tide gauges and satellite altimeters measure sea level; satellites measure sea-surface temperature and sea ice; research ships lower instruments (CTD: conductivity–temperature–depth) to sample salinity and temperature at depth. A line of moored buoys across the equatorial Pacific monitors the ocean to give warning of El Niño.
- [ ] M11 Accuracy: instruments must be correctly sited (open site, standard height, shade for thermometers, away from buildings and heated surfaces), regularly calibrated, read at the same times each day, and long records kept at the same site. Urban growth around a station (urban heat island) can bias records upward.

Importance of measurements (one block; exams always ask it). Each bullet a separate use.
- [ ] I1 Weather forecasting: observations are the starting point (initial conditions) for computer forecast models; more and better observations give better forecasts.
- [ ] I2 Warnings that save lives and property: storm, rain, snow and heat warnings (Met Éireann's colour codes, `geo-weather-climate-s1`); storm surge and flood warnings; tropical cyclone tracking (s4); a falling barograph trace warns of an approaching depression.
- [ ] I3 Aviation (wind, visibility, icing, pressure for altimeter settings), shipping and fishing (sea area forecasts, wave heights from buoys), and offshore work.
- [ ] I4 Agriculture: timing of silage and hay making, spraying, sowing and harvesting; frost warnings; soil temperature and moisture show when grass starts to grow; drought monitoring (link s6).
- [ ] I5 Energy and other economic uses: wind measurements for siting wind farms and predicting wind power output; temperature forecasts for electricity and gas demand; construction, retail, insurance, sport and tourism planning.
- [ ] I6 Climate records and climate change: long, consistent records (e.g. the Mauna Loa CO2 record, Valentia's long temperature record) show trends such as global warming, sea-level rise and ocean warming; they are the evidence used by the IPCC and by governments (s5).
- [ ] I7 Understanding the ocean–atmosphere link: buoy and Argo data on sea temperature and salinity track El Niño, ocean heat content and the Atlantic circulation; they improve seasonal forecasts.

## 3. Traps

- Temperature falls with height in the troposphere and mesosphere but **rises** in the stratosphere (ozone) and thermosphere. Don't say it falls all the way up.
- The ozone layer is in the **stratosphere**, not the troposphere; ground-level ozone is a pollutant. The ozone hole is not the cause of global warming (reuse the trap from `geo-economic-environment-s3`).
- The troposphere is **thicker over the equator** than over the poles, not thinner.
- A wind vane points **into** the wind (towards where it comes from). A barometer measures pressure, a hygrometer humidity, an anemometer wind speed; don't swap them. "Aneroid" means without liquid.
- In a wet-and-dry bulb hygrometer, a **large** difference means **dry** air (low relative humidity); no difference means saturated air.
- Ireland's Stevenson screen door faces **north** (away from the sun in the northern hemisphere).
- Don't give the number of Met Éireann stations, buoy names, or salinity figures beyond section 2. Don't copy station readings or tables from past papers; use invented illustrative values if an example reading is needed (e.g. "illustrative: dry bulb 15 °C, wet bulb 12 °C, a 3 °C difference").
- Chart-reading skills, isolines, station models and the Beaufort scale's details are taught in `geo-weather-climate-s1`; refer to them in one clause.
- Satellite image interpretation belongs to `geo-weather-climate-s2`.
- The heat budget and greenhouse effect belong to s2; humidity, dew point and cloud formation to s3; ocean currents and the thermohaline circulation to s4. Here, one linking clause each.
