## 1. Must cover (one bullet or block for every item; tick each off)

What a multinational is
- [ ] M1 Definition: a company with its headquarters in one country (its home country) and production or service branches in several other countries (host countries). Also called a transnational corporation (TNC).
- [ ] M2 Typical structure: headquarters, research and development (R&D) and top management in the home country (the core); branch plants, regional headquarters, sales and service offices abroad. Decisions are made at headquarters; branch plants compete with each other inside the company for investment.
- [ ] M3 Typical organisation of production: the work is split into stages (design and R&D → basic processing of raw materials and components → assembly and testing → distribution and sales), each placed where it is cheapest or most efficient. This spread of stages is what the syllabus means by sourcing, basic processing units and markets.
- [ ] M4 Why MNCs matter: the largest have sales or market values bigger than the GDP of many countries; they control much of world trade and investment (figure in s4's checked facts, don't repeat it here). The largest by market value in the mid-2020s are US technology companies (named in section 2; give no values).
- [ ] M5 Foreign direct investment (FDI): investment by a company in another country by building or buying a plant there. IDA Ireland is the State agency that attracts and supports FDI (facts in section 2), and its client companies are concentrated in the Dublin and Mid-East region (reasons: largest labour pool, universities, Dublin Port and Airport, services, existing clusters).
- [ ] M6 Why MNCs choose Ireland (the Ordinary "advantages of Ireland" question; one point each, developed): low corporation tax; young, educated, English-speaking workforce; EU membership and access to the single market (and since Brexit, the only euro-area country where English is the main language; Malta also has English as an official language); IDA grants and support; good international links (ports, airports, broadband); clusters of similar firms and suppliers; universities and research links; a stable democracy with a pro-business government.

Case study 1: Intel (main case study)
- [ ] I1 The company: founding, founders, headquarters, what it makes (section 2).
- [ ] I2 Structure and organisation: headquarters and main R&D in the USA; wafer fabrication plants ("fabs") in the USA, Ireland and Israel; assembly and test plants in Asia; sales offices worldwide (named sites in section 2).
- [ ] I3 Intel in Ireland: location (Leixlip, Co. Kildare), arrival date, investment, the fabs, Fab 34 and the 2024 joint-venture deal, what the plant does (section 2). Workforce in the thousands; give no exact number.
- [ ] I4 Why Leixlip (local site factors): on the M4 motorway and near the M50; about 30 minutes from Dublin Airport and Dublin Port; a large, flat greenfield site; close to Maynooth University and the Dublin universities; large Dublin labour pool; water and electricity supply (fabs use huge amounts of both).
- [ ] I5 The product, the microprocessor (chip): the "brain" of a computer; made by building billions of tiny transistors in layers on a thin disc (wafer) of pure silicon in a dust-free clean room.
- [ ] I6 Sourcing of raw materials and components: silicon wafers (silicon refined from quartz sand) bought from specialist wafer makers; chemicals, specialty gases, metals; huge volumes of ultra-pure water; electricity; the manufacturing machines themselves, especially lithography machines from ASML in the Netherlands (section 2). Give no wafer supplier names.
- [ ] I7 Location of basic processing units (the production chain): wafers are processed in fabs (Leixlip, Oregon, Arizona, Israel) → finished wafers are flown to assembly and test plants in Asia (Malaysia, Vietnam, China) where they are cut into individual chips, packaged and tested → chips go to computer makers' assembly plants, mostly in Asia. Chips are small, light and very valuable, so they are moved by air.
- [ ] I8 Location of markets: computer manufacturers (named in section 2), makers of servers for data centres and cloud computing, and governments and businesses worldwide; markets in North America, Europe and Asia, with China one of its largest. Exams ask for two markets outside Ireland: name countries or regions (e.g. the USA, China, Germany).
- [ ] I9 Difficulties facing Intel (exams ask for one; give several to choose from): competition from AMD, TSMC, Samsung and Nvidia; loss of Apple as a customer from 2020; very high cost of new fabs; falling profits and global job cuts in 2024–25; risk of production moving if the Irish tax advantage narrows (15% minimum rate) or costs rise; high energy costs and pressure on Ireland's electricity grid; dependence on Asian supply chains (2021 chip shortage).
- [ ] I10 Benefits to Ireland (impact): direct and indirect (spin-off) jobs; local suppliers and services; corporation tax; exports; skills and research links.

Case study 2: Apple (alternative)
- [ ] A1 The company: founding, founders, headquarters, main products (section 2).
- [ ] A2 Apple in Cork: arrival date, site, workforce of about 6,000, and what the Cork campus does (European headquarters, operations and supply chain, customer support in many languages, finance, and built-to-order iMac assembly). It is Apple's European headquarters, not a phone factory.
- [ ] A3 Structure and organisation: design, software and management in California; Apple owns very little manufacturing itself. It outsources production to contract manufacturers, chiefly Foxconn (a Taiwanese company) in China, and increasingly in India and Vietnam; it runs its own retail stores and online store.
- [ ] A4 The product, the iPhone: launched 2007. Sourcing of raw materials and components: processor chips designed by Apple and made by TSMC in Taiwan; screens and memory from South Korean and other Asian suppliers; raw materials such as aluminium, lithium and cobalt for batteries, rare earth metals and gold.
- [ ] A5 Location of basic processing units: final assembly by Foxconn at very large plants in China (Zhengzhou, "iPhone City", and Shenzhen); a growing share assembled in India (near Chennai) to reduce dependence on China.
- [ ] A6 Location of markets: sold worldwide; the Americas, Europe and Greater China are its largest regions; sold through Apple Stores, its online store and mobile phone networks.
- [ ] A7 Difficulties and issues: the EU state-aid tax case (section 2); dependence on China for assembly and the trade tensions between the USA and China; competition from Samsung and Chinese phone makers; criticism of working conditions in supplier factories; the Athenry data centre that was abandoned after planning delays.

## 3. Traps

- Apple's Cork campus does **not** make iPhones. iPhones are assembled by Foxconn and others in China and India. Cork assembles some built-to-order iMacs and runs European operations.
- Intel's Leixlip plant makes **wafers of processors**; the chips are cut, packaged and tested in Asia, not in Ireland. Don't say Intel Ireland makes computers.
- Don't give an employment figure for Intel Ireland (consistent with `geo-region-ireland-s1`). Apple Cork: only "about 6,000".
- Don't call the Apple tax case a fine. It was recovery of illegal State aid (unpaid tax), and Ireland had argued against collecting it.
- Intel was founded in 1968, Apple in 1976, Dell in 1984: don't mix them up.
- Corporation tax is 12.5%, with 15% for very large groups from 2024. Don't write "10%" (an old manufacturing rate) or say 12.5% applies to every MNC now.
- Don't copy MNC values, IDA regional job counts or export figures from past-paper charts.
- Product life cycle, branch-plant openings and closures, the Dell closure, mobility, and teleservices/e-commerce belong to `geo-economic-globalisation-s2`. Here, give only one sentence per difficulty, no relocation case study.
- The general list of industrial location factors (raw materials, labour, markets, transport, capital, government policy) and the Irish developed-economy case study belong to `geo-economic-activities`. Use only the MNC-specific advantages of Ireland here.
- "The impact of global trading patterns on producer and consumer regions, with reference to one MNC" is the Global Interdependence option (`geo-option-global-interdependence-s2`), not this subsection; don't write that essay here.
- Foxconn is Taiwanese, not Chinese, though its main plants are in mainland China. TSMC is in Taiwan, Samsung in South Korea.
