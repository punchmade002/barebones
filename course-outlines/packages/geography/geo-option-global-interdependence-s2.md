- **loId:** `geo-option-global-interdependence-s2` · **chapterId:** `geo-option-global-interdependence` · **syllabusRef:** 6.2
- **Syllabus:** Statement: "We live in an interdependent global economy. Actions or decisions
  taken in one area have an impact on other areas." Students should study "a case study of a
  specific multinational company with reference to the impact of global trading patterns in
  relation to both producer and consumer regions" (national settings: "Irish trade, MNCs in
  Ireland"; international: "Appropriate examples from a continental or sub continental

  warming" (international: "European examples, e.g. managed forests in Scandinavia"); "the
  impact of social and political decisions, including – economic and political refugees –

  "European examples").

## 1. Must cover (one bullet or block for every item; tick each off)

Interdependence
- [ ] N1 Interdependence: countries and regions rely on each other for raw materials, components, markets, investment, labour, food and a shared environment, so a decision in one place has effects elsewhere. Globalisation (trade, MNCs, communications, migration) has deepened it. One short opening block.

Part A: an MNC and global trading patterns (each MNC block self-contained)
- [ ] T1 Producer and consumer regions defined: producer regions supply raw materials, components and assembly (often developing countries with low wages); consumer regions buy the finished product (mostly rich countries, increasingly China and India). The value added is shared very unequally along the chain.
- [ ] T2 Apple — the company and its chain: headquarters and design in California; components from Taiwan (TSMC chips), South Korea (screens), Japan and elsewhere; minerals including cobalt from the DR Congo; assembly by Foxconn in China (Zhengzhou, Shenzhen) and increasingly in India; Cork as European headquarters and support centre.
- [ ] T3 Apple — impact on producer regions, positive: jobs for millions in assembly and supply firms; skills and industrial growth in China; export earnings; supplier firms grow (TSMC, Foxconn). Negative: low share of value for assembly workers (the 2011 value study); long hours and pressure (Foxconn 2010, Zhengzhou 2022); dependence on Apple's orders, which can move (shift to India); mining conditions and child labour in cobalt supply chains.
- [ ] T4 Apple — impact on consumer regions: access to new technology and services; most profit and high-paid design and marketing jobs stay in the USA; consumers in rich countries pay high prices but benefit from low assembly costs; electronic waste exported to poorer countries (one bullet, no named site).
- [ ] T5 Apple — Ireland as both: Cork employment, European operations, and the EU state-aid tax case (€13 billion; €14 billion recovered 2024) showing how a decision in Brussels affected Ireland and the USA; US tariff decisions (2018 China trade war, 2025) pushing Apple to move assembly to India.
- [ ] T6 Cadbury / Mondelēz — the company: Cadbury's British origins; Kraft takeover (2010) and Mondelēz International (2012, USA); Irish plants at Coolock (Dublin) and Rathmore (Co. Kerry); Rathmore makes chocolate crumb from Irish milk, sugar and cocoa, sent to factories in Britain.
- [ ] T7 Cadbury / Mondelēz — producer regions: cocoa from Côte d'Ivoire and Ghana (smallholder farms); low farm incomes and the Living Income Differential; child labour (2020 study figure); deforestation for cocoa; dependence on a single export whose price swings (2024 record price, yet farmers' fixed farm-gate prices); value added mostly outside Africa (processing in Europe, e.g. Amsterdam); company responses (Fairtrade Dairy Milk 2009, Cocoa Life from 2012 and its own scheme replacing Fairtrade in 2017). Irish milk producers also as a producer region.
- [ ] T8 Cadbury / Mondelēz — consumer regions: Europe and North America consume most of the world's chocolate; brands, marketing and retail take most of the price; consumer campaigns and EU rules (Deforestation Regulation) pass consumer pressure back to producers.
- [ ] T9 Intel (short block for students who studied it in the economic elective): research and design in Oregon/California; wafers made in Leixlip, Arizona, Oregon and Israel; assembly and test in Malaysia and Vietnam; customers are computer makers (Lenovo, HP, Dell) selling worldwide; effects of decisions elsewhere (US CHIPS Act, 2024 job cuts, Apollo stake in Fab 34). Reuse `geo-economic-globalisation-s1` facts only.
- [ ] T10 Irish trade and MNCs in Ireland (national setting): Ireland as a producer region for US MNCs (pharmaceuticals, medical devices, ICT); exports over €200 billion (2024), a third to the USA; dependence on US firms and markets makes Ireland vulnerable to US tax and tariff decisions (2025); Brexit as a political decision affecting Irish trade routes and agri-food exports; corporation tax and the OECD 15% minimum tax.

Part B: global environmental issues (each: causes in one place, effects in others, responses)
- [ ] D1 Deforestation — the global picture: forest area and annual loss (FAO figures in section 2); most loss in the tropics (South America, Africa, South-East Asia), driven largely by demand in other regions for beef, soya, palm oil, timber, cocoa and minerals.
- [ ] D2 Deforestation — the Amazon (Brazil): causes (cattle ranching, soya exported as animal feed to Europe and China, roads, mining, dams); PRODES trend figures; effects elsewhere (carbon released, reduced rainfall recycling, biodiversity loss, indigenous peoples); responses that involve other countries (the Amazon Fund with Norway, the Soy Moratorium agreed by traders, the EU Deforestation Regulation). Reuse geoecology/environment facts.
- [ ] D3 Deforestation — Indonesia and Malaysia: palm oil for foods, cosmetics and biofuels sold worldwide; peat swamp clearance and fires; haze affecting Singapore and Malaysia (1997, 2015) as a transboundary effect; orangutan.
- [ ] D4 Managed forests in Scandinavia (syllabus setting): Sweden and Finland as Europe's most forested countries; a long tradition of replanting by law (Sweden 1903); sustainable yield (more grows than is cut; growing stock has increased); certification (FSC, PEFC) demanded by buyers in other countries; exports of timber, pulp and paper; criticism (clear-felling, monoculture, loss of old-growth forest and biodiversity, conflict with Sámi reindeer herding). Shows that a forest economy can be managed sustainably when the state, owners and consumers act together.
- [ ] D5 Ireland and forests: Ireland has low forest cover, mostly Sitka spruce plantations run by Coillte and private owners; Ireland imports timber and products linked to deforestation (beef feed, palm oil), so Irish consumers are linked to tropical deforestation.
- [ ] S1 Desertification — definition and scale: land degradation in drylands (not the desert "advancing"); drylands cover about two-fifths of the land surface and are home to about 2 billion people.
- [ ] S2 Desertification — the Sahel: physical (unreliable rainfall, drought) and human causes (population growth, overgrazing, overcultivation, fuelwood, cash crops such as groundnuts and cotton for export taking the best land, settling of nomads); global warming linked to emissions elsewhere; effects (famine, conflict over land and water, migration to cities and to Europe, Lake Chad's shrinking); responses (UNCCD 1994, Great Green Wall, zaï pits and stone lines, FMNR in Niger). Reuse `geo-option-geoecology-s3` facts.
- [ ] S3 Desertification — the Aral Sea: decisions by Soviet planners in Moscow to divert rivers to grow cotton destroyed a sea and its fishing economy in Kazakhstan and Uzbekistan; salt and dust storms; the Kok-Aral dam (2005) partial recovery. Reuse facts.
- [ ] S4 Desertification in Europe and China: south-east Spain (Almería), where irrigated greenhouse farming supplies winter vegetables to northern European supermarkets (including Ireland) and draws down groundwater; China's Three-North Shelter Forest Programme (from 1978) against the spread of the Gobi and dust storms. One or two bullets each.
- [ ] G1 Global warming — causes: greenhouse gases from burning fossil fuels in industrialised and emerging economies, deforestation and agriculture; China the largest emitter now, the USA the largest per person among major economies and historically; the rich countries' historical responsibility. Reuse `geo-economic-environment-s3` W4 figures.
- [ ] G2 Global warming — effects on other areas (the interdependence point): poor countries that emitted least suffer most: Bangladesh (floods, cyclones, sea-level rise), small island states (Tuvalu, Kiribati, the Maldives), the Sahel (drought), Pakistan's 2022 floods; melting Arctic sea ice; coral bleaching on the Great Barrier Reef; climate-driven migration; food price rises when harvests fail.
- [ ] G3 Global warming — effects and responsibilities for Ireland: agriculture over a third of Irish emissions; Ireland's warming; storms (Éowyn 2025); Ireland's targets (51% by 2030, net zero 2050).
- [ ] G4 Global warming — responses requiring co-operation: UNFCCC (1992), Kyoto (1997), Paris (2015), the EU Emissions Trading System and Green Deal, the EU Carbon Border Adjustment Mechanism (charging imports for their carbon, so EU climate decisions affect exporters such as China and India), climate finance and the Loss and Damage Fund (2022–23). Evaluation: national pledges still fall short of the 1.5 °C goal.

Part C: the impact of social and political decisions
- [ ] R1 Definitions, one bullet each: economic migrant (moves voluntarily for work and a better life); refugee (1951 Convention definition, reuse); asylum seeker; internally displaced person (forced to flee but still inside their own country); stateless person. Political refugees flee persecution or war that results from political decisions; economic "refugees" flee poverty, often caused by decisions elsewhere (trade, debt, conflict). The line between them is often blurred.
- [ ] R2 Global scale: forcibly displaced people passed 120 million in 2024 (UNHCR); most refugees stay in neighbouring, mostly low- and middle-income countries, not in Europe; Turkey hosted the world's largest refugee population for most of the 2010s and early 2020s.
- [ ] R3 Syria (political refugees, full block): the regime's violent response to protests in 2011 led to civil war; over half the pre-war population displaced; refugees in Turkey, Lebanon (the most refugees per head of any country) and Jordan (Zaatari camp); the 2015 movement to Europe (Alan Kurdi photograph; Germany's decision; Hungary's fence; EU–Turkey statement 2016); Ireland's Refugee Protection Programme; the fall of Assad (December 2024) and the question of return. Each a decision in one place with effects in another.
- [ ] R4 The Rohingya (statelessness, full block): Myanmar's 1982 citizenship law left them stateless; the 2017 military campaign drove about 740,000 into Bangladesh; Cox's Bazar camps (Kutupalong, the world's largest refugee camp); restrictions on work and movement; Bangladesh's relocation of some to Bhasan Char; the genocide case at the International Court of Justice (brought by The Gambia, 2019); the 2021 coup in Myanmar ended hopes of return.
- [ ] R5 Ukraine: Russia's invasion (24 February 2022) is a political decision that displaced millions; the EU's decision to activate temporary protection for the first time (March 2022) gave immediate rights to live and work; over 4 million under temporary protection in the EU and over 100,000 in Ireland; contrast with treatment of asylum seekers from Africa and the Middle East (a human rights and equality question).
- [ ] R6 Ireland — immigration of refugees (national setting): international protection system and direct provision (2000), right to work from 2018, rising applications 2022–23, accommodation shortages and protests (East Wall 2022, Dublin riot 2023); the Irish Refugee Protection Programme (2015). Reuse population-migration facts; keep to 4–6 bullets.
- [ ] M1 Migration patterns: about 281 million international migrants (2020), about 3.6% of the world's people; most migrate within their own region (South–South as well as South–North); main corridors: Mexico–USA, North and West Africa–Europe, South Asia–the Gulf, Eastern–Western Europe after EU enlargement (2004).
- [ ] M2 Economic migration and the decisions behind it: EU enlargement (2004) and Ireland's decision to open its labour market immediately; the Gulf states' recruitment of South Asian workers under the kafala sponsorship system and Qatar's reforms around the 2022 World Cup; the Atlantic route to the Canary Islands from West Africa (record arrivals in 2024), partly linked to the decline of local fishing; the Central Mediterranean route (Lampedusa).
- [ ] M3 Effects on source regions: remittances (about $650 billion to low- and middle-income countries in 2023; India the largest recipient, Mexico second); brain drain of doctors and nurses; loss of young workers; return migrants bring skills and capital.
- [ ] M4 Effects on receiving regions: labour for health care, construction, farming and hospitality; younger populations offsetting ageing; cultural diversity; pressure on housing and services; political reaction and anti-immigration parties; border and asylum policies (EU Pact 2024).
- [ ] M5 Ireland's own experience as an example of political and economic decisions driving migration: the Famine (land system and government policy), 1950s emigration, 1980s, post-2008 crash; returning to net immigration. Reuse population-migration-s3 facts; 3–4 bullets.
- [ ] H1 Human rights framework: the Universal Declaration of Human Rights (1948), including the right to seek asylum (Article 14); the principle of non-refoulement (refugees must not be returned to danger); the European Convention on Human Rights. Political decisions can protect or violate these rights.
- [ ] H2 Human rights in global supply chains: Rana Plaza (2013) and the Accord; forced labour of Uyghurs in Xinjiang, China, and the US import ban (2021–22); cobalt and child labour; migrant workers in Qatar. Consumers', companies' and governments' decisions in rich countries affect workers in poor ones.
- [ ] H3 Decisions for human rights in one country affecting another: the Dunnes Stores anti-apartheid strike (1984–87) leading to Ireland's ban on South African fruit and vegetables; sanctions on apartheid South Africa, Myanmar and Russia; the EU Corporate Sustainability Due Diligence Directive (2024).
- [ ] H4 Human rights and the treatment of refugees: detention, pushbacks and deaths at sea in the Mediterranean; long stays in direct provision; children's education in camps; women's safety; statelessness. One block, examples from R3–R6.
- [ ] E1 Evaluation (end of Part C): decisions about war, borders, trade and aid in one country shape the lives of people far away; effective responses need international co-operation (UNHCR, EU, the UN Refugee Convention).

| G2 | Bangladesh (Ganges–Brahmaputra delta), the Maldives, Tuvalu and Kiribati threatened by sea-level rise; Great Barrier Reef repeated mass bleaching since the late 1990s; Arctic summer sea ice shrinking about 13% per decade since 1979; global sea level rose about 20 cm between 1901 and 2018 (IPCC) (same as `geo-economic-environment-s3`). | G3 | Agriculture produces over a third of Ireland's greenhouse gas emissions; transport about a fifth (EPA, early 2020s). Ireland's average temperature has risen about 0.9 °C since 1900. | Item | Example(s) |
|---|---|
| R1 | UN Refugee Convention, 1951 (Geneva): a refugee has a well-founded fear of persecution for reasons of race, religion, nationality, membership of a particular social group or political opinion; its 1967 Protocol removed time and place limits. UNHCR is the UN refugee agency (same as `geo-population-migration-s4`). |
| R2 | UNHCR: more than 120 million people were forcibly displaced worldwide in 2024 (refugees, asylum seekers and internally displaced people). Turkey hosted the largest refugee population in the world for most of the 2010s and early 2020s, mostly Syrians. |
| R3 Syria | Protests in March 2011 were met with force by President Bashar al-Assad's government, leading to civil war. Syria's pre-war population was about 22 million; over half were displaced at the height of the war, including over 6 million refugees abroad and a similar number displaced inside Syria. Turkey hosted over 3 million Syrian refugees; Lebanon has the most refugees per head of population of any country; Zaatari camp, Jordan, opened in 2012. Alan Kurdi, a three-year-old Syrian boy, drowned and was photographed on a beach near Bodrum, Turkey, on 2 September 2015; the image changed public opinion in Europe. About 1.3 million asylum applications in the EU in 2015. Merkel's "Wir schaffen das" (August 2015); Hungary's border fence with Serbia (2015); EU–Turkey statement (March 2016): irregular migrants returned from the Greek islands to Turkey, the EU to provide €6 billion for refugees in Turkey. The Assad government fell on 8 December 2024; many refugees began to consider return, but homes, services and the economy were destroyed. |
| R4 Rohingya | The Rohingya are a mainly Muslim minority from Rakhine State, Myanmar. Myanmar's 1982 Citizenship Law did not recognise them as one of the country's national groups, leaving most stateless. From August 2017 a military campaign of killings and village burning drove about 740,000 Rohingya into Bangladesh. About 1 million Rohingya live in camps around Cox's Bazar, Bangladesh, including Kutupalong, the world's largest refugee camp. ## 3. Traps (known errors)

- **Write every issue as interdependence**: name the decision or action, where it was taken, and where the effects fall. An essay that only describes deforestation or refugees without the link scores poorly.
- **Apple's Cork campus does not make iPhones**; Foxconn is **Taiwanese** with its main plants in mainland China. Intel Leixlip makes processor wafers, not computers (same traps as `geo-economic-globalisation-s1`).
- The Apple tax case was recovery of illegal State aid, **not a fine**, and Ireland argued against collecting it.
- Rathmore makes **chocolate crumb**, not finished bars. Don't say Cadbury still belongs to a British company: Mondelēz is American.
- Don't give a cocoa farmer's share of the chocolate price, a Foxconn wage, Apple's revenue or profits, or child-labour figures other than the NORC 1.56 million: past-paper model answers in the question bank invent such figures (e.g. "Foxconn employs 300,000+", "$1,000 iPhone contains ~$400", "Syrian employment rates 30–40%", "900,000 residents in a space designed for 200,000", Zambian debt-service percentages). Use none.
- Desertification is degradation of dryland, **not** the Sahara advancing a set distance each year. The Aral Sea shrank because of irrigation, not climate change.
- Global warming ≠ ozone depletion ≠ acid rain. Keep them separate (see `geo-economic-environment-s2`/`-s3`).
- Don't say the Amazon produces 20% of the world's oxygen (a widely repeated error).
- Refugee ≠ asylum seeker ≠ economic migrant ≠ internally displaced person. Ukrainians in the EU have **temporary protection**, not asylum.
- Syria figures are rounded and approximate; don't give a death toll for the Syrian war or the Rohingya campaign (estimates vary).
- Sámi grievances are about reindeer grazing and land use; don't describe the Sámi as opposing all forestry.
- **Neighbouring subsections and chapters:**
  - MNC structure, sourcing, basic processing units and markets (Intel, Apple), mobility and the product life cycle (Dell) are `geo-economic-globalisation-s1`/`-s2`; world trade patterns and Irish trade data are `-s3`. Here the angle is the **impact on producer and consumer regions**; reuse their facts, don't re-teach structure.
  - Soil-focused desertification (overgrazing, conservation methods in detail, the Burren, Iceland) is `geo-option-geoecology-s3`; rainforest felling as biome alteration is `-s5`. Global warming's science and pollution case studies are `geo-economic-environment-s3`; the Amazon as an economic–environmental conflict is `geo-economic-environment-s5`.
  - Irish and EU migration policy (direct provision, Dublin system, the 2024 Pact) is `geo-population-migration-s4`; Irish migration history is `-s3`; ethnic tension is `-s5`; refugees' impact on racial patterns is `geo-option-culture-identity-s1`. Use only the facts here, as short bullets.
  - Worker exploitation as an empowerment issue and gender roles are s3; justice issues for minority groups (indigenous peoples, Travellers, Roma, Sámi rights over wind farms) are s4; fair trade is s4.

## 4. What exams ask (topics only)

- 80 marks: the impact of global trading patterns on producer and consumer regions, with reference to one MNC (2026). Expected: the MNC and its product; producer regions with named places and positive and negative impacts; consumer regions; Ireland's role; the uneven share of value; evaluation.
- 80 marks: deforestation, global warming and desertification as interdependence (2024; two of them in 2021). Expected for each: causes (with the distant demand or decision), effects locally and elsewhere, responses needing co-operation, named examples (Amazon, Scandinavia, Sahel, Aral Sea, Pakistan/Bangladesh).
- 80 marks: global environmental issues **and** the impact of social and political decisions (2018, 2020). Expected: two or three aspects, e.g. deforestation, refugees, human rights.
- 80 marks: economic and political refugees, migration patterns and human rights issues (2022, 2023, 2025). Expected: definitions; Syria and one other refugee case (Rohingya or Ukraine); economic migration patterns with remittances and the Gulf; human rights (UDHR, supply chains, treatment of refugees); Ireland's experience.

## 5. Practice questions to write

1. 80 marks, Higher: "Discuss the impact of global trading patterns on both producer and consumer regions, with reference to one multinational company that you have studied." Model answer on **Apple only**: the company and product; producer regions (China, Taiwan, South Korea, India, DR Congo) with positive and negative impacts; consumer regions; Ireland's position (Cork, tax case, US tariffs). About 25–30 SRPs. No Cadbury material.
2. 80 marks, Higher: "We live in an interdependent global economy where decisions taken in one area have an impact on other areas. Examine this statement with reference to deforestation, global warming and desertification." Model answer: three aspects of about 9–10 SRPs each: deforestation (Amazon plus managed forests in Scandinavia), global warming (emitters vs Bangladesh/Pakistan, responses), desertification (the Sahel, with the Aral Sea as a second example).
3. 80 marks, Higher: "Discuss the impact of social and political decisions with reference to economic and political refugees, migration patterns and human rights issues." Model answer: Syria and the Rohingya as political refugee cases; economic migration patterns (Gulf, West Africa–Canaries, remittances, Ireland); human rights (UDHR and non-refoulement, Xinjiang forced labour, the Dunnes Stores strike). About 25–30 SRPs.
