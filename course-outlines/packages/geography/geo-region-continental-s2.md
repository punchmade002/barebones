## 1. Must cover (one bullet or block for every item; tick each off)

The region and the sketch map
- [ ] O1 Location and size: eastern South America; area and rank (section 2); the equator crosses the far north and the Tropic of Capricorn crosses the south-east near São Paulo, so most of Brazil is tropical; a long Atlantic coastline; borders all but two South American countries.
- [ ] O2 Why Brazil counts as a continental region: its continental size; it is a single federal state with one language (Portuguese) surrounded by Spanish-speaking countries; its own culture and economy; its own internal regions.
- [ ] O3 The five official macro-regions (North, Northeast, Centre-West, Southeast, South), with a line on the character of each. Students use these names in every later answer.
- [ ] O4 Sketch-map skill: draw the outline (a broad northern bulge across the Amazon, the eastward "shoulder" at the Northeast, then the coast running south-west and the country tapering to the south); show and name one relief feature (an outlined area), one river (its course across Brazil), urban centres (dots, coastal or inland correctly) and an agricultural or industrial region (outlined area). Give the recommended set and placing cues from section 2. Names may go on the map or in a key.

Physical processes: climate
- [ ] C1 Controls on Brazil's climate: latitude (mostly tropical), the seasonal movement of the ITCZ, the south-east trade winds off the Atlantic, altitude (the plateau is cooler than the coast), relief (the coastal escarpment forces air to rise) and cold fronts from the south.
- [ ] C2 Equatorial climate (Amazon/North): hot all year, small annual range, heavy convectional rain in every month (figures for Manaus from section 2). One sentence only on the biome; the biome itself is `geo-option-geoecology-s4`.
- [ ] C3 Tropical wet-and-dry (savanna) climate (Centre-West, the Cerrado): a rainy summer (about October–April) when the ITCZ and the overhead sun move south, and a long dry winter (about May–September); Brasília as the example.
- [ ] C4 Semi-arid climate of the Northeast interior (the sertão): low, unreliable rain concentrated in a few months; reasons: the region lies in the rain shadow of the coastal plateau, sinking air limits convection, the ITCZ reaches it only briefly, and El Niño years bring drought. The "drought polygon" (section 2).
- [ ] C5 Humid tropical east coast: the south-east trades bring moisture off the warm Atlantic; relief rain on the coastal escarpment (Serra do Mar); a wet coastal strip that once carried the Atlantic Forest.
- [ ] C6 Tropical highland climate of the Southeast plateau: altitude moderates temperatures (São Paulo is cooler than Rio de Janeiro on the coast).
- [ ] C7 Humid subtropical climate of the South: four seasons, rain all year, winter frosts and occasional snow in the highlands; frost risk shaped where coffee is grown (link to A4).
- [ ] C8 Climate hazards and change: Northeast droughts; Amazon drought (2023); floods and landslides in the South and Southeast (section 2); El Niño links; deforestation reduces rainfall recycling (one sentence; detail belongs to the Geoecology option).
- [ ] C9 Importance of climate for human activity: two or three crops a year in the tropics, drought-driven migration from the sertão, hydroelectric potential from heavy rainfall, tourism.

Physical processes: soils and vegetation regions
- [ ] S1 Latosols (ferralsols/oxisols) of the Amazon and Cerrado: deep, red-yellow, heavily leached by heat and rain, acidic, low in nutrients; fertility is held in the vegetation, so cleared land loses it fast (link to `geo-option-geoecology-s2` for laterisation; one sentence).
- [ ] S2 Cerrado soils: acidic and high in aluminium, once thought useless for crops; liming (adding crushed limestone) and fertiliser, developed by EMBRAPA, made them productive (link to A5).
- [ ] S3 Terra roxa: deep, dark red, fertile soil weathered from the basalt of the Paraná Plateau; the soil of the coffee boom.
- [ ] S4 Massapê: dark, clay-rich, fertile soil of the humid Northeast coast (Zona da Mata); the base of colonial sugar plantations.
- [ ] S5 Várzea (floodplain) soils of the Amazon: renewed each year by silt from floods, so they are among the few fertile Amazon soils.
- [ ] S6 Terra preta ("black earth"): dark, fertile soil made by pre-Columbian Amazon peoples by adding charcoal, food waste and pottery; shows people can improve tropical soils.
- [ ] S7 Sertão soils: thin, stony and easily eroded; salinisation where irrigation water evaporates.
- [ ] S8 Soil problems: erosion after forest clearance; nutrient loss and compaction on old pasture; salinisation in irrigated areas.
- [ ] S9 Brazil's six biomes as natural vegetation regions, with their share of Brazil's area (section 2): Amazon rainforest, Cerrado (tropical savanna), Atlantic Forest, Caatinga (dry thorn scrub of the sertão), Pantanal (wetland) and Pampa (grassland). One or two features of each; the Atlantic Forest's heavy loss.

Physical processes: relief
- [ ] R1 Main relief divisions: the Brazilian Highlands (Planalto) covering the centre, east and south; the Amazon lowland (basin); the Guiana Highlands on the northern border; the Pantanal lowland; the narrow coastal plain.
- [ ] R2 Geology: Brazil sits in the middle of the South American plate, far from its boundaries, so it has no active volcanoes and few strong earthquakes; the highlands are an ancient crystalline shield, once part of Gondwanaland (one sentence; Gondwanaland is in `geo-plate-tectonics-s2`). The Andes are not in Brazil.
- [ ] R3 The Brazilian Highlands: old, worn-down plateaus and tablelands, mostly 300–900 m; they tilt from the high eastern edge towards the interior, so many rivers rise near the coast but flow inland.
- [ ] R4 The Great Escarpment: the Serra do Mar and Serra da Mantiqueira rise steeply behind the coast of the Southeast, so the coastal plain is very narrow; a barrier to transport between ports and the plateau (São Paulo–Santos).
- [ ] R5 The Paraná Plateau: thick layers of basalt from ancient fissure eruptions in the south; source of terra roxa (link to S3); the Iguaçu Falls drop over its basalt edge.
- [ ] R6 The Amazon lowland: a vast, low, flat basin of sedimentary rocks and alluvium between the Guiana and Brazilian Highlands, widening to the west.
- [ ] R7 The Guiana Highlands: Brazil's highest peak (section 2) on the Venezuelan border.
- [ ] R8 The Pantanal: a huge, flat, seasonally flooded depression in the Centre-West; the world's largest tropical wetland.
- [ ] R9 Coastal landforms: granite domes and steep hills at Rio de Janeiro (Sugarloaf), sand dunes and lagoons in the Northeast (Lençóis Maranhenses).
- [ ] R10 Relief and human activity: the plateau carries most farming, industry and cities; the escarpment limited early inland settlement and needs costly roads and railways; the lowland Amazon is sparsely settled; steep coastal hills host favelas prone to landslides.

Physical processes: drainage
- [ ] D1 Three main river basins: the Amazon (north), the Paraná–Paraguay (south and centre) and the São Francisco (east), plus the Tocantins–Araguaia.
- [ ] D2 The Amazon: source in the Peruvian Andes; enters Brazil and is called the Solimões until it meets the Rio Negro at Manaus (the "Meeting of the Waters"); flows east to the Atlantic near the equator, ending in a wide estuary around Marajó Island rather than a large delta; the world's largest river by discharge and basin area (section 2); over a thousand tributaries; navigable by ocean ships to Manaus.
- [ ] D3 Amazon features: the annual flood rises many metres and spreads into the várzea and flooded forest (igapó); black-water (Rio Negro, acidic and low in silt) and white-water (Solimões, silt-laden from the Andes) rivers; the tidal bore (pororoca) at the mouth.
- [ ] D4 The Paraná: rises on the Brazilian Highlands, flows south-west into Argentina and the Río de la Plata; heavily dammed for hydroelectricity; Itaipu on the Brazil–Paraguay border; the Iguaçu Falls on a tributary.
- [ ] D5 The São Francisco: rises in Minas Gerais, flows north through the semi-arid Northeast and turns east to the Atlantic; a lifeline for the sertão ("river of national integration"); dams for power; irrigated fruit at Petrolina–Juazeiro; the transfer (transposition) project (section 2).
- [ ] D6 Hydroelectric dams and their controversy: Itaipu, Tucuruí on the Tocantins, Belo Monte on the Xingu (displacement of people, flooding of forest, opposition by indigenous peoples).
- [ ] D7 River pollution and threats: mercury from illegal gold mining in Amazon rivers; the Rio Doce mine-waste disaster (link to P6); urban sewage in the Tietê at São Paulo.
- [ ] D8 Rivers and people: transport (the Amazon is the main highway of the North), fish, water for irrigation and cities, hydroelectricity; floods and droughts both disrupt river transport (2023).

Economic processes: primary activities — agriculture
- [ ] A1 Importance: Brazil is an agricultural superpower; its world rankings (section 2); agribusiness as a large share of exports, but farming itself is a small share of GDP and jobs (section 2), showing a highly mechanised commercial sector.
- [ ] A2 Types of agriculture (exams ask for two): large commercial mechanised farms (soya, maize, cotton in the Cerrado); plantations (sugar cane, coffee, oranges); extensive cattle ranching; subsistence and family farming (cassava/manioc, beans, maize), including shifting cultivation in the Amazon and small farms in the sertão; irrigated fruit farming.
- [ ] A3 The farming calendar in the Cerrado: soya planted with the first rains (about October), harvested about January–February, followed by a second crop of maize (safrinha) in the same rainy season; two crops a year on the same land.
- [ ] A4 Coffee: history (the 19th-century boom in São Paulo, worked first by enslaved Africans and then by European immigrants, exported through Santos); the move north after frosts (1975) to Minas Gerais, now the leading coffee state; Brazil's world rank.
- [ ] A5 The Cerrado soya revolution: how infertile savanna became a soya belt: liming and fertiliser, tropical soya varieties bred by EMBRAPA (founded 1973), cheap flat land suited to machines, reliable summer rain; Mato Grosso as the leading state; China as the main market (section 2).
- [ ] A6 Sugar cane and ethanol: colonial plantations on the Northeast coast (massapê soils, enslaved labour); today mainly São Paulo state; the Proálcool programme (1975) after the oil crisis; flex-fuel cars (from 2003) run on ethanol, petrol or any mix.
- [ ] A7 Cattle ranching: one of the world's largest herds; extensive ranching on the Cerrado, Pantanal and cleared Amazon land; ranching is the largest driver of Amazon clearance (one sentence; detail in `geo-option-geoecology-s5`); gaúcho ranching on the Pampa of Rio Grande do Sul.
- [ ] A8 Irrigated farming in the São Francisco valley: grapes and mangoes for export from Petrolina–Juazeiro, using water from the river and its dams.
- [ ] A9 Physical advantages: huge area of flat land; tropical temperatures allow two or three crops a year; heavy rainfall over most of the country; fertile terra roxa and massapê; rivers for irrigation.
- [ ] A10 Physical problems: leached, acidic latosols; drought in the sertão; frost in the south; erosion after clearance; pests and diseases in the humid tropics.
- [ ] A11 Human and economic factors: very unequal land ownership (huge estates, latifúndios, beside tiny plots, minifúndios); landless rural workers and the MST; family farms grow most of the food eaten at home while big agribusiness exports; credit and technology favour large farms; high transport costs to ports.
- [ ] A12 Markets and policy: export markets (China, EU); Mercosur; EMBRAPA research; the Amazon soy moratorium (2006); farm credit for family farms.
- [ ] A13 Challenges and conflict: land conflict and violence in the Amazon frontier; deforestation for pasture and soya; heavy pesticide use; dependence on world commodity prices.

Economic processes: primary activities — forestry, fishing, mining and energy
- [ ] P1 Forestry and extraction: Amazon timber, much of it illegal; Brazil nuts and rubber collected from the standing forest; the rubber boom centred on Manaus (about 1879–1912) and its collapse when rubber plantations in Asia undercut it.
- [ ] P2 Extractive reserves: Chico Mendes and the rubber tappers; reserves where local people harvest forest products without clearing the forest (section 2).
- [ ] P3 Plantation forestry: fast-growing eucalyptus plantations in the Southeast and South supply pulp and paper; Brazil is a leading pulp exporter (section 2). Deforestation figures (PRODES) in one block, consistent with section 2.
- [ ] P4 Fishing: small for so long a coastline, because the warm Brazil Current is low in plankton; artisanal (small-boat) fishing on the Northeast coast; Amazon river fisheries (pirarucu, tambaqui); growing aquaculture (tilapia, shrimp farms in the Northeast).
- [ ] P5 Mining: Carajás in Pará, the world's largest iron-ore mine, run by Vale, with a railway to the port of São Luís; the Iron Quadrangle of Minas Gerais (iron ore and the 18th-century gold rush at Ouro Preto); bauxite in Pará; niobium.
- [ ] P6 Costs of mining: the tailings dam (mine-waste dam) disasters at Mariana (2015) and Brumadinho (2019); illegal gold mining (garimpo) in Yanomami territory with mercury pollution and the 2023 health emergency.
- [ ] P7 Energy mix: hydroelectricity supplies most electricity; Brazil's electricity is among the cleanest of large economies (section 2); wind and solar growing fast in the Northeast.
- [ ] P8 Oil and gas: Petrobras (1953); offshore fields in the Campos and Santos basins; the pre-salt fields discovered in 2006–07, deep under the ocean floor beneath a thick salt layer; Brazil is now a major oil producer and exporter (section 2).
- [ ] P9 Biofuels: ethanol from sugar cane as a transport fuel (link to A6); nuclear power at Angra dos Reis (one line).

Economic processes: secondary activities (patterns in manufacturing)
- [ ] M1 Colonial and export-cycle legacy (brief): brazilwood, sugar, gold, rubber and coffee were exported raw, so little manufacturing developed before 1900; coffee profits later financed São Paulo's first factories. Detail on colonialism belongs to `geo-economic-development-s3`.
- [ ] M2 Import substitution industrialisation (ISI): from the 1930s under Getúlio Vargas the state protected industry with tariffs and built heavy industry (the CSN steelworks at Volta Redonda; Petrobras); under Juscelino Kubitschek (1956–61) foreign car makers were invited into the ABC region of São Paulo.
- [ ] M3 The military period (1964–85): the "Brazilian miracle" of fast growth (about 1968–73), big state projects (Itaipu, Carajás, the Trans-Amazonian Highway), the founding of Embraer; then the debt crisis and hyperinflation of the 1980s.
- [ ] M4 Liberalisation and stabilisation: the Real Plan (1994) ended hyperinflation; privatisation of state firms in the 1990s (Vale, Embraer); Mercosur (1991) opened a regional market.
- [ ] M5 Factor — raw materials: iron ore of Minas Gerais and Carajás → steel; sugar cane → ethanol; soya and meat → food processing; eucalyptus → pulp and paper; oil → petrochemicals.
- [ ] M6 Factor — energy: cheap hydroelectricity (Itaipu supplies the Southeast industrial core); aluminium smelting in the Amazon powered by Tucuruí.
- [ ] M7 Factor — labour: a large, low-cost workforce; historic European and Japanese immigrant skills in São Paulo and the South; migrants from the Northeast to the ABC factories; skilled engineers at São José dos Campos (aerospace).
- [ ] M8 Factor — markets: a domestic market of over 200 million with a growing middle class; Mercosur; export markets.
- [ ] M9 Factor — capital and infrastructure: coffee wealth, foreign multinationals, state firms and the BNDES development bank (1952); the port of Santos, the largest in Latin America; the dense road network of the Southeast; weak infrastructure elsewhere.
- [ ] M10 Factor — government policy: ISI tariffs; the Manaus Free Trade Zone (1967) using tax breaks to bring electronics and motorbike assembly to the Amazon; SUDENE (1959) incentives for the Northeast (petrochemicals at Camaçari, Bahia).
- [ ] M11 The manufacturing pattern: heavy concentration in the Southeast "industrial triangle" of São Paulo, Rio de Janeiro and Belo Horizonte; São Paulo state alone produces the largest share; secondary clusters in the South (Porto Alegre, Curitiba, Joinville), Manaus and the Northeast coast. Explain the concentration (cumulative causation, a core region).
- [ ] M12 Key industries with named examples: cars (ABC region; Fiat/Stellantis at Betim); aircraft (Embraer, São José dos Campos); steel (CSN at Volta Redonda); oil and petrochemicals (Petrobras); meat processing (JBS); pulp and paper; shoes and textiles in the South.
- [ ] M13 Challenges: the "Brazil cost" (Custo Brasil) of high taxes, complex bureaucracy and poor transport; deindustrialisation since the 2010s and competition from China; Ford's closure of its Brazilian plants (2021); regional imbalance.

Economic processes: tertiary activities
- [ ] T1 Services are the largest sector of GDP and of employment (section 2); São Paulo as Latin America's leading financial centre (B3 stock exchange; Avenida Paulista).
- [ ] T2 Tourism attractions (exams ask for two named attractions): Rio de Janeiro (Christ the Redeemer, Sugarloaf Mountain, Copacabana and Ipanema beaches, Carnival); the Iguaçu Falls; the Amazon (Manaus, jungle lodges, the Teatro Amazonas); the Pantanal (wildlife, jaguars); Salvador (the Pelourinho, Afro-Brazilian culture); colonial Ouro Preto; Brasília's modernist architecture; Fernando de Noronha; the Lençóis Maranhenses dunes.
- [ ] T3 Why tourists come: tropical beaches and climate; Carnival and festivals; unique wildlife and landscapes; culture (music, football, food, Afro-Brazilian heritage); events such as the 2014 World Cup and 2016 Olympics.
- [ ] T4 Development and access: foreign arrivals are small for a country of its size (section 2), but domestic tourism is far larger; long-haul distance and flight costs; government marketing (Embratur); the mega-events brought airport and metro investment in Rio.
- [ ] T5 Positive impacts: jobs and income, foreign exchange, infrastructure, funding for conservation (eco-tourism in the Amazon and Pantanal), pride in culture.
- [ ] T6 Negative impacts and challenges: crime and safety fears; pollution (Guanabara Bay); environmental pressure on fragile places (visitor limits on Fernando de Noronha); costly stadiums left little used after the World Cup; evictions for Olympic projects; seasonality around Carnival.
- [ ] T7 Transport — road: roads carry most freight (section 2); the BR-116 coastal artery; the Trans-Amazonian Highway (BR-230, 1972), much still unpaved; the BR-163 soya route from Cuiabá to Santarém; roads open the Amazon to settlement and clearance (the fishbone pattern along roads).
- [ ] T8 Transport — rail, water and ports: a small rail network mostly used for ore (the Carajás railway); the Amazon and its tributaries as waterways; the Tietê–Paraná waterway; ports of Santos, Paranaguá and São Luís; the "Northern Arc" of Amazon ports for soya exports.
- [ ] T9 Transport — air and urban: great distances make air travel vital (São Paulo–Guarulhos is the busiest airport); Curitiba's bus rapid transit (1974) copied worldwide; São Paulo's metro and congestion (rodízio scheme, link to U4).
- [ ] T10 Importance of transport: integrates a continental country, lowers export costs for soya and ore, opens the interior (Brasília, the Centre-West), but road-building in the Amazon brings deforestation.

Human processes: population dynamics and distribution
- [ ] Y1 Size and growth: 2022 Census total; the census was delayed from 2020 to 2022; growth in the 2010s was the slowest ever recorded (section 2).
- [ ] Y2 Demographic transition: death rates fell from the mid-20th century (vaccination, clean water, medicine) while birth rates stayed high, giving fast growth; then fertility collapsed from about 6 children per woman (1960) to well below replacement (section 2) because of urbanisation, women's education and paid work, contraception and the cost of raising children in cities.
- [ ] Y3 Population structure: an ageing population; a shrinking base to the pyramid; rising old-age dependency; the 2019 pension reform; life expectancy (section 2).
- [ ] Y4 Population distribution (a frequent 30-mark question): the share of people in each macro-region (section 2); dense along the coast and in the Southeast; sparse in the Amazon and the interior; national density.
- [ ] Y5 Reasons for dense areas: colonial settlement from the sea ("clinging to the coast like crabs"), ports, fertile coastal soils (massapê, terra roxa), the industrial jobs and services of the Southeast, a cooler plateau climate.
- [ ] Y6 Reasons for sparse areas: the Amazon's dense forest, heat, humidity, leached soils, disease and poor access; the Centre-West's former isolation; the drought-prone sertão.
- [ ] Y7 Density contrasts: São Paulo state vs Amazonas or Roraima (section 2).
- [ ] Y8 Policies to move people inland: building Brasília (1960); the "March to the West"; Amazon colonisation along the Trans-Amazonian Highway in the 1970s ("a land without people for people without land") and in Rondônia along the BR-364 in the 1980s; many settlement schemes failed because of poor soils and distance from markets.
- [ ] Y9 Internal migration: nordestinos moving from the sertão to São Paulo and Rio de Janeiro (1950s–1980s), pushed by drought, poverty and land concentration and pulled by factory jobs; more recently to the Centre-West farm frontier and back to the Northeast.
- [ ] Y10 International migration: Portuguese settlers; about 4–5 million enslaved Africans; Italian, German and Japanese immigrants (from 1908); recent arrivals from Venezuela (Operation Welcome, 2018) and Haiti; Brazilian emigration to the USA, Portugal, Japan and Ireland (Gort, Co. Galway).

Human processes: ethnicity, language and religion
- [ ] E1 Ethnic composition (2022 Census figures, section 2): pardo (mixed) the largest group, white, Black (preto), Indigenous and Asian (amarelo); built from Indigenous peoples, Portuguese colonists, enslaved Africans and later immigrants.
- [ ] E2 Indigenous peoples: about 1.7 million people (2022) in hundreds of ethnic groups; Yanomami, Kayapó and Guarani as examples; demarcated Indigenous lands cover about one-eighth of Brazil, mostly in the Amazon; threats from mining, ranching and dams.
- [ ] E3 Racial inequality: Black and pardo Brazilians earn less on average and are over-represented in favelas; the idea of "racial democracy" is a claim, not a reality; racial quotas in federal universities (2012).
- [ ] L1 Portuguese: Brazil is the only Portuguese-speaking country in the Americas and the largest Portuguese-speaking country in the world; a legacy of the Treaty of Tordesillas (1494) and colonisation from 1500. One language unites a continental country, unlike India.
- [ ] L2 Brazilian Portuguese differs from European Portuguese in sound and vocabulary; it absorbed Tupi words (place names, plants and animals) and African words.
- [ ] L3 Indigenous languages: many languages survive, mostly in the Amazon (section 2), but most are endangered; some Amazon municipalities made Indigenous languages co-official (São Gabriel da Cachoeira, 2002).
- [ ] L4 Immigrant languages and other languages: German and Italian dialects in the South; Japanese in São Paulo; Brazilian Sign Language (Libras) recognised in 2002.
- [ ] L5 Language and the economy: Portuguese ties Brazil to Portugal and Lusophone Africa; few Brazilians speak English, a barrier for tourism and global business compared with India; Mercosur neighbours speak Spanish.
- [ ] G1 Religious composition (2010 Census figures, section 2): Roman Catholic majority, the largest Catholic population of any country; fast-growing evangelical (mainly Pentecostal) churches; a rising share with no religion; Spiritism.
- [ ] G2 Origins: Catholicism came with Portuguese colonisation and Jesuit missions (São Paulo was founded as a Jesuit mission in 1554).
- [ ] G3 Afro-Brazilian religions: Candomblé and Umbanda, rooted in West African beliefs, mixed (syncretism) with Catholic saints; strongest in Salvador, Bahia.
- [ ] G4 Change and conflict: the decline of Catholicism and the rise of Pentecostal churches, especially among the urban poor; evangelicals' growing political power; intolerance against Afro-Brazilian religions. Brazil has been a secular state since 1891.
- [ ] G5 Religion and economic life: Carnival (the festival before Lent) as a huge tourist and economic event; pilgrimage to Aparecida; the Círio de Nazaré procession in Belém; religious tourism.

Human processes: urban development
- [ ] U1 Urbanisation: the urban share in 1950 and today (section 2); causes: rural-urban migration, mechanisation of farming, land concentration, drought, industrial jobs.
- [ ] U2 The urban hierarchy: São Paulo and Rio de Janeiro as the two megacities, then regional capitals (Brasília, Salvador, Fortaleza, Belo Horizonte, Manaus); 2022 Census populations (section 2).
- [ ] U3 Case study of one urban area (exams ask "factors influencing the development of one urban area" and "reasons for the growth of an urban area"): **São Paulo** in full: site on the plateau behind the Serra do Mar, the Jesuit mission (1554), the Tietê river; growth through the coffee boom, the railway to Santos (1867), immigrants (Italians, Japanese in Liberdade), industrialisation and the ABC car plants, migration from the Northeast; now Brazil's financial and service capital.
- [ ] U4 São Paulo problems and responses: favelas and self-built housing on the urban edge (Heliópolis, Paraisópolis beside wealthy Morumbi); traffic (rodízio scheme, 1997) and air pollution; the 2014–15 water crisis (Cantareira system); flooding; inequality and crime. Responses: metro and bus corridors, favela upgrading, Minha Casa Minha Vida social housing (2009).
- [ ] U5 Rio de Janeiro (short): site on Guanabara Bay between mountains and sea; capital 1763–1960; port, tourism, oil services; hillside favelas (Rocinha); landslides in the mountain region (2011); Pacifying Police Units (from 2008).
- [ ] U6 Brasília (short): planned capital inaugurated 21 April 1960; Lúcio Costa's Pilot Plan and Oscar Niemeyer's buildings; built to open the interior; unplanned satellite cities for workers show segregation.
- [ ] U7 Manaus (short): river port in the heart of the Amazon; wealth from the rubber boom (Teatro Amazonas); revival through the Free Trade Zone; growth clearing nearby forest.
- [ ] U8 Curitiba (short): model of planning: bus rapid transit, parks on flood-prone land, recycling schemes.
- [ ] U9 Urban problems in general: favelas (2022 Census figure, section 2); informal jobs; poor sanitation; violence; gated communities beside favelas. Keep developing-world city theory to `geo-urban`.

Human processes: rural development
- [ ] V1 Rural Brazil: a small and falling share of the population, concentrated in the Northeast and the North.
- [ ] V2 Rural problems: poverty, especially in the Northeast sertão; drought; land concentration and landlessness; poor services (water, electricity, health, schools); rural violence over land; out-migration of young people.
- [ ] V3 Land reform and the MST: the Landless Workers' Movement (founded 1984) occupies unused estates to press for redistribution; INCRA settlements; slow and contested progress.
- [ ] V4 Rural development policies: Bolsa Família (2003), cash paid on condition that children attend school and are vaccinated; Brazil left the UN Hunger Map in 2014; One Million Cisterns (rainwater tanks in the sertão, from 2003); Luz para Todos ("Light for All", rural electrification, 2003); credit for family farms; SUDENE.
- [ ] V5 Contrast in outcomes: the agribusiness boom towns of Mato Grosso vs the poor sertão of the Northeast. Use this to show human processes interacting with economic and physical ones.

Interaction of processes (the syllabus statement)
- [ ] I1 Regional inequality within Brazil: the richer Southeast and South (the core) vs the poorer Northeast and North (the periphery); causes rooted in climate, soils, colonial history and the concentration of industry; regional agencies (SUDENE, SUDAM) and the Manaus Free Trade Zone as responses.
- [ ] I2 Chains of interaction to use in conclusions: coffee on terra roxa → wealth → São Paulo industry → migration from the drought-hit sertão → favelas; Cerrado liming and EMBRAPA → soya → BR-163 and northern ports → pressure on the Amazon; rainfall and plateau rivers → hydroelectricity → industry; Portuguese colonisation → language, religion and an export economy.

## 3. Traps (known errors)

- **The capital is Brasília, not Rio de Janeiro or São Paulo.** Rio was the capital until 1960; São Paulo is the largest and richest city.
- **Brazilians speak Portuguese, not Spanish.**
- **The Andes are not in Brazil.** The Amazon rises in the Peruvian Andes. Brazil has no active volcanoes (it is mid-plate). Brazil's highest peak is Pico da Neblina, not Aconcagua.
- **Shared features:** Itaipu is shared with Paraguay; the Iguaçu Falls with Argentina; the Pantanal extends into Bolivia and Paraguay; the Amazon basin spans several countries. Say so when naming them.
- **Amazon length:** don't call it "the longest river in the world" (disputed with the Nile). It is the largest by discharge and basin area. It ends in a wide estuary, not a large delta.
- **Not all of Brazil is rainforest.** The Amazon biome covers about half. The Northeast interior is semi-arid; the south is subtropical with frost.
- **Census:** the latest census is 2022, not 2020. Religion figures are from the 2010 Census; label them so.
- **Terra roxa** comes from basalt (Paraná Plateau); latosols are infertile; don't call Amazon soils fertile (except várzea and terra preta).
- **Bolsa Família is a cash-transfer scheme, not land reform.** The MST is a movement, not a government body.
- **Carnival** is the festival before Lent (Catholic calendar), not a harvest festival.
- **Favelas:** don't say all urban poor live in favelas, and don't give a population for any single favela.
- **Racial democracy is a myth, not a fact.** Don't say Brazil has no racism.
- **Neighbouring chapters:**
  - The rainforest biome (climate, soils, layers, adaptations) is `geo-option-geoecology-s4`; the felling of the Amazon (causes, impacts, responses) is `geo-option-geoecology-s5`. Here deforestation appears only as part of forestry, ranching and transport, in a few points.
  - Colonialism and Brazil's export cycles as a development issue are `geo-economic-development-s3`. Here give only the short background in M1.
  - Developing-world city theory is `geo-urban`; general migration theory is `geo-population-migration`. Use them only as applied to Brazil.
  - Gondwanaland and continental drift are `geo-plate-tectonics-s2`; one sentence only.
  - Defining climatic, language and religious regions in general is `geo-region-concept`.
- Don't add attractions, firms, dams or cities not in section 2, even well-known ones.

## 4. What exams ask (topics only)

The questions are set on "a continental/subcontinental region you have studied", so they are the
same for Brazil students as for India students.

- **Sketch/outline map** (Higher 20 marks, Ordinary 30 marks, almost every year): the outline of the region, a named relief feature (shown as an outline), the course of a named river, one or two named urban centres, and sometimes the outline of a named agricultural or industrial region. Marks for an accurate outline, for showing each feature and for naming it.
- **Physical processes** (Higher 30): how climate, relief, soils or drainage influence the region or its economic activity.
- **Agriculture / primary activity** (Higher 30): the factors influencing agriculture or primary activity (climate, relief and soils, technology such as EMBRAPA's work, markets, land ownership, government policy). Ordinary: name two types of agriculture or one primary activity, its advantages and one problem.
- **Secondary activity** (Higher 30, frequent): two factors that influenced manufacturing (raw materials, energy, markets, infrastructure, government policy, labour). Ordinary: name a secondary activity, its advantages, one challenge.
- **Tertiary activity** (Higher 30): the development of tourism or transport, or two factors influencing tourism. Ordinary: two types of transport and its importance; two named tourist attractions and why tourists come; positive and negative impacts of tourism.
- **Population distribution** (Higher 30, frequent): account for the distribution of population, with dense and sparse areas explained by physical and human factors.
- **Urban area** (Higher and Ordinary 30): factors that influenced the development of, or reasons for the growth of, one urban area in the region (São Paulo is the expected case study).
- **Human processes** (Higher 30): the impact of two of population dynamics, language, religion, urban development and rural development on the region's development.
- **Culture** (Ordinary 30, any region): the importance of religion, language and other cultural factors.

## 5. Practice questions to write

1. 20 marks, Higher: "Draw an outline map of Brazil. On it, show and name a feature of relief, the course of a river, two urban centres and an agricultural region." Model answer: a written description of the map using the recommended set in section 2 (the Brazilian Highlands, the Amazon, São Paulo and Rio de Janeiro, the Mato Grosso soya region), saying exactly where each feature goes and how to show it (outline, line, dot). No other features.
2. 30 marks, Higher: "Examine how two factors have influenced the development of secondary economic activity in Brazil." Model answer on **raw materials and energy** and **government policy only**, about 7–8 SRPs each: Minas Gerais and Carajás iron ore, CSN at Volta Redonda, sugar to ethanol, hydroelectricity from Itaipu; then ISI under Vargas, Kubitschek's car industry in the ABC region, the Manaus Free Trade Zone, SUDENE, the Real Plan and privatisation, with their effects.
3. 30 marks, Higher: "Account for the distribution of population in Brazil." Model answer: national density and the regional shares (2022 Census, section 2); dense areas (the Southeast, the coast, the Northeast sugar coast, the cities) each with reasons; sparse areas (the Amazon, the Centre-West before Brasília, the sertão) each with reasons; the São Paulo–Amazonas contrast; policies to move people inland. About 15 SRPs. No population-dynamics material (fertility, ageing) beyond one sentence.

Each model answer covers exactly what the question asks for, nothing appended.
