# Package: geo-economic-development-s1 — Measuring economic development

- **loId:** `geo-economic-development-s1` · **chapterId:** `geo-economic-development` · **syllabusRef:** 4.1
- **Syllabus:** Statement: "Economic activities are unevenly distributed over the earth."
  Students should study "gross national product as a measure of economic development" and "the
  human development index as a broad measure of development." National setting: "Ireland."
  International settings: "Appropriate relevant European and global examples." GDP is not named
  in the syllabus but is taught alongside GNP (SimpleStudy) and appears in exam data questions.
- **Elective:** Elective Unit 4 (Economic). Students take this **or** Elective Unit 5, both
  levels.

  `course-outlines/NOTES_STANDARD.md`.

## 1. Must cover (one bullet or block for every item; tick each off)

What "development" means
- [ ] D1 Economic development: growth in a country's output and income, with rising living standards. Development is wider: health, education, freedom, equality and quality of life. One bullet each; the distinction is the reason HDI exists.
- [ ] D2 Developed (high-income) and developing (low- and middle-income) economies; the World Bank classes countries as low, lower-middle, upper-middle or high income by GNI per head (thresholds change each year: give none). Newly industrialised countries (NICs) sit between.

GDP, GNP and GNI
- [ ] G1 Gross domestic product (GDP): the total value of all goods and services produced within a country's borders in one year, whoever owns the firms.
- [ ] G2 Gross national product (GNP): GDP plus income earned abroad by the country's residents and firms, minus income sent out of the country to foreign owners (profits, interest, dividends). It measures the output owned by the nation's residents.
- [ ] G3 Gross national income (GNI): the World Bank and UN now use GNI, which is essentially the same as GNP. Say so in one bullet so students can read data tables that use either term.
- [ ] G4 Per capita (per head): the total divided by the population; needed to compare countries of different size. A large total GNP (e.g. India) can hide a low GNP per head.
- [ ] G5 Measured in US dollars for comparison; purchasing power parity (PPP) adjusts for differences in prices between countries, so a dollar buys more in a low-cost country.
- [ ] G6 GDP growth rate (% change per year) shows whether an economy is expanding or in recession (two quarters of falling output). Exams ask for one benefit of economic growth: more jobs, higher incomes, more tax revenue for services and infrastructure, less emigration.
- [ ] G7 Advantages of GNP/GDP per head as a measure, one bullet each: simple, a single comparable figure; data available for almost every country, collected in a standard way; allows change over time to be tracked; closely linked to a government's ability to fund services.
- [ ] G8 Disadvantages, one bullet each: it is an average and hides inequality between rich and poor and between regions; it ignores the informal economy and subsistence farming (large in developing countries) and unpaid work (caring, housework, volunteering); it counts harmful spending (pollution clean-up, disaster repair, crime) as growth; it ignores environmental damage and resource depletion; exchange-rate swings distort dollar comparisons (unless PPP is used); it says nothing directly about health, education, freedom or happiness.
- [ ] G9 Ireland's special case (the syllabus's national setting): because multinationals own much of Irish output and send their profits abroad, Irish GNP has long been well below GDP. In 2015 Irish GDP jumped by about a quarter in one year, mainly because multinationals moved intellectual property (patents) and aircraft-leasing assets to Ireland, not because Irish people became richer ("leprechaun economics"). The CSO now publishes modified GNI (GNI*), which strips out these effects and is the better measure of Irish living standards. Ireland's GDP per head is one of the highest in the world, so GDP overstates Irish prosperity.

The human development index (HDI)
- [ ] H1 Origin: published by the United Nations Development Programme (UNDP) in its annual Human Development Report since 1990; designed by Mahbub ul Haq with Amartya Sen to measure people's well-being, not just income.
- [ ] H2 Three dimensions and four indicators: a long and healthy life (life expectancy at birth); knowledge (mean years of schooling of adults aged 25 and over, and expected years of schooling for children starting school); a decent standard of living (GNI per head, PPP). Exams ask students to name two or three of these.
- [ ] H3 How it is calculated (in outline only): each dimension is turned into an index from 0 to 1, and the three are combined into one value between 0 and 1; the closer to 1, the higher the development.
- [ ] H4 The four categories: very high (0.800 and above), high (0.700–0.799), medium (0.550–0.699), low (below 0.550).
- [ ] H5 The pattern of HDI: highest in Europe (especially Scandinavia and Switzerland), North America, Australia, Japan; lowest mostly in sub-Saharan Africa. Ireland ranks near the top (section 2). One example of each category from section 2.
- [ ] H6 Advantages of HDI, one bullet each: it is broader than income alone, combining health, education and income; it focuses on people and outcomes; it shows countries whose income is high but human development lags (oil-rich states) and countries that achieve good health and education on modest incomes; it can be tracked over time, showing progress or decline; it helps governments and aid agencies target spending (e.g. on schooling or health).
- [ ] H7 Disadvantages of HDI, one bullet each: a national average that hides inequality between rich and poor, men and women, regions and ethnic groups; leaves out political freedom, human rights, gender equality, environmental sustainability, safety and happiness; relies on data that are poor or out of date in some developing countries; is published about two years after the data year; the three dimensions are weighted equally, which is a judgement; very high-income countries cluster close to 1, so small differences mean little.
- [ ] H8 Newer UNDP measures that answer some criticisms (one bullet listing them): the inequality-adjusted HDI, the Gender Inequality Index and the Multidimensional Poverty Index (all from 2010).
- [ ] H9 GNP vs HDI compared (the 30-mark question): GNP measures output and income; HDI measures human outcomes. A country can rank much higher on one than the other (example in section 2). Using both, plus other indicators, gives the fullest picture. Write this as paired points, not a second list of the same pros and cons.

Other indicators of development (data-response questions)
- [ ] O1 Social indicators: life expectancy, infant and under-five mortality, literacy rate, years of schooling, doctors per 1,000 people, access to safe water and sanitation, daily calorie intake.
- [ ] O2 Economic indicators: share of workers in the primary sector (high in developing economies), unemployment rate, energy use per head, internet and phone access. One bullet listing them.
- [ ] O3 Multiple indicators together give a more reliable picture than any one measure.
- [ ] O4 Why life expectancy differs between countries (exams ask for one or two reasons): access to health care, doctors and hospitals; vaccination; clean water and sanitation; nutrition; infectious disease (malaria, HIV/AIDS) in tropical and poorer countries; lifestyle (diet, smoking, alcohol, obesity) in rich countries; war and conflict; income and poverty.
- [ ] O5 Why years of schooling differ: government spending on schools and teachers; poverty forcing children to work or stay at home; distance to schools in rural areas; fees and costs of uniforms and books; cultural attitudes to girls' education and early marriage; conflict closing schools.
- [ ] O6 Why an educated workforce benefits an economy (exams ask for one): it attracts foreign investment in high-tech and service industries; higher productivity and wages, so more tax revenue; more innovation and new firms; healthier, smaller families. Ireland example in section 2.
- [ ] O7 Unemployment as an indicator: one disadvantage of high unemployment (lost income and poverty, emigration of young people, lower tax revenue and higher welfare costs, social problems) and one way to reduce it (attract investment, retraining and education, support for small firms, infrastructure spending, tourism promotion). One bullet each with two or three named effects/remedies.
- [ ] O8 Skill: drawing a bar chart (or pie chart) from a table of development data: title, labelled axes with units, suitable scale, accurate bars, key. Use invented, labelled illustrative values only (say "illustrative"). Calculating a difference between two values in a table. One bullet each; detailed graph method is in `geo-graph-skills`.

## 3. Traps (known errors)

- **GDP vs GNP:** GDP = produced inside the country (location); GNP = owned by the country's residents (ownership). In Ireland GNP is **lower** than GDP because profits flow out to foreign owners. Don't swap them.
- **HDI's income indicator is GNI per head (PPP)**, not GDP and not "wealth". Older textbooks list "adult literacy" and "gross enrolment"; since 2010 the education indicators are mean and expected years of schooling. Use the current ones; at most one bullet saying the older ones were replaced.
- HDI values run from **0 to 1**, not 0 to 100. A higher value means more developed.
- **Report year vs data year:** the Human Development Report 2020 uses 2019 data. Never mix them; quote rankings exactly as in section 2.
- Don't say Ireland "is the richest country in the world" or give Irish GDP per head in dollars; say GDP per head is among the highest and overstates living standards (GNI*).
- Don't call Luxembourg's or Ireland's high GDP per head proof of the highest living standards: both are inflated (cross-border commuters in Luxembourg; multinationals in Ireland). One bullet at most; don't give a figure.
- Don't copy any figure from past-paper data tables (life expectancy by country, HDI of Bolivia, schooling in Cameroon, GNI of Australia, unemployment in Poland, EU STEM rankings etc.). Use invented illustrative values for graph and calculation practice.
- Past-paper model answers in the question bank contain invented statistics (e.g. doctors per 1,000 in Africa, STEM salary percentages, tertiary-education rates). Never use them.
- **Neighbouring subsections:**
  - Why development varies between and within countries (physical, social, cultural, political factors) and change over time are `geo-economic-development-s2`. Here give only the short reasons tied to individual indicators (O4–O7).
  - The developing-economy case study (India, Brazil, Sudan) is `-s3`; justice issues (aid, debt, fair trade) are `-s4`.
  - Ireland's economy in depth (footloose industry, financial services, tourism, MNCs, corporation tax) is `geo-economic-activities`. Here use Ireland only for GDP/GNP/GNI* and its HDI rank.
  - Graph-drawing method in full is `geo-graph-skills`; census and population indicators (birth and death rates, dependency) are `geo-population-migration`.

## 4. What exams ask (topics only)

- Higher 30 marks (most recent years): discuss GNP and HDI as measures of economic development, with examples. Expected: definitions; what each measures; advantages and disadvantages of each; Ireland's GDP/GNP gap as an example; HDI components and categories; one or two examples of countries ranking differently on the two.
- Short questions: name two or three HDI indicators; one advantage and one disadvantage of the HDI; two reasons some countries have a higher HDI than others; one advantage of a country having a high GNP; one benefit of economic growth.
- Data-response (both levels): read values from tables of HDI, GNI per head, life expectancy, schooling, unemployment or GDP growth; calculate differences; identify highest/lowest; draw a bar chart; give reasons why life expectancy or schooling is lower in some countries; why an educated workforce helps an economy; one disadvantage of high unemployment and one way to reduce it; why some countries' GDP is higher.
- A link question: why countries with the highest expected years of schooling also have the highest GNI (two-way link: wealth funds schools; education raises productivity and incomes).
