# Package: geo-option-geoecology-s3 — Human interference with soils

- **loId:** `geo-option-geoecology-s3` · **chapterId:** `geo-option-geoecology` · **syllabusRef:** 7.2
- **Syllabus:** Students should make a general study of "human interference with soil
  characteristics including: – over-cropping and over-grazing – desertification and
  conservation." National settings: "Irish examples e.g. the Burren." International settings:
  "Appropriate global examples, e.g. the Sahel, Iceland."
- **Level:** Higher Level only; 80-mark essays. Each of the three syllabus settings (the Burren,
  the Sahel, Iceland) is a full case study, plus the west of Ireland hill commonage and the
  Dust Bowl reused from `geo-rocks-weathering-s7`. Exams also ask for **positive** as well as
  negative human impacts, so conservation and soil improvement get equal weight.
- **Prepared by:** a higher model, 2026-10-20. The writer works only from this package and
  `course-outlines/NOTES_STANDARD.md`.

## 1. Must cover (one bullet or block for every item; tick each off)

The idea
- [ ] I1 Soil forms very slowly but can be lost quickly, so on a human timescale it is a non-renewable resource. Human activity can degrade soil (negative) or conserve and improve it (positive).

Overcropping (its own block; a student must be able to answer on it alone)
- [ ] OC1 Definition: growing crops on the same land too intensively, without fallow, rotation or enough organic matter returned, so the soil is exhausted.
- [ ] OC2 Effects on characteristics: nutrients removed faster than they are replaced; humus content falls; crumb structure breaks down; the soil becomes dusty or compacted, holds less water, and is easily eroded by wind and rain; yields fall. Monoculture (one crop year after year) takes the same nutrients each time and encourages pests.
- [ ] OC3 Case study: the Dust Bowl, 1930s (reuse; one compact block): prairie grassland ploughed for wheat, then drought and wind erosion.
- [ ] OC4 The Sahel: cash crops and shorter fallow periods exhaust soils (part of the Sahel case study below).

Overgrazing (its own block)
- [ ] OG1 Definition: keeping more animals on land than its vegetation can support (exceeding its carrying capacity).
- [ ] OG2 Effects on characteristics: vegetation cover is eaten down and roots die, so soil is exposed to rain-splash and wind; trampling compacts the soil (poaching on wet Irish ground), reducing infiltration and increasing runoff; sheet, rill and gully erosion remove topsoil and humus; less litter means less humus; on peat, bare surfaces erode quickly.
- [ ] OG3 Irish case study: hill commonage in the west of Ireland after headage payments (reuse from `geo-rocks-weathering-s7`).
- [ ] OG4 International: the Sahel and Iceland (case studies below).

Desertification (block, then the Sahel case study)
- [ ] D1 Definition: land degradation in arid, semi-arid and dry sub-humid areas resulting from climatic variations and human activities. It is the degradation of land on desert margins, not the advance of sand dunes from an existing desert.
- [ ] D2 Causes: physical (drought, unreliable rainfall, high temperatures and evaporation) and human (population growth, overgrazing, overcropping, cutting trees and shrubs for fuelwood, poorly managed irrigation causing salinisation). Usually the two act together: human pressure leaves the soil bare, then drought and storms remove it.
- [ ] D3 Effects on soil characteristics: loss of topsoil and humus; lower fertility; surface crusting so rain runs off rather than soaking in; reduced water retention; wind-blown sand and dust; salt crusts where irrigation is poorly managed (salinisation); in extreme cases bare, sterile ground.
- [ ] D4 Human effects: falling yields, food shortages and famine, loss of livestock, migration to cities and abroad.
- [ ] D5 Sahel case study in full (location, climate, causes, effects, responses, evaluation).

Other negative human impacts (one bullet each)
- [ ] N1 Deforestation exposes soil to erosion and stops the supply of litter (the felling of rainforest is s5; mass movement link in `geo-rocks-weathering-s7`).
- [ ] N2 Heavy machinery and livestock on wet ground compact soil and destroy structure.
- [ ] N3 Excess fertiliser and slurry: nitrates and phosphates wash out of the soil into rivers and lakes (eutrophication); acidification from some fertilisers.
- [ ] N4 Salinisation and waterlogging under badly managed irrigation.
- [ ] N5 Peat extraction removes an entire soil (Irish bogs; s5 owns the Bord na Móna case study).
- [ ] N6 Urban sprawl and roads seal soil under concrete and tarmac.

Conservation and positive impacts (each method: how it works and which soil characteristic it protects)
- [ ] CM1 Contour ploughing: ploughing across a slope along the contours so furrows trap water instead of channelling it downhill.
- [ ] CM2 Terracing of steep slopes into level steps (reuse Banaue).
- [ ] CM3 Strip cropping: alternate strips of crops and grass, so grass traps soil washed or blown from the cropped strip.
- [ ] CM4 Shelter belts and windbreaks of trees and hedges to cut wind speed and wind erosion.
- [ ] CM5 Crop rotation, fallow, cover crops and green manure (e.g. clover) to restore nutrients and humus; adding manure and compost.
- [ ] CM6 Reduced or zero tillage and mulching: leaving crop residue on the surface to protect it.
- [ ] CM7 Controlled stocking and rotational grazing; fencing; reseeding.
- [ ] CM8 Afforestation and replanting to bind soil.
- [ ] CM9 Dryland methods: stone lines (bunds) along contours and zaï planting pits; farmer-managed natural regeneration of trees (Sahel case study).
- [ ] CM10 Improving soils: liming acid soils; field drainage and arterial drainage of gleys; deep ploughing to break an iron pan; irrigation with proper drainage; building soil where none existed (the Aran Islands).
- [ ] CM11 Policy: Irish agri-environment schemes and EU rules (REPS, GLAS, ACRES; the Nitrates Directive).
- [ ] CM12 Evaluation: conservation works best when farmers gain from it and take part (the Burren and Sahel examples); positive and negative impacts can occur on the same land at different times (Iceland).

Case studies (each its own block or blocks with the facts in section 2)
- [ ] B1 The Burren (Irish setting): early farmers, thin rendzinas, winterage, undergrazing and scrub, the BurrenLIFE project and the results-based Burren Programme.
- [ ] B2 The Sahel (desertification setting).
- [ ] B3 Iceland (overgrazing and conservation setting).
- [ ] B4 West of Ireland hill commonage (overgrazing, reused).
- [ ] B5 The Dust Bowl (overcropping, reused).

## 2. Checked facts and examples (the only named places, dates and figures you may use)

Do not name any place, date or figure that is not on this list. Give no soil-loss tonnages,
famine death tolls, livestock numbers or desert-advance rates: none are checked.

| Item | Example(s) |
|---|---|
| I1 | The UN Food and Agriculture Organization (FAO) has stated that it can take up to 1,000 years to form 1 cm of soil (quoted for the International Year of Soils, 2015). |
| B1 setting | The Burren, north-west Co. Clare: Carboniferous limestone; extensive limestone pavement of clints and grikes; thin, dark, lime-rich rendzina soils, mainly in grikes and hollows (reuse `geo-region-concept-s1`). Plants from Arctic-alpine and Mediterranean environments grow side by side, e.g. the spring gentian and mountain avens. |
| B1 early farming | Poulnabrone, a Neolithic portal tomb (dolmen) on the Burren, is over 5,000 years old: evidence of early farming communities. The widely taught explanation is that the Burren once had a thicker soil and woodland cover, and that clearance and grazing by early farmers, followed by erosion of soil into the grikes and underground, helped expose the bare pavement. Present this as the accepted textbook explanation ("it is thought that"), not as proven fact. The Burren was also glaciated: don't say ice never reached it. |
| B1 winterage | Winterage: cattle are grazed on the upland limestone in winter, because the rock stores heat and the ground is dry; grazing keeps grass and scrub down and maintains the flower-rich grassland. |
| B1 undergrazing | From the late 20th century fewer farmers grazed the uplands in winter (farm changes, supplementary feeding, labour shortage), so hazel and blackthorn scrub spread over grassland and pavement. On the Burren the main threat is **undergrazing**, not overgrazing. |
| B1 BurrenLIFE | BurrenLIFE (2005–2010), an EU LIFE-funded project led by Dr Brendan Dunford with local farmers, the National Parks and Wildlife Service and Teagasc, tested ways of farming for conservation: removing scrub, better feeding systems, and grazing plans. It was followed by the Burren Farming for Conservation Programme (2010–2015) and the Burren Programme (from 2016), funded by the Department of Agriculture. These pay farmers according to results: each field is scored for habitat quality (e.g. on a scale of 1 to 10) and a higher score earns a higher payment, so farmers decide how best to manage their land. The model has been copied in other Irish agri-environment schemes. |
| B4 / OG3 | From `geo-rocks-weathering-s7`: from the 1980s, EU (CAP) headage payments paid farmers per animal, so sheep numbers rose sharply on hill commonage in the west of Ireland, including Connemara, Co. Galway, and the mountains of Co. Mayo, Co. Donegal and Co. Kerry. Overgrazing stripped heather and grass, exposed and eroded blanket peat, and sent silt into rivers, harming salmon and trout spawning grounds. Responses: payments changed from per-head to per-area; Commonage Framework Plans (from the early 2000s) set lower stocking rates; REPS (the Rural Environment Protection Scheme, introduced in 1994) paid farmers to farm in environmentally friendly ways. |
| CM11 | REPS 1994; GLAS (Green, Low-Carbon, Agri-Environment Scheme) from 2015; ACRES (Agri-Climate Rural Environment Scheme) from 2023. The EU Nitrates Directive (1991) limits how much organic nitrogen can be spread per hectare and bans spreading slurry during a closed period in winter. |
| B5 / OC3 | From `geo-rocks-weathering-s7`: the Dust Bowl, Great Plains of the USA (including Oklahoma and Kansas), 1930s: the natural grassland was ploughed up for wheat; severe drought then left bare, dry soil that strong winds blew away in huge dust storms; many farming families were forced to leave. The US Soil Conservation Service was set up in 1935; shelter belts of trees, contour ploughing and crop rotation were promoted. The prairie soils are chernozems (s2). |
| CM2 | From `geo-rocks-weathering-s7`: rice terraces of Banaue, Luzon, Philippines: terracing of steep slopes, over 2,000 years old. |
| CM10 Aran | On the bare limestone of the Aran Islands, Co. Galway, islanders made soil by layering sand and seaweed (and later manure) on the rock, inside stone-walled fields. |
| CM10 drainage | The Arterial Drainage Act, 1945, began state schemes to deepen and straighten Irish rivers to drain wet farmland. |
| N4 | From `geo-region-continental-s1`: salinisation and waterlogging under canal irrigation in Punjab and Haryana, India. |
| B2 location and climate | The Sahel: the semi-arid belt along the southern edge of the Sahara, stretching across Africa from Senegal and Mauritania in the west through Mali, Burkina Faso, Niger and Chad to Sudan. Annual rainfall roughly 200–600 mm, falling in one short summer wet season when the ITCZ (the rain belt) moves north; rainfall is highly unreliable from year to year. |
| B2 causes | From `geo-rocks-weathering-s7`: population growth led to more cattle, goats and sheep concentrated around wells and boreholes, shorter fallow periods, more cash crops and more fuelwood cutting; with droughts (notably in the 1970s and 1980s), the bare soil was eroded by wind and by the heavy rain of the short wet season. Cash crops such as groundnuts (peanuts) in Senegal and cotton took the best land, pushing food crops and herders onto marginal land. Nomadic herders, whose movements once let pasture recover, became more settled. |
| B2 Lake Chad | Lake Chad, on the Sahel's border of Chad, Niger, Nigeria and Cameroon, has shrunk dramatically since the 1960s (a widely quoted UN figure is by about 90%), through drought and water use for irrigation. Use only "about 90%, widely quoted" if a figure is given. |
| B2 responses: UN | The UN Convention to Combat Desertification (UNCCD) was adopted in 1994; 17 June is World Day to Combat Desertification. |
| B2 responses: Great Green Wall | The Great Green Wall, launched by the African Union in 2007: a planned band of restored land about 8,000 km long across Africa from Senegal to Djibouti, aiming to restore 100 million hectares of degraded land by 2030. Progress has been slow and well short of target; the project has shifted from a line of planted trees towards a mosaic of local land-restoration projects. |
| B2 responses: local | Burkina Faso (Yatenga region, northern Burkina Faso): farmers such as Yacouba Sawadogo revived the traditional zaï technique (small pits dug in hard, crusted soil and filled with manure or compost, which catch rainwater and concentrate nutrients around the seed) and built stone lines (bunds) along the contours to slow runoff and trap soil. Niger: farmer-managed natural regeneration (FMNR), in which farmers protect and prune tree shoots that regrow from old stumps and roots in their fields, has regreened about 5 million hectares since the 1980s, especially in the Maradi and Zinder regions. Trees shade the soil, cut wind speed, add leaf litter and, with species such as Faidherbia albida, add nitrogen. |
| B3 Iceland setting | Iceland was settled by Norse people from about AD 874. At settlement birch woodland and scrub covered a large part of the island (estimates are about a quarter or more); today native birch woodland covers only about 1–2%. Settlers cut and burned the birch for fuel, charcoal (for iron working) and grazing land, and kept large numbers of sheep, which grazed the year round and prevented regrowth. Iceland's soils are andosols formed from volcanic ash: light, loose, low in clay, so once the vegetation cover is broken they are easily blown away by strong winds and washed out by rain. Erosion escarpments (in Icelandic, rofabörð), steep banks of soil being eaten away from their edges, are typical. Volcanic eruptions add ash that smothers vegetation. A cold climate and short growing season mean vegetation recovers very slowly. |
| B3 Iceland responses | The Soil Conservation Service of Iceland (Landgræðslan, now part of Land and Forest Iceland) was founded in 1907, one of the oldest soil conservation services in the world. Methods: fencing off eroding land to keep sheep out; sowing lyme grass (Leymus arenarius) to stabilise drifting sand; sowing grasses with fertiliser from the air; planting Nootka lupin (which fixes nitrogen but has become an invasive plant that crowds out native species, so it is controversial); replanting birch; farmer-led reclamation projects ("Farmers Heal the Land", from 1990). Sheep numbers have fallen from their peak in the late 1970s. Use the dates given here only. |
| B3 positive vs negative | Iceland shows both sides: centuries of negative impact (deforestation, overgrazing, erosion) and over a century of positive impact (conservation and reclamation). |

## 3. Traps (known errors)

- The Burren's present problem is **undergrazing** and scrub encroachment, not overgrazing. Grazing is part of the solution there. Don't give the Burren's or the national park's area: sources disagree.
- Desertification is land degradation on desert margins, not the Sahara "advancing south" by a fixed distance each year. Don't give advance rates (they are unreliable).
- Don't give death tolls for the 1970s and 1980s Sahel droughts or famines: estimates vary widely and none is checked.
- Don't claim the Great Green Wall has succeeded; describe the aim and the mixed progress.
- Iceland's figures are estimates; use "about a quarter or more" and "about 1–2%" only. Don't give eroded-area percentages.
- Nootka lupin is a double-edged example: it fixes nitrogen and binds soil but spreads invasively. Say both.
- The Dust Bowl and Banaue are reused facts; don't add new detail. Deflation and wind processes belong to `geo-rocks-weathering-s6`; the mass-movement side of overgrazing and deforestation belongs to `geo-rocks-weathering-s7`.
- Desertification as a **global environmental issue** (its global causes and impacts on people, alongside deforestation and global warming) belongs to the Global Interdependence option (`geo-option-global-interdependence`). Here keep the focus on **soil characteristics**. Drought and desertification under climate and economic development belong to `geo-option-atmosphere-ocean`.
- The felling of tropical rainforests, intensive agriculture and industrial development as **alterations of biomes** belong to `geo-option-geoecology-s5`. Here name deforestation only for its effect on soil.
- Bord na Móna and peat extraction belong to s5; one bullet only here.
- The soil processes (erosion, leaching, salinisation) are explained in s2; refer to them by name.

## 4. What exams ask (topics only)

- How overgrazing, desertification and conservation methods each impact on soil characteristics (asked as one 80-mark question in several years): three aspects, each tied to named soil characteristics and a case study.
- How desertification and conservation have impacted on soil: the Sahel (causes, effects on soil, local and international responses) plus conservation methods.
- How human activity can have both positive and negative impacts on soil (asked in several years, including the most recent): negative (overgrazing, overcropping, desertification, deforestation, compaction, pollution) and positive (conservation methods, liming, drainage, reclamation, schemes), with Irish and global examples.

## 5. Practice questions to write

1. 80 marks: "Examine how overgrazing, desertification and conservation methods each affect soil characteristics." Model answer: three aspects in turn: overgrazing (west of Ireland commonage and Iceland), desertification (the Sahel), conservation (methods with the Sahel's zaï pits and stone lines, Iceland's reclamation, the Burren Programme). About 22 points; each point names the soil characteristic changed.
2. 80 marks: "Discuss how human activity can have both positive and negative impacts on soil." Model answer: negative (overcropping with the Dust Bowl, overgrazing, desertification) and positive (conservation methods, liming and drainage in Ireland, results-based farming in the Burren, Iceland's Soil Conservation Service). Balanced roughly half and half.
3. 80 marks: "Examine how desertification and conservation have impacted on soil, with reference to a region you have studied." Model answer: the Sahel only, from location and climate through causes, soil effects and responses to an evaluation.
