## 1. Must cover (one bullet or block for every item; tick each off)

The idea
- [ ] P1 Economic growth (rising GDP) does not automatically bring human development (health, education, freedom, choice); growth can be captured by elites. Empowerment means giving people control over resources (land, credit, income) and a voice in decisions, so that growth reaches them. One short opening block.

National debt and the cycle of poverty
- [ ] D1 How developing countries became indebted: oil-price rises in the 1970s; banks lent "petrodollars"; interest rates soared about 1980 and commodity prices fell; Mexico's default (1982) started the debt crisis; loans to dictators and for prestige projects (reuse `geo-economic-development-s4` D1).
- [ ] D2 The cycle of poverty explained step by step: debt repayments take government revenue → less spent on health, schools, water and infrastructure → poor health and education keep productivity and incomes low → low tax revenue and export earnings → more borrowing → more debt. Pressure to export cash crops and raw materials to earn foreign currency (land taken from food crops; environment damaged). A cycle diagram is expected.
- [ ] D3 Structural adjustment programmes: IMF and World Bank conditions (cut public spending, introduce fees for schools and clinics, privatise, remove food subsidies, devalue, open to imports); effects on the poorest and on women; Zambia's copper mines privatised. Reuse elective wording.
- [ ] D4 Debt relief: Jubilee 2000; HIPC (1996) and MDRI (2005); savings tied to poverty reduction. Results with examples: Uganda (first to receive HIPC relief, universal primary education), Tanzania (abolished primary school fees), Zambia (free basic education and rural health care after reaching completion point).
- [ ] D5 Debt today: rising again in the 2020s; Zambia's default (2020) and restructuring; Sri Lanka's default and collapse (2022) and Ghana's (2022); 3.3 billion people live in countries that spend more on interest than on health or education (UNCTAD). Chinese lending (Belt and Road) and the "debt-trap" debate (Hambantota port).
- [ ] D6 Ireland's experience of debt (national example): the 2008 crash, the EU–IMF bailout (2010) and austerity (cuts to services, tax rises, emigration of young people), exit in 2013. Shows debt's weight even in a rich country.
- [ ] D7 "Odious debt" and the justice argument (one bullet).

The aid debate: who benefits?
- [ ] A1 Types of aid, one bullet each: emergency/humanitarian; long-term development; bilateral; multilateral; NGO/voluntary; tied; loans vs grants (reuse elective A1).
- [ ] A2 The 0.7% of GNI target and who meets it (reuse); recent cuts by big donors (UK cut; USAID dismantled 2025) showing aid depends on donor politics.
- [ ] A3 How aid benefits recipients: emergency relief saves lives; schools, clinics, wells, vaccination; training; support for women's groups and small businesses; infrastructure.
- [ ] A4 How aid benefits donors (the "who benefits?" core): tied aid returns money to donor firms (UK Pergau Dam case); aid used to win political allies, trade deals and influence (Cold War aid; China's lending and the EU's Global Gateway as competing offers); food aid disposing of donor surpluses and supporting donor farmers and shipping; consultants' fees.
- [ ] A5 How aid can harm recipients: corruption and elite capture; dependency; food aid and subsidised exports undercutting local farmers (Haiti rice); loans adding to debt; large projects displacing people; unsuitable technology.
- [ ] A6 The debate in named arguments: Jeffrey Sachs (more aid can end extreme poverty), Dambisa Moyo ("Dead Aid": aid breeds corruption and dependency in Africa), William Easterly (top-down planners fail; bottom-up "searchers" succeed). Effective aid: untied, targeted at the poorest, locally owned, transparent, supporting self-help.
- [ ] A7 Irish aid programmes (national setting): Irish Aid (Department of Foreign Affairs; bilateral programme since 1974; untied; partner countries mainly in sub-Saharan Africa; "A Better World" 2019 priorities: the furthest behind first, gender equality, climate, governance); Ireland has not reached 0.7%. 5–6 bullets.
- [ ] A8 EU aid programmes (international setting): the EU and its member states together are the world's largest aid donor ("Team Europe"); the Lomé Convention (1975), Cotonou Agreement (2000) and Samoa Agreement (2023) with African, Caribbean and Pacific states; ECHO humanitarian aid (1992); Everything But Arms (2001); Global Gateway (2021). Critique: EU farm and fishing policies have at times undermined the same countries.

The role of NGOs
- [ ] N1 What NGOs are: non-profit, non-governmental organisations funded by donations, governments and the EU; independent of governments.
- [ ] N2 Their roles, one bullet each: emergency relief; long-term development (water, health, education, farming); empowering communities (co-operatives, women's groups, microcredit); advocacy and campaigning on justice (trade, debt, climate, human rights); development education in Ireland; monitoring human rights.
- [ ] N3 Irish NGOs (national setting), each with founding facts from section 2 and one line on its work: Concern Worldwide, Trócaire, GOAL, Self Help Africa (with Gorta), Bóthar; Dóchas as the umbrella body.
- [ ] N4 International NGOs: Oxfam, Médecins Sans Frontières, Amnesty International, the Red Cross. One bullet each.
- [ ] N5 Advantages of NGO work: close to communities, bottom-up, small-scale and appropriate, fast in emergencies, not tied to donor trade interests, reach places governments can't.
- [ ] N6 Limits and criticisms: depend on donations and government grants (donor priorities), small scale, can duplicate or replace state services, accountability and scandal (Oxfam Haiti 2018), use of negative images (link to s1), foreign staff vs local leadership.

Land ownership patterns and their impact on development
- [ ] L1 Why land matters: in rural societies land is the main source of food, income, security, credit (as collateral) and status; who owns it decides who benefits from growth.
- [ ] L2 Nineteenth-century Ireland (national setting, full block): the landlord system after the plantations (a small, mainly Protestant landlord class, many absentee); tenants-at-will with no security; rack-rents and evictions; subdivision into tiny holdings and conacre; dependence on the potato; the Great Famine (1845–49) and its toll; the Encumbered Estates Act (1849) and consolidation; emigration.
- [ ] L3 Land reform in Ireland: the Land League (1879; Davitt and Parnell) and the "Three Fs" (fair rent, fixity of tenure, free sale); the Land War and boycotting (Captain Boycott, 1880); Land Acts 1870 and 1881 (the Three Fs); the Ashbourne Act (1885) and Wyndham Act (1903) let tenants buy their farms with government loans repaid by annuities; the Congested Districts Board (1891) in the west; the Land Commission. Outcome: by the early 20th century most farmers owned their land (a peasant proprietorship); security encouraged investment, but many farms remained small and fragmented, and the west stayed poor. Link to empowerment: ownership gave tenants security, income and political power.
- [ ] L4 Latin America: latifundia and minifundia; Brazil's land concentration (reuse) and the MST; land conflicts in the Amazon.
- [ ] L5 Africa: colonial land seizure (South Africa's Natives Land Act 1913; Kenya's "White Highlands"); post-independence and post-apartheid reform; Zimbabwe's fast-track land seizures (2000) and the collapse of farm output (reuse) as an example of badly managed reform.
- [ ] L6 Successful land reform and growth: South Korea (1950) and Taiwan ("land to the tiller", 1953) gave farmers land and raised rural incomes before industrialisation; Kerala's land reforms (abolishing tenancy) contributed to its high human development (literacy reuse).
- [ ] L7 Women and land: women do much of the farm work in developing countries but own little land, so they cannot get credit or make farm decisions (FAO figures in section 2).
- [ ] L8 Land grabbing today: large foreign purchases or leases of farmland in poor countries (one bullet, no new named cases).
- [ ] L9 Co-operatives (national and European setting): pooling land, labour and capital to empower small producers. Ireland: Plunkett, the first co-operative creamery (Dromcollogher, 1889), the IAOS (1894); dairy co-ops growing into Kerry Group, Glanbia and Ornua (Kerrygold); credit unions (1958). Europe: the Rochdale Pioneers (1844); Danish dairy co-operatives (1882); Mondragon in the Basque Country. Developing world: Amul (India), SEWA (India), fair-trade co-ops (link to s4).

Decision-making processes and levels of participation
- [ ] P2 Top-down vs bottom-up decision-making: governments, MNCs and international agencies vs local communities; participation ladder from being informed, to consulted, to sharing decisions, to controlling them.
- [ ] P3 Why participation matters: local knowledge; projects that people own are maintained; it builds skills and confidence; it reduces corruption; it gives women and minorities a voice.
- [ ] P4 Ireland (national setting): County Enterprise Boards (1993) replaced by Local Enterprise Offices (2014) in each local authority, supporting small local businesses with grants, training and mentoring; LEADER local action groups (from 1991); Údarás na Gaeltachta (1980); public consultation in planning. Critique: Ireland is highly centralised; local government has few powers.
- [ ] P5 Developing-world examples: participatory budgeting in Porto Alegre, Brazil (1989); India's panchayati raj with one-third of local council seats reserved for women (1992); the Narmada dams (Sardar Sarovar) and the Narmada Bachao Andolan protest by displaced people, leading the World Bank to withdraw (1993).
- [ ] P6 Lack of participation: dictatorships, corruption, MNCs and lenders setting conditions (SAPs), women excluded by custom.

Levels of exploitation at local and global scales
- [ ] X1 Definition: exploitation is the unfair use of people or resources for another's benefit (low pay, unsafe work, forced labour, taking resources without fair return).
- [ ] X2 Global scale: MNCs and supply chains (Rana Plaza; garment wages; Nike's 1990s sweatshop criticism); child labour (global figure; cocoa, cobalt); terms of trade and value chains (cocoa, coffee); resource extraction (cobalt in the DR Congo; Nigeria's Niger Delta oil pollution); profit and tax shifting out of poor countries.
- [ ] X3 Modern slavery: about 50 million people (2021 estimate), including forced labour and forced marriage; migrant workers under the kafala system (link to s2).
- [ ] X4 Local/national scale: landlords and moneylenders exploiting tenants (19th-century Ireland, rack-rents); bonded labour in South Asia (illegal in India since 1976, yet persisting in brick kilns and farms); caste discrimination; exploitation of migrant workers in Ireland (the atypical work permit scheme for non-EU fishermen, 2016); low-paid informal work in cities.
- [ ] X5 Exploitation of women and children: lowest-paid factory jobs; trafficking; unpaid domestic work.
- [ ] X6 Responses that empower: trade unions, co-operatives, fair trade, ethical consumer campaigns, the Bangladesh Accord, laws on supply-chain due diligence (EU 2024), ILO conventions.

Differing gender roles in society
- [ ] G1 Gender roles are the tasks and behaviour a society expects of men and women; they differ between and within societies and change over time.
- [ ] G2 Women's work: women do most unpaid care work (ILO figure); in many developing countries women grow much of the food, collect water and fuel and care for children, while men control cash crops and income.
- [ ] G3 Barriers: less schooling for girls (early marriage, fees, chores); less land ownership and credit; lower pay and informal jobs; maternal health risks; violence; exclusion from decision-making. Extreme case: Afghanistan's bans on girls' education (2021–22).
- [ ] G4 Why empowering women links growth to development: educated, earning women have fewer, healthier, better-educated children; household income is spent more on food, health and schooling; more workers and entrepreneurs raise GDP. Kerala as an example of women's literacy and low fertility (reuse).
- [ ] G5 Empowerment in practice: microfinance (Grameen Bank, Muhammad Yunus) and its limits (Andhra Pradesh 2010 over-indebtedness crisis); SEWA; women's quotas in Rwanda's parliament and India's panchayats; girls' education campaigns (Malala Yousafzai); women in the Bangladesh garment industry (jobs and independence vs exploitation and Rana Plaza).
- [ ] G6 Gender roles in Ireland (changing): the marriage bar (removed 1973); rising women's participation in work and education; candidate gender quotas (2012 law); women's share of the Dáil after 2024; the global ranking of Iceland as most equal.

## 3. Traps (known errors)

- **Link every item back to empowerment**: how it lets (or stops) people turning growth into better lives. Exams reward this link in each aspect.
- **Tied aid** (must be spent on donor goods) ≠ **conditional loans** (policy conditions such as SAPs). Don't swap them.
- The Wyndham Act was **1903**; the Land League **1879**. Don't say the Land League "won" ownership in 1881; the 1881 Act gave the Three Fs, and purchase came with the Ashbourne and Wyndham Acts.
- 19th-century Ireland's landlords were not all absentee and the Famine had many causes (blight, dependence on the potato, the land system, government policy). Present the land system as a major cause, not the only one.
- Zimbabwe's land seizures were a reform that **damaged** development; don't present all land reform as positive.
- Microfinance is not a cure-all: give both Grameen's success and the Andhra Pradesh crisis. Don't quote repayment rates or borrower numbers (none checked).
- Don't give euro figures for Irish Aid or EU aid, or Irish NGO budgets. Don't give Irish national-debt ratios.
- Past-paper model answers in the question bank contain invented details (e.g. Grameen "9 million borrowers, 97% women, repayment above 95%", Kerala paddy yields, a 1998 "Land Act" for women, Zambian debt-service percentages, Bangladesh garment figures). Use none.
- Local Enterprise Offices replaced County Enterprise Boards in **2014**; don't call them "local enterprise boards" as if they still existed, except when quoting the syllabus's term.
- **Neighbouring subsections and chapters:**
  - The elective treatment of debt, aid, NGOs, worker exploitation and fair trade is `geo-economic-development-s4`. This subsection uses the same facts in essay depth; keep them identical.
  - Colonial land seizure and the developing-economy case studies (India, Brazil, Sudan) are `geo-economic-development-s3`; refer to them in one bullet.
  - Global supply chains as trade patterns (Apple, cocoa) and human rights decisions (Xinjiang, Dunnes Stores, refugees) are s2; here exploitation is the empowerment angle. Don't repeat the MNC case study.
  - Fair trade, self-reliance and justice for minority groups are s4. Co-operatives belong here (land and participation), but fair-trade co-operatives are developed in s4.
  - EU regional and social funds in Ireland (ERDF, ESF, CAP) are `geo-economic-eu`; here EU aid means aid to developing countries.
  - Models of development and images of the developing world are s1.

## 5. Practice questions to write

1. 80 marks, Higher: "Empowering people is a way of linking economic growth with human development. Discuss this statement with reference to national debt and aid programmes." Model answer: two aspects of about 13–15 SRPs each: debt (origins, cycle of poverty, SAPs, HIPC results in Uganda/Tanzania/Zambia, the 2020s crisis, Ireland 2010); aid (types, who benefits with Pergau and Haiti, harms, the Sachs–Moyo debate, Irish Aid, EU aid). No land or gender material.
