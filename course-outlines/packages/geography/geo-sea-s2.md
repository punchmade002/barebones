# Package: geo-sea-s2 — Human interaction: coastal processes

- **loId:** `geo-sea-s2` · **chapterId:** `geo-sea` · **syllabusRef:** 1.7
- **Syllabus:** "Human interaction. Statement: Human activities can impact on the operation of
  surface processes." Students study one of three options; this is the coastal one: "coastal

## 1. Must cover (one bullet or block for every item; tick each off)

The idea
- [ ] H1 Coasts are a balance between erosion, transport and deposition. Human activity can upset that balance: interrupting longshore drift, removing sediment, destroying the vegetation that holds dunes together, or reflecting wave energy. Effects often appear further along the coast (downdrift), not where the work was done.

Recreational pressures
- [ ] R1 Trampling of sand dunes by walkers and sunbathers kills marram grass; with no roots binding it, the wind removes sand and cuts **blowouts** (hollows) into the dunes; the dune ridge can be breached, letting the sea reach the land behind.
- [ ] R2 Vehicles and parking on dunes and beaches, caravan parks and holiday homes built on dunes destroy vegetation and compact the sand.
- [ ] R3 Golf courses on dunes (links) stabilise some dunes but replace natural habitats and lead to demands for defences to protect the course.
- [ ] R4 Footpath erosion and cliff-edge damage at busy viewing points; litter and pollution; disturbance of nesting and feeding birds.
- [ ] R5 Resort development: hotels, apartments and promenades built on the beach or dune line remove the natural sediment store and block the natural landward movement of dunes and beaches, so the beach narrows and defences become necessary ("coastal squeeze"). Use the Spanish Mediterranean coast as the international example.

Coastal defence work (each: how it works; its effect on coastal processes; advantages and disadvantages)
- [ ] D1 Hard vs soft engineering: hard engineering builds structures to resist the sea; soft engineering works with natural processes. Hard is usually expensive, effective locally, and can increase erosion elsewhere.
- [ ] D2 Sea walls: concrete or stone walls, often curved (recurved) to throw waves back. Protect the land behind but reflect wave energy, so backwash scours the beach in front, lowering and narrowing it, and the wall is undermined. Erosion often increases at the ends of the wall. Expensive to build and maintain.
- [ ] D3 Rock armour (rip-rap): large boulders piled at the foot of a cliff or in front of a sea wall; the gaps absorb and break up wave energy. Cheaper than a sea wall and quick to place, but can be unsightly, hard to walk over, and may be moved by big storms. (Exams ask students to identify it from a photo.)
- [ ] D4 Groynes: low wooden, rock or concrete barriers built out from the shore at right angles, across the beach. They trap sand moving by longshore drift on their updrift side, widening the beach, which absorbs wave energy. **Effect on processes:** the beach downdrift is starved of sediment, so erosion increases there.
- [ ] D5 Gabions (wire cages filled with stones) and revetments (sloping wooden, concrete or rock structures that break waves): one bullet each.
- [ ] D6 Offshore breakwaters and reefs: structures parallel to the shore, just offshore, that make waves break before reaching the beach. One bullet.
- [ ] D7 Beach nourishment: sand or shingle pumped or trucked onto a beach to widen it. Looks natural and keeps the beach for tourism, but must be repeated as waves remove the sand, and dredging the source can harm the sea bed.
- [ ] D8 Dune regeneration: sand fencing (or brushwood) traps blown sand; replanting marram grass; fencing off damaged areas. Cheap and natural, but takes years and needs visitors to keep off.
- [ ] D9 Managed retreat (managed realignment) and "do nothing": defences are removed or not replaced, and the sea is allowed to flood low-value land, creating salt marsh that absorbs wave energy and provides habitat. Cheap and sustainable, but landowners lose land.
- [ ] D10 Training walls and harbour piers interrupt longshore drift and cause deposition in new places. Case: North Bull Wall and North Bull Island, Dublin Bay (see section 2).

Case studies of coastal defence
- [ ] C1 Holderness coast, England (international): soft boulder clay cliffs eroding rapidly; eroded sediment carried south by longshore drift feeds Spurn Head. At Mappleton, a 1991 scheme of two rock groynes and rock armour at the cliff foot protected the village and the main coast road. Result: the beach built up between the groynes, but cliffs immediately south of the scheme, starved of sediment, eroded faster than before. This is the textbook example of defence moving the problem downdrift.
- [ ] C2 North Bull Island, Dublin Bay (Irish): the North Bull Wall was built in the 1820s to stop sand silting up the entrance to Dublin Port. Sand then built up against and beside the wall, and North Bull Island grew from a sandbank into an island with dunes, salt marsh and mudflats. An example of a human structure causing deposition.
- [ ] C3 Rosslare Strand, Co. Wexford (Irish): a sandy resort coast protected by groynes and rock armour. One or two bullets; give no dates or costs.
- [ ] C4 The Netherlands (international): the North Sea storm surge of 1953 flooded the south-west of the country and killed about 1,800 people. The response was the Delta Works: dams, sluices and storm-surge barriers that shortened and sealed the coast of Zeeland. The Oosterschelde (Eastern Scheldt) barrier, completed in 1986, was built with movable gates that stay open in normal weather, so the tides and the estuary's ecosystem and shellfish industry were kept: an example of defence balanced with conservation.

Conservation and management measures
- [ ] M1 Why manage: coasts are under pressure from erosion, development, tourism and rising sea levels; management tries to protect people and property while keeping natural processes and habitats working.
- [ ] M2 Integrated coastal zone management (ICZM): planning a whole stretch of coast (a sediment cell) as one unit, balancing defence, tourism, development and conservation, because work in one place affects places downdrift.
- [ ] M3 Legal protection: EU Habitats and Birds Directives; Special Areas of Conservation (SACs) and Special Protection Areas (SPAs) for birds, together forming the Natura 2000 network; in Ireland, the National Parks and Wildlife Service oversees them. Planning controls on building close to the shore. Spain's Coastal Law (Ley de Costas) of 1988, passed in response to overdevelopment, restricts building on the shore zone.
- [ ] M4 Blue Flag beaches: an international award run by the Foundation for Environmental Education and administered in Ireland by An Taisce; criteria cover water quality, environmental education and information, environmental management, and safety and services. Encourages councils to manage beaches well.
- [ ] M5 Dune management at busy beaches: boardwalks and marked paths, fencing off eroded areas, marram replanting, moving car parks back from the dunes, information signs, seasonal wardens. Case: Brittas Bay, Co. Wicklow (see section 2).
- [ ] M6 Management of a busy cliff site: Cliffs of Moher, Co. Clare: visitor centre opened in 2007, built into the hillside; paths and a wall/barrier set back from the cliff edge to control trampling and keep people safe. Give no visitor numbers.
- [ ] M7 Case: North Bull Island as a conservation site: UNESCO Biosphere Reserve since 1981 (extended in 2015 as the Dublin Bay Biosphere); a nature reserve whose salt marsh and mudflats are a major wintering ground for Brent geese and other wildfowl and waders. Pressures: two golf courses on the island, cars driving onto the beach, many visitors from the city. Shows recreation, conservation and a human-made landform in one place.
- [ ] M8 Managed realignment as management: Medmerry, West Sussex, England, completed in 2013: the shingle bank was no longer maintained, a new embankment was built further inland, and the sea was let in to create intertidal habitat that absorbs wave energy.

Evaluation
- [ ] V1 Short evaluation block for 30-mark answers: hard defences protect a place but move erosion downdrift and are costly; soft measures are cheaper and sustainable but slower and less certain; the best management works with the sediment system along a whole coast. Paired points, no filler.

## 3. Traps

- Groynes trap sand on the **updrift** side; erosion increases on the **downdrift** side. Don't reverse this.
- Rock armour is not groynes: rock armour lies along the shore at the base of a cliff or wall; groynes run out across the beach at right angles to it. Exams test this with photos.
- Sea walls reflect energy and scour the beach in front of them; don't describe them as building beaches.
- Don't re-teach how dunes, spits or longshore drift form; that is `geo-sea-s1`. Use the terms.
- Keep the focus on the impact on **coastal processes**. The economic and social effects of tourism belong to the economic elective (tourism) and to regional chapters; don't drift into tourist numbers or jobs.
- Don't use the Aswan Dam or other river dams cutting sediment supply to deltas: dams belong to `geo-rivers-s4`.
- Don't use figures from past-paper model answers (e.g. a Holderness "1 metre per year" rate, or a 1,300 hectare Medway realignment). None is checked.
- Don't name "the Thames Barrier", "Chesil Beach", "Bray", "Lahinch", "Courtown" or any other defence scheme not in section 2; their details are not checked.
- The Dutch 1953 death toll is "about 1,800" for the Netherlands only; don't give a figure for England or a total.
- The two other 1.7 options (mass movement in `geo-rocks-weathering`, river processes in `geo-rivers-s4`) are separate; don't mix them in.

## 4. What exams ask (topics only)

- Identifying coastal defence structures from a photograph: rock armour; groynes.
- 30 marks: examine how human activity impacts on coastal processes (one of three options). Strong answers name the structure or activity, explain the process it alters (longshore drift, wave reflection, wind erosion of dunes), give the effect on the coast, and support it with Irish and international examples.
- 30 marks: examine the impact of coastal defence measures on coastal processes.
