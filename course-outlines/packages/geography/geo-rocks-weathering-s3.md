## 1. Must cover (one bullet or block for every item; tick each off)

The idea
- [ ] W1 Weathering is the breakdown of rock in situ (in place) at or near the earth's surface, by the weather, water, plants and animals. Erosion is the wearing away and removal of material by a moving agent (rivers, sea, ice, wind). Weathering prepares rock for erosion and mass movement.
- [ ] W2 Regolith: the layer of loose, weathered rock fragments lying on top of solid bedrock. Define it on its own: exams ask for it.
- [ ] W3 Two main types: physical (mechanical) weathering breaks rock into smaller pieces without changing its minerals; chemical weathering changes or dissolves the minerals. Biological weathering (plants and animals) works by both. Exams ask students to classify named processes as physical or chemical.

Physical (mechanical) weathering (each: conditions, step-by-step process, result)
- [ ] P1 Freeze-thaw action (frost shattering): water enters cracks and joints; it freezes at night and expands by about 9%, widening the crack; it thaws by day and water seeps deeper; repeated cycles split off angular fragments. Needs temperatures that move above and below 0 °C often, plus water and jointed rock. Most effective on mountains in upland Ireland, high mountains and in periglacial areas. Result: scree (talus) slopes of angular fragments at the foot of the slope; jagged peaks and blockfields.
- [ ] P2 Exfoliation / onion weathering (insolation weathering): in hot deserts with a large daily temperature range, the outer layer of rock heats and expands by day and cools and contracts at night more than the interior (rock conducts heat poorly), so stress builds and curved outer sheets peel off like the layers of an onion, leaving rounded boulders. Many geographers stress that some moisture (dew) must be present for it to work effectively; one bullet.
- [ ] P3 Granular disintegration: dark and light minerals in a rock such as granite heat and expand at different rates, so individual grains loosen and fall away, leaving gravelly sand.
- [ ] P4 Pressure release (unloading, sheeting): when overlying rock is eroded, buried rock such as granite expands upward and cracks into sheets parallel to the surface; these peel off to leave domes (example in section 2).
- [ ] P5 Salt crystallisation: salt water enters pores and cracks; as it evaporates, salt crystals grow and force the rock apart. Common in hot deserts and on coasts.
- [ ] P6 Biological (physical): plant roots grow into cracks and widen them; burrowing animals expose rock.

Chemical weathering (each: what reacts, what is produced, which rocks, result)
- [ ] C1 Carbonation: rainwater absorbs carbon dioxide from the air and soil to form weak carbonic acid; it reacts with calcium carbonate in limestone to form calcium bicarbonate, which is soluble and is carried away in solution; it works along joints and bedding planes. This is the process behind karst (named only here; landforms in `geo-rocks-weathering-s4`). The word equation (calcium carbonate + water + carbon dioxide → calcium bicarbonate) may be given.
- [ ] C2 Hydrolysis: water reacts with minerals such as feldspar in granite, turning it into clay (kaolin, china clay); the quartz grains are left as sand; the granite crumbles.
- [ ] C3 Oxidation: oxygen dissolved in water reacts with iron in rock to form iron oxide (rust), giving red, yellow or brown colours and weakening the rock.
- [ ] C4 Hydration: some minerals absorb water and swell, setting up stress (e.g. anhydrite to gypsum).
- [ ] C5 Solution: some minerals, such as rock salt, dissolve directly in water.
- [ ] C6 Biological (chemical): decaying plants produce humic acids; lichens and mosses release acids that attack rock surfaces.

Factors influencing weathering (exams ask for two, then an explanation)
- [ ] F1 Climate: temperature and rainfall decide which type dominates. Warm, wet climates: fast chemical weathering (reaction rates roughly double for every 10 °C rise, Van 't Hoff's rule). Cold climates with temperatures around freezing: freeze-thaw. Hot deserts: exfoliation and salt crystallisation. Ireland's mild, wet climate favours chemical weathering, with freeze-thaw on mountain tops.
- [ ] F2 Rock type (mineral composition): limestone is vulnerable to carbonation; granite to hydrolysis; quartzite resists both.
- [ ] F3 Rock structure: joints, bedding planes and faults let water in; heavily jointed rock weathers faster.
- [ ] F4 Vegetation and soil cover: roots and humic acids speed weathering; soil holds moisture against the rock.
- [ ] F5 Time, relief and aspect: older surfaces are more weathered; steep slopes remove debris and expose fresh rock; aspect affects freeze-thaw (shady slopes stay frozen longer).
- [ ] F6 The role of temperature across all processes (asked as a 30-mark Higher question): freeze-thaw needs crossing of 0 °C; exfoliation and granular disintegration need a large daily range; temperature controls the rate of chemical reactions. Write the notes so a student can build this answer from P1–P3 and F1 without new facts.

## 3. Traps (known errors)

- Freeze-thaw is physical, carbonation and hydrolysis are chemical, exfoliation is physical. Exams test this classification directly; state the type in each process's first sentence.
- Do not give a pressure for ice in cracks (marking schemes quote figures such as "2,100 kg per cm²"; they are not checked).
- Do not quote desert day and night temperatures from marking schemes.
- Weathering is not erosion: no movement by an agent is involved. Keep the two definitions separate.
- Freeze-thaw's role in supplying debris to glaciers and in glacial erosion is in `geo-glaciation-s1`. Here, teach freeze-thaw as a weathering process and its scree result only.
- Karst landforms (limestone pavement, clints, grikes, caves, stalactites) are in `geo-rocks-weathering-s4`. Teach carbonation as a process here; name the Burren as where it operates and say no more.
- Granite tors and the deep-weathering theory belong to `geo-rocks-weathering-s4`.
- Rockfall and scree as mass movement belong to `geo-rocks-weathering-s5`. Here, scree is the product of freeze-thaw; one bullet.
- Hydraulic action is a river and sea erosion process, not weathering (it appears on a weathering past paper only as a separate part). It belongs to `geo-rivers` and `geo-sea`.

## 4. What exams ask (topics only)

- Matching photos to freeze-thaw, carbonation, exfoliation/onion weathering, hydrolysis; classifying each as chemical or physical.
- Matching features to processes: scree with freeze-thaw; limestone pavement with chemical weathering.
- Naming one process of physical and one of chemical weathering; naming two processes of chemical weathering and explaining one.
- Naming two factors that influence weathering and explaining them.
- Ordinary 20–40 marks: describe and explain two of freeze-thaw action, carbonation, onion weathering.
- Higher 30 marks: the role of temperature in physical/mechanical weathering.
- Defining regolith.
