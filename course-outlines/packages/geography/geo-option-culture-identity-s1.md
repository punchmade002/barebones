## 1. Must cover (one bullet or block for every item; tick each off)

Race and ethnicity as classifications
- [ ] R1 Definitions: race (a grouping of people by inherited physical traits such as skin colour, hair type, facial features); ethnicity / ethnic group (a group sharing culture, ancestry, language, religion or history and seen as distinct by itself and others). The syllabus treats race as one part of ethnicity. Physical indicators (race) vs cultural indicators (language, religion, nationality: s2, s3, s5).
- [ ] R2 Modern view: all humans are one species with about 99.9% of DNA in common; there is more genetic variation within any so-called race than between races; physical traits grade gradually across space (clines) with no sharp boundaries. Race is therefore largely a social classification, but it matters geographically because people, laws and states have acted on it (segregation, apartheid, genocide).
- [ ] R3 The traditional textbook classification (present it explicitly as a historical, now outdated classification, not as science): Caucasoid, Mongoloid, Negroid and Australoid groupings, with the physical characteristics textbooks attach to each (skin colour, hair form, eye shape, nose shape).
- [ ] R4 Locational patterns before about 1500 (before European colonisation): Europeans, North Africans, Middle Eastern and South Asian peoples (Caucasoid); East Asians, Central Asians, the Inuit and the native peoples of the Americas (Mongoloid); peoples of sub-Saharan Africa (Negroid); Aboriginal Australians and Melanesians (Australoid). These were shaped by long isolation (oceans, deserts, mountains).
- [ ] R5 Why physical traits vary: adaptation to environment over thousands of years, especially skin pigmentation (melanin) increasing towards the tropics where ultraviolet radiation is strongest, and lighter skin at high latitudes where less sunlight allows vitamin D production.
- [ ] R6 Today's pattern: colonialism and migration since 1500 have overturned the old pattern, so that most large countries are now multi-racial (lead-in to R7–R14).

The impact of colonialism on racial patterns
- [ ] C1 European colonisation of the Americas: the native population collapsed through disease, war and forced labour after 1492; Spanish and Portuguese settlers; racially mixed populations in Latin America (mestizo: European and Native American; mulatto/pardo: European and African).
- [ ] C2 The Atlantic slave trade: the forced movement of Africans to the Americas; numbers; the main destinations (Brazil, the Caribbean, the American South); the legacy of large Black populations and racial inequality in the Americas.
- [ ] C3 Settler colonies: Australia (Aboriginal dispossession, the White Australia Policy, the Stolen Generations, the 2008 apology, the 2023 Voice referendum); New Zealand (Treaty of Waitangi, Māori share of population). Native Americans in the USA (removal and reservations).
- [ ] C4 Colonial labour movements within empires: indentured Indian workers taken to the Caribbean, Fiji, South Africa (Natal) and East Africa, creating Indian communities there.
- [ ] C5 Colonialism hardening racial categories: Belgian identity cards in Rwanda fixing Hutu/Tutsi labels (link to R12).
- [ ] C6 Post-colonial migration to Europe: people from former colonies moving to the "mother country" after 1945: the Caribbean and South Asia to Britain (the Windrush); North and West Africa to France; Indonesia and Suriname to the Netherlands. This created multi-racial European cities.

The impact of migration on racial patterns
- [ ] M1 Internal migration: the Great Migration of African Americans from the rural South to northern and western cities; it changed the racial make-up of both regions and created segregated inner-city neighbourhoods.
- [ ] M2 Labour migration into Europe: guest workers (Gastarbeiter), e.g. Turks in Germany from 1961, North Africans in France and Belgium.
- [ ] M3 Refugees and asylum seekers (syllabus setting): definitions (refugee, asylum seeker, economic migrant, internally displaced person); the 1951 Refugee Convention; the global scale (UNHCR figures); the Syrian crisis and the 2015 movement into Europe; the Rohingya.
- [ ] M4 Ireland (national setting): from a largely white, Irish, Catholic population to a multi-ethnic one: Census 2022 figures; asylum seekers and the Direct Provision system (2000); the right to work; Ukrainians under EU temporary protection since 2022; Irish Travellers recognised as an ethnic group (2017); pressures and tensions (the November 2023 Dublin riot).
- [ ] M5 Effects of migration on the receiving area: a more diverse population; ethnic neighbourhoods and clustering; new places of worship, shops and food; labour supply; and also discrimination, segregation and tension. Effects on the source area: loss of young adults; remittances. Keep to the racial/ethnic pattern; general migration theory is in another chapter (see Traps).

Multi-racial societies and racial mixing
- [ ] S1 Multi-racial society: definition; examples: the USA, Brazil, South Africa, the UK, Ireland today.
- [ ] S2 USA: Census 2020 composition; "melting pot" (assimilation) vs "salad bowl" (groups keep distinct identities); growth of the Hispanic and multiracial populations.
- [ ] S3 Brazil as the main example of racial mixing: origins (Portuguese, African, Indigenous peoples); Census 2022 composition, in which people of mixed race (pardo) became the largest group; the claim of "racial democracy" vs the reality that White Brazilians on average remain wealthier.
- [ ] S4 UK as a European multi-racial society: post-war Commonwealth migration; Census 2021 (England and Wales) composition; the Windrush scandal (2018).
- [ ] S5 Multiculturalism as a policy (exams ask about it): accepting and supporting distinct cultures within one society (Canada, Australia), compared with assimilation (France's model); arguments for (enrichment, labour, tolerance) and against (segregation, parallel societies, backlash and far-right politics).

Racial conflict
- [ ] K1 Causes of racial conflict: competition for jobs and housing, segregation, unequal power inherited from colonialism and slavery, racist ideologies, fear of newcomers.
- [ ] K2 South Africa's apartheid (main case study): 1948 to 1994; the four official racial classes; the laws (Population Registration Act, Group Areas Act, homelands); forced removals (District Six); resistance (Sharpeville, Soweto); the end (Mandela's release, 1994 election, Truth and Reconciliation Commission); today's composition and lasting inequality.
- [ ] K3 USA: slavery and the Civil War; segregation ("Jim Crow"); the civil rights movement and its laws; continuing tension (George Floyd, Black Lives Matter).
- [ ] K4 Nazi Germany and the Holocaust: racist ideology turned into law and genocide.
- [ ] K5 Rwanda 1994: genocide of Tutsi by Hutu extremists; link to colonial classification (C5).
- [ ] K6 European urban unrest: Britain (Notting Hill 1958, Brixton 1981) and France (the 2005 riots in the banlieues).

## 3. Traps

- Present Caucasoid/Mongoloid/Negroid/Australoid only as the traditional textbook classification, now rejected by biologists. Never imply that any group is superior, or that race determines ability or culture. Use neutral wording ("people of African descent").
- Race (physical indicators) ≠ ethnicity (cultural group) ≠ nationality (citizenship of a state). Keep the terms distinct; Irish Travellers are an ethnic group, not a race.
- Refugee ≠ asylum seeker ≠ economic migrant. An asylum seeker becomes a refugee only once the claim is accepted.
- Northern Ireland, the Balkans and Israel–Palestine are religious/national conflicts, not racial ones. They belong to s3 (religion), s5 (nation states) and s6 (Balkans). Don't use them as racial conflict here.
- Past-paper model answers for this chapter contain unchecked or wrong figures (e.g. "up to 90% of population in some Black Belt counties", "30-40%" Black populations of northern cities by 1970, "1-1.5 billion" people in stateless groups). Use none of them.
- Don't give a Direct Provision weekly allowance, the number of asylum centres, or 2024–2026 asylum application figures: not checked.
- Don't give a figure for the number of Indigenous Americans in 1492: estimates vary hugely.
- Ownership of neighbouring content: Irish migration patterns since 1950 and the causes of migration belong to `geo-population-migration-s3`; Irish and EU migration policy to `geo-population-migration-s4`; "Ethnic, racial and religious issues arising from migration" in Ireland in depth to `geo-population-migration-s5`. Here, use migration only to explain changing racial patterns, with one compact Irish block (M4). Kurds, Palestinians and other stateless groups belong to s5. Language and religion are s2 and s3.
- Don't say Brazil has no racism ("racial democracy" is a claim, not a fact).
