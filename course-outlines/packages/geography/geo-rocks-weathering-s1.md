# Package: geo-rocks-weathering-s1 — The rock cycle and rock formation

## 1. Must cover (one bullet or block for every item; tick each off)

The rock cycle
- [ ] R1 The rock cycle: rocks are formed, modified, destroyed and reconstituted. Endogenic (internal) forces form and modify rock (magma, heat, pressure); exogenic (external) forces destroy it (weathering, erosion); deposition of sediment reconstitutes it.
- [ ] R2 The cycle as a diagram: magma → cooling/crystallisation → igneous rock → weathering and erosion → sediment → deposition, compaction and cementation → sedimentary rock → heat and pressure → metamorphic rock → melting → magma, with the short-cuts (any rock can be weathered; any rock can be metamorphosed or melted). Exams ask students to label the stages and the processes (crystallisation of magma; weathering and erosion; compaction and cementation; heat and pressure).
- [ ] R3 Link to the tectonic cycle: rocks are created at plate boundaries and hotspots, metamorphosed at collision zones and destroyed or recycled at subduction zones.

Igneous rocks
- [ ] I1 Formed when magma (molten rock below the surface) or lava (molten rock at the surface) cools and crystallises. Crystal size depends on cooling rate.
- [ ] I2 Plutonic (intrusive) rock: magma cools slowly deep in the crust, giving large crystals. Granite: quartz, feldspar and mica crystals visible. Intrusions: batholiths (huge dome-shaped masses), dykes (vertical, cutting across beds) and sills (horizontal, along bedding planes). Plutonic rock reaches the surface only after the rock above is eroded away. Define "plutonic rock" on its own: exams ask for it.
- [ ] I3 Volcanic (extrusive) rock: lava cools quickly at the surface, giving small crystals. Basalt: dark, fine-grained, the commonest volcanic rock, formed from runny lava at constructive boundaries and hotspots.
- [ ] I4 Geotectonic settings of igneous rock: basalt at constructive (divergent) boundaries and hotspots; andesite and granite batholiths above subduction zones at destructive boundaries.
- [ ] I5 Uses of igneous rock: granite for building stone, kerbs, worktops and headstones; basalt crushed for road chippings and aggregate. (Exams ask for two uses.)

Sedimentary rocks
- [ ] S1 Formed from sediment: weathered and eroded fragments of older rock, or the remains of plants and animals, or minerals precipitated from water. Laid down in layers (strata) separated by bedding planes, usually in seas, lakes, river basins and deserts.
- [ ] S2 Lithification: compaction (the weight of overlying layers squeezes out water and air) and cementation (minerals such as silica, calcite and iron oxide carried by groundwater bind the grains together). Define it on its own: exams ask for it.
- [ ] S3 Three groups: clastic (sandstone from sand; shale from mud/clay), organic (limestone from shells and skeletons of sea creatures; coal from plant remains in swamps), chemical (rock salt and other evaporites from evaporating seawater).
- [ ] S4 Features: often contain fossils (true/false favourite); bedding planes (horizontal) and joints (vertical cracks); many are permeable or porous.
- [ ] S5 Formation of sedimentary rocks with Irish examples, in enough depth for a 30-mark Higher answer: Carboniferous limestone (warm shallow tropical sea), Old Red Sandstone (hot, semi-arid conditions, deposited by rivers), shale and sandstone of the Cliffs of Moher, chalk under the Antrim basalt, coal, rock salt. Explain the conditions at the time each formed.
- [ ] S6 Geotectonic setting: sediments build up in subsiding basins, on continental shelves and along trailing (passive) margins.

Metamorphic rocks
- [ ] M1 Formed when existing rock is changed by great heat and/or pressure without melting: minerals recrystallise; the rock usually becomes harder and denser, and may develop banding or cleavage. The two factors that cause metamorphism are heat and pressure.
- [ ] M2 Thermal (contact) metamorphism: rock next to an igneous intrusion is baked by its heat, forming a ring of altered rock called a metamorphic aureole. Small scale.
- [ ] M3 Regional metamorphism: heat and pressure over huge areas during plate collision and mountain building. Large scale.
- [ ] M4 Parent → metamorphic rock pairs: limestone → marble; sandstone → quartzite; shale (mudstone) → slate (→ schist with more heat and pressure); granite → gneiss. Exams ask these both ways round.

Irish rock types (national setting)
- [ ] IR1 Map pattern of Ireland: limestone floors the central lowland; Old Red Sandstone forms the ridges of Munster; granite in Wicklow, Donegal and the Mournes; basalt in Co. Antrim; metamorphic rocks in Donegal, Connemara and Mayo. Exams use a map with lettered locations.
- [ ] IR2 One named Irish location for each rock type: plutonic, volcanic, sedimentary (several), metamorphic (several).
- [ ] IR3 Ireland's geological past in one or two bullets: the closing of the Iapetus Ocean (Caledonian mountain building) produced the Leinster and Donegal granites and regional metamorphism; Carboniferous Ireland lay under a warm shallow sea; the opening of the Atlantic produced the Antrim basalts.

North America (international setting)
- [ ] NA1 Active (western) margin: the Pacific plates subduct or slide past North America. Volcanic rocks (Cascade Range volcanoes such as Mount St Helens, above the subducting Juan de Fuca Plate); plutonic rock (the Sierra Nevada granite batholith); regional metamorphism in the western mountain belts; the San Andreas Fault as a transform boundary.
- [ ] NA2 Trailing (passive) eastern margin: no plate boundary at the coast, so no active volcanoes; thick sediments accumulate on the Atlantic coastal plain and continental shelf and in the Mississippi delta; the Appalachians are old, worn-down fold mountains of sedimentary and metamorphic rock.

Identifying rocks (photo questions)
- [ ] ID1 How to tell common rocks apart by appearance: granite (speckled, large interlocking crystals); basalt (dark, fine-grained, may form columns); sandstone (gritty grains, often reddish or buff, layered); limestone (pale grey, may show fossils); marble (white or coloured, sparkling crystals, no fossils); quartzite (pale, very hard, glassy); slate (dark grey, splits into thin sheets).

## 3. Traps (known errors)

- **Croagh Patrick is quartzite, not granite.** A marking-scheme answer calls it granite. Never use it as a granite example.
- Do not pair basalt (or any igneous rock) with schist, as one marking-scheme answer does. Schist forms from shale/mudstone under strong heat and pressure. Give only the parent pairs in M4.
- Kilkenny "black marble" is a polished dark limestone, not a true marble. Don't use it as a metamorphic example.
- Basalt is volcanic (extrusive), not plutonic. Granite is igneous, not sedimentary. Limestone becomes marble, not quartzite.
- Porous (water held in spaces between grains, e.g. sandstone) and permeable (lets water pass) are different. Permeability belongs to `geo-rocks-weathering-s4`; mention it here only in passing.
- Give no ages, depths or temperatures other than those in the table. Do not give a pressure or temperature at which metamorphism starts.
- Formation of the Giant's Causeway columns (columnar jointing) and the landforms of the Antrim plateau and Leinster batholith belong to `geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s3`. Name them here only as rock locations.
- Plate boundary processes (sea-floor spreading, subduction) are taught in `geo-plate-tectonics`. Refer to them in one line each.
- Quarrying and mining rocks belong to `geo-rocks-weathering-s2`; give uses in I5 in one bullet only.

## 4. What exams ask (topics only)

- Matching photos of rocks (granite, basalt, sandstone, limestone, marble, slate, quartzite) to their names, and classifying each as igneous, sedimentary or metamorphic.
- Matching lettered locations on a map of Ireland to rock types (limestone, basalt, sandstone, granite).
- True/false: granite is sedimentary (false); fossils occur in sedimentary rock (true); basalt is plutonic (false); limestone becomes quartzite (false); marble is metamorphic (true); silica can bind sedimentary particles (true).
- Naming two metamorphic rocks; naming the sedimentary parent of marble and quartzite; source material of limestone (shells and skeletons of sea creatures) and sandstone (sand).
- Labelling a rock cycle diagram with rock types and processes.
- Short definitions: lithification; plutonic rock; thermal vs regional metamorphism; two factors causing metamorphism.
- Naming an igneous or metamorphic rock and an Irish location for it; two uses of rocks.
- Ordinary 30–40 marks: how igneous (or metamorphic) rocks are formed.
- Higher 30 marks: the formation of sedimentary rocks with Irish examples.
