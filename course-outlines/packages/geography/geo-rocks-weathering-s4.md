## 1. Must cover (one bullet or block for every item; tick each off)

Karst: the conditions
- [ ] K1 Karst: a landscape on limestone shaped mainly by carbonation (solution), with few surface streams and many underground features. Named after the Kras region of Slovenia.
- [ ] K2 Why limestone: it is calcium carbonate, which dissolves in weak carbonic acid; it is permeable because water passes along its bedding planes (horizontal) and joints (vertical), not through the rock itself. Define permeable rock and contrast with porous and impermeable rock: exams ask for it.
- [ ] K3 Ground water table: the upper surface of the zone where all cracks in the rock are saturated; above it is the zone of aeration. It rises in wet weather and falls in dry.
- [ ] K4 Percolation: the downward movement of water through soil and rock.

Surface karst landforms (each: what it is, formation in stages, Irish example, diagram cue)
- [ ] S1 Limestone pavement: a bare, flat limestone surface. Soil was scraped off by ice during glaciation, exposing the rock to rain. Diagram: blocks and cracks, with bedding planes and joints in cross-section.
- [ ] S2 Grikes: deep, narrow vertical cracks widened by carbonation along joints.
- [ ] S3 Clints: the flat-topped blocks of limestone between grikes.
- [ ] S4 Karren (runnels): small grooves cut into the clint surface by rainwater running across it.
- [ ] S5 Solution hollows (kamenitza, solution pans): small enclosed hollows on the surface of clints where rainwater collects and dissolves the rock.
- [ ] S6 Swallow hole (slugga): a surface stream flowing off impermeable rock reaches limestone and disappears underground down a hole widened by carbonation along a joint.
- [ ] S7 Doline (sinkhole): a hollow formed by solution of the surface or by collapse of a cave roof. Uvala: several dolines merged.
- [ ] S8 Polje: a large, flat-floored depression, seasonally flooded (Slovenia).
- [ ] S9 Dry valley: a valley cut by a surface river when the ground was frozen (impermeable) or the water table was higher, now without a stream.
- [ ] S10 Turlough: a seasonal lake in a hollow in limestone; it fills in winter when the water table rises and drains through the floor in summer. Unique to Ireland as a common landform.
- [ ] S11 River of resurgence: an underground river that re-emerges at the surface, usually where limestone meets impermeable rock below.

Underground karst landforms (each: formation, example, diagram cue)
- [ ] U1 Caves and caverns: water dissolves passages along bedding planes and joints; underground rivers enlarge them by erosion; a cavern is a very large cave.
- [ ] U2 Dripstone formation: water dripping from the roof carries dissolved calcium bicarbonate; when it loses carbon dioxide (and evaporates) it deposits calcite (calcium carbonate).
- [ ] U3 Stalactites: hang from the roof, growing downward.
- [ ] U4 Stalagmites: grow upward from the floor beneath, usually thicker and blunter.
- [ ] U5 Pillars (columns): where a stalactite and stalagmite join.
- [ ] U6 Curtains: sheets of calcite formed where water trickles along a crack in the roof.

The karst cycle and settings
- [ ] KC1 Karst cycle (one short block): youth (surface drainage begins to sink, swallow holes form), maturity (most drainage underground, caves, dolines, uvalas), old age (cave roofs collapse, limestone mostly removed, isolated residual hills).
- [ ] KC2 Irish karst settings: the Burren; the Marble Arch upland; the Mask–Corrib lowland. Facts in section 2.
- [ ] KC3 International karst settings, one or two bullets each: Slovenia, S.W. China, Kentucky, Jamaica. Facts in section 2.

Granite landscapes
- [ ] G1 Granite's characteristics: hard, crystalline, resistant to erosion; impermeable (water runs off, so many surface streams and boggy ground); jointed; weathers mainly by hydrolysis of its feldspar, which forms clay.
- [ ] G2 Wicklow granite landscape: rounded, dome-shaped uplands of the Leinster batholith (worn down from rock that once covered it); thin acidic soils and blanket bog because the rock is impermeable and rainfall is high; many surface streams; deep glacial valleys cut into it.
- [ ] G3 Tors: blocks of granite standing on a hilltop. Formation (two-stage theory): (1) under warm, wet conditions, deep chemical weathering (hydrolysis) attacked the granite along its joints, rotting it most where joints were close together; (2) the rotted material was later stripped away (e.g. by mass movement under periglacial conditions), leaving the solid, widely jointed blocks standing as tors. Mention that a second theory explains tors by freeze-thaw and solifluction alone.
- [ ] G4 Other granite features: clitter (blockfields of boulders on slopes below tors); china clay (kaolin) from hydrolysis.
- [ ] G5 Dartmoor as the international granite setting (section 2).

Spatial variation in rock type
- [ ] V1 Ireland's saucer shape: a low central lowland of Carboniferous limestone ringed by uplands of harder, more resistant rocks (granite, quartzite, sandstone).
- [ ] V2 Differential erosion: resistant rock forms uplands, headlands and peaks; weaker rock forms lowlands, valleys and bays. Quartzite peaks stand above surrounding rocks.
- [ ] V3 Rock type and drainage: impermeable granite and shale give many surface streams and bog; permeable limestone gives underground drainage, few rivers, dry surfaces.
- [ ] V4 Two contrasting landscapes side by side: the limestone lowland east of Lough Corrib vs the granite and metamorphic uplands of Connemara to the west; and the Marble Arch upland where streams flow over impermeable rock and sink at the limestone.

## 3. Traps (known errors)

- The Burren's karst was not "preserved by a lack of glaciation", as one marking scheme claims. The Burren was glaciated; ice scraped off the soil and exposed the pavement, and glacial erratics rest on it.
- Do not give the Burren's area: sources disagree.
- Don't list Meteora, Lascaux or Waitomo (from a marking scheme) as karst examples. Use only the table.
- A doline formed by collapse of a cave roof is still a doline (true/false favourite).
- A river of resurgence is one that comes back to the surface; a stream that disappears goes down a swallow hole. One past-paper answer key marks "a river of resurgence is one that disappears underground" as true; the notes must give the correct definition.
- Stalactites hang from the roof (c for ceiling); stalagmites grow from the ground (g for ground).
- Do not say granite is permeable or that granite uplands have underground drainage.
- Carbonation as a process is taught in `geo-rocks-weathering-s3`: use the term here and give the reaction in one bullet, without repeating the full process.
- Landforms of bedding planes and joints in the Dartry–Cuilcagh upland (e.g. limestone escarpments such as Benbulbin) belong to `geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s4`. Landforms of the Antrim plateau and the Leinster batholith as volcanic and plutonic structures (e.g. columnar basalt) belong to `geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s3`. Here, treat Wicklow only as a granite landscape shaped by weathering.
- Munster ridge-and-valley (sandstone ridges, limestone valleys) is a fold structure in `geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s5`. Refer to it in at most one bullet under V2.
- Glacial valleys of Wicklow (Glendalough) are in `geo-glaciation-s1`. Mention only that ice cut valleys into the granite.

## 4. What exams ask (topics only)

- Labelling a limestone pavement photo: grike, clint, solution hollow (kamenitza), karren, bedding plane.
- Labelling a karst diagram: underground features (stalactite, stalagmite, pillar, cave, swallow hole); naming surface and underground features.
- True/false: percolation; river of resurgence; a doline forms by cave collapse.
- The rock most associated with karst (limestone); the chemical weathering involved (carbonation); bedding planes and joints by name.
- Defining permeable rock; explaining the ground water table.
- Naming an Irish karst landscape.
- Ordinary 40 marks: name a karst landscape, then explain with diagrams one surface feature and one underground feature.
