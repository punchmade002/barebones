## 1. Must cover (one bullet or block for every item; tick each off)

The idea
- [ ] I1 Extrusive (volcanic) activity: magma reaches the surface and cools quickly as lava, ash and other material, building landforms on the surface. Intrusive (plutonic) activity: magma cools slowly underground and forms bodies of rock that become landforms only when the rock above is eroded away. Both are endogenic processes (exams ask true/false on this; plutonic features are **not** exogenic).
- [ ] I2 Joints: cracks in rock along which no movement has taken place. In igneous rock they form by contraction as the rock cools, and in granite also by expansion when overlying rock is removed (pressure release). Weathering and erosion work along joints, so joints shape the landform.

Extrusive landforms (each: formation in numbered stages, example from section 2, diagram cue)
- [ ] E1 Lava plateau from fissure eruptions: runny basic lava pours out of long cracks, spreads widely and builds up flow upon flow into a high, flat-topped plateau with steep edges. The Antrim plateau in full here.
- [ ] E2 Columnar jointing / basalt columns (the Giant's Causeway): a thick lava flow cools slowly and evenly; it contracts; cracks form at about 120° to each other, producing mainly six-sided columns at right angles to the cooling surface. Erosion by the sea exposes them.
- [ ] E3 Shield volcano: low-viscosity basic lava flows far before cooling, building a broad cone with gentle slopes; mostly effusive eruptions. Exams ask true/false that shield volcanoes form from low-viscosity lava.
- [ ] E4 Composite (strato) volcano: viscous acidic or intermediate magma; explosive eruptions alternate with lava flows, building a steep cone of alternating layers of ash and lava, with a crater, main vent and secondary vents.
- [ ] E5 Cinder (ash) cone: a small, steep cone built mainly of pyroclastic fragments from short explosive eruptions.
- [ ] E6 Caldera: a large basin formed when the summit of a volcano collapses into the emptied magma chamber after a huge eruption; it may fill with water to form a crater lake. Exams ask the name for a collapsed volcanic cone and to explain the term.
- [ ] E7 Volcanic plug (neck): magma solidifies in the vent of a volcano; the softer cone around it is eroded, leaving the resistant plug as a steep-sided hill.
- [ ] E8 Minor extrusive features, one bullet each: geysers (hot water and steam erupt at intervals), hot springs, fumaroles (vents emitting gases and steam).

Intrusive (plutonic) landforms (each: what it is, how it forms, effect on landscape when exposed, example where given, diagram cue)
- [ ] P1 Batholith: a huge dome-shaped mass of magma cooled slowly deep underground into granite. When overlying rock is eroded it forms upland areas; the heat altered the surrounding rocks into a metamorphic aureole (e.g. mica schist). The Leinster batholith in full here.
- [ ] P2 Laccolith: magma forced between layers of rock, arching the layers above into a dome.
- [ ] P3 Lopolith: a saucer-shaped intrusion that sags downwards in the centre.
- [ ] P4 Sill: a horizontal sheet of magma injected along a bedding plane, parallel to the layers. When exposed, a resistant sill forms cliffs, ledges and waterfalls.
- [ ] P5 Dyke: a vertical or near-vertical sheet of magma cutting across the layers. If harder than the surrounding rock it stands up as a wall-like ridge; if softer it is eroded into a trench.
- [ ] P6 One labelled diagram cue showing batholith, laccolith, lopolith, sill, dyke and volcanic plug in one block of crust (exams ask students to name these from a diagram and to name two plutonic features).
- [ ] P7 Differential erosion: plutonic rocks are usually more resistant than the rocks around them, so they end up standing as uplands once the cover is removed.

Settings (place-based landform development; use these as the case studies)
- [ ] S1 Antrim plateau and associated landforms (Ireland, extrusive): lava plateau, Giant's Causeway, basalt cliffs over chalk, volcanic plug at Slemish, sill at Fair Head; Mourne Mountains as granite intrusions of the same period.
- [ ] S2 Leinster batholith (Ireland, intrusive): Wicklow Mountains granite uplands; schist aureole; rounded summits.
- [ ] S3 Hawaii: shield volcanoes over a hot spot; calderas; lava types.
- [ ] S4 Iceland: fissure eruptions, lava fields, geysers and hot springs, new islands.
- [ ] S5 Devon and Cornwall: granite moorland uplands (Dartmoor, Bodmin Moor) formed from an exposed batholith; china clay from decomposed granite. Tors get one sentence (their formation is in `geo-rocks-weathering-s4`).

## 3. Traps (known errors)

- The Giant's Causeway is **not** a volcano and was not built by crystals growing; it is a lava flow that cracked into columns as it cooled and contracted.
- Basalt is extrusive (volcanic); granite is intrusive (plutonic). The Antrim plateau is extrusive; the Leinster batholith and the Mournes are intrusive.
- The Mourne Mountains are not Caledonian and not part of the Leinster batholith.
- A sill runs **along** the layers (horizontal); a dyke cuts **across** them (vertical). A laccolith bulges up; a lopolith sags down.
- Plutonic features form by endogenic processes. They are revealed by exogenic erosion, but they are not "formed by exogenic processes".
- Castle Rock, Edinburgh is a volcanic plug with a crag-and-tail; it is not a roche moutonnée.
- A caldera is much larger than a crater and forms by collapse, not by explosion alone.
- Don't give heights or areas not in section 2 (no area for the Antrim plateau, no height for Slieve Donard).
- Tors and the weathering of granite and limestone landscapes are in `geo-rocks-weathering-s4`; how granite and basalt form as rock types is in `geo-rocks-weathering-s1`. One sentence each here, no formation.
- Why volcanoes occur where they do, eruption effects and prediction are in `geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s1`. The Antrim plateau's tectonic cause is one sentence here; the landforms are the focus.
- Joints and bedding planes in sedimentary rock belong to `geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s4`.
