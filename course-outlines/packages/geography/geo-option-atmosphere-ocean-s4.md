## 1. Must cover (one bullet or block for every item; tick each off)

Forces governing the movement of air
- [ ] F1 Pressure gradient force: air moves from high to low pressure; the steeper the gradient (closer isobars), the stronger the force and the wind. Pressure differences are caused by unequal heating (thermal) and by air converging or diverging aloft (dynamic).
- [ ] F2 Coriolis force (effect): because the earth rotates, moving air and water are deflected to the right in the northern hemisphere and to the left in the southern hemisphere. Zero at the equator, strongest at the poles; greater for faster-moving air. It changes direction, not speed. Diagram cue: a globe with arrows deflected right and left.
- [ ] F3 Geostrophic wind: above about 1 km, the pressure gradient force and the Coriolis force balance, so the wind blows parallel to the isobars.
- [ ] F4 Friction: near the surface, friction with the ground slows the wind, weakening the Coriolis effect, so surface winds blow at an angle across the isobars, into lows and out of highs. Friction is greater over land (rough) than over sea (smooth), so winds are stronger over the sea and on coasts.
- [ ] F5 Result: in the northern hemisphere air spirals anticlockwise into a depression and clockwise out of an anticyclone; reversed in the southern hemisphere. (Buys Ballot's law is in `geo-weather-climate-s1`; one clause.)

Forces governing the movement of water
- [ ] W1 Wind drag: prevailing winds blowing over the sea surface drive the surface currents (the trade winds drive the equatorial currents westward; the westerlies drive currents eastward in the mid-latitudes).
- [ ] W2 Coriolis effect on water and the Ekman spiral: the surface current is deflected about 45° from the wind direction, and the net movement of the whole wind-driven layer is about 90° to the right of the wind in the northern hemisphere (Ekman transport). This makes currents circle in gyres and causes upwelling along coasts where water is moved offshore.
- [ ] W3 Density differences: colder and saltier water is denser and sinks; warmer, fresher water rises or spreads at the surface. This drives the deep thermohaline circulation (O6).
- [ ] W4 Shape of the continents and the ocean floor: land masses block and deflect currents north or south, turning them into closed gyres. Gravity and tides (one bullet).

General circulation of the atmosphere (diagram cue: the tri-cellular model with pressure belts, cells and surface winds for both hemispheres)
- [ ] G1 Pressure belts: equatorial low (the ITCZ, or doldrums: calm, rising air, heavy rain); subtropical highs at about 30° N and S (the horse latitudes: sinking, dry air; deserts); subpolar lows at about 60° (the polar front, where depressions form); polar highs (cold, sinking air).
- [ ] G2 Planetary winds: north-east and south-east trade winds blowing from the subtropical highs to the ITCZ; westerlies (south-westerly in the northern hemisphere, north-westerly in the southern) from the subtropical highs towards the subpolar lows; polar easterlies from the poles. Each is deflected by the Coriolis force.
- [ ] G3 The three cells: Hadley (thermal, driven by equatorial heating), Ferrel (an indirect cell driven by the other two), Polar (thermal, cold sinking air). One sentence each; the energy view is in s2.
- [ ] G4 Seasonal shift: the pressure belts and the ITCZ move north in the northern summer and south in the northern winter, following the overhead sun, and move further over continents than over oceans. This explains seasonal climates: the Mediterranean's dry summer under the subtropical high, the monsoon's summer rain, the Sahel's single wet season (links s5, s6).
- [ ] G5 Jet streams: narrow, fast bands of westerly wind in the upper troposphere, near the tropopause, where temperature contrasts are sharpest. The polar front jet stream meanders in large waves (Rossby waves) and steers depressions; its position decides whether Ireland gets a run of wet, windy depressions or settled weather. A strong, straight jet over the Atlantic brings stormy winters; a looping, slow jet can block weather systems in place for weeks. The subtropical jet's role in the Indian monsoon is in `geo-region-continental-s1`; one clause.
- [ ] G6 The North Atlantic setting: the semi-permanent Icelandic Low and Azores High dominate Ireland's weather; the pressure difference between them (the North Atlantic Oscillation) controls the strength of the westerlies. A strong difference (positive phase): strong westerlies, mild, wet, stormy Irish winters. A weak difference (negative phase): weaker westerlies, more blocking highs, colder and drier winters.
- [ ] G7 Air masses affecting Ireland, each with source, weather (one bullet each): tropical maritime (from the Atlantic near the Azores: warm, moist, mild, cloudy, drizzle); polar maritime (from the North Atlantic near Greenland and Iceland: cool, moist, showery, Ireland's most frequent); arctic maritime (from the Arctic: cold, snow showers in the north and west in winter); polar continental (from Russia and Scandinavia in winter: very cold, dry; snow on east coasts after crossing the Irish Sea; example in section 2); tropical continental (from North Africa and southern Europe in summer: hot, dry, hazy; heatwaves).

General circulation of the oceans (diagram cue: a world map of the main surface currents with warm currents in red and cold in blue, and the five gyres)
- [ ] O1 Gyres: large circular systems of surface currents, clockwise in the northern hemisphere oceans and anticlockwise in the southern hemisphere oceans (wind plus Coriolis plus continents). Five main gyres: North and South Atlantic, North and South Pacific, Indian Ocean.
- [ ] O2 The North Atlantic gyre (the syllabus's setting): the North Equatorial Current flows west with the trade winds; it turns north as the Gulf Stream along the North American coast; the westerlies carry it north-east across the Atlantic as the North Atlantic Drift towards Ireland, Britain and Norway; part turns south as the cold Canary Current along north-west Africa and rejoins the North Equatorial Current. The calm Sargasso Sea sits in the gyre's centre. The cold Labrador Current flows south from the Arctic between Greenland and Canada.
- [ ] O3 Effects of the North Atlantic circulation on weather and climate: mild Irish winters and ice-free ports; warm sea adds moisture and energy to Atlantic depressions; fog where the Gulf Stream meets the Labrador Current; icebergs carried south in the Labrador Current; the arid Saharan coast beside the cold Canary Current. Figures and comparisons in section 2 (reuse the s2 comparisons, don't repeat them at length).
- [ ] O4 Other currents named with their effects, one bullet: warm western-boundary currents (Kuroshio, Brazil, East Australian, Agulhas); cold eastern-boundary currents (California, Humboldt, Benguela); the Antarctic Circumpolar Current flowing eastward round Antarctica, the largest current by volume, unblocked by land.
- [ ] O5 Upwelling: where winds push surface water away from a coast (Ekman transport), cold, nutrient-rich deep water rises in its place; plankton thrive, supporting rich fisheries (off Peru, south-west Africa, California, north-west Africa); the cold water also chills the air, giving fog and aridity on the coast.
- [ ] O6 Thermohaline circulation (the global ocean conveyor belt): in the North Atlantic (Greenland, Norwegian and Labrador Seas) and around Antarctica, surface water is cooled and made saltier by evaporation and sea-ice formation; it becomes dense and sinks to the ocean floor; this deep water flows slowly south through the Atlantic, around Antarctica and into the Indian and Pacific Oceans, where it gradually rises and returns as warm surface currents. One circuit takes about 1,000 years. The Atlantic part (Atlantic Meridional Overturning Circulation, AMOC) pulls the Gulf Stream's warm water north. Diagram cue: the conveyor-belt world map.
- [ ] O7 A threat to the circulation: melting Greenland ice and more rain make North Atlantic water fresher and less dense, so it may sink less and the AMOC may weaken; the IPCC judges it very likely to weaken this century, which could cool north-west Europe relative to the rest of the world and change rainfall patterns. One bullet; give no dates for a collapse.

Mid-latitude depressions (formation in numbered stages, structure, weather sequence, impact). Diagram cues: a plan view of a mature depression with isobars, fronts and warm sector; a cross-section along a line through both fronts showing cloud types and rain belts.
- [ ] D1 Definition: an area of low pressure in the mid-latitudes, roughly 1,000–3,000 km across, with closed isobars, winds blowing anticlockwise inwards (northern hemisphere), and warm and cold fronts. They move from west to east, steered by the jet stream, and bring most of Ireland's rain and wind. Polar front theory (section 2).
- [ ] D2 Formation, numbered: (1) at the polar front, cold polar maritime air from the north meets warm, moist tropical maritime air from the south-west, moving in opposite directions; (2) a small wave or kink develops on the front, often where the jet stream above is diverging and removing air aloft; (3) warm air pushes into the cold air and begins to rise over it; pressure at the surface falls and air is drawn in, turning anticlockwise (Coriolis); (4) a warm front forms ahead of the warm air and a cold front behind it, with the warm sector between; the system deepens (pressure falls) as more warm air rises and latent heat is released; (5) the cold front moves faster than the warm front and catches up with it, lifting the warm sector off the ground to form an occluded front (occlusion); (6) once the warm air is cut off from the surface, the depression fills (pressure rises) and dies, usually within a few days to a week.
- [ ] D3 Weather sequence as a depression passes over Ireland, in order (one bullet per stage):
  - Approach: pressure starts to fall; high cirrus, then cirrostratus (halo round the sun); wind backs (e.g. from westerly to southerly or south-easterly) and strengthens.
  - Warm front: cloud thickens and lowers (altostratus, then nimbostratus); long period of steady, moderate rain, sometimes for several hours; pressure falls steadily; temperature rises slightly.
  - Warm sector: rain eases to drizzle or stops; low stratus, mist on hills; mild and humid; wind veers to south-westerly; pressure steady.
  - Cold front: cumulonimbus; short, heavy showers, sometimes hail and thunder; gusty squalls; temperature falls sharply; wind veers to westerly or north-westerly; pressure begins to rise.
  - Behind the cold front: clearer, colder, brighter air with scattered cumulus and showers; good visibility; pressure rises.
  - Occluded front (one bullet): combined cloud and rain of both fronts, often with lighter rain as the system ages.
- [ ] D4 Impact on weather patterns, one bullet each: Ireland's changeable weather and rain in every month; the west wetter because depressions arrive there first; strong winds and storms, especially in autumn and winter when the temperature contrast and jet stream are strongest; coastal storm surges; flooding when depressions follow each other in quick succession; storms named by Met Éireann (reuse Ophelia and Éowyn only from `geo-weather-climate-s1`, one clause each).

Anticyclones
- [ ] A1 Definition: an area of high pressure with widely spaced, closed isobars, light winds blowing clockwise outwards (northern hemisphere), and no fronts. Large and slow-moving; can last for days or weeks.
- [ ] A2 Formation: air converging high in the atmosphere sinks (subsidence); as it sinks it is compressed and warms adiabatically, so its relative humidity falls and clouds evaporate. Two origins, one bullet each: dynamic (warm) highs from sinking air in the general circulation, e.g. the Azores High, which often extends a ridge over Ireland in summer; thermal (cold) highs formed by intense cooling of the ground in winter, e.g. the Siberian High over Central Asia. A temperature inversion often forms in the sinking air, trapping moisture and pollution beneath it.
- [ ] A3 Summer weather: clear skies, strong sunshine, high daytime temperatures, light winds; cool nights with dew and sometimes morning mist; occasional heat-triggered thunderstorms at the edges; sea breezes on coasts (link B1). Prolonged summer highs cause heatwaves and drought (Ireland 2018, s6).
- [ ] A4 Winter weather: clear skies at night let the ground lose heat rapidly: hard frost, freezing fog in valleys (radiation fog, s3), which may persist all day; cold, crisp sunny days; or "anticyclonic gloom" when a layer of stratocumulus is trapped under the inversion; smog in towns where pollution is trapped (link to `geo-economic-environment`).
- [ ] A5 Blocking anticyclones: a high that stays in place (often over Scandinavia or west of Ireland) blocks the westerly flow and diverts depressions north or south; the result is a long spell of dry weather, a heatwave in summer or a cold spell in winter, while other regions get prolonged rain. A block to the east can draw very cold polar continental air to Ireland (2018 example in section 2).
- [ ] A6 Comparison table cue: depression vs anticyclone (pressure, isobar spacing, wind direction and strength, air movement, cloud, rain, typical weather, speed of movement, size). One block.

Land and sea breezes (local winds from differential heating; formation in steps; diagram cue: two cross-sections, day and night, with arrows, relative pressure and the circulation cell)
- [ ] B1 Sea breeze (by day): land heats faster than the sea under sunshine; air over the land warms, expands and rises, creating lower pressure over the land; relatively higher pressure over the cooler sea; cool air flows onshore from sea to land; the air that rose returns seaward at height and sinks over the sea, completing a circulation cell. Strongest on hot, calm, sunny afternoons in summer (often under an anticyclone) and in the tropics, where it may blow most days.
- [ ] B2 Land breeze (by night): land loses heat faster than the sea after sunset; air over the land cools and becomes denser; higher pressure over the land, lower over the warmer sea; air flows offshore. Weaker than the sea breeze because the night-time temperature difference is smaller.
- [ ] B3 Impact on weather: sea breezes lower coastal temperatures in the afternoon and raise humidity; a sea-breeze front can trigger a line of cumulus or showers inland; land breezes help clear coastal fog; traditionally used by sailing fishermen (out at dawn with the land breeze, back in the afternoon with the sea breeze); sea breezes can carry sea fog onshore and trap urban pollution. Lake breezes form the same way on large lakes.

Mountain and valley winds (diagram cue: day and night cross-sections of a valley)
- [ ] V1 Valley wind (anabatic, by day): sunshine heats the valley sides; air in contact warms and rises upslope; cumulus may form over the peaks in the afternoon.
- [ ] V2 Mountain wind (katabatic, by night): slopes lose heat by radiation; air in contact cools, becomes denser and drains downslope under gravity, collecting on the valley floor. Effects: frost hollows and temperature inversions (valley floors colder than the slopes above; orchards and vineyards planted on slopes above the frost line), radiation fog in valleys; in Antarctica and Greenland, katabatic winds flowing off the ice sheets can reach gale force.
- [ ] V3 Föhn winds: warm, dry winds on the lee side of mountains: air rising on the windward side cools at the saturated rate after condensation; descending on the lee side, it warms at the faster dry rate, so it arrives warmer and drier than it started. Effects: rapid snowmelt, avalanche and fire risk, sudden temperature rises. Named examples in section 2.
- [ ] V4 Cold mountain-gap winds of the Mediterranean (one bullet): the mistral and the bora (section 2), cold, dry, strong winds that pour from high ground towards the sea, usually when high pressure lies inland and low pressure over the sea.

Thunderstorms (formation in stages; diagram cue: a cumulonimbus cell in its three stages, with updraft and downdraft arrows, the freezing level, the anvil at the tropopause)
- [ ] T1 Conditions: plentiful moisture, unstable air, and a trigger that lifts it: strong surface heating (convection), a cold front, mountains, or converging air.
- [ ] T2 Cumulus (developing) stage: warm, moist air rises in strong updrafts; condensation releases latent heat, which keeps the air warmer than its surroundings, so it rises faster; cloud grows upward above the freezing level; no rain yet.
- [ ] T3 Mature stage: water drops and ice grow large enough to fall, dragging air down and creating downdrafts beside the updrafts; heavy rain, hail, lightning, thunder and gusty winds at the surface; the cloud top spreads out at the tropopause into an anvil. The most violent stage.
- [ ] T4 Dissipating stage: downdrafts spread through the cloud, cutting off the supply of warm, moist air; rain becomes lighter and the cloud breaks up. A single cell lasts about an hour.
- [ ] T5 Lightning and thunder: collisions between ice crystals, hailstones and supercooled droplets separate electrical charges (usually positive at the top, negative at the base); a lightning discharge occurs within the cloud, between clouds or to the ground; it heats the air along its path enormously (section 2), making the air expand explosively: thunder. Light travels faster than sound, so the flash is seen first; counting the seconds to the thunder gives the distance (about 3 seconds per km).
- [ ] T6 Types: air-mass (convectional) thunderstorms in hot humid air; frontal thunderstorms along vigorous cold fronts; orographic thunderstorms over mountains; supercells (long-lived, rotating storms that can produce tornadoes and very large hail, common on the US Great Plains). One bullet each.
- [ ] T7 Distribution and impact: most frequent over tropical land (daily in the equatorial belt), less frequent in the mid-latitudes and rare at the poles; Ireland has relatively few, mainly in summer inland and with active cold fronts. Impacts: flash flooding, hail damage to crops, lightning strikes (fires, power cuts, deaths), downbursts dangerous to aircraft.

Tropical cyclones (a circulation feature students use in this question and in s2; one block)
- [ ] C1 Conditions: sea-surface temperature of at least about 26–27 °C over a depth of ocean, at least about 5° from the equator (Coriolis needed to start rotation), light winds aloft (low wind shear), and warm, moist, unstable air. Season in the North Atlantic: June to November.
- [ ] C2 Formation and structure: clusters of thunderstorms over warm ocean; rising air and released latent heat lower the pressure; inflowing air spirals in and rises in the eyewall; the calm, clear eye has sinking air; spiral rain bands. They weaken over land or cold water because their energy (evaporation from warm sea) is cut off. Diagram cue: a cross-section with the eye and eyewall.
- [ ] C3 Names and scale: hurricane (Atlantic and north-east Pacific), typhoon (north-west Pacific), cyclone (Indian Ocean and South Pacific). Saffir–Simpson scale, categories 1–5 (section 2).
- [ ] C4 Impacts: destructive winds, torrential rain and flooding, storm surge (usually the biggest killer); case examples in section 2, one bullet each.

## 3. Traps

- Coriolis deflects to the **right** in the northern hemisphere and the **left** in the southern; it is **zero at the equator** (which is why hurricanes don't form within about 5° of it).
- Northern hemisphere: **anticlockwise** round a depression, **clockwise** round an anticyclone. Reverse for the southern hemisphere. Ocean gyres: clockwise in the north, anticlockwise in the south.
- In a depression air **rises** (cloud, rain); in an anticyclone air **sinks** and warms (clear skies). Don't say anticyclones are "warm" in winter: they bring frost and fog.
- The cold front moves faster than the warm front; occlusion happens when the cold front **catches up**. Warm-front rain is long and steady; cold-front rain is short and heavy.
- Wind **backs** (anticlockwise change) ahead of the depression and **veers** (clockwise) at each front; don't reverse them.
- A sea breeze blows **from the sea** by day; a land breeze blows **from the land** at night. Name winds by the direction they come from.
- Anabatic = upslope (valley wind, by day); katabatic = downslope (mountain wind, by night).
- The föhn is warm because of the difference between the **saturated** (slower) cooling going up and the **dry** (faster) warming coming down, not because it comes from a warm place.
- Thunder is caused by the explosive **expansion of heated air**, not by clouds colliding.
- The Gulf Stream becomes the North Atlantic Drift off Europe; the Canary Current is cold. The Sargasso Sea is calm, not a current.
- Don't give storm wind speeds, rainfall totals or damage costs for Ophelia, Éowyn or Emma. Don't name other Irish storms. Don't claim a confirmed AMOC collapse date.
- El Niño (Walker circulation) is in s5; give one clause pointing there. The Indian monsoon's mechanism is in `geo-region-continental-s1` and s5.
- Chart reading, front symbols and station models are in `geo-weather-climate-s1`. Don't redraw the symbols here; describe fronts as weather.
