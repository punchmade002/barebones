- **Scale:** Elective Unit 4. Higher Level 30-mark questions ask students to describe the EU's

## 1. Must cover (one bullet or block for every item; tick each off)

The EU as a trading bloc
- [ ] B1 Trading bloc: a group of countries that remove trade barriers between themselves and act together in trade with the rest of the world. The EU is one of the world's three largest traders (with China and the USA).
- [ ] B2 Customs union: no tariffs between members and a common external tariff on imports from outside. Completed 1968.
- [ ] B3 Single market (from 1993): the four freedoms (free movement of goods, services, capital and people); removal of non-tariff barriers (border checks, different product standards, national preference in public contracts); common standards (e.g. the CE mark).
- [ ] B4 The single market extends beyond the 27 members: Norway, Iceland and Liechtenstein through the European Economic Area; Switzerland through bilateral agreements.
- [ ] B5 Key terms: imports, exports, visible (goods) and invisible (services) trade, balance of trade (exports minus imports), trade surplus and deficit, tariff, quota, intra-EU (internal) and extra-EU (external) trade. Exams ask for "balance of trade".

Internal trade: patterns
- [ ] I1 Scale: most EU members' trade is with each other. About three-fifths of member states' goods exports go to other EU countries (2024).
- [ ] I2 Germany is by far the largest intra-EU trader; the core (the "Blue Banana" from south-east England through the Benelux and the Rhine to northern Italy, and the Paris Basin) dominates. Peripheral members (e.g. Cyprus, Greece) trade much less within the EU.
- [ ] I3 What is traded: mainly manufactured goods (machinery, vehicles and parts, chemicals and pharmaceuticals), then food and farm produce, plus services (finance, tourism, transport).
- [ ] I4 Intra-industry trade and supply chains: members swap similar goods (cars for cars); components cross borders several times. Example: the car industry linking Germany with Czechia, Slovakia, Hungary and Poland since the 2004 enlargement.
- [ ] I5 Complementary trade: Mediterranean fruit and vegetables (e.g. Spain) go north; northern manufactured goods go south and east.
- [ ] I6 Eastern members are the most dependent on intra-EU trade (Czechia's trade is over four-fifths intra-EU), because of proximity to Germany and their integration into its supply chains.
- [ ] I7 Gateway ports: Rotterdam and Antwerp handle goods entering and leaving the single market, so the Netherlands and Belgium have very large trade totals (the "Rotterdam effect": goods recorded as trade with the Netherlands may be going elsewhere).

Internal trade: factors (exams ask to "explain the factors")
- [ ] IF1 Removal of tariffs and border controls (customs union, single market, Schengen border-free travel for most members) cuts costs and delays.
- [ ] IF2 The euro: no currency exchange costs or exchange-rate risk among the 21 euro countries (2026); easy price comparison. Point to `geo-region-european-s3` for the euro's history.
- [ ] IF3 Proximity and transport: short distances; dense motorway and rail networks; the Rhine waterway; the Channel Tunnel; the Øresund Bridge; EU-funded Trans-European Transport Networks (TEN-T).
- [ ] IF4 Similar, wealthy consumer markets: about 450 million people with high incomes and similar tastes.
- [ ] IF5 Enlargement: each enlargement widened the single market (e.g. 2004 and 2007), opening new markets and lower-cost production sites in the east. Exams ask "one way a change in EU membership has affected EU trade".
- [ ] IF6 Brexit as a membership change that shrank the internal market: the UK moved from internal to external partner (left 31 January 2020; Trade and Cooperation Agreement from 1 January 2021: no tariffs or quotas on qualifying goods, but customs declarations, checks and rules-of-origin paperwork). Trade with the UK became more costly.
- [ ] IF7 Common policies: CAP and common standards make farm and food trade easy within the EU. One bullet; CAP detail is in `geo-economic-eu-s3`.

External trade: patterns
- [ ] E1 Common Commercial Policy: the European Commission negotiates trade for all 27 members as one bloc; one voice in the World Trade Organization (WTO); the common external tariff.
- [ ] E2 Main export partners (2024): USA (largest, about a fifth), UK, China, Switzerland, Turkey.
- [ ] E3 Main import partners (2024): China (largest, about a fifth), USA, UK, Switzerland, Turkey.
- [ ] E4 Balance of trade: the EU had an overall goods trade surplus in 2024; surplus with the USA and the UK, large deficit with China.
- [ ] E5 What the EU exports: high-value manufactured goods (machinery and vehicles about two-fifths of extra-EU exports; chemicals including pharmaceuticals about a fifth), plus food and drink and services.
- [ ] E6 What the EU imports: energy (oil and gas), raw materials, consumer electronics and other manufactured goods (from China and Asia), some food (tropical products such as coffee, cocoa, bananas).
- [ ] E7 Energy imports and Russia: Russia was a major supplier of gas before its 2022 invasion of Ukraine; sanctions and REPowerEU cut its share sharply; LNG from the USA replaced much of it. Shows how political events change trade patterns.
- [ ] E8 Trade agreements widen external trade: examples with Canada (CETA), Japan and South Korea; negotiations with Mercosur concluded in 2024 (opposed by many Irish and French farmers).
- [ ] E9 Trade with developing countries: preferential access for poorer countries (Lomé Convention 1975, Cotonou Agreement 2000; Everything But Arms 2001 gives the least developed countries duty-free, quota-free access for all goods except arms). Justice point: tariffs on processed goods (tariff escalation) and past subsidised farm exports harmed developing-world producers.
- [ ] E10 Trade disputes and protection: the 2025 EU–USA deal capping US tariffs on most EU goods at 15%; EU duties on Chinese electric cars (2024). Shows the EU acting as one bloc.

External trade: factors
- [ ] EF1 Size and wealth of the EU market gives bargaining power in negotiations.
- [ ] EF2 High-tech, high-value industry makes manufactured goods the main exports.
- [ ] EF3 Lack of energy and some raw materials makes the EU import-dependent for fuels.
- [ ] EF4 Historical and colonial links (former colonies in Africa, the Caribbean and Pacific; Commonwealth ties of the former UK member) shaped preferential agreements.
- [ ] EF5 Globalisation: low-cost manufacturing in China and Asia; the trading triad (USA, EU, Pacific Rim). One bullet; the triad is taught in `geo-economic-globalisation`.
- [ ] EF6 Politics: sanctions (Russia), tariff disputes (USA), and Brexit all redirect trade.

Skill
- [ ] S1 Reading trade pie charts and tables: identify largest partner; calculate a missing percentage (100 minus the rest); compare export and import shares; say whether there is a surplus or deficit. Use invented, labelled illustrative values only.

## 3. Traps

- Customs union (no internal tariffs, common external tariff, 1968) ≠ single market (also removes non-tariff barriers and frees services, capital and people, 1993). Don't merge them.
- Switzerland and Norway are not EU members. Turkey is a candidate (`geo-region-european-s3`), not a member.
- The UK is now an **external** partner. Don't count it in intra-EU trade after 2020, and don't say Brexit brought tariffs on goods: the TCA has zero tariffs on qualifying goods; the new costs are customs paperwork, checks and rules of origin.
- The euro is not the currency of the whole EU (21 of 27 in 2026).
- Never use the past-paper pie-chart figures for 2021 (e.g. Switzerland 6%, USA 18%, Russia X). Use the 2024 figures above or invented, labelled values in skill items.
- Past-paper model answers contain unchecked claims (e.g. "EU–US trade exceeds €600 billion", "Germany surplus €45+ billion", "Russia supplied 40% of EU gas"): use only the figures in section 2. The checked Russian share is about 45% (2021).
- Ireland's own trade with the EU belongs to `geo-economic-eu-s2`; EU policies' effects on Ireland (CAP, CFP, funds) to `geo-economic-eu-s3`. Give only one Irish example here if any (e.g. Irish pharmaceutical exports to the USA as part of EU–US trade).
- The history of enlargement and the EU's future (political union, sovereignty, euro crisis) belongs to `geo-region-european-s3`/`s4`. Use enlargement and Brexit here only for their effect on trade.
- MNCs, the trading triad and the international division of labour belong to `geo-economic-globalisation`; refer to them in one bullet.
- Don't call Rotterdam "the world's largest port" (Asian ports are larger); it is Europe's largest.

## 4. What exams ask (topics only)

- 30 marks, Higher: describe the EU's current trading patterns, internal and external, and explain the factors that influence them. Expected: share of intra-EU trade; main internal traders and goods; external partners (USA top export market, China top import source, UK, Switzerland); what is exported and imported; surplus/deficit; factors (single market, euro, proximity and transport, enlargement, Brexit, Common Commercial Policy, energy dependence, trade agreements, sanctions).
- Data questions on pie charts of EU export and import partners: read a percentage; calculate a missing share; compare exports and imports with one partner; name a partner with which the EU has a surplus.
- 2 marks: explain "balance of trade".
- 2 marks: one way a change in EU membership has affected EU trade (Brexit making the UK an external partner; enlargement widening the market).
