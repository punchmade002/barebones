# Package: geo-economic-eu-s3 — How the EU influences the Irish economy

- **loId:** `geo-economic-eu-s3` · **chapterId:** `geo-economic-eu` · **syllabusRef:** 4.4
- **Syllabus:** Students should study "Irish trading patterns with the EU and how the EU
  influences the Irish economy, for example: – common agricultural policy – common fisheries
  policy – regional development funds – social funding." (National setting: "Appropriate
  relevant national examples".)
- **Prepared by:** a higher model, 2026-10-20. The writer works only from this package and
  `course-outlines/NOTES_STANDARD.md`.
- **Scale:** the four named policies are each a studied area. Each needs its own block able to
  carry a 30-mark Higher answer (about 15 SRPs): aims, how it works and has changed, effects on
  Ireland (positive and negative), named Irish examples and dated figures. Ordinary questions
  ask students to name a policy and describe its effects, or give positive and negative
  impacts of EU policies.

## 1. Must cover (one bullet or block for every item; tick each off)

Overview
- [ ] O1 How the EU budget works for Ireland: Ireland was a net recipient from 1973 until about 2014 (receiving over €40 billion more than it paid in, mostly through the CAP); a net contributor since then because of its economic growth.
- [ ] O2 Advantages of EU membership for Ireland, short list (exams ask for one or two): access to the single market, FDI, CAP payments, structural funds for infrastructure, social and equality laws, free movement to work and study. Costs: loss of control over some policies (fishing, farm prices), net contributions now, competition from lower-cost members. Enlargement's effects on Ireland are taught in `geo-region-european-s4`; sovereignty in `geo-region-european-s3`. One block.

A. Common Agricultural Policy (CAP)
- [ ] A1 Origins and aims: set up in 1962; aims in the Treaty of Rome: raise farm productivity, give farmers a fair standard of living, stabilise markets, secure food supplies, reasonable prices for consumers. Once the largest part of the EU budget; still about a third of it.
- [ ] A2 Early CAP (1962–1992): guaranteed (intervention) prices above world levels, intervention buying of surpluses, export subsidies, import levies. Results: overproduction ("butter mountains", "wine lakes"), high cost, dumping on world markets.
- [ ] A3 Effects on Ireland after 1973: farm prices and incomes rose sharply; guaranteed market for beef and dairy; farm modernisation (machinery, fertiliser, silage, land drainage, farm buildings); the "golden age" of the 1970s.
- [ ] A4 Milk quotas (1984–2015): capped each farm's milk output to cut surpluses; held back Irish dairying for 30 years. Abolition in 2015 led to rapid expansion of dairy herds and milk output, especially in the south (Munster).
- [ ] A5 Reforms: MacSharry reform (1992, Irish Commissioner Ray MacSharry): price cuts replaced by direct payments, set-aside. Agenda 2000: rural development became the CAP's "second pillar". Fischler reform (2003): decoupling, payments no longer tied to production; Single Farm Payment (2005); Basic Payment Scheme (2015); cross-compliance with environmental and animal-welfare rules.
- [ ] A6 CAP 2023–2027: Ireland's CAP Strategic Plan; Basic Income Support for Sustainability (BISS); eco-scheme; convergence (moving payments toward the national average); payments for young farmers; ACRES agri-environment scheme; Areas of Natural Constraint (ANC) payments for disadvantaged land.
- [ ] A7 Rural development: REPS (1994–2009) agri-environment scheme; LEADER (from 1991) for local rural enterprise (farm diversification, rural tourism, crafts); grants for forestry and young farmers.
- [ ] A8 Irish farm structure today and types of farm (exams ask to name farm types): dairy, cattle (beef) rearing, sheep, tillage, mixed; specialist beef is the most common type. Number and size of farms; ageing farmers.
- [ ] A9 Positive effects summary: stable incomes, guaranteed market, modernisation, food quality standards, environmental schemes, rural communities supported; dependence on direct payments for many beef and sheep farms.
- [ ] A10 Negative effects: payments historically favoured bigger farmers (most money to a minority); dependence on subsidies; bureaucracy and inspections; intensification damaging water quality (nitrates) and raising greenhouse gas emissions (dairy herd growth); fall in farm numbers and ageing farmers; small western farms remain uneconomic; trade deals (e.g. Mercosur) feared to undercut Irish beef.
- [ ] A11 Nitrates Directive: limits organic nitrogen per hectare; Ireland has had a derogation allowing higher stocking on intensive farms, conditional on water quality. Shows EU environmental rules shaping farming. One bullet, no figures beyond section 2.

B. Common Fisheries Policy (CFP)
- [ ] B1 Aims and principles: a "common pond" of EU waters with equal access for all members' fleets beyond coastal limits; conservation of stocks; agreed in full in 1983. Ireland had to share its rich waters with much larger fleets (Spain, France, the Netherlands).
- [ ] B2 How it works: Total Allowable Catches (TACs) set yearly by the Council of Ministers, divided into national quotas using fixed shares (relative stability); a coastal band (12 nautical miles) reserved mainly for local boats; days-at-sea limits; mesh sizes; closed areas and seasons.
- [ ] B3 The Hague Preferences (1976): give Ireland a smaller cut when TACs fall, in recognition of its coastal communities' dependence on fishing.
- [ ] B4 Irish fishing industry: Killybegs (Co. Donegal) the largest port, pelagic fishing for mackerel, herring and blue whiting; other fishery harbour centres (Castletownbere, Ros an Mhíl, Dingle, Howth, Dunmore East); Bord Iascaigh Mhara (BIM) develops the industry; processing jobs in peripheral coastal areas.
- [ ] B5 Positive effects: EU funding for boats, harbours and processing plants (now the European Maritime, Fisheries and Aquaculture Fund); conservation aims to protect stocks long-term; access to EU seafood markets; protection for some stocks through the Hague Preferences.
- [ ] B6 Negative effects: Ireland's quota share is small relative to the size and richness of its waters; overfishing and stock collapses despite the CFP; discards (throwing back dead fish) until the landing obligation; decommissioning of boats; decline of jobs in coastal communities; quota cuts under Brexit.
- [ ] B7 2013 reform: landing obligation (discard ban) phased in 2015–2019; fishing at maximum sustainable yield.
- [ ] B8 Brexit and fishing: the Trade and Cooperation Agreement transferred EU quota to the UK; Ireland lost a disproportionately large share, notably of mackerel; the Seafood Task Force; the Brexit Adjustment Reserve funded a vessel decommissioning (permanent cessation) scheme and pelagic support schemes.
- [ ] B9 Depletion of fish stocks as an economic–environmental conflict is taught in `geo-economic-environment`. One cross-reference bullet only.

C. Regional development funds
- [ ] C1 Why: regional inequality in the EU; Ireland was a poor, peripheral member in 1973.
- [ ] C2 European Regional Development Fund (ERDF), set up 1975: infrastructure, enterprise, research and innovation in poorer regions. Structural Funds (ERDF, ESF and the farm guidance fund) doubled after 1988–89 to prepare poorer regions for the single market.
- [ ] C3 Ireland as one Objective 1 (poorest) region, 1989–1999: the whole country qualified (income per head below 75% of the EU average). National Development Plans co-financed by the EU.
- [ ] C4 Cohesion Fund (1993/94, Maastricht): for member states with income below 90% of the EU average (Ireland, Spain, Portugal, Greece); funded transport and environmental projects. Ireland ceased to qualify as its income rose in the 2000s.
- [ ] C5 Regionalisation for 2000–2006: Ireland split into two regions so that the Border, Midland and Western (BMW) region kept Objective 1 status while the Southern and Eastern region was phased out (facts as in `geo-region-european-s4`).
- [ ] C6 What the funds built: roads (the inter-urban motorways and bypasses), public transport, water and wastewater treatment, ports, airports, broadband, research centres and enterprise support. Effects: better access for peripheral regions, attraction of FDI, contribution to the Celtic Tiger. No checked project-by-project figures.
- [ ] C7 2021–2027: ERDF regional programmes run by the regional assemblies; the Northern and Western Region reclassified as a "transition region" (its income relative to the EU average fell); Southern and Eastern & Midland are "more developed" regions.
- [ ] C8 Other EU regional funds affecting Ireland: PEACE PLUS and INTERREG for the border region (facts in `geo-region-european-s3`); the Just Transition Fund for the Midlands after the end of peat harvesting for electricity by Bord na Móna; the Brexit Adjustment Reserve (Ireland the largest recipient).
- [ ] C9 Evaluation: funds helped Ireland converge with EU incomes and modernise infrastructure, but the gap between the Dublin-centred east and the Northern and Western region persists; Ireland now receives far less as a wealthy member and funds go mainly to eastern Europe (an effect of enlargement).

D. Social funding
- [ ] D1 European Social Fund (ESF), set up by the Treaty of Rome (1957), the oldest structural fund: improves employment, skills and social inclusion.
- [ ] D2 ESF in Ireland: co-financed training and education through FÁS (now SOLAS and the Education and Training Boards), Youthreach for early school-leavers, Post-Leaving Cert courses, apprenticeships, back-to-education schemes, programmes for the long-term unemployed, people with disabilities, Travellers and women returning to work. Helped create the skilled workforce that attracted FDI.
- [ ] D3 ESF+ 2021–2027 in Ireland: the Employment, Inclusion, Skills and Training (EIST) programme: EU and national money; priorities: social inclusion, youth employment, upskilling and lifelong learning.
- [ ] D4 European Globalisation Adjustment Fund (EGF, 2007): retraining for workers made redundant by global trade shifts; Irish cases include Dell (Limerick) and Waterford Crystal.
- [ ] D5 EU social legislation as "social" influence: equal pay and equal treatment for women (end of the civil service marriage bar, 1973; Irish equality laws of 1974 and 1977 following EEC directives); working-time limits; health and safety; maternity and parental leave; rights of part-time workers.
- [ ] D6 Free movement and education: Irish people can live and work anywhere in the EU; Erasmus student exchanges (from 1987); EU workers in Ireland after 2004 (details in `geo-region-european-s4`).
- [ ] D7 Evaluation: raised skills and employment, especially in the 1990s; criticism that some training schemes were poorly targeted; funding now much smaller relative to the economy.

## 3. Traps

- The syllabus calls them "regional development funds" and "social funding". Use these headings; don't swap ERDF (regional) and ESF (social).
- Milk quotas ran 1984–2015, not from 1973. The MacSharry reform was 1992; decoupling came with the 2003 Fischler reform, not MacSharry.
- Don't give a single "CAP payments per year to Irish farmers" figure (a past-paper answer gives €1.5 billion): use the 2023–27 plan figures only.
- Don't say Ireland "receives" more than it pays today: it has been a net contributor since 2014.
- Ireland was one Objective 1 region in 1989–99; the BMW/S&E split started in 2000. Don't say the whole country was Objective 1 in 2000–06.
- Don't give a figure for Ireland's share of EU waters versus its share of quota (often quoted, unchecked). Say Ireland's quota share is small relative to its waters.
- The "Irish Box" is commonly confused in textbooks; don't use it.
- Fish-stock depletion as an environmental conflict belongs to `geo-economic-environment`; enlargement's effects on Ireland to `geo-region-european-s4`; sovereignty, the euro and bailout to `geo-region-european-s3`. Cross-reference only.
- Past-paper answers mention a "Common Commercial Policy" and the "four freedoms" as Irish-economy policies. The syllabus's four are CAP, CFP, regional funds and social funding; the single market is covered in `geo-economic-eu-s1`/`s2`.
- Don't use past-paper milk-table figures (e.g. the Netherlands' 2018 milk output).
- Don't give a derogation stocking limit (170/220/250 kg) or say whether Ireland's nitrates derogation has been renewed: not checked.

## 4. What exams ask (topics only)

- Ordinary 30 marks (recurring): name one EU policy that affects Ireland's economy; describe and explain its effects. Expected: CAP or CFP (or regional/social funds) with positive and negative effects.
- Ordinary: name two EU policies; positive and negative impacts of EU policies; two challenges facing the EU (challenges are in `geo-region-european-s3`).
- Higher 20-mark data question: two purposes of the ERDF (reduce regional inequality; fund infrastructure, enterprise and innovation in poorer regions).
- Short questions: one advantage of EU membership; one advantage for Irish farmers; types of farm in Ireland.
- Higher 30 marks: examine how the EU has influenced the Irish economy with reference to two of CAP, CFP, regional funds, social funding.

## 5. Practice questions to write

1. 30 marks, Higher: "Examine the impact of the Common Agricultural Policy on Irish agriculture." Model answer: CAP **only**: aims and early price supports, effects after 1973, milk quotas and their abolition with checked figures, MacSharry and decoupling, CAP 2023–27, rural development schemes, Census of Agriculture 2020 figures, positive and negative effects. About 15 SRPs.
2. 30 marks, Higher: "Discuss the impact of the Common Fisheries Policy on the Irish fishing industry." Model answer: CFP **only**: common pond and TACs/quotas, Hague Preferences, Killybegs and other ports, funding, conservation, discards and the 2013 reform, Brexit quota losses and the Brexit Adjustment Reserve, effects on coastal communities. About 15 SRPs.
3. 30 marks, Ordinary: "(i) Name two ways the EU has funded regional or social development in Ireland. (ii) Explain the benefits of each." Model answer: ERDF/Cohesion Fund (Objective 1 1989–99, infrastructure, BMW region, Northern and Western transition region) and ESF (training, Youthreach, EIST programme, EGF for Dell workers). About 7 SRPs each.
