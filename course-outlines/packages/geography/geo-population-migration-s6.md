# Package: geo-population-migration-s6 — Rural-to-urban migration in developed and developing regions

- **loId:** `geo-population-migration-s6` · **chapterId:** `geo-population-migration` · **syllabusRef:** 5.3
- **Syllabus:** Statement: "Population movements have an impact on the donor and receiver
  regions." "Students should study • contrasting impacts of rural/urban migration in the
  developed and developing regions." National setting: "Growth in Irish cities."
- **Prepared by:** a higher model, 2026-10-20. The writer works only from this package and
  `course-outlines/NOTES_STANDARD.md`.
- **Studied areas:** developed: Ireland (the West and North-West as donor, Dublin and the
  other cities as receivers) with Italy's Mezzogiorno and Paris as European examples;
  developing: India (Bihar and Uttar Pradesh to Mumbai and Kolkata), Brazil (the North-East to
  São Paulo and Rio de Janeiro) and China (rural interior to the coastal cities). Each has its own
  block below. The answer must contrast impacts on donor (rural) and receiver (urban) regions in
  both kinds of region.

## 1. Must cover (one bullet or block for every item; tick each off)

The process
- [ ] P1 Urbanisation (the growing share of people living in towns) vs urban growth; rural-to-urban migration as its main driver in developing countries, natural increase in the cities as the other.
- [ ] P2 World trend: over half the world's people urban since about 2007; about two-thirds expected by 2050; the fastest growth now in Asia and Africa; megacities.
- [ ] P3 Push factors from rural areas (exams ask for two reasons people move): lack of jobs and low farm incomes; mechanisation of farming; small, subdivided holdings; poor services (schools, hospitals, broadband, transport); drought, famine and land degradation (developing world); isolation and lack of social life.
- [ ] P4 Pull factors to cities: jobs in industry and services; higher wages; third-level education; better health care and services; entertainment; "bright lights"; existing family networks (chain migration).
- [ ] P5 Counter-urbanisation (people moving out of cities to towns and the countryside) in developed countries, and remote working since COVID-19; one bullet.

Developed region: Ireland (main case study)
- [ ] I1 History: a rural country until the later 20th century; the urban share crossed half in 1971; flight from the land as farming mechanised and small farms became unviable; industrial and service jobs concentrated in Dublin and the cities.
- [ ] I2 Growth in Irish cities (the syllabus setting): Dublin's dominance (primate city), the Greater Dublin Area's share of the population; growth of the commuter counties; growth of Cork, Limerick, Galway and Waterford.
- [ ] I3 Impacts on the receiver region (Dublin), positive: large labour pool attracts multinationals and services; economies of scale for transport, universities and hospitals; cultural life. Negative: housing shortages and high rents and prices; homelessness; urban sprawl into Meath, Kildare and Wicklow; long commutes and traffic congestion; pressure on water supply and services; high-rise and infill pressure.
- [ ] I4 Impacts on the donor region (the West and North-West, e.g. Leitrim and Mayo), negative: ageing population and higher dependency ratios; fewer young adults and lower birth rates; closure of schools, post offices, Garda stations, shops and pubs; derelict houses; loss of community and sporting clubs (GAA teams amalgamating); farms with no successor; cultural loss (Gaeltacht decline). Positive: less pressure on land; remittances and returning migrants; some farm consolidation.
- [ ] I5 Responses: the National Spatial Strategy (2002, gateways and hubs); the National Planning Framework / Project Ireland 2040 (2018) to balance growth between Dublin and the regional cities; decentralisation of public service jobs; the CLÁR programme and Town and Village Renewal Scheme; the Western Development Commission; rural broadband and remote working hubs. Evaluation one line each.

Developed region: European examples (brief, reuse other packages)
- [ ] E1 Italy: migration from the Mezzogiorno to the industrial cities of the north (Turin, Milan) after the Second World War; depopulating southern villages; €1 house schemes.
- [ ] E2 France: Paris's dominance and Gravier's "Paris and the French desert".

Developing regions (each with donor and receiver impacts)
- [ ] D1 India: migrants from poorer, rural states (Bihar, Uttar Pradesh) to Mumbai, Delhi and Kolkata; push of small holdings, debt, landlessness and drought; pull of jobs in construction, factories and the informal sector. Receiver impacts: slums (Dharavi in Mumbai, bustees in Kolkata), pressure on water, sanitation and transport, informal work, but a large cheap labour supply and remittances home. Donor impacts: male out-migration leaves women, children and the old to farm; remittances fund houses, education and farm inputs; loss of labour; the 2020 lockdown return.
- [ ] D2 Brazil: migration from the drought-prone North-East (the sertão) to São Paulo and Rio de Janeiro; favelas on hillsides and at the urban edge; informal work; crime and gang violence in some favelas; donor region losing young people. Brazil is now highly urbanised.
- [ ] D3 China: the hukou (household registration) system ties public services to a person's registered home; hundreds of millions of rural migrant workers, many in coastal cities such as Shenzhen, Guangzhou and Shanghai; "left-behind" children and elderly in rural villages; migrants lack full access to urban schools, health care and housing; gradual hukou reform.
- [ ] D4 The contrast (the syllabus's "contrasting impacts"): in developed countries migration is slower, cities can largely absorb it with planned housing and services, and the donor problem is ageing rural decline; in developing countries migration is fast and huge, cities cannot provide housing, jobs or services (slums, informal sector), while donor villages depend on remittances and lose working-age men; the scale of urban growth comes from both migration and high natural increase among young migrants.

## 3. Traps

- Don't copy past-paper city population charts (world's largest cities, projected 2025 growth); use only the checked figures.
- Census 2022 showed every county growing; don't say western counties lost population between 2016 and 2022. Rural decline is now local (remote parishes and townlands).
- Dublin city and suburbs (about 1.26 million) is not the same as Co. Dublin (about 1.46 million) or the Greater Dublin Area (about 2.1 million). Use the right one.
- Dharavi is in Mumbai, not Kolkata; Rocinha is in Rio de Janeiro, not São Paulo.
- The causes, problems and planning of developing-world cities (traffic, sprawl, shanty-town upgrading) as a full case study belong to `geo-urban` (developing-world cities); the problems of Irish cities (sprawl, congestion, urban decay, renewal) also belong to `geo-urban`. Here, describe them only as impacts of migration on the receiver region, one bullet each, and keep the focus on the donor/receiver contrast.
- Dublin's core status and the West's peripherality as regions belong to `geo-region-ireland`; India's regional geography to `geo-region-continental-s1`. Reuse their checked facts; don't extend them.
- International migration belongs to `geo-population-migration-s3` and `-s4`.

## 4. What exams ask (topics only)

- 40 marks, Ordinary (asked twice): two reasons people migrate from rural to urban areas; the effect on the urban areas they go to; the effect on the rural areas they leave.
- 30 marks, Higher: contrasting impacts of rural-to-urban migration on donor and receiver regions in the developed and developing world (from the syllabus wording).
- Data questions: one reason for a falling rural population; why urban dependency ratios are lower than rural ones (taught in `geo-population-migration-s1`).
- Related questions on rapidly growing developing-world cities, urban sprawl and the impact of expanding cities on surrounding areas are `geo-urban` questions; the migration causes here support them.
