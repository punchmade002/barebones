# Package: geo-option-global-interdependence-s1 — Views of development and underdevelopment

- **loId:** `geo-option-global-interdependence-s1` · **chapterId:** `geo-option-global-interdependence` · **syllabusRef:** 6.1
- **Syllabus:** Statement: "Views of development and underdevelopment are subject to change."
  Students should "challenge all views of development", "critically examine contrasting models
  and approaches to development including: determinist and modernisation approaches to
  development; images and language associated with developing societies; a critical examination
  of the idea of 'first world and third world' north and south; eurocentric thinking."
  International setting: "Appropriate examples from a world region." Outcome: "evaluate the
  differing views of development and underdevelopment."
- **Level and exam form:** Higher Level only option. 80-mark essays (about four aspects, about
  25–30 SRPs). Set as "views of development are subject to change", "contrasting models and
  approaches" (asked in 2023 and 2026) and the North–South idea (2024). Each model needs its
  own block: what it says, an example of it applied, and a critique.
- **Studied areas (each its own block of facts):** environmental determinism; Rostow's
  modernisation model (applied to Ireland and South Korea); dependency and world-systems theory
  (applied to Latin America and African commodity exporters); alternative views (basic needs,
  human development, bottom-up and sustainable development); images and language (the 1984–85
  Ethiopian famine coverage and later critiques); the First/Third World and North–South labels;
  Eurocentric thinking (map projections, history, the meaning of "development"). The world
  region for examples is **sub-Saharan Africa**, with Latin America and East Asia as contrasts.

## 1. Must cover (one bullet or block for every item; tick each off)

What development means and how views change
- [ ] V1 Development is a contested idea: economic development (growth in output and income) vs human development (health, education, freedom, choice) vs sustainable development (meeting today's needs without harming the future). Who defines it matters: usually rich countries and international institutions.
- [ ] V2 Views have changed over time, one bullet per era: colonial era (colonies as suppliers of raw materials, "civilising mission"); 1950s–60s growth and modernisation (GNP growth, industrialisation, Rostow); 1960s–70s dependency theory from Latin America; 1970s basic needs; 1980s–90s neoliberal Washington Consensus and structural adjustment; 1990 onward human development (HDI) and Sen's "development as freedom"; 1987 onward sustainable development; 2000s–2010s goal-based (MDGs, SDGs) and rights-based views.
- [ ] V3 Measures follow views: GNP per head reflects the growth view; the HDI reflects the human view (one bullet pointing to the GNP and HDI notes in the economic development chapter; don't re-teach them).
- [ ] V4 "Underdevelopment" is itself a loaded word: it can mean "not yet developed" (modernisation) or "made poor by others" (dependency). Students should explain both meanings.

Determinist approaches
- [ ] D1 Environmental (climatic) determinism: the physical environment, especially climate, decides how far a society can develop; temperate climates were said to produce energetic, "advanced" peoples and tropical climates poor ones.
- [ ] D2 Its origins and use: 19th- and early 20th-century geographers (named in section 2); used to justify colonialism and racial hierarchies.
- [ ] D3 Critique: ignores history (colonialism, slavery, trade rules), politics and people's choices; contradicted by rich tropical states (Singapore) and by poverty in temperate places; racist assumptions. Possibilism (the environment offers possibilities; people choose) as the reply.
- [ ] D4 A softer modern version ("geography matters"): landlocked location, malaria and poor soils raise costs but do not decide outcomes (Sachs, Diamond). Botswana (landlocked, yet developed) shows physical limits can be overcome. One or two bullets; link to the physical factors in the economic development chapter.

The modernisation approach: Rostow
- [ ] M1 Modernisation theory: all societies pass along one path from traditional to modern, as Western Europe and North America did; poor countries are "behind" and need investment, technology, industry and Western values to catch up.
- [ ] M2 Rostow's model (book and date in section 2), five stages, one bullet each with what happens: traditional society; preconditions for take-off; take-off; drive to maturity; age of high mass consumption.
- [ ] M3 Rostow applied to Ireland (a textbook interpretation, not Rostow's own): traditional (farming economy to the 1950s); preconditions (Whitaker's 1958 programme, opening to foreign investment); take-off (1960s–70s industrial growth, free secondary education 1967, EEC 1973); drive to maturity (1980s–90s, MNC-led growth); high mass consumption (Celtic Tiger). Use the Irish dates already checked in section 2.
- [ ] M4 Rostow applied to an East Asian success: South Korea's rapid industrialisation (facts in section 2), which seems to fit the model.
- [ ] M5 Critique of modernisation, one bullet each: Eurocentric (assumes the Western path is the only one and the goal); linear and stage-bound (countries can skip, stall or go backwards: Zimbabwe, Syria); ignores colonialism and unequal trade as causes of poverty; assumes "trickle-down" of wealth, which often does not happen; growth-centred and environmentally unsustainable (mass consumption for all is impossible); ignores culture, inequality and gender; many African states that followed modernisation advice did not take off.
- [ ] M6 Modernisation in policy: large prestige projects (dams, steelworks) and the Washington Consensus / structural adjustment of the 1980s–90s as a market version of the same top-down thinking (one bullet; debt and SAPs in detail belong to s3).

Dependency and world-systems approaches
- [ ] P1 Dependency theory: underdevelopment is caused by the way poor countries were drawn into the world economy, not by their own backwardness. Rich "core" (metropolis) countries develop by draining wealth from poor "periphery" (satellite) countries. Development of the core and underdevelopment of the periphery are two sides of one process.
- [ ] P2 Origins: Latin American economists and thinkers (Prebisch and ECLA; Frank's "development of underdevelopment"); the falling terms of trade for primary exporters.
- [ ] P3 Mechanisms, one bullet each: colonial extraction of raw materials and slavery; dependence on one or two primary exports with low, unstable prices; tariff escalation and rich-country subsidies; profit repatriation by MNCs; debt repayments; brain drain; local elites who benefit from the link with the core.
- [ ] P4 Examples: Latin America after independence (Brazil's export cycles, reuse); West African cocoa exported unprocessed (Ghana, Côte d'Ivoire); Zambian copper; Sudan's Gezira cotton for Lancashire (all reuse).
- [ ] P5 Wallerstein's world-systems theory: core, semi-periphery and periphery; countries can move between them (South Korea, China moved up). One block of 3–4 bullets.
- [ ] P6 Critique of dependency: cannot explain how the Asian Tigers and China developed through trade with the core; some countries cut links (self-reliance) and stayed poor; blames outside forces and can excuse corruption and poor government at home; the remedy (withdrawal from world trade) has rarely worked.

Alternative views (bottom-up, human and sustainable)
- [ ] A1 Basic needs approach (1970s): food, water, shelter, health and education first, not GNP.
- [ ] A2 Small-scale and intermediate technology: Schumacher and "Small is Beautiful"; appropriate technology that local people can afford, use and repair.
- [ ] A3 Human development and Sen's "development as freedom": widening people's choices and capabilities; the HDI (1990).
- [ ] A4 Bottom-up (grassroots) development vs top-down: participation, co-operatives, NGOs, women's groups. One bullet pointing to s3 and s4.
- [ ] A5 Sustainable development (Brundtland, 1987) and the SDGs (2015): one bullet pointing to s4.
- [ ] A6 Alternative measures of well-being: Bhutan's Gross National Happiness; Rosling's four income levels. One bullet each.

Images and language associated with developing societies
- [ ] I1 Media and charity images: famine, war, disease and helpless victims (especially children) dominate coverage of Africa; good news and ordinary life are rarely shown.
- [ ] I2 Case: the 1984–85 Ethiopian famine (facts in section 2): powerful coverage raised huge sums (Band Aid, Live Aid) but fixed an image of Africa as helpless and dependent and hid the political causes (civil war, government policy).
- [ ] I3 Effects of negative images: stereotypes ("Africa is a country"); a "saviour" view of donors; lower investor and tourist confidence; self-image of people in the countries shown; donor fatigue.
- [ ] I4 Responses: codes of conduct on images used in fundraising (Dóchas, section 2); NGOs using dignified images and people's own voices; writers and speakers challenging single stories (section 2).
- [ ] I5 Counter-images from the world region, one bullet each with facts from section 2: Rwanda's recovery and women in parliament; Kenya's mobile money (M-Pesa); Botswana's stable, diamond-funded growth.
- [ ] I6 Language: loaded terms, one bullet each: "underdeveloped", "backward", "primitive", "Third World"; "developing" implies one direction of travel; "less economically developed country" (LEDC); "Global South"; "majority world" (most of the world's people live there); the UN "least developed countries" category as an official term.

"First World / Third World" and North–South
- [ ] F1 Origin of "Third World" (1952) and its Cold War meaning: First World = the USA-led capitalist West; Second World = the Soviet-led communist bloc; Third World = the rest, mostly newly independent, non-aligned former colonies (Bandung 1955, Non-Aligned Movement 1961).
- [ ] F2 Why it is outdated: the Second World disappeared with the end of the Soviet Union (1991); the term became a ranking (first = best) and a byword for poverty; it lumps together very different countries (Singapore and Niger).
- [ ] F3 The North–South divide: the Brandt Report and line (reuse facts); the North rich and industrialised, the South poor; Australia and New Zealand placed in the North.
- [ ] F4 Critique of North–South: many "South" countries are now high-income or industrial powers (Singapore, South Korea, Taiwan, the Gulf states); China is the second-largest economy and India among the five largest; BRICS and the G20 give the South a stronger voice; poverty and deprivation exist within the North; middle-income countries now hold most of the world's people (Rosling); the World Bank stopped grouping countries simply as "developing" in 2016.
- [ ] F5 Why the idea still has some use: the gap in income, health and power between most of Africa and Europe remains very large; global institutions (IMF, World Bank voting, UN Security Council) are still dominated by the North.

Eurocentric thinking
- [ ] E1 Definition: seeing the world from a European (Western) point of view and judging other societies by European standards, as if Europe's path, values and history were the norm.
- [ ] E2 Maps: the Mercator projection puts Europe at the top centre and exaggerates the size of high-latitude regions (Greenland looks similar in size to Africa); the Peters (equal-area) projection as a challenge; the Greenwich prime meridian; regional names such as "Middle East" and "Far East" measured from Europe.
- [ ] E3 History: the "discovery" of lands that were already inhabited (Americas 1492); colonial borders drawn in Europe (Berlin Conference, reuse); African and Asian achievements ignored; Said's "Orientalism".
- [ ] E4 Development: "developed" was defined as becoming like Western Europe (industry, urbanisation, mass consumption); traditional knowledge and ways of life were dismissed; Western experts designed projects without local input.
- [ ] E5 Changing view: the rise of Asia, South–South trade and aid (China in Africa), and local voices challenge Eurocentric models. One bullet.

## 3. Traps (known errors)

- **Determinism ≠ racism-free physical factors.** Present environmental determinism as a discredited theory and say why; never state as fact that climate makes people less capable or less hard-working.
- Rostow's model is a **theory**, and its application to Ireland is a textbook interpretation: write "can be applied", not "Ireland passed through Rostow's stages in these years". Don't give Rostow take-off dates for any country other than Britain.
- Dependency theory is not the same as "dependence on aid". It is about structural dependence on the core through trade, investment and debt.
- **Third World** was a Cold War political term (1952, Sauvy), not originally a ranking of wealth. The Second World was the communist bloc.
- The Brandt Line is from **1980**; it is not a current UN or World Bank classification. Australia and New Zealand are in the "North".
- The Mercator projection was designed for navigation (lines of constant compass bearing are straight), not to make Europe look bigger. The point is its effect, not a plot.
- Don't use "Africa" as if it were one country. Name the country for every African example.
- Don't give Ethiopian famine death tolls, M-Pesa user numbers, or Rwandan growth rates: none are checked.
- Past-paper model answers in the question bank contain invented statistics and doubtful claims (e.g. growth rates for Bangladesh, Kerala yields, percentages of women in textile work). Use none.
- **Neighbouring subsections and chapters:**
  - GNP, HDI and other measures are taught in `geo-economic-development-s1`; patterns and factors (physical, social, cultural, political) and change over time in `-s2`; colonialism case studies (India, Brazil, Sudan) in `-s3`. Refer to them in a bullet; reuse their facts only as given here.
  - Core–periphery regions and cumulative causation (Myrdal) are in `geo-economic-globalisation-s4` and `geo-region-concept-s4`. Here only as part of dependency/world-systems theory.
  - Debt, aid, NGOs, land ownership, participation and gender are s3; fair trade, self-reliance and sustainable development are s4. Name them only as alternative views (A4, A5).
  - Racial classification and colonialism's effect on race belong to `geo-option-culture-identity-s1`.

## 4. What exams ask (topics only)

- 80 marks: "views of development and underdevelopment involve contrasting models and approaches" (2023, 2026). ## 5. Practice questions to write

1. 80 marks, Higher: "Views of development and underdevelopment involve contrasting models and approaches to development. Discuss." Model answer in four aspects, about 25–30 SRPs: (a) Rostow's model with its stages, an application to Ireland or South Korea and a critique; (b) dependency theory with mechanisms, Latin American and West African examples and a critique; (c) environmental determinism and its critique; (d) bottom-up / human development views (basic needs, Schumacher, Sen). About 25–30 SRPs.
3. 20 marks (short): "Explain how images and language used about developing countries can shape our view of development." Model answer: the 1984–85 Ethiopian famine coverage and its effects; loaded terms; one response (Dóchas code); one counter-image (M-Pesa or Rwanda). About 8–10 SRPs.
