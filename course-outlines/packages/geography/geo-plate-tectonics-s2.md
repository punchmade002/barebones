## 1. Must cover (one bullet or block for every item; tick each off)

Continental drift (the first stage of the theory)
- [ ] D1 Alfred Wegener's theory of continental drift: the continents were once joined in one supercontinent, Pangaea, surrounded by one ocean, Panthalassa, and have since drifted apart.
- [ ] D2 Pangaea split into Laurasia (north) and Gondwanaland (south), separated by the Tethys Sea; these broke up further into today's continents.
- [ ] D3 Evidence 1, jigsaw fit: the coastlines of South America and Africa fit together; the fit is even better at the edge of the continental shelves.
- [ ] D4 Evidence 2, fossils: identical fossils of land plants and freshwater animals on continents now separated by oceans (they could not have crossed an ocean).
- [ ] D5 Evidence 3, rocks and mountains: matching rock types and mountain ranges of the same age on opposite sides of the Atlantic.
- [ ] D6 Evidence 4, past climates: evidence of ancient glaciation in places now near the equator, and coal (formed in warm swamps) in places now very cold.
- [ ] D7 Why the theory was rejected at first: Wegener could not explain what force moved the continents.

Sea-floor spreading (the second stage)
- [ ] F1 Mapping the ocean floor by echo-sounding (sonar) revealed long mid-ocean ridges with a central rift valley, and deep ocean trenches.
- [ ] F2 Harry Hess's theory of sea-floor spreading: magma rises at mid-ocean ridges, cools to form new oceanic crust (basalt), and pushes older crust away on both sides; crust is destroyed again at trenches.
- [ ] F3 Evidence 1, age of rocks: oceanic rocks are youngest at the ridge and get progressively older with distance from it, symmetrically on both sides.
- [ ] F4 Evidence 2, sediment: ocean-floor sediment is thin or absent at the ridge and thicker further away.
- [ ] F5 Evidence 3, palaeomagnetism (magnetic striping): the earth's magnetic field reverses from time to time; iron minerals in cooling basalt line up with the field at that moment, so the ocean floor holds stripes of normal and reversed magnetism that are a mirror image on either side of the ridge. Diagram cue: plan view of a ridge with symmetrical stripes and arrows of movement.
- [ ] F6 Sea-floor spreading supplied the missing mechanism and turned continental drift into the theory of plate tectonics.

The plate tectonics model today
- [ ] P1 Definition of a plate: a rigid slab of lithosphere (crust plus solid upper mantle) that floats and moves on the asthenosphere. A plate can carry continental crust, oceanic crust or both.
- [ ] P2 Global geography of lithospheric plates: seven major plates and a number of smaller ones (names in section 2). Students must be able to name and locate plates on a world map.
- [ ] P3 How plates move: convection currents in the mantle drag the plates (the currents themselves are defined in s1; use the term without redefining it). Higher Level: ridge push (new, hot crust at the ridge slides away from it under gravity) and slab pull (the cold, dense subducting edge of a plate pulls the rest of the plate after it).
- [ ] P4 Plates move a few centimetres a year, so the map of continents and oceans changes over geological time.
- [ ] P5 Three ways plates meet: moving apart (constructive/divergent), moving together (destructive/convergent), sliding past (passive/conservative/transform). One sentence each; full detail is in s3.
- [ ] P6 The mobility of the crust produces endogenic forces: plate movement causes folding, faulting, volcanic activity and earthquakes, mostly concentrated along plate boundaries.

Ireland's position now and in past geological periods (the syllabus's national setting)
- [ ] I1 Ireland now: on the Eurasian plate, near its western edge, far from any plate boundary. The nearest boundary is the Mid-Atlantic Ridge to the west. As the Atlantic widens, Ireland moves slowly away from North America.
- [ ] I2 Consequence: Ireland has no active volcanoes and only rare, minor earthquakes.
- [ ] I3 About 500 million years ago, Ireland was in two parts on opposite sides of the Iapetus Ocean: the north-west part on the North American (Laurentian) side and the south-east part on the European side.
- [ ] I4 The Iapetus Ocean closed as the plates converged; the two parts of Ireland were joined along the line of the old ocean (the Iapetus suture). The collision caused the **Caledonian** folding, about 400 million years ago, with mountains running north-east to south-west.
- [ ] I5 Ireland has drifted north over hundreds of millions of years. Rocks record the climates it passed through: Old Red Sandstone formed in hot, dry conditions after the Caledonian folding; Carboniferous limestone formed when Ireland lay near the equator under a warm, shallow tropical sea.
- [ ] I6 The **Armorican** (Variscan) folding, about 300 million years ago: the collision of Africa (Gondwanaland) with Europe as Pangaea came together; mountains running east to west across the south of Ireland.
- [ ] I7 The opening of the Atlantic after Pangaea split: as North America and Greenland separated from Europe, magma rose through cracks in north-east Ireland and formed the Antrim basalts. One sentence only (detail is in `geo-volcanoes-earthquakes`).
- [ ] I8 The Alpine folding is the most recent period of fold mountain building (the Alps, the Himalayas). It did not build Ireland's mountains. Exams ask students to name two periods of fold mountain building that shaped the Irish landscape: the answer is Caledonian and Armorican.

## 3. Traps

- The Wicklow Mountains are **Caledonian** (with the Leinster granite), not Armorican. Some existing app answers wrongly call them Armorican. Use Macgillycuddy's Reeks or the Galtees for Armorican.
- The Mourne Mountains are **not** Caledonian: their granite is far younger. Do not use them for either folding period.
- Wegener proposed continental drift; Hess proposed sea-floor spreading. Don't credit Wegener with sea-floor spreading or a convection mechanism.
- Pangaea = the supercontinent; Panthalassa = the ocean around it; Tethys = the sea between Laurasia and Gondwanaland; Iapetus = the much older ocean that closed across Ireland. Keep them apart.
- Ireland is on the Eurasian plate, not the North American plate. Ireland is not at a plate boundary today.
- Don't give plate speeds other than the 2.5 cm figure for the Atlantic, and don't copy plate speeds or directions from past-paper maps.
- The anatomy of folds (anticline, syncline, overfold, nappe), the formation of fold mountains as landforms, faulting, and the Munster ridge-and-valley landscape in detail belong to `geo-volcanoes-earthquakes` (subsection 5). Here give only the folding periods, their dates, the plate collision behind each, the trend and named mountain areas.
- The Antrim basalt extrusion and the Giant's Causeway in detail belong to `geo-volcanoes-earthquakes`. One sentence here.
- What happens at each boundary type (ridges, trenches, subduction, volcanoes, fold mountains, the Ring of Fire) belongs to `geo-plate-tectonics-s3`. Here only name the three types.
- Rock formation (how limestone, sandstone and granite form) belongs to `geo-rocks-weathering`. Here use the rocks only as evidence of Ireland's past positions.
