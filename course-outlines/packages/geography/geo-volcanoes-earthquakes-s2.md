## 1. Must cover (one bullet or block for every item; tick each off)

How earthquakes happen
- [ ] Q1 An earthquake is a sudden shaking of the ground caused by the release of built-up stress in the crust.
- [ ] Q2 Cause (elastic rebound): plates moving past, towards or away from each other lock together by friction; stress builds up in the rocks; when it exceeds the strength of the rock, the rock fractures or slips suddenly along a fault and the stored energy is released as seismic waves.
- [ ] Q3 Key terms, for diagram matching: focus (hypocentre): the point inside the crust where the rupture begins; epicentre: the point on the surface directly above the focus, where shaking is usually strongest; fault (fault line): the fracture along which the rocks move; seismic (shock) waves: waves of energy spreading out from the focus. One labelled diagram cue showing all four.
- [ ] Q4 Shallow-focus earthquakes cause more surface damage than deep-focus ones of the same magnitude.
- [ ] Q5 Seismic waves: P (primary) waves are fastest and arrive first; S (secondary) waves are slower and arrive second; surface waves travel along the surface, arrive last and cause most of the damage. One bullet each. Their use as evidence for the earth's interior is in `geo-plate-tectonics-s1`; don't repeat it.
- [ ] Q6 Foreshocks (smaller tremors before) and aftershocks (tremors after the main shock, which can bring down weakened buildings).

Measuring earthquakes
- [ ] M1 Seismograph (seismometer): the instrument that detects and records seismic waves; the record is a seismogram. A barometer measures air pressure, not earthquakes (a true/false trap).
- [ ] M2 Seismic stations: record the arrival times of P and S waves; the gap between them shows distance to the earthquake; three or more stations locate the epicentre (triangulation).
- [ ] M3 Richter scale: measures magnitude (energy released) from the size of the waves on a seismogram. It is logarithmic: each whole number up is ten times greater wave amplitude and about 30 times more energy. The moment magnitude scale is the modern replacement for large earthquakes; both are accepted answers for "a scale that measures magnitude".
- [ ] M4 Modified Mercalli scale: measures intensity, i.e. the observed effects and damage at a place, on a scale of I to XII. Intensity varies from place to place for one earthquake; magnitude doesn't.

Global distribution
- [ ] G1 Most earthquakes occur in narrow belts along plate boundaries. One sentence per boundary type on why; the boundary processes are taught in `geo-plate-tectonics-s3`.
- [ ] G2 The Pacific Ring of Fire has about 90% of the world's earthquakes, including most of the most powerful ones.
- [ ] G3 Constructive boundaries: frequent, shallow, generally weaker earthquakes (mid-ocean ridges, Iceland).
- [ ] G4 Destructive (subduction) boundaries: the most powerful earthquakes; foci range from shallow to very deep along the sinking plate; undersea earthquakes here cause tsunamis.
- [ ] G5 Collision boundaries: powerful, shallow earthquakes in fold-mountain belts (the Himalayas).
- [ ] G6 Transform (conservative) boundaries: plates slide past each other and stick, then slip; shallow and often very damaging (San Andreas Fault; East Anatolian Fault, Turkey).
- [ ] G7 Ireland lies in the middle of the Eurasian plate, so it has only rare, minor tremors.

Effects
- [ ] F1 Primary effects (direct from shaking): building and bridge collapse; deaths and injuries; ground rupture; broken gas, water and power lines; damaged roads and railways.
- [ ] F2 Secondary effects: fires from broken gas mains and power lines; tsunamis; landslides; liquefaction (saturated soft sediment behaves like a liquid and buildings sink or topple); disease from contaminated water; homelessness; economic losses.
- [ ] F3 Factors affecting the damage: magnitude; depth of the focus; distance from the epicentre; rock and soil type (soft sediment shakes more and can liquefy); population density; building quality and building codes; time of day; level of development and preparedness (wealth to prepare, respond and rebuild).
- [ ] F4 Case studies with date, place, plate setting, magnitude, effects and response, enough for a 30–40 mark answer: Kobe 1995 and the Indian Ocean 2004 earthquake and tsunami (section 2). Shorter examples for comparison from section 2.

Prediction and reducing the effects
- [ ] R1 Earthquakes cannot be predicted precisely (exact time, place and magnitude). Scientists can identify where they are likely and estimate how often.
- [ ] R2 Prediction methods: mapping active faults and plate boundaries; studying the history and frequency of past earthquakes; seismic gap theory (a section of an active fault that has not moved for a long time has the most stored stress and is the most likely to slip next); monitoring foreshocks, ground deformation (GPS, tiltmeters, laser measurements across faults), radon gas release and changes in well-water levels; unusual animal behaviour (anecdotal and unreliable).
- [ ] R3 Prediction success and failure: Haicheng 1975 and Tangshan 1976, China; the L'Aquila 2009 trial, Italy (section 2).
- [ ] R4 Early-warning systems: detecting the fast P waves gives seconds of warning before the destructive waves arrive, enough to stop trains, shut gas supplies and take cover. Tsunami warning systems use seabed pressure sensors and buoys.
- [ ] R5 Earthquake-proof building and infrastructure: deep foundations anchored to bedrock; base isolation (rubber and steel bearings or rollers under the building so the ground moves beneath it); flexible steel or reinforced-concrete frames that sway rather than crack; cross-bracing and shear walls; counterweights (tuned mass dampers) in tall buildings; automatic shut-off valves on gas mains; flexible joints on pipes; retrofitting older buildings.
- [ ] R6 Planning and preparedness: strict building codes; land-use planning (avoiding soft ground and fault lines); emergency drills and education; emergency supplies; tsunami walls and evacuation routes.

## 3. Traps (known errors)

- Focus is underground; epicentre is on the surface directly above. Never swap them.
- Richter (and moment magnitude) measure **magnitude**; Mercalli measures **intensity** (effects). A barometer is not an earthquake instrument.
- "About 30 times more energy" per Richter unit, and "ten times the amplitude". Don't say "ten times stronger" without saying what is ten times.
- Kobe: don't give both 6.9 and 7.3 as if they disagreed; name the scale. Don't confuse Kobe 1995 with Tōhoku 2011.
- The 2011 Christchurch earthquake was **not** on the Alpine Fault. Don't name the fault.
- The 1994 Northridge earthquake was not on the San Andreas Fault itself. Don't use Northridge at all: its figures appeared on a past paper.
- Don't copy figures from past papers (Northridge 1994, Afghanistan 2023, wave travel times). Use only section 2. For the Ring of Fire use "about 90%" of earthquakes (the figure agreed with `geo-plate-tectonics-s3`), not 80%.
- Keep hazard figures identical to `geo-plate-tectonics-s3`: Tōhoku about 18,000 dead and missing; Nepal about 9,000; Turkey–Syria over 50,000; Indian Ocean about 230,000; San Francisco 1906 magnitude about 7.8; Loma Prieta magnitude 6.9.
- Haiti death toll: say "over 100,000" with "estimates vary"; do not give a single precise figure.
- How P and S waves reveal the earth's layers and the S-wave shadow zone belong to `geo-plate-tectonics-s1`. Here: speed, order of arrival and damage only.
- What happens at each boundary type (subduction, collision, sea-floor spreading) is taught in `geo-plate-tectonics-s3`. Here: one cause sentence per boundary type.
- Fault types (normal, reverse, tear) and landforms of faulting (horst, graben, fault scarp) belong to `geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s5`. Here only "fault" as the fracture along which an earthquake occurs.
