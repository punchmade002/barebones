# Package: geo-economic-environment-s2 — Burning fossil fuels and using alternative energy sources

- **loId:** `geo-economic-environment-s2` · **chapterId:** `geo-economic-environment` · **syllabusRef:** 4.5
- **Syllabus:** students should study "the impact of the burning of fossil fuels and the use of
  alternative energy sources". National settings: "Smoke free zones. Patterns of production and
  consumption." International setting: "Acid rain - a European issue." Studied areas: **acid
  rain in Europe**, **smoke and smog: Dublin's smoke-free zone** (with the London smog as the
  classic precedent), **global patterns of fossil-fuel production and consumption**, and **each
  alternative energy source** (wind, hydro, solar, wave and tidal, geothermal, biomass, nuclear),
  each with its own block of facts.
- **Prepared by:** a higher model, 2026-10-20. The writer works only from this package and
  `course-outlines/NOTES_STANDARD.md`.

## 1. Must cover (one bullet or block for every item; tick each off)

Patterns of production and consumption
- [ ] P1 Fossil fuels supply about four-fifths of the world's primary energy.
- [ ] P2 Production is concentrated: the Middle East holds about half the world's proven oil reserves; the USA is the largest producer of oil and gas; Russia is a major gas and oil exporter; China produces and burns about half the world's coal.
- [ ] P3 Consumption: developed countries use far more energy per person than developing countries; demand is now growing fastest in Asia (China, India). China is the largest emitter of CO2 in total; the USA emits far more per person.
- [ ] P4 Fossil fuels are traded long distances (tankers, pipelines, LNG ships), so importers depend on exporters (energy security; link to Russia and the EU in "Renewable and non-renewable resources in the economy").

Impacts of burning fossil fuels (each a block: cause → process → effects → responses)
- [ ] F1 What burning releases: carbon dioxide (CO2), sulphur dioxide (SO2, mainly from coal and heavy oil), nitrogen oxides (NOx, mainly from vehicles and power stations), smoke and fine particles (soot), carbon monoxide.
- [ ] F2 Enhanced greenhouse effect: CO2 traps more heat, causing global warming. One or two bullets only here; the full global warming case study is in "Environmental pollution".
- [ ] F3 Acid rain, a European issue (full case study, section 2): formation (SO2 and NOx dissolve in water vapour to form sulphuric and nitric acids; wet and dry deposition); normal rain is slightly acidic (about pH 5.6), acid rain is below that; sources (coal-fired power stations, heavy industry, vehicles); transboundary pollution carried by the prevailing south-westerly winds from Britain and Germany to Scandinavia; the Black Triangle.
- [ ] F4 Effects of acid rain: forest damage (needle and leaf loss, nutrients leached from soil, Waldsterben); lake acidification (aluminium released from soil kills fish, especially in Scandinavia); damage to limestone and marble buildings and statues; corrosion of metal; health (respiratory problems).
- [ ] F5 Responses to acid rain: the 1979 Convention on Long-Range Transboundary Air Pollution; EU directives limiting emissions from large power stations; flue-gas desulphurisation (scrubbers) at power stations (Moneypoint); low-sulphur fuels; catalytic converters on cars; liming of lakes in Sweden and Norway; switch from coal to gas and renewables. Outcome: European SO2 emissions have fallen by well over three-quarters since 1990 and many lakes are recovering.
- [ ] F6 Smoke and smog: burning coal and turf in homes gives smoke and fine particles; in calm, cold winter weather a temperature inversion (anticyclone) traps the smoke near the ground, creating smog. Health effects: bronchitis, asthma, heart and lung disease, extra deaths.
- [ ] F7 Smoke-free zones (syllabus setting, full case study): the Dublin smoky-coal ban of 1990 (cause: winter smogs of the 1980s), its results, and its extension to other towns; current restrictions on smoky fuel. London's Great Smog of 1952 and the Clean Air Act 1956 as the earlier precedent.
- [ ] F8 Other impacts: oil spills during transport (one line, the case study is in "Environmental pollution"); landscape damage from coal mining and peat cutting; thermal pollution of rivers by power-station cooling water.

Alternative energy sources (each its own block: how it works, Irish and/or European example, advantages, disadvantages)
- [ ] A1 Wind: onshore and offshore; Irish examples; Denmark.
- [ ] A2 Hydroelectric power: dams and reservoirs, run-of-river, pumped storage; Ardnacrusha, Turlough Hill; Three Gorges Dam (China) as a global case with its benefits and costs.
- [ ] A3 Solar: photovoltaic panels and solar thermal water heating; rapid growth in Ireland; limited by Ireland's cloud cover and short winter days.
- [ ] A4 Wave and tidal: wave test site off Belmullet; tidal barrage at La Rance (France); tidal stream turbine SeaGen in Strangford Lough; predictable tides vs high cost and marine impacts.
- [ ] A5 Geothermal: Iceland; low-temperature heat pumps in Ireland (one short block; full detail is in "Human interaction with the rock cycle").
- [ ] A6 Biomass and biofuels: wood chip, energy crops (willow, miscanthus), biogas from slurry and food waste (anaerobic digestion), biofuels blended in petrol and diesel; Edenderry; carbon-neutral in principle, but land competes with food.
- [ ] A7 Nuclear: how fission works in one line; France; advantages (low carbon, reliable, large output); disadvantages (radioactive waste, accident risk: Chernobyl, Fukushima; very high cost; decommissioning).
- [ ] A8 Why Ireland has no nuclear power (exam topic): banned by law (Electricity Regulation Act 1999); public opposition, e.g. the abandoned Carnsore Point plan in the 1970s; Ireland's small electricity grid; cost; waste disposal; fears raised by Chernobyl and by Sellafield across the Irish Sea.
- [ ] A9 General advantages of renewables: inexhaustible; little or no CO2, SO2 or NOx; reduce the fossil-fuel import bill and improve energy security; create jobs, often in rural areas; free fuel once built; help meet EU and national targets and avoid penalties.
- [ ] A10 General disadvantages of renewables: intermittent (wind and sun vary), so backup or storage is needed; high initial cost; visual impact, noise and local objections; grid connections needed in remote places; some damage habitats (birds and turbines, dams flooding valleys, peat slides at wind farms on bog).
- [ ] A11 Why renewable energy is crucial for Ireland's environment and economy (30-mark exam topic): climate targets, cleaner air and water, cutting the import bill and price shocks, energy security, jobs and rural income, export potential via interconnectors, attracting multinationals that want green power.
- [ ] A12 Siting a wind farm (map-based exam topic): high exposed upland or coast with strong winds, away from houses (noise, shadow flicker), close to an access road for construction, close to the grid (transmission line), not in a protected area or on deep peat, land of low farming value. Use invented illustrative maps only.

## 3. Traps

- Acid rain is caused by SO2 and NOx, not by CO2. CO2 causes global warming. The ozone hole is caused by CFCs. Keep all three separate.
- Acid rain is any rain below about pH 5.6, not "below pH 7" (all natural rain is slightly acidic).
- Acid rain is a transboundary problem: the country that suffers is often not the one that pollutes. Scandinavia suffered from British and German emissions.
- The Dublin ban was on smoky (bituminous) coal, not on all solid fuel; smokeless fuel and turf could still be burned there at first.
- The 2002 Lancet figure is an estimate; keep "about 350 a year" and "about 70%" exactly.
- Nuclear is not renewable. Ireland has never had a nuclear power station; Carnsore Point was never built.
- La Rance is a tidal barrage; SeaGen was a tidal-stream turbine; Belmullet is a wave test site. Don't mix the technologies.
- Do not name or use the solar farms and articles from past papers (Millvale, Emerald Park) or their figures.
- The full global warming case study (greenhouse gases, effects, Kyoto and Paris) belongs to "Environmental pollution". Here, one or two bullets on CO2 and the enhanced greenhouse effect.
- Wind-farm conflicts (local objections) are taught in full in "Conflicts between economic interests and environmental interests"; here, list them only as disadvantages.
- Geothermal detail is in `geo-rocks-weathering-s2`; Three Gorges is in `geo-rivers-s4`. Reuse their facts in one or two bullets.
- Do not give Irish smoky-coal ban dates for towns other than Dublin (1990) and Cork (1995).

## 4. What exams ask (topics only)

- Higher 30 marks (asked more than once): examine the environmental impact of burning fossil fuels. Expected answer: global warming (briefly), acid rain with European detail, smog and smoke-free zones, with responses.
- Ordinary: name two fossil fuels; describe and explain the impact of burning them; explain what a fossil fuel is.
- Ordinary short: name two renewable sources; one advantage and one disadvantage of renewable energy; why countries are investing more in renewables; why wind use has grown; why nuclear energy is not used in Ireland.
- Higher 30 marks: why renewable energy is crucial for Ireland's environment and economy; the economic and environmental advantages of one specific renewable source.
- Map-based: choose and justify a site for a wind farm or other renewable source on an Ordnance Survey map, and explain how conflict may arise from it.
- Data questions on energy-mix and emissions charts; drawing a graph of emissions data and naming a negative effect of CO2.
