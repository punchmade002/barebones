## 1. Must cover (one bullet or block for every item; tick each off)

The idea
- [ ] X1 Climate is a resource and a constraint: temperature, rainfall amount, its seasonal timing and its reliability, sunshine and wind decide what can be grown, how much water is available, where tourists go and what it costs to adapt. Rich economies can overcome limits with technology (irrigation, reservoirs, desalination, snow-making); poor economies that depend on rain-fed farming are most exposed. Climate change is shifting all of these (one bullet, links s5).

Rainfall and agriculture: Irish agriculture (national setting; full case)
- [ ] A1 Ireland's rainfall (amounts and distribution from s3/section 2) and mild temperatures give a long grass-growing season, so grass is Ireland's most important crop: most farmland is grassland, used for dairy, beef and sheep (pastoral farming). Grass grows when soil temperature is above about 6 °C, which in Ireland's mild climate is for most of the year, longest in the south and south-west.
- [ ] A2 Regional contrast, one bullet each: the drier, sunnier south and east (rain shadow, s3) suit tillage: barley, wheat, oats, potatoes and vegetables can be harvested in dry conditions (e.g. Co. Wexford, Co. Carlow, Co. Kildare); the Golden Vale's moderate rainfall and good soils suit intensive dairying; the very wet west and north-west, with poorly drained gley and peat soils, suit mainly beef and sheep rearing on rough grazing, with small farms and low incomes (link `geo-region-ireland-s2`).
- [ ] A3 Problems of too much rain, one bullet each: waterlogged soils that cattle poach (trample), shortening the grazing season so animals must be housed and fed silage for longer, raising costs; difficulty making silage and hay and harvesting cereals in wet spells; leaching of nutrients (and slurry runoff when it rains after spreading, link `geo-economic-environment-s3`); diseases favoured by damp weather (potato blight; liver fluke in sheep and cattle). Example: the wet summer of 2012 and the fodder crisis of spring 2013 (section 2).
- [ ] A4 Problems of too little rain: grass stops growing in prolonged dry spells, so farmers have to feed winter fodder in summer, milk yields fall and fodder runs short the following winter; light soils of the south-east suffer most. Example: the 2018 drought (section 2).
- [ ] A5 Climate change and Irish farming (one bullet): warmer temperatures lengthen the growing season; wetter winters and more frequent summer droughts add risk; maize and other crops become more feasible; agriculture is also Ireland's largest source of greenhouse gases (reuse `geo-economic-environment-s3`).

Rainfall and agriculture: global examples (each a self-contained case)
- [ ] G1 India's monsoon farming: the monsoon's arrival decides the farming year: the kharif (summer) crops such as rice and cotton are sown with the monsoon rains; rabi (winter) crops such as wheat rely on stored water, irrigation and western disturbances; a late or weak monsoon cuts harvests, farm incomes and GDP; irrigation (canals, tube wells) in Punjab and Haryana supported the Green Revolution but brought salinisation and falling water tables. Reuse `geo-region-continental-s1` facts; one compact block.
- [ ] G2 Egypt and the Nile: Egypt is almost rainless, so farming depends on irrigation from the Nile, whose flood came from summer rain over the Ethiopian Highlands. The Aswan High Dam stores water in Lake Nasser, ending the flood's uncertainty and allowing year-round (perennial) irrigation and two or three crops a year, plus hydroelectricity. Costs: the fertile silt is trapped behind the dam, so farmers need artificial fertiliser; salinisation and waterlogging; the delta is eroding.
- [ ] G3 California's Central Valley (Mediterranean climate): dry summers mean high-value fruit, nuts and vegetables depend on irrigation from Sierra Nevada snowmelt carried by large state and federal water projects, and on groundwater. Drought (section 2) forced farmers to leave land fallow and over-pump groundwater, causing land subsidence; cities had to cut water use.
- [ ] G4 Rain-fed farming in the Sahel (one bullet linking to D-blocks below): one short wet season; millet and sorghum and pastoral herding; any shortfall in rain means crop failure.
- [ ] G5 Rainfall reliability matters as much as the total: the lower the average rainfall, the more variable it is from year to year (s3), so dry regions face both low and unpredictable harvests.

Rainfall and domestic water supplies
- [ ] W1 Domestic water depends on rainfall refilling rivers, lakes, reservoirs and groundwater; supply must be stored to cover dry periods; demand peaks in hot, dry weather, exactly when supply is lowest.
- [ ] W2 Ireland's paradox: plenty of rain overall, but most rain falls in the west while most people live in the east, where rainfall is lowest (Dublin about 750 mm a year; Census 2022 Dublin county about 1.45 million people). Old, leaking pipes waste a large share of treated water; treatment capacity is tight.
- [ ] W3 Dublin's supply: mainly the River Liffey (Poulaphouca reservoir, the Blessington lakes) and the Vartry reservoir in Co. Wicklow; the planned Water Supply Project would pipe water from the Shannon to the Greater Dublin Area (section 2). Uisce Éireann (Irish Water) runs public water supply.
- [ ] W4 The 2018 drought and hosepipe ban (section 2): an example of a rainfall shortfall affecting domestic supply in a wet country.
- [ ] W5 Group water schemes and private wells serve many rural homes; wells can run dry in drought and be contaminated after heavy rain (one bullet).
- [ ] W6 Global case: Cape Town "Day Zero" (section 2): a Mediterranean-climate city whose winter rains failed for three years running; reservoirs fell so low that the city planned to turn off household taps; strict limits per person, higher tariffs, pressure reduction, and public campaigns cut use, and Day Zero was avoided when the rains returned. Lessons: diversify supply (groundwater, recycling, desalination), reduce leaks, plan for longer droughts.
- [ ] W7 Water supply in arid and monsoon countries (one bullet): dependence on seasonal rain, groundwater and long-distance transfer; desalination in rich, dry countries (e.g. the Gulf states, Israel); women and children in poor rural areas spend hours a day collecting water, keeping girls out of school.

The impact of drought (definitions and cases)
- [ ] D1 Drought: a long period with much less precipitation than normal for that place, so that water supply falls short of demand. Types, one bullet: meteorological (rain shortfall), agricultural (soil moisture too low for crops), hydrological (low rivers, reservoirs, groundwater). Causes: blocking anticyclones (s4), failure or shift of the ITCZ or monsoon, El Niño (s5), and climate change.
- [ ] D2 Economic effects in general, one bullet each: crop failure and loss of livestock; higher food prices; loss of farm income and rural jobs; famine in poor countries; migration to cities and abroad; less hydroelectricity and cooling water for power stations; river transport disrupted; wildfires; government spending on relief.
- [ ] D3 Drought in a developed economy: the Murray–Darling Basin, Australia (Millennium Drought) and California (section 2): losses absorbed through insurance, government aid, water trading and technology, but farm output fell, towns faced water restrictions and governments invested in desalination plants. One block contrasting rich and poor responses.
- [ ] D4 Drought in a developing economy: the Horn of Africa, 2011 (section 2): drought followed two failed rainy seasons; crops failed and livestock died; food prices soared; conflict blocked aid; famine was declared in parts of Somalia; hundreds of thousands fled to camps in Kenya and Ethiopia. Ethiopia's famine of 1983–85, made worse by civil war, prompted Band Aid and Live Aid (section 2; give no death toll).
- [ ] D5 Responses to drought, one bullet each: early warning systems using rainfall, satellite and market data; water storage (dams, tanks, rainwater harvesting); drought-tolerant crops and seed varieties; irrigation efficiency (drip irrigation); food aid and cash transfers; insurance; restrictions such as hosepipe bans; desalination.

The impact of desertification
- [ ] F1 Desertification: land degradation in arid, semi-arid and dry sub-humid areas caused by climatic variations and human activities (UNCCD definition, 1994). It is the spread of desert-like conditions, not the advance of sand dunes from the Sahara.
- [ ] F2 The Sahel (reuse `geo-option-geoecology-s3`; one compact block framed on climate and economy): location; rainfall 200–600 mm in one short summer wet season when the ITCZ moves north; droughts of the 1970s and 1980s; human pressures; effects on the economy: failed harvests, livestock deaths, loss of pastoralists' livelihoods, food dependence, migration to cities (e.g. towards coastal West African cities) and abroad, conflict between herders and farmers over shrinking land and water; Lake Chad's shrinkage hitting fishing and farming.
- [ ] F3 Responses (reuse, one bullet each): the UNCCD; the Great Green Wall (aim and slow progress); zaï pits and stone lines in Burkina Faso; farmer-managed natural regeneration in Niger.
- [ ] F4 The Dust Bowl, 1930s Great Plains (reuse `geo-rocks-weathering-s7`; one compact block): drought plus ploughed grassland; dust storms; farm failures and migration; government response (Soil Conservation Service 1935, shelter belts, contour ploughing): shows desertification-type damage in a rich economy.

The impact of climate on tourism
- [ ] T1 Climate as a tourism resource: sunshine, warmth, low rainfall and warm sea attract "sun, sea and sand" tourism; reliable snow attracts winter sports; a mild climate allows outdoor activities; climate decides the tourist season.
- [ ] T2 Mediterranean sun tourism (main case): hot, dry, sunny summers (s5) made the Mediterranean the world's largest tourist region; mass package tourism grew from the 1960s with cheap jet flights; Spain is among the world's most visited countries and the most popular destination for Irish holidaymakers (section 2). Benefits: jobs, foreign earnings, infrastructure, regional development (link the Mezzogiorno, `geo-region-european-s2`). Problems linked to climate: tourists arrive in the driest season, so water demand (hotels, pools, golf courses) peaks when supply is lowest; seasonal jobs; coastal overdevelopment; protests against overtourism (section 2).
- [ ] T3 Winter-sun tourism: the Canary Islands (section 2) offer warm, dry winters for northern Europeans, so their tourist season is year-round.
- [ ] T4 Alpine ski tourism: depends on reliable snow cover; it supports mountain economies in Switzerland, Austria, France and Italy. Climate change is raising the snow line and shortening seasons, so lower-altitude resorts struggle or close; resorts invest in artificial snow-making (needs water and energy, and cold enough nights) and diversify into summer walking and cycling.
- [ ] T5 Tropical tourism: Caribbean and Indian Ocean destinations rely on a warm climate and coral reefs; the peak season is the dry season (northern winter); the Atlantic hurricane season (June–November) is the low season. Hazards: hurricanes destroy resorts and infrastructure (section 2); coral bleaching damages reef tourism (Great Barrier Reef, reuse); sea-level rise threatens low-lying island nations such as the Maldives (reuse).
- [ ] T6 Ireland: the cool, cloudy, wet and unreliable summer climate limits beach tourism and pushes Irish holidaymakers abroad; Irish tourism instead markets scenery (green landscape produced by the rain), culture, heritage, golf, walking, surfing and other outdoor activities, e.g. the Wild Atlantic Way (section 2); domestic holidays rise in warm, sunny summers.
- [ ] T7 Climate change and tourism, one bullet each: hotter Mediterranean summers bring heatwaves and wildfires that can deter or endanger tourists (section 2), shifting visits to spring and autumn or to more northern destinations (possibly including Ireland); ski seasons shorten; reefs bleach; islands face flooding.

Other links between climate and development (short block, one bullet each)
- [ ] O1 Energy: climate provides renewable energy (Ireland's Atlantic winds supply about a third of its electricity, reuse `geo-economic-environment-s1`; solar power in sunny deserts; hydroelectricity from high rainfall) and drives energy demand (heating in cold climates, air-conditioning in hot ones).
- [ ] O2 Health and productivity: tropical climates favour diseases such as malaria; extreme heat lowers outdoor work capacity; heatwaves kill (Europe 2003, reuse s5).
- [ ] O3 Assessment (for "assess" questions): climate influences development but does not decide it alone: wealthy countries with harsh climates (e.g. Israel, the Gulf states, Australia) overcome limits through capital and technology, while poor, rain-fed economies are the most vulnerable; climate change widens this gap.

## 3. Traps

- Desertification is **land degradation** by climate and people together, not the Sahara "advancing". Drought is a climatic event; desertification is a process of land degradation that drought can trigger.
- Ireland's water problem is not lack of rain overall: it is **location of demand vs supply**, storage, treatment capacity and leakage.
- Too much rain is as much a problem for Irish farming as too little; give both sides.
- Don't give death tolls for Sahel or Ethiopian famines, or any famine figure except the Somalia 2011 estimate.
- Don't give tourism revenue, ski-season length or snow-line figures, or Irish fodder, rainfall or temperature figures for 2012, 2013 or 2018.
- Hurricane Irma's Barbuda damage and Maria's Puerto Rico toll are separate events; don't mix them.
- The Aswan High Dam (1970) is not the same as the older Aswan Low Dam.
- The full Sahel soil case (causes, soil effects, conservation detail) is in `geo-option-geoecology-s3`; the Dust Bowl in `geo-rocks-weathering-s7`; global-warming policy in `geo-economic-environment-s3`. Here keep each to its economic and climatic angle.
- Tourism as an economic activity in general (types, multiplier effects) belongs to the economic chapters; here only climate's influence on it.
- "Assess" questions need a judgement: climate influences, but technology and wealth modify, development (O3). Avoid saying climate "determines" development.
