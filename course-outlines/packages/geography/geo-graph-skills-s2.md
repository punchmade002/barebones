## 1. Must cover (one bullet or block for every item; tick each off)

What the census is
- [ ] C1 Definition: a census of population is an official count of every person in the country on one night, collecting information on each person and household. In Ireland it is carried out by the Central Statistics Office (CSO); completing the form is a legal obligation.
- [ ] C2 Frequency: normally every five years, in years ending in 1 and 6, on a Sunday night in April. Exceptions in section 2 (2002, 2022).
- [ ] C3 Recent censuses and the next one (dates and totals in section 2).
- [ ] C4 De facto census: Ireland counts everyone present in the State on census night (including visitors), wherever they usually live; the form also records each person's usual residence, so results can be given both ways.
- [ ] C5 How it is collected: enumerators (field staff) deliver a household form to every dwelling before census night and collect it afterwards; one form per household, with a page for each person; communal establishments (hotels, hospitals, prisons) are covered separately; vacant dwellings are recorded.
- [ ] C6 Confidentiality: information is used only for statistics; personal forms are kept closed for 100 years, after which they become historical records (the 1901 and 1911 census returns are available free online from the National Archives of Ireland and are used for family history).
- [ ] C7 What the census asks (topics): age, sex, marital status; relationships in the household; place of birth, citizenship, ethnicity, religion; ability to speak Irish (and how often); other languages; education level; work: occupation, industry, unemployment; commuting (means of travel, journey time); housing (type, age, tenure: owned with or without a mortgage, rented; rent paid; heating; vacancy); internet access, car ownership; health and disability; carers. Census 2022 added questions including working from home and renewable energy sources (e.g. solar panels) at home.
- [ ] C8 How results are published: preliminary results within months, then detailed results by theme over the following year or two; data for the State, provinces, counties, cities, towns, Electoral Divisions and Small Areas (the smallest units, roughly a street or estate); online tables and interactive maps on the CSO website; county profiles and infographics.

Uses, benefits and limitations
- [ ] U1 Uses by government and planners (each one bullet): planning schools (number of young children), healthcare and nursing homes (older population), housing (household numbers, vacancy), transport (commuting flows), pensions and social welfare; targeting resources at disadvantaged areas; regional and county development plans.
- [ ] U2 Political use: Dáil constituencies are reviewed after each census so that each TD represents roughly the same number of people; the Constitution sets between 20,000 and 30,000 people per TD and requires constituencies to be revised at least every 12 years. The Electoral Commission carries out the review.
- [ ] U3 Other uses: businesses choosing where to locate shops and services; researchers and students; tracking the Irish language; historical and genealogical research (old returns).
- [ ] U4 Limitations: a snapshot of one night; out of date between censuses; long wait for detailed results; possible undercount (e.g. homeless people, people avoiding the count); answers may be inaccurate (self-reported); very expensive; privacy concerns; areas (EDs) change over time, making comparisons difficult.
- [ ] U5 Other population data between censuses (one bullet): civil registration of births, deaths and marriages; the CSO's annual population and migration estimates; the Labour Force Survey.

Demographic structure (the census as evidence)
- [ ] D1 Demographic structure = the make-up of a population by age, sex and other characteristics (marital status, nationality/ethnicity, religion, language, education, occupation, household size, urban/rural).
- [ ] D2 Age structure: young dependents (0–14), working age (15–64), old dependents (65 and over). Dependency ratio = (0–14 + 65+) ÷ (15–64) × 100. A high young dependency ratio needs schools; a high old dependency ratio needs pensions and healthcare.
- [ ] D3 Sex structure: sex ratio = males ÷ females × 100 (males per 100 females). Women outnumber men in the older age groups (longer life expectancy); local imbalances arise from migration (e.g. young women moving to cities for work).
- [ ] D4 Census data shown as a population pyramid: one bullet; how to draw and read it is in `geo-graph-skills-s1`, what pyramid shapes mean is in `geo-population-migration-s1`.
- [ ] D5 Population change = natural increase + net migration. Natural increase = births − deaths; net migration = immigrants − emigrants. Comparing two censuses with births and deaths from civil registration gives the estimated net migration (net migration = total change − natural increase).
- [ ] D6 Measures: crude birth rate and crude death rate (per 1,000 people per year); population density (people per km²); percentage change between censuses; percentage of the population in an age group.
- [ ] D7 Ireland's long-term trend from the census (link only, detail in `geo-population-migration`): a steep fall after the Great Famine, a low point in 1961, then growth, passing 5 million in 2022. Present features: population growth driven by both natural increase and immigration; an ageing population (a growing share aged 65+); rising cultural diversity (many non-Irish citizens).

Population trends in the local area
- [ ] L1 Method for a local-area census study: (1) choose the area (an Electoral Division, Small Area, town or county); (2) find its census tables for two or more censuses on the CSO website or interactive map; (3) record total population, age groups, sex, households, nationality, commuting; (4) calculate changes (number and %), dependency ratio, sex ratio, share in each age group; (5) present them with graphs (line graph of population over several censuses, bar charts, a pyramid, a choropleth of EDs); (6) describe the trends; (7) explain them; (8) note limitations.
- [ ] L2 Typical explanations for local trends: new housing estates and commuter growth near cities and along motorways (rising population, young families, high young dependency); rural decline in remote areas (falling population, ageing, more single-person households); immigration; closure or opening of a major employer; town-centre decline vs suburban growth. Link to `geo-population-migration-s6` and `geo-urban`.
- [ ] L3 Census data for the Irish language and Gaeltacht areas: the census records ability to speak Irish and frequency (daily within/outside education, weekly, less often, never). One bullet; the cultural detail is in `geo-option-culture-identity-s2`.
- [ ] L4 Census data in the GIS study of urban sprawl: one bullet linking to `geo-weather-climate-s3`.

### Worked-example specs (all invented; the writer must say "illustrative" each time)

Invented setting: **Rivertown** (from `geo-aerial-photographs-s1`), an invented market town; its
Electoral Division is "Rivertown ED". Do not attach a real county to it.

- **W1 Population change (D5–D6).** Illustrative: Rivertown ED population 4,200 in the earlier
  census and 4,830 in the later census (six years apart). Change = +630; percentage change =
  630 ÷ 4,200 × 100 = 15%. Births over the period 380, deaths 210: natural increase = 170;
  net migration = 630 − 170 = +460 (more people moved in than out).
- **W2 Age structure and dependency (D2).** Illustrative later-census age groups: 0–14 = 1,050;
  15–64 = 3,120; 65+ = 660 (total 4,830). Dependency ratio = (1,050 + 660) ÷ 3,120 × 100 = 54.8
  (about 55 dependents per 100 people of working age). Share aged 65+ = 660 ÷ 4,830 × 100 = 13.7%.
- **W3 Sex ratio (D3).** Illustrative: males 2,390, females 2,440 (total 4,830). Sex ratio =
  2,390 ÷ 2,440 × 100 = 98 males per 100 females (to the nearest whole number).
- **W4 Crude birth rate (D6).** Illustrative: 65 births in Rivertown ED in the census year; CBR =
  65 ÷ 4,830 × 1,000 = 13.5 per 1,000.
- **W5 Explaining the trend (L2).** Illustrative: the growth and high young dependency fit the new
  housing estate on Rivertown's edge (from the aerial-photograph description) attracting young
  families who commute, and the positive net migration. Three linked points only.
- **W6 Presenting it (L1).** Which graphs suit the Rivertown data: a line graph of population over
  several censuses (writer may invent two more earlier values, labelled illustrative); a bar
  chart or pie chart of the three age groups; a pyramid if five-year bands were available.

## 3. Traps

- The Republic's latest census was **2022**, not 2021; Northern Ireland's was 2021. Don't merge
  their figures. The 2027 census is the next one.
- The census is every **five** years (not ten as in some other countries), apart from the
  postponements listed.
- Don't invent national census statistics (age shares, county populations, non-Irish shares,
  vacancy, average age, Irish speakers). Only the totals in section 2 may be quoted. Use the
  illustrative Rivertown values for every calculation.
- Don't copy figures from past-paper infographics or tables, even when they look official.
- Dependency ratio divides by the working-age group (15–64), not by the total population.
- Net migration = immigrants − emigrants; natural increase = births − deaths. Students swap these
  and the order of subtraction.
- De facto (people present on census night) vs de jure (people who usually live there): Ireland's
  headline count is de facto.
- Population pyramids: drawing and reading are in `geo-graph-skills-s1`; demographic transition,
  pyramid stages, fertility and mortality change and Ireland's 20th-century history are in
  `geo-population-migration-s1` and `-s3`. One link sentence each here.
- Migration patterns, migration policy and the reasons people migrate belong to
  `geo-population-migration-s3`–`s6`. Here, migration appears only as the net migration term in
  the population-change equation.
- The GIS study of urban sprawl is in `geo-weather-climate-s3`; one link bullet only.
