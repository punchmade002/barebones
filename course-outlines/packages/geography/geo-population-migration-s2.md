## 1. Must cover (one bullet or block for every item; tick each off)

The idea
- [ ] O1 Overpopulation: when the population of an area is too large for its resources, technology and economy to support at an acceptable standard of living. It is a balance between people and resources, not a matter of numbers or density alone.
- [ ] O2 Underpopulation (too few people to develop the resources fully) and optimum population (the number that gives the highest standard of living with the existing resources and technology). One line each.
- [ ] O3 Density is not overpopulation: the Netherlands is densely populated but not overpopulated (wealth, technology, trade); the Sahel is thinly populated but overpopulated (fragile resources, drought, poverty).
- [ ] O4 Theories, one short bullet each: Malthus (population grows geometrically, food arithmetically, so famine, disease and war act as "positive checks"); Boserup (population pressure drives farming innovation, so food supply can rise to meet demand); the Club of Rome's "Limits to Growth". Ireland's Great Famine is often cited in support of Malthus; the Green Revolution in support of Boserup.

Causes of overpopulation (the four syllabus factors; each must carry a full 30-mark essay with two case studies)
- [ ] R1 The development of resources: where resources are not developed, are being degraded, or shrink, the same population becomes too many. Sahel (desertification, drought, overgrazing, fuelwood cutting, shrinking Lake Chad). Pre-Famine Ireland (over-reliance on one resource, the potato; subdivision of holdings). Contrast: developing resources raises carrying capacity (India's Green Revolution; Ireland's industrial growth since the 1960s).
- [ ] R2 Society and culture: religion and attitudes to contraception; status and education of women; early marriage; preference for sons; children as farm labour and old-age security; prestige of large families. Case studies: the Sahel/Niger (early marriage, low female literacy, large-family prestige); India (son preference, low status of women in some states vs Kerala); Ireland before the 1970s (Catholic teaching, large families, contraception illegal) and the change since.
- [ ] R3 Income levels: low-income countries have high birth rates (children as economic assets, high infant mortality, no pensions); rising incomes bring falling fertility (cost of raising children, women's paid work, education). Contrast low-income Niger with high-income Ireland, the EU and Japan. Poverty also limits the ability to develop resources or import food. Exception: Bangladesh cut fertility sharply while still poor (family planning, girls' education, women's jobs in the garment industry).
- [ ] R4 Technology: medical technology (vaccines, antibiotics, clean water) cut death rates far faster than birth rates fell, causing the population explosion of the 20th century (link to DTM stage 2 in `geo-population-migration-s1`); agricultural technology (Green Revolution: high-yield seeds, irrigation, fertilisers) increased carrying capacity; contraceptive technology lowered birth rates; lack of technology (subsistence farming) keeps carrying capacity low in the Sahel.
- [ ] R5 Government policy as a further factor: anti-natalist policies (China's one-child policy; India's family planning programme, including forced sterilisations during the Emergency) and pro-natalist policies; their success and human cost.

Effects of overpopulation
- [ ] X1 Food shortages, malnutrition and famine.
- [ ] X2 Environmental damage: soil erosion, desertification, deforestation, overgrazing, water shortages.
- [ ] X3 Unemployment and underemployment; poverty; low incomes.
- [ ] X4 Pressure on services: schools, hospitals, housing, water and sanitation.
- [ ] X5 Migration: rural-to-urban migration to shanty towns and emigration (link to `geo-population-migration-s6`); conflict over land and water.
- [ ] X6 A high young dependency ratio: many children per worker.

The impact of growth rates on development
- [ ] D1 Rapid growth slows development: savings go on feeding and educating more people rather than investment; per-capita income gains are cancelled out; jobs cannot keep up; environment degraded. The "poverty trap" of high fertility.
- [ ] D2 Slowing growth can speed development: the "demographic dividend" (a large working-age population with fewer dependants), e.g. East Asian economies such as South Korea and China, and Ireland from the 1990s.
- [ ] D3 Very slow or negative growth also harms development: ageing, labour shortages, shrinking tax base, high pension and health costs (Japan, Italy, South Korea).

## 3. Traps

- Never equate overpopulation with high density (exam markers reward the distinction). Bangladesh is very dense but is used here for its fertility fall, not as "overpopulated because dense".
- Malthus wrote in 1798, not 1898; Boserup in 1965. Do not attribute "Limits to Growth" to Malthus.
- The Great Famine began in 1845. The 8.2 million figure is the whole island; the 26-county figure (about 6.5 million) is in `geo-population-migration-s1`. Don't mix them.
- Don't say the one-child policy applied to every family: rural and minority exceptions existed. Don't give the Chinese government's "400 million births prevented" claim as fact.
- Don't call India's Green Revolution an unqualified success: give its side effects.
- Don't give past-paper model-answer figures (e.g. Niger growth rates, female literacy percentages, Nigerian birth rates); many are invented. Use only section 2.
- The DTM itself, pyramids and dependency-ratio definitions are taught in `geo-population-migration-s1`; refer to them, don't re-teach them.
- Developing-world city problems (shanty towns, traffic, sprawl) belong to `geo-urban` (developing-world cities); rural-to-urban migration impacts belong to `geo-population-migration-s6`. Mention them only as effects in one line each.
- Debt, aid and the cycle of poverty in depth belong to `geo-option-global-interdependence`; desertification as an environmental issue is also taught there. Keep to population pressure here.
- India's population distribution and regional economy belong to `geo-region-continental-s1`.
