# Package: geo-region-european-s3 — The complexity of regions (i)

- **loId:** `geo-region-european-s3` · **chapterId:** `geo-region-european` · **syllabusRef:** 2.3
- **Syllabus:** Statement: "The study of regions illustrates the geographical complexity of the
  interaction between economic, cultural and physical processes." Students should study "the
  interaction of economic, political and cultural activities" (national setting: "The Republic
  of Ireland and Northern Ireland"; international: "European examples"); "the interaction of
  different cultural groups and political regions"; "the future of the Europe and the European
  Union, with particular reference to the issues relating to political union, economic union,
  and sovereignty" (setting: "The EU").
- **Prepared by:** a higher model, 2026-10-20. The writer works only from this package and
  `course-outlines/NOTES_STANDARD.md`.
- **Scale:** three topics, each able to carry a 30-mark Higher answer (about 15 SRPs). Use the
  syllabus's own wording for headings. Keep to this 2003 syllabus: no "geopolitics" or
  "territorial disputes" framing (that is the 2026 specification).

## 1. Must cover (one bullet or block for every item; tick each off)

A. Interaction of economic, political and cultural activities: Republic of Ireland and Northern Ireland
- [ ] I1 Two political regions on one island since 1921: the Republic (Irish Free State from 1922) and Northern Ireland (part of the UK). The creation of the border itself is taught in `geo-region-european-s4`; here start from the two regions as they exist.
- [ ] I2 Cultural groups in Northern Ireland: nationalist/Catholic and unionist/Protestant communities; Census 2021 religion figures; segregation in housing and schooling; identity (Irish, British, Northern Irish); language (Irish, Ulster-Scots) and the 2022 language law.
- [ ] I3 Political interaction: the Troubles (deaths, effects); the Good Friday (Belfast) Agreement 1998: power-sharing Assembly, North/South Ministerial Council and cross-border bodies, the consent principle, the right to Irish or British citizenship or both; the referendums; Assembly suspensions and restoration (2024).
- [ ] I4 Economic interaction: the effect of conflict on investment and tourism; recovery after 1998; cross-border trade and bodies (InterTradeIreland, Tourism Ireland); the all-island electricity market; Dublin–Belfast corridor (rail and road); EU PEACE programmes and the International Fund for Ireland.
- [ ] I5 Cultural interaction: all-island cultural and sporting bodies (GAA, Irish rugby team, the Churches); the Irish language; parades and flags as sources of tension.
- [ ] I6 Brexit as an example of all three interacting: Northern Ireland voted to remain; the border became the UK–EU land border; the Northern Ireland Protocol and the Windsor Framework (2023) avoided a hard border; Irish passports; the Common Travel Area.

B. Interaction of cultural groups and political regions: European examples
- [ ] B1 Belgium (main European example, the syllabus's language setting in `geo-region-concept`): Flemish (Dutch-speaking) north, French-speaking Wallonia in the south, bilingual Brussels, small German-speaking community; historic French dominance; economic reversal (Wallonia's coal and steel decline, Flanders' growth); political consequences (separate parties, long government formations, rise of Flemish nationalism); federal solution. The 1962 language border and the federal reforms as *boundary changes* are taught in s4; here focus on how the groups and regions interact now.
- [ ] B2 Switzerland as a contrasting, stable multilingual state: four national languages, 26 cantons, federal power-sharing. Short block.
- [ ] B3 Spain: the Basque Country and Catalonia: distinct languages and cultures, regional autonomy, wealthy regions, independence movements (ETA's violence and end; Catalonia's 2017 referendum). Short block.
- [ ] B4 One further brief example of a cultural group and a political region: Scotland's 2014 independence referendum. One or two bullets.
- [ ] B5 General principle: where cultural and political regions do not match, tension arises; outcomes range from devolution/federalism and power-sharing to conflict.

C. The future of Europe and the European Union
- [ ] F1 Political union: how far the EU has moved from economic co-operation towards political union: the Maastricht Treaty (EU created), the failed Constitution (2005), the Lisbon Treaty (2009) and its new posts; the European Parliament; qualified majority voting vs unanimity; differing visions (federal Europe vs a Europe of nation states); rise of eurosceptic parties.
- [ ] F2 Economic union: the single market; the euro (launch, notes and coins, 21 members in 2026); the ECB and a single interest rate; the convergence criteria; why some EU states are not in the euro (opt-out, choice, criteria not met); the benefits of the common currency for people in the eurozone; the eurozone debt crisis and bailouts (Ireland 2010–2013, Greece); banking union; NextGenerationEU joint borrowing.
- [ ] F3 Sovereignty: pooling sovereignty; areas where member states keep control (tax, defence, foreign policy largely by unanimity); Irish examples (referendums on EU treaties after the Crotty case; the rejection and later acceptance of Nice and Lisbon; corporation tax and the 15% minimum; the Apple tax ruling 2024; neutrality); Brexit as a reassertion of national sovereignty; rule-of-law disputes with Poland and Hungary.
- [ ] F4 Challenges facing the EU (exams ask for social and economic challenges): social (ageing population, migration and asylum pressures, youth unemployment, rising populism and euroscepticism, regional inequality); economic (debt, competitiveness, energy security after Russia's invasion of Ukraine in 2022, the cost of enlargement, Brexit's effects).
- [ ] F5 Future enlargement as part of the EU's future: named candidate countries; arguments for and against. Keep to one block; the history of enlargement is in s4.

## 2. Checked facts and examples (the only named places, dates and figures you may use)

Do not name any place, date or figure that is not on this list.

| Item | Example(s) |
|---|---|
| I1 | Northern Ireland was established in 1921 with six counties: Antrim, Armagh, Down, Fermanagh, Londonderry (Derry) and Tyrone. The Irish Free State was established in 1922; Ireland left the Commonwealth and was declared a republic in 1949. Republic of Ireland population about 5.15 million (Census 2022); Northern Ireland about 1.9 million (Census 2021). |
| I2 | Census 2021 (NI), religion or religion brought up in: Catholic about 46%, Protestant and other Christian about 43%; the first census in which Catholics outnumbered Protestants. Most children attend schools of their own community. Identity and Language (Northern Ireland) Act 2022: official recognition for Irish and for Ulster-Scots. Republic: Catholic about 69%, no religion about 14% (Census 2022). |
| I3 | The Troubles: from the late 1960s to 1998; over 3,500 people were killed. Good Friday (Belfast) Agreement signed 10 April 1998; approved by referendum on 22 May 1998, about 71% Yes in Northern Ireland and about 94% Yes in the Republic. The Republic changed Articles 2 and 3 of its Constitution. The Northern Ireland Assembly was suspended several times; after a two-year collapse it was restored in February 2024, when Michelle O'Neill became the first nationalist First Minister. |
| I4 | Cross-border bodies set up under the Agreement include InterTradeIreland and Waterways Ireland; Tourism Ireland markets the whole island abroad. Single Electricity Market for the whole island since 2007. Dublin–Belfast Enterprise rail service and the M1/A1 road corridor. International Fund for Ireland set up 1986. EU PEACE programme began in 1995 (PEACE I); the current programme is PEACE PLUS (2021–27). |
| I5 | GAA, the Irish rugby team, and the Catholic Church and Church of Ireland are organised on an all-island basis. The Catholic and Church of Ireland archbishops of Armagh are each Primate of All Ireland. |
| I6 | UK referendum on EU membership, 23 June 2016: about 52% Leave overall; Northern Ireland about 56% Remain; Scotland about 62% Remain. The UK left the EU on 31 January 2020; the transition period ended 31 December 2020. The Northern Ireland Protocol kept Northern Ireland aligned with many EU single-market rules for goods, avoiding checks at the land border; the Windsor Framework (February 2023) revised it. Common Travel Area between Ireland and the UK since 1923. Irish–Northern Irish land border about 499 km long. |
| B1 | Belgium: independent 1830; population about 11.8 million (2024). Flanders about 6.8 million, Wallonia about 3.7 million, Brussels-Capital about 1.2 million (2020s). Dutch speakers about 6 in 10 of the population, French speakers about 4 in 10; the German-speaking community in the east has fewer than 80,000 people. French was long the language of government, courts and universities. The Catholic University of Leuven split in 1968; the French-speaking section moved to a new town, Louvain-la-Neuve, in Wallonia. Wallonia's coal and steel (Liège, Charleroi, Sambre–Meuse valley) declined; Flanders grew through the port of Antwerp (Europe's second-largest port) and modern industry. There are no nationwide parties: each party stands in one language community only. Belgium went 541 days without a new government in 2010–11. The Flemish nationalist N-VA has been the largest party since 2010; its leader Bart De Wever became Prime Minister in February 2025. Brussels hosts the main institutions of the EU and NATO's headquarters. |
| B2 | Switzerland: four national languages: German (about 6 in 10), French (about 2 in 10), Italian (under 1 in 10) and Romansh (under 1%); 26 cantons with wide powers. |
| B3 | Basque Country (Spain and France): Basque (Euskara) is unrelated to any other European language. ETA declared a definitive end to violence in 2011 and dissolved in 2018. Catalonia: Catalan language; held an independence referendum on 1 October 2017 that Spain's courts declared illegal; Spain imposed direct rule using Article 155 of its constitution. Both are autonomous communities of Spain and among its wealthiest regions. |
| B4 | Scottish independence referendum, 18 September 2014: about 55% No. |
| F1 | Treaty on European Union (Maastricht) signed 7 February 1992, in force 1 November 1993; it created the EU and planned the single currency. The draft EU Constitution was rejected by French and Dutch voters in 2005. Lisbon Treaty signed 2007, in force 1 December 2009: created a permanent President of the European Council and the High Representative for Foreign Affairs, extended qualified majority voting and the European Parliament's powers. European Parliament: 720 MEPs after the 2024 elections; Ireland elects 14. Article 7 procedures (possible suspension of voting rights) were opened against Poland (2017) and Hungary (2018) over rule-of-law concerns. |
| F2 | Single market from 1 January 1993. Euro launched 1 January 1999 (for banks and electronic payments); notes and coins from 1 January 2002. Bulgaria joined the euro on 1 January 2026, replacing the lev, making 21 euro countries (Austria, Belgium, Bulgaria, Croatia, Cyprus, Estonia, Finland, France, Germany, Greece, Ireland, Italy, Latvia, Lithuania, Luxembourg, Malta, Netherlands, Portugal, Slovakia, Slovenia, Spain). Croatia joined in 2023. Not in the euro: Denmark (legal opt-out), Sweden (voted No in a 2003 referendum; stays out by not joining the exchange-rate mechanism), Poland, Czechia, Hungary, Romania. Convergence (Maastricht) criteria: low inflation, government deficit no more than 3% of GDP, government debt no more than 60% of GDP, stable exchange rate (two years in ERM II), low long-term interest rates. European Central Bank in Frankfurt sets one interest rate for the euro area. Benefits for people: no currency exchange costs, easy price comparison, no exchange-rate risk in trade and travel, generally low interest rates and inflation. Ireland's EU/IMF bailout of about €85 billion, agreed November 2010, exited December 2013. Greece received three bailouts (2010, 2012, 2015), ending in 2018. Single Supervisory Mechanism (ECB supervision of big banks) began November 2014. NextGenerationEU recovery fund agreed 2020, about €750 billion, financed by joint EU borrowing. |
| F3 | Crotty v An Taoiseach (1987): Ireland's Supreme Court ruled that significant EU treaty changes need a referendum. Irish voters rejected the Nice Treaty (2001) and approved it in 2002; rejected the Lisbon Treaty (June 2008) and approved it (October 2009). Ireland's 12.5% corporation tax rate; under the OECD agreement, Ireland applies a 15% minimum rate to large firms (turnover over €750 million) from 2024. Apple ruling: on 10 September 2024 the EU Court of Justice ruled that Ireland must recover €13 billion (plus interest) in unpaid tax from Apple as illegal state aid. Ireland is militarily neutral and not in NATO. Ireland is outside the Schengen area because of the Common Travel Area with the UK. |
| F4 | EU population about 450 million (2024), 27 member states. Russia's full-scale invasion of Ukraine began 24 February 2022, causing an energy price shock and a large refugee movement into the EU. |
| F5 | Ukraine and Moldova became candidate countries in June 2022; Georgia in December 2023; Bosnia and Herzegovina in December 2022. Other candidates: Albania, Montenegro, North Macedonia, Serbia, Turkey (candidate since 1999; talks frozen). |

## 3. Traps

- Keep the split with s4: s4 teaches **how boundaries changed** (partition 1920–25, Belgium's 1962 language border and federalisation, Yugoslavia, EU enlargement dates). s3 teaches **how groups and regions interact now** and the EU's **future**. Don't repeat partition history or the list of enlargement dates here.
- The Good Friday Agreement did not move the border; it changed how the border and the two communities relate.
- The UK did not leave the EU in 2016 (that was the referendum) or on 31 December 2020 (end of transition); it left on 31 January 2020.
- Don't call the euro the currency of the whole EU; 6 of the 27 members don't use it (2026).
- Sweden has no formal opt-out; Denmark does. Don't swap them.
- Don't say Switzerland, Norway or Iceland are EU members.
- Belgian population shares: use the rounded ones in section 2 only. A past-paper answer gives Brussels as "85% French-speaking": unchecked, don't use it.
- Don't state the outcome of Ireland's planned changes to the "triple lock" on overseas deployment of Defence Forces: not checked.
- EU policies' effects on the Irish economy (CAP, CFP, regional and social funds) and EU trading patterns belong to `geo-economic-eu`. Migration policy belongs to `geo-population-migration`. Mention them only as challenges, without detail.
- Don't use 2026-specification framings ("geopolitics", "territorial disputes", "contested spaces"). Use the syllabus wording.

## 4. What exams ask (topics only)

- 30 marks, Higher: potential challenges facing the future development of the EU with reference to political union, economic union and sovereignty.
- 30 marks, Higher: social and economic challenges currently facing the EU.
- 20-mark data question on the EU and the euro: the year the Maastricht Treaty was signed (1992); a country's former currency (Bulgaria: the lev); naming euro countries; benefits of the common currency; why not all EU countries use the euro; a benefit of further expansion.
- Short questions: advantages for UK citizens of holding Irish citizenship after Brexit (EU free movement, right to live and work in the EU); potential impacts of the UK leaving on Irish trade.
- 30 marks: interaction of cultural groups and political regions with reference to examples (Northern Ireland, Belgium).
