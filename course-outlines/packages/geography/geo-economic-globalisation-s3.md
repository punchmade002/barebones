## 1. Must cover (one bullet or block for every item; tick each off)

Trade vocabulary (data-response questions use all of these)
- [ ] V1 Exports and imports; visible trade (goods) and invisible trade (services such as software, finance, tourism, aircraft leasing).
- [ ] V2 Balance of trade: value of exports minus imports. Trade surplus (exports greater) and trade deficit (imports greater).
- [ ] V3 Balance of payments: the record of all money flows between a country and the rest of the world, i.e. trade in goods and services plus investment, profits sent home, borrowing and transfers. Distinguish it from the balance of trade.
- [ ] V4 Disadvantages of a persistent trade deficit: money flows out, the country borrows or sells assets to pay for imports, home industries and jobs are lost to imports, the currency may weaken. Advantages of a surplus and of exports: jobs, tax revenue, foreign earnings, growth.
- [ ] V5 Advantages of many export partners: less dependence on one market, so a recession, tariff or crisis in one country (e.g. Brexit, US tariffs) does less damage.
- [ ] V6 Free trade vs protectionism: tariffs (taxes on imports), quotas (limits on quantity), subsidies and regulations as barriers; trade blocs remove barriers between members. The World Trade Organisation (WTO) sets world trade rules and settles disputes.
- [ ] V7 Fair trade (one bullet): a guaranteed minimum price and premium for producers in developing countries, shown by the Fairtrade mark. Detail belongs to the Global Interdependence option.

Linked in a global framework
- [ ] G1 World trade has grown much faster than world output since 1950; most countries now depend on imports and exports. Drivers: falling trade barriers (GATT and the WTO), containerisation and bigger ships, air freight, ICT, MNCs that trade components between their own plants, and the entry of China and Eastern Europe into world trade.
- [ ] G2 Global linkages: a single product links many countries (the iPhone or Intel chip chains from s1); shipping lanes and chokepoints (Suez and Panama canals) link the triad; disruption in one place spreads worldwide (examples in section 2).

The trading triad (each area its own block)
- [ ] T1 What the triad is: the three core economic areas of North America, Europe and the Pacific Rim (East Asia), which produce most of the world's manufactured goods, hold the headquarters of most MNCs, and carry most world trade among themselves. Most foreign direct investment flows between them too.
- [ ] T2 North America (USA): the world's largest economy and largest importer; persistent trade deficit, largest with China; its trade bloc (NAFTA, then USMCA); Mexico's role; the 2018 and 2025 tariff rounds. Draw on facts in section 2.
- [ ] T3 Europe (EU): the single market of 27 countries; the euro; the largest trading bloc by value; strong trade with the USA and China. One block only; EU trade patterns in detail belong to `geo-economic-eu`.
- [ ] T4 The Pacific Rim: define it (countries around the Pacific Ocean, but in the triad it means mainly East and South-East Asia: Japan, China, South Korea, Taiwan, Hong Kong, Singapore and the ASEAN countries, with Australia). Its trade organisations: ASEAN, APEC, RCEP.
- [ ] T5 Japan: post-war growth based on manufactured exports (cars, electronics), few raw materials so it imports fuel and ores; Toyota's just-in-time production; Japanese firms opened factories inside the EU and USA to avoid trade barriers; slower growth since the 1990s.
- [ ] T6 China: from closed economy to "workshop of the world": 1978 reforms, Special Economic Zones, WTO entry, largest exporter of goods, huge trade surplus, Belt and Road Initiative, trade war with the USA.
- [ ] T7 South Korea, a newly industrialised country (NIC): one of the "Asian Tigers"; from war-damaged and poor to high-income through export-led industrialisation; the chaebol (family-controlled business groups); shipbuilding, cars, electronics and memory chips. One full block (about 8 SRPs).
- [ ] T8 Excluded areas (one bullet): Africa, much of Latin America and South Asia hold only a small share of world trade, mostly raw materials. Reasons belong to `geo-economic-globalisation-s4`.

International division of labour
- [ ] D1 Meaning: different countries and regions specialise in different stages of production and different types of work.
- [ ] D2 The old (colonial) division of labour: colonies and developing countries exported raw materials and food; Europe and later the USA manufactured goods and sold them back. Examples in section 2.
- [ ] D3 The new international division of labour (from the 1960s–70s): MNCs moved assembly and routine manufacturing to NICs and low-wage countries; the core kept headquarters, R&D, design, finance and marketing; the periphery still supplies raw materials. Example: Apple's "Designed in California, assembled in China"; Intel's R&D in Oregon, fabs in Ireland, assembly in Malaysia.
- [ ] D4 It keeps changing: services are now divided too (IT and call centres in India); China is moving into high-value products, and low-cost assembly is spreading to Vietnam, Bangladesh and India.
- [ ] D5 Ireland's place in it: from food exporter to the UK, to a location for high-value MNC manufacturing and services.

Ireland in world trade (data response)
- [ ] I1 Ireland is a very open economy: exports are larger than its GDP. Main goods exports: pharmaceuticals and chemicals, medical devices, computer chips and electronics, food and drink (beef, dairy, whiskey). Main services exports: computer services, financial services, aircraft leasing, tourism. Exams ask for two goods or two services.
- [ ] I2 Main export destinations: the USA is the largest single market for Irish goods, then EU partners (Belgium, Germany, the Netherlands), the UK, China. Reasons the USA dominates: US pharmaceutical and technology MNCs export from their Irish plants back to the USA and worldwide.
- [ ] I3 Reasons for a change in exports to the UK (e.g. Brexit: customs checks, the UK leaving the single market, a weaker pound, Irish firms diversifying to other markets). Detailed Irish–EU and Irish–UK trade belongs to `geo-economic-eu`.
- [ ] I4 Risks: dependence on a few US MNCs and the US market; exposure to US tariffs (2025).

## 3. Traps

- Balance of trade (goods and services exports minus imports) is **not** the same as balance of payments (all money flows, including investment and profits). Exams ask for both.
- Don't take export percentages or values from past-paper pie charts or bar charts.
- The Pacific Rim includes the Americas' Pacific coasts too, but in the triad it means mainly East and South-East Asia. Don't put India in the Pacific Rim.
- The Asian Tigers are South Korea, Taiwan, Hong Kong and Singapore. China and Japan are not Tigers.
- Shenzhen is in mainland China beside Hong Kong; it is not part of Hong Kong.
- WTO is 1995 (GATT 1947). Don't say the WTO was set up after the Second World War.
- Ireland's large exports reflect MNC production; don't claim Irish-owned firms produce most exports.
- EU internal trade, external EU trade and Irish–EU trade belong to `geo-economic-eu`. Here the EU appears only as one of the three triad areas.
- Reasons some regions are excluded from world manufacturing, core–periphery, and the impact of globalisation on world trade belong to `geo-economic-globalisation-s4`. Keep T8 to one bullet.
- Colonialism's wider effects on a developing economy belong to `geo-economic-development-s3`. Use colonial trade only as the old division of labour.

## 4. What exams ask (topics only)

- Data response (charts and tables of trade): read values, calculate differences and totals, identify trade surpluses and deficits, name countries with the highest exports, draw a suitable graph.
- Short definitions: balance of payments; one disadvantage of a trade deficit; one advantage of many export partners; one advantage of exports for the Irish economy.
- Name two goods or two services exported from Ireland; the main destination of Irish exports; why exports to the UK changed.
- 30 marks (expected from the syllabus): the trading triad and the international division of labour, with global linkages.
