# Package: geo-population-migration-s3 — Changing migration patterns in Ireland

- **loId:** `geo-population-migration-s3` · **chapterId:** `geo-population-migration` · **syllabusRef:** 5.3
- **Syllabus:** Statement: "Population movements have an impact on the donor and receiver
  regions." "Students should study • changing migration patterns in Ireland". National setting:
  "20th century Irish census. Post-1950 migration to/from Ireland."
- **Prepared by:** a higher model, 2026-10-20. The writer works only from this package and
  `course-outlines/NOTES_STANDARD.md`.
- **Studied migration streams:** (1) the Famine and post-Famine emigration (background only);
  (2) 1950s emigration to Britain; (3) 1970s return migration; (4) 1980s emigration to Britain
  and the USA; (5) Celtic Tiger immigration 1996–2008, including EU accession migrants from
  2004; (6) post-2008 emigration to Australia, Canada and the UK; (7) immigration since the
  mid-2010s, including Ukrainians from 2022. Each has its own block of facts below, with causes
  and impacts on Ireland as donor or receiver.

## 1. Must cover (one bullet or block for every item; tick each off)

Migration concepts
- [ ] M1 Definitions: migration (a permanent or long-term change of residence); emigration and immigration; net migration; internal and international migration; voluntary and forced migration; economic migrants, refugees and asylum seekers (one line each; asylum policy is in `geo-population-migration-s4`); return migration; chain migration.
- [ ] M2 Push factors and pull factors, with Irish examples of each (exams ask for one of each associated with Ireland).
- [ ] M3 Barriers (intervening obstacles) to migration (exams ask for two): distance and cost of travel; immigration laws, visas and quotas; language; family ties and age; lack of information; cultural difference.
- [ ] M4 Models, one line each: Ravenstein's "laws of migration" (most migrants move short distances, in steps; each stream has a counter-stream; young adults most likely to migrate; cities attract migrants); Lee's push–pull model with intervening obstacles.
- [ ] M5 Who migrates: mostly young adults (age-selective); in the 1950s more women than men left some rural areas.

The streams (each: when, how many where checked, where to, why, impact)
- [ ] T1 Background: the Great Famine and continuous emigration from the 1840s; the State's population fell at almost every census from 1841 to 1961 (the exception is in section 2). One block only.
- [ ] T2 1950s: the highest emigration since the 1880s; mostly to Britain; causes (economic stagnation and protectionism, unemployment, the decline of small farms and farm labouring as farming mechanised, Britain's post-war labour demand for building, factories, nursing and transport, low fares and the Common Travel Area, chain migration); impacts.
- [ ] T3 1960s–1970s: the First Programme for Economic Expansion and foreign investment cut emigration; the 1970s were the first decade of net immigration since the Famine (returning emigrants and their children; EEC entry 1973; jobs in new factories).
- [ ] T4 1980s: recession, high unemployment and high taxes; heavy emigration again, to Britain and the USA (many undocumented in the USA), including graduates ("brain drain"); US visa lotteries.
- [ ] T5 1996–2008 (Celtic Tiger): net immigration; returning Irish; EU migrants, especially after the 2004 enlargement (Ireland one of three countries giving immediate access); non-EU workers on work permits; asylum seekers; Ireland became a country of immigration.
- [ ] T6 2008 to mid-2010s: the property crash, banking crisis and EU–IMF bailout (2010); unemployment; net emigration returned; destinations Australia, Canada, the UK and the USA; many were skilled young graduates; many recent immigrants also left.
- [ ] T7 Mid-2010s to now: economic recovery and net immigration again; job growth in multinationals and services; returning Irish; non-EU skilled workers; international protection applicants; Ukrainians under EU temporary protection from 2022; housing pressure.
- [ ] T8 Ireland's current immigrant population: size and main nationalities (Census 2022).
- [ ] T9 The Irish diaspora: Irish communities and people of Irish descent abroad (Britain, the USA, Australia).

Impacts on Ireland
- [ ] P1 Ireland as donor region, negative: loss of young workers and skills (brain drain); ageing rural population and imbalanced age structure; rural decline (closed schools, fewer marriages, depopulated parishes); lower birth rates; family separation; loss of the State's investment in education.
- [ ] P2 Ireland as donor region, positive: emigrant remittances; less pressure on jobs and welfare; emigrants return with skills, capital and experience (1970s, 1990s); diaspora links for trade, tourism and investment.
- [ ] P3 Ireland as receiver region, positive: fills labour shortages (construction, health care, hospitality, technology); young workers pay taxes and offset ageing; new skills and enterprise; cultural diversity (food, sport, languages, religions); keeps rural schools and parishes open; boosts population growth (net migration was the larger component of growth 2016–2022).
- [ ] P4 Ireland as receiver region, negative or challenging: pressure on housing, schools and health services; exploitation of some migrant workers; language barriers in schools; integration challenges and racism (detail in `geo-population-migration-s5`); remittances leave Ireland.
- [ ] P5 Reasons immigration rose after 2010 (an Ordinary data question): economic recovery and job creation; multinational investment; EU free movement; return of Irish emigrants; Ireland English-speaking and in the EU.
- [ ] P6 Reasons immigration fell between 2007 and 2010 (a Higher data question): the recession, collapse of construction, rising unemployment; return of many EU accession migrants home.

## 2. Checked facts and examples (the only named places, dates and figures you may use)

Every census figure here is for the State (26 counties). Give no other figures.

| Item | Example(s) |
|---|---|
| T1 | Great Famine 1845–49: about 1 million died and about 1 million emigrated (whole island). State population: about 6.5 million (Census 1841); about 3.0 million (Census 1926); about 2.8 million (Census 1961), the lowest. The only break in the decline was a very slight rise between Census 1946 and Census 1951 (give no figure). Over 30 million Americans report Irish ancestry (US Census Bureau surveys, 2020s). |
| T2 1950s | Net emigration of about 400,000 between 1951 and 1961 (net outflow from the State); most went to Britain (London, Birmingham, Manchester, Coventry, Liverpool). The Commission on Emigration and Other Population Problems reported in 1954. The Common Travel Area between Ireland and Britain dates from the 1920s; Irish citizens can live and work in the UK without permits. |
| T3 | "Economic Development" (T. K. Whitaker) and the First Programme for Economic Expansion, 1958. IDA (Industrial Development Authority), set up 1949 and given a central role in attracting foreign investment from 1969. Ireland joined the EEC on 1 January 1973. Net immigration in the 1970s; State population rose from about 3.0 million (Census 1971) to about 3.4 million (Census 1981). |
| T4 1980s | Net emigration of over 200,000 between 1981 and 1991; the State's population fell slightly between Census 1986 and Census 1991 (both about 3.5 million). Destinations: Britain and the USA. US visa programmes in the late 1980s and early 1990s (the Donnelly and Morrison visas) regularised many Irish emigrants. |
| T5 Celtic Tiger | Net immigration from 1996. EU enlargement on 1 May 2004 (10 new members, including Poland, Lithuania, Latvia, Slovakia and the Czech Republic); Ireland, the UK and Sweden were the only existing members to open their labour markets immediately. Census 2006: about 420,000 non-Irish nationals (about 10% of the population); Polish about 63,000, the largest group after UK nationals. Census 2006 State population about 4.2 million. |
| T6 | The banking crisis began in 2008; EU–IMF bailout agreed November 2010. Net emigration from about 2009 to the mid-2010s. Emigration peaked at about 89,000 in the year to April 2013 (CSO estimate), the highest since the 1980s; about half were Irish nationals. Main destinations: Australia, the UK, Canada and the USA. Australia's working holiday visa was widely used. |
| T7 | Net immigration returned in the mid-2010s and continued. Census 2022: net migration of about 190,000 between 2016 and 2022, larger than natural increase (about 170,000). Russia's full-scale invasion of Ukraine, 24 February 2022; over 100,000 people from Ukraine came to Ireland under EU temporary protection by 2024. |
| T8 Census 2022 | About 630,000 non-Irish nationals (about 12% of the population), plus about 170,000 dual Irish citizens. Just over 1 million people (about one in five) born outside Ireland. Largest non-Irish groups: Polish about 94,000; UK about 83,000; Indian about 45,000; Romanian about 43,000; Lithuanian about 31,000; Brazilian about 27,000. Polish is the most common language other than English and Irish spoken at home (Census 2016 and 2022). |

## 3. Traps

- Don't copy figures from past-paper tables (Irish emigrants to the UK or USA by year; immigration and emigration lines; Ireland vs Greece immigrants). Model answers on those papers contain invented figures (e.g. "1.5 million left 1951–1991", "20% foreign-born by 2020", "over 70,000 net immigration a year"); never use them.
- Lowest census population is 1961, not 1991. The 1980s did not make the State's population fall below its 1961 level.
- The 1950s outflow was mainly to Britain, not America; the 1980s went to both; post-2008 mainly to Australia, Canada and the UK.
- Don't say the 1970s had net emigration: they were net immigration.
- Don't say all 2004 accession states' citizens were Polish; Polish was the largest group.
- Ukrainians are under EU temporary protection, not the international protection (asylum) process. Policy detail on asylum, direct provision, work permits, the Common Travel Area and EU free movement belongs to `geo-population-migration-s4`; mention them here only as causes.
- Racism, integration and religious change belong to `geo-population-migration-s5`.
- Internal rural-to-urban migration in Ireland belongs to `geo-population-migration-s6`.
- Refugee migration as a global "social and political decision" issue belongs to `geo-option-global-interdependence`; migration's effect on racial patterns belongs to `geo-option-culture-identity`.
