# Package: geo-population-migration-s1 — Changing population characteristics

- **loId:** `geo-population-migration-s1` · **chapterId:** `geo-population-migration` · **syllabusRef:** 5.1
- **Syllabus:** Statement: "Population characteristics change over time and space." "Students
  should study changes in • population distribution • population density • growth patterns
  • population structure, changing fertility, and mortality rates." National setting: "Census
  material to show patterns of change in Ireland." International setting: "Appropriate European
  and global examples." Elective Unit 5 (Human), so write in full Higher Level detail.
- **Prepared by:** a higher model, 2026-10-20. The writer works only from this package and
  `course-outlines/NOTES_STANDARD.md`.
- **Studied areas in this subsection:** the world; Ireland (the census record 1841–2022, with
  1950s to today in detail); Europe (EU ageing, Germany, Italy, Bulgaria); Niger (a young,
  fast-growing population); Japan (an old, shrinking population). Each has its own block in
  section 2.

## 1. Must cover (one bullet or block for every item; tick each off)

Key measures and the census
- [ ] K1 Definitions, one line each: crude birth rate and crude death rate (per 1,000 per year); natural change (birth rate minus death rate; natural increase or natural decrease); net migration (immigrants minus emigrants); total fertility rate (average number of children per woman); replacement level (about 2.1); infant mortality rate (deaths under one year per 1,000 live births); life expectancy.
- [ ] K2 Population change = natural change + net migration. Exams ask whether natural increase or net migration drove growth in an inter-censal period: answer from the two components.
- [ ] K3 The census: a count of every person on one night, every five years in Ireland, run by the Central Statistics Office (CSO); what it records (age, sex, place of residence, birthplace, nationality, religion, ethnicity, Irish language, housing, work, commuting). Irregular years (see section 2).
- [ ] K4 How governments use population statistics: planning schools, hospitals and housing; pensions and the State pension age; transport and infrastructure; drawing constituency boundaries (TDs per population); allocating funding to regions; workforce and immigration planning. Exams ask for two.

Distribution and density
- [ ] D1 Population distribution (where people live) vs population density (people per km²). Density = population ÷ area; show a worked example with invented, labelled figures.
- [ ] D2 Physical factors: relief (lowlands vs mountains), climate (temperate vs too hot, cold or dry), soils and water supply, natural resources (coalfields drew 19th-century industrial populations).
- [ ] D3 Human factors: economic opportunity and jobs, transport routes, cities and ports, history (colonial ports, plantation), government policy.
- [ ] D4 Low-density areas, with reasons (exams ask for two): high, steep or cold land; deserts; dense forest; poor soils and remoteness; few jobs, so young people leave.
- [ ] D5 Global pattern: dense in East Asia, South Asia, Europe, north-eastern USA, the Nile valley; sparse in the Sahara, Amazon Basin, Siberia, northern Canada, Greenland and Antarctica. Most people live in the northern hemisphere and near coasts and rivers.
- [ ] D6 European pattern: a dense core (the Benelux countries, the Rhine–Ruhr, south-east England, the Po valley, the Paris region) and a sparse periphery (northern Scandinavia, the Scottish Highlands, interior Spain).
- [ ] D7 Irish pattern: concentration in the east (the Greater Dublin Area) and in the five cities; low density in the west and north-west (Mayo, Leitrim, Roscommon) and uplands. Changes over time: from a rural, western-weighted population before the Famine to an eastern, urban one; growth of Dublin's commuter counties (Meath, Kildare, Wicklow).
- [ ] D8 Why Ireland's population density is rising: population growth from natural increase and net immigration since the 1990s, with a fixed land area.

Growth patterns
- [ ] G1 World population growth: slow until about 1800, then rapid (exponential "J-curve"), driven by falling death rates; growth rate now slowing. Milestones in section 2.
- [ ] G2 Contrast: rapid growth in sub-Saharan Africa; slow growth, stagnation or decline in Europe and East Asia.
- [ ] G3 The Demographic Transition Model (DTM), all five stages, each with birth rate, death rate, natural change, reasons and an example country:
  Stage 1 high stationary (high BR, high DR, little growth; no country today; Ireland before the 19th century is not a checked example, so use "pre-industrial societies").
  Stage 2 early expanding (high BR, rapidly falling DR, rapid growth; e.g. Niger). The death rate falls most in stage 2.
  Stage 3 late expanding (falling BR, low DR, growth slows; e.g. India).
  Stage 4 low stationary (low BR, low DR, little growth; e.g. Ireland, USA). Ireland is in stage 4 (exam answer).
  Stage 5 declining (BR below DR, natural decrease; e.g. Germany, Italy, Japan).
- [ ] G4 Why the death rate falls in stage 2: clean water and sanitation, vaccination, better food supply, medical care.
- [ ] G5 Why the birth rate falls after stage 2: female education and paid work, contraception, falling infant mortality (fewer "insurance" births), urbanisation (children cost more, earn less), pensions replacing children as old-age security, later marriage, weaker religious influence.
- [ ] G6 Challenges for stage 5 countries: shrinking workforce, labour shortages, rising pension and health costs, closing schools, rural depopulation; responses (immigration, pro-natalist incentives, later retirement).
- [ ] G7 Limitations of the DTM: based on European experience; ignores migration; developing countries' death rates fell much faster (imported medicine) so growth is faster; some countries may not complete it; no fixed time scale.

Population structure
- [ ] S1 Population pyramid: age cohorts (5-year bands), males left, females right, as % or numbers. Uses: plan services, forecast future needs, compare countries, show past events (baby booms, wars, emigration). Exams ask for one use.
- [ ] S2 Shapes: wide base and narrow top (high fertility, short life expectancy, e.g. Niger); straight-sided (stable, low BR and DR); narrow base and wide top, top-heavy (ageing, e.g. Japan). How a developed country's pyramid differs from a developing country's.
- [ ] S3 Reading a pyramid: bulges (baby booms, immigration of young adults), notches (emigration, low birth years, war), more women than men at the oldest ages (women live longer).
- [ ] S4 Dependency ratio = (people aged 0–14 + people aged 65+) ÷ people aged 15–64 × 100; young dependency and old-age dependency separately. Work an invented example.
- [ ] S5 Why dependency ratios are usually lower in urban areas than rural ones: young working adults migrate to cities for jobs and college, leaving older people (and sometimes children) behind in rural areas.
- [ ] S6 Effects of a rising old-age dependency ratio (exams ask for two): higher pension costs; more demand for health care, nursing homes and carers; smaller tax base from fewer workers; pressure to raise the pension age; housing and transport needs change. Planning responses for Ireland's ageing population: home-care supports, pension reform, more geriatric services, encouraging immigration of workers, age-friendly towns.
- [ ] S7 Effects of a high young dependency ratio: demand for schools, maternity and child health care, future jobs and housing; but a future workforce (link to the "demographic dividend" in `geo-population-migration-s2`).

Changing fertility and mortality, and their impact on structure
- [ ] F1 Factors in fertility: income and economic development; status and education of women; religion and culture; access to contraception; government policy; infant mortality; urban vs rural living. Exam (Ordinary) asks for the factors that influence birth rates and death rates under income, culture and society, and medical technology: answer here for both rates.
- [ ] F2 Factors in mortality: medical technology (vaccines, antibiotics), sanitation, diet, income, health services, lifestyle diseases (heart disease, cancer) in rich countries; conflict, famine and disease (malaria, HIV/AIDS) in poor ones.
- [ ] F3 How falling fertility and mortality change structure: falling fertility narrows the base; rising life expectancy widens the top; the result is an older population with a higher median age.
- [ ] F4 Ways life expectancy can be raised in a country such as Niger: clean water and sanitation, vaccination, mosquito nets and malaria treatment, more doctors and clinics, better nutrition, female education.
- [ ] F5 Why people in Ireland have a long life expectancy: good diet and housing, health services, vaccination, clean water, fewer smokers, safer work.

Ireland's population since the 1950s (a 30-mark essay in its own right; use the census blocks in section 2)
- [ ] I1 Size: the long decline from 1841 to the low of 1961, growth since (with a dip in 1986–1991), over 5 million in Census 2022.
- [ ] I2 Births: high fertility into the 1970s (Catholic culture, large families, little access to contraception); births peaked in 1980; fertility fell below replacement around 1990 and is now about 1.5.
- [ ] I3 Reasons fertility fell: free secondary education (1967) and more women in third level; the marriage bar lifted (1973); contraception legalised (1979) and widened later; women's paid employment; later marriage and first births; secularisation; cost of housing and childcare.
- [ ] I4 Deaths: life expectancy rose and infant mortality fell to very low levels.
- [ ] I5 Migration: heavy net emigration in the 1950s and 1980s; net immigration in the 1970s, from the mid-1990s to 2008, and again from the mid-2010s. Give the essentials only; detail belongs to `geo-population-migration-s3`.
- [ ] I6 Structure: from a young population with a wide base to an ageing one; average age rising; more people over 65; more diverse (about one in five born abroad).
- [ ] I7 Distribution: urbanisation and the shift to the east (detail on rural-to-urban migration is in `geo-population-migration-s6`).
- [ ] I8 Household change: smaller households, more people living alone (describe without figures).

European and global examples of changing structure
- [ ] E1 The EU: an ageing population with below-replacement fertility; natural decrease in many member states; immigration supplies most growth.
- [ ] E2 Germany: deaths have exceeded births every year since 1972; immigration has kept its population stable or growing.
- [ ] E3 Italy: among the EU's lowest fertility rates and oldest populations (reuse `geo-region-european-s2`).
- [ ] E4 Eastern Europe (Bulgaria): population decline from both low fertility and emigration after 1989.
- [ ] E5 Niger: a very young population with very high fertility (stage 2; pyramid with a very wide base).
- [ ] E6 Japan: the world's oldest population; falling population (stage 5; top-heavy pyramid).

## 3. Traps

- Density is people per km², not per hectare or per mile. Work density examples with invented figures and label them "illustrative"; never reuse past-paper table values (e.g. country areas and populations from a data question).
- Ireland's census figures above are for the 26-county area (the State). The whole-island figure for 1841 (about 8.2 million) is used in `geo-population-migration-s2`; never mix the two.
- 1961 is the lowest census population, not 1926 or 1991.
- Do not say Ireland's population is falling or that Ireland has natural decrease: births still exceed deaths.
- Do not say Ireland has the EU's highest fertility rate today; give only the checked figure.
- The DTM stage numbering must match: stage 2 = early expanding (death rate falls most), stage 5 = declining. Do not call India stage 2 or Niger stage 3.
- Do not invent DTM birth/death rate numbers for the stages; describe them as high/low/falling.
- Overpopulation (causes, effects, the four syllabus factors as essays) belongs to `geo-population-migration-s2`. Here the factors explain birth and death rates only. Keep the s2 30-mark "impact of one factor on population growth" essays out of here, but the Ordinary "factors that influence birth rates and death rates" question is answered here.
- Migration streams in detail belong to `geo-population-migration-s3` (Ireland), `-s4` (policy) and `-s6` (rural to urban). Only summarise migration as a component of change.
- "Account for the distribution of population throughout a continental/sub-continental region" is a question on `geo-region-continental` (India), not this chapter. Do not teach India's population distribution here.
- Graph drawing from a data table (bar charts, line graphs, pyramids) is taught in `geo-graph-skills`; here only interpretation.
- Questions on tourism, the Gaeltacht, settlement on OS maps and aerial photographs are tagged to this chapter in error; ignore them.

## 4. What exams ask (topics only)

- Short data questions on census tables, pyramids and graphs: highest/lowest census year, increase or decrease, percentages in an age group, dependency ratios by county, density calculations, natural increase vs net migration in inter-censal periods.
- Definitions: natural change, net migration, dependency ratio.
- DTM: name stages 1 and 2, give an example country for stage 2, Ireland's current stage (4), the stage where the death rate falls most (2), why the birth rate falls after stage 2, a challenge for stage 5 countries.
- One reason why some areas have low density; why Ireland's density is rising; why urban dependency ratios are lower than rural ones.
- One or two effects of a rising old-age dependency ratio or of many people over 65; one challenge of population increase; how an ageing population can be planned for.
- One use of population pyramids; how a developed country's pyramid differs from a developing country's; why Irish life expectancy is long; how life expectancy could be raised in poor countries.
- Two ways governments use population statistics.
- 30 marks, Higher: how Ireland's population characteristics have changed since the 1950s; how changing fertility and mortality rates affect population structure, with examples.
- 40 marks, Ordinary: factors that influence birth rates and death rates (income, culture and society, medical technology).

## 5. Practice questions to write

1. 30 marks, Higher: "Describe and explain how the characteristics of Ireland's population have changed since the 1950s." Model answer: about 15 SRPs across size, births/fertility (with reasons), deaths/life expectancy, migration (brief), age structure and distribution, using only the census facts in section 2.
2. 30 marks, Higher: "Examine how changing fertility and mortality rates impact on population structure, with reference to example(s) you have studied." Model answer: the DTM link; Niger (wide base, young); Japan (top-heavy, shrinking); Ireland's shift from young to ageing. Pyramid descriptions for each.
3. 2+2+2 marks, Higher short questions: "Explain briefly the term natural change"; "State one reason why the birth rate falls after stage 2 of the DTM"; "Explain briefly one challenge facing countries in stage 5 of the DTM." One point each, no extras.
