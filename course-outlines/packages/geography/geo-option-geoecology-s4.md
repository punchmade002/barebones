## 1. Must cover (one bullet or block for every item; tick each off)

Biomes in general (short, shared by both options)
- [ ] BI1 Biome: a large world region with a distinctive climate, soil, and community of plants and animals adapted to it. Climate is the main control: it shapes soil, and climate and soil together shape vegetation, which shapes the animals.
- [ ] BI2 Biotic inter-relationships: food chains and webs (producers, herbivores, carnivores, decomposers); competition; mutual dependence such as pollination and seed dispersal; nutrient cycling between plants, litter and soil.
- [ ] BI3 The world pattern: biomes form broad belts that follow climate belts (tundra, boreal forest, temperate deciduous forest, temperate grassland, Mediterranean, hot desert, savanna, tropical rainforest); mountains show vertical zonation. One bullet; no detail on the other biomes.
- [ ] BI4 Skill: reading a climate graph (temperature line, rainfall bars) to identify a biome: annual range = warmest minus coolest month mean; total rainfall = sum of monthly bars; describe seasonality. Use invented, clearly labelled illustrative values only, never figures from past papers.

### Option A: the hot desert biome
- [ ] A1 Definition and location: annual rainfall under 250 mm; between about 15° and 30° north and south of the equator, mainly on the west sides of continents and in continental interiors. Named deserts and continents (section 2). Map cue.
- [ ] A2 Causes of aridity (each its own bullet, explained): (1) subtropical high pressure: air that rose at the equator descends at about 30° (the Hadley cell), warming as it sinks, so clouds cannot form; (2) offshore trade winds that blow from the dry land interior; (3) cold ocean currents off west coasts cool the air above, causing fog but not rain, because the cooled lower air is stable (an inversion); (4) rain-shadow effect of mountains; (5) continentality: distance from the sea.
- [ ] A3 Temperature: very high daytime temperatures in summer (often over 40 °C); clear skies and dry air give intense insolation by day and rapid heat loss by night, so the diurnal (daily) range is very large and nights can be near freezing in winter; annual range larger than in the tropics.
- [ ] A4 Precipitation and other climate features: rain is scarce and unreliable; when it falls it often comes as short, intense convectional downpours causing flash floods; very high evaporation (potential evaporation far exceeds rainfall); low humidity; strong, hot, dusty winds (named winds optional, none checked); coastal fog deserts.
- [ ] A5 Landscape context (one bullet only): most of the desert is rock and stone, not sand: hamada (bare rock plateau), reg (gravel/stony plain), erg (sand sea with dunes); wadis are dry river beds. Aeolian landforms are taught in `geo-rocks-weathering-s6`; don't explain them.
- [ ] A6 Soils: aridisols. Thin and immature (weathering is slow without water, mainly physical); coarse, sandy or stony; little vegetation, so very low humus and a pale grey, yellow or red colour; little leaching; capillary rise brings salts and calcium carbonate to the surface (salinisation, calcification) forming salt crusts and lime hardpans (calcrete or caliche); alkaline; desert pavement where wind has removed fine particles. Fertile where irrigated, because nutrients have not been leached out; alluvial soils along exotic rivers such as the Nile are an azonal exception.
- [ ] A7 Vegetation pattern: sparse cover with much bare ground, plants widely spaced to reduce competition for water (some, like the creosote bush, release chemicals that discourage neighbours); low biomass and low diversity; concentration along wadis and at oases.
- [ ] A8 Plant adaptations (each with the named plant in section 2): xerophytes (plants adapted to drought); succulents store water in stems or leaves (cacti in the Americas, euphorbias in Africa look similar through convergent evolution); pleated or ribbed stems that swell after rain; spines in place of leaves to cut water loss and deter animals; thick, waxy cuticle; few, sunken stomata, which in many succulents open only at night (CAM photosynthesis); long tap roots reaching groundwater; shallow, widespread roots to catch brief rain; small or light-coloured leaves, or leaves shed in drought; ephemerals whose seeds lie dormant for years, then germinate, flower and seed within weeks after rain ("desert bloom"); halophytes tolerate salty soils; fog-harvesting plants in coastal deserts.
- [ ] A9 Animal adaptations (each with named animal in section 2): nocturnal or crepuscular activity and burrowing to escape the heat; obtaining water from food and from metabolism; producing very concentrated urine and dry droppings; storing fat; large ears to radiate heat; pale colours to reflect sunlight; fur on the soles of feet or broad padded feet for hot sand; estivation (dormancy through the hottest, driest period); fog collection; rapid breeding after rain.
- [ ] A10 Biotic inter-relationships in the desert: short food chains with few producers; bats and birds pollinating cacti; cactus holes used as nests by several species; seed-eating rodents as prey for snakes, owls and foxes.
- [ ] A11 European example: the Tabernas Desert, Almería, south-east Spain, a semi-arid area often called Europe's only desert (one bullet).
- [ ] A12 People in the desert (one bullet, adaptation only): nomadic herding (camels, goats) and oasis farming (date palms). Human alteration and desertification are in s5 and s3.

### Option B: the tropical rainforest biome
- [ ] B1 Definition and location: a dense, evergreen, broadleaved forest in the hot, wet equatorial climate; mainly between about 10° north and south of the equator; three main regions: the Amazon Basin, the Congo Basin and South-East Asia, plus smaller areas (section 2). Map cue.
- [ ] B2 Temperature: high all year, averaging about 27 °C, with a very small annual range (about 2–3 °C) because the sun is always high in the sky and day length barely changes; the daily range is greater than the annual range; no seasons in the temperate sense.
- [ ] B3 Rainfall and its cause: high (over 2,000 mm a year), falling throughout the year; convectional rain: intense morning heating makes moist air rise, cumulonimbus clouds build, and heavy afternoon thunderstorms follow, almost daily. The low-pressure belt of rising air along the equator (the ITCZ, or doldrums) drives this; some areas have a short drier season as the ITCZ shifts. Humidity is very high (often over 80%), and much of the rain is recycled by evapotranspiration from the forest itself.
- [ ] B4 Soils: latosols (from s2; one compact block here, not a repeat): deep, red/yellow, laterised, acidic, infertile, with little humus; the forest survives through a rapid, closed nutrient cycle: litter decays in weeks in the heat and humidity, and shallow roots and fungi (mycorrhizae) take up nutrients almost at once; most nutrients are stored in the living vegetation, not the soil.
- [ ] B5 Vegetation structure: layers (stratification), each with typical height and conditions: emergents rising above the canopy; the dense, continuous canopy that intercepts most sunlight and rain and holds most of the animal life; the understorey of young trees and palms; the shrub layer; the dark, humid forest floor where only a tiny fraction of sunlight arrives. Diagram cue: a labelled vertical profile.
- [ ] B6 Plant adaptations (each with named plant where given): buttress roots for support of tall trees on shallow roots; shallow roots to catch nutrients from litter; drip tips and waxy leaves shed heavy rain; large leaves in the understorey to capture dim light; thin, smooth bark (no need to insulate; epiphytes struggle to cling); evergreen, with leaves shed and grown all year; rapid growth upward into light gaps when a tree falls; lianas (woody climbers) rooted in the soil climb trees to the light; epiphytes (orchids, bromeliads, ferns) grow on branches and take moisture from the air and rain; strangler figs start as epiphytes and enclose their host; carnivorous and parasitic plants cope with nutrient-poor conditions.
- [ ] B7 Biodiversity: extremely high species diversity of plants and animals (figure in section 2); many tree species per hectare but few individuals of each.
- [ ] B8 Animal adaptations (each with named animal): most animals live in the canopy (arboreal): prehensile tails, long arms, gliding; camouflage; bright warning colours of poisonous animals; specialised beaks and diets; slow metabolism; nocturnal activity; strong swimmers in flooded forest.
- [ ] B9 Biotic inter-relationships: pollination by insects, birds and bats; seed dispersal by animals; mutualism (ants defending trees in return for shelter and food; leafcutter ants farming fungus); the Brazil nut example.
- [ ] B10 Indigenous people adapted to the forest (one bullet): hunting, gathering and shifting cultivation (slash and burn) at low density allowed the forest and soil to recover. Commercial felling is s5.
- [ ] B11 European link: there is no tropical rainforest in Europe; the syllabus's "European" setting is met by noting Europe's role in the trade (s5), not by inventing a European rainforest.

## 3. Traps (known errors)

- Don't use 58 °C at El Azizia (rejected in 2012) as the world record. The Death Valley figure is the official record.
- The Gobi and Patagonian deserts are **cold** deserts (cold winters); the Kalahari is largely semi-desert. Don't use them as typical hot deserts; use the Sahara, Namib, Atacama, Sonoran, Mojave or Arabian.
- A camel's hump stores **fat**, not water.
- Saguaros grow **only** in the Sonoran Desert; don't put them in the Sahara. Cacti are native only to the Americas (cacti in Africa or Australia are introduced); African "cactus-like" plants are euphorbias.
- Most of the Sahara is rock and gravel, not sand dunes.
- Desert soils are **infertile in humus** but can be fertile when irrigated; rainforest soils are **infertile** despite the lush forest. Explain both contrasts.
- Tropical rainforest daily range is **greater** than its annual range. Don't give "seasons" with summer and winter.
- Orangutans are not found in Africa or South America; gorillas and okapi are not in the Amazon; jaguars are not in Africa or Asia; toucans are South and Central American.
- Don't give a rate of rainforest loss or the story of its felling here: that is `geo-option-geoecology-s5`. Desertification and the Sahel belong to `geo-option-geoecology-s3`.
- Soil processes (laterisation, calcification, salinisation) are explained in s2; here describe the resulting soil and refer to the process by name.
- Climate types as climatic regions (the hot desert climate and others) are also in the Atmosphere–Ocean option (`geo-option-atmosphere-ocean`); here explain only what the biome needs.
- Do not copy climate-graph figures from past papers; invent illustrative values for BI4 and label them.

## 4. What exams ask (topics only)

- For one biome studied: account for the climate and explain how it affects soils and vegetation (asked in several recent years). Expected: causes of the climate, its characteristics, the soil it produces, the vegetation pattern and adaptations it causes.
- For one biome: describe its climatic and soil characteristics and their impact on vegetation development.
- For one biome: examine its characteristics with reference to any three of climate, soils, flora and fauna. Expected: three aspects, each in depth; fauna adaptations with named animals.
- Matching photographs or descriptions to biomes (e.g. tropical rainforest, hot desert) appears on Ordinary short questions; recognition from vegetation and landscape.

## 5. Practice questions to write

1. 80 marks: "With reference to one biome you have studied, account for its climate and explain how this climate affects its soils and vegetation." Model answer: hot desert only: causes of aridity, temperature and rainfall characteristics, aridisol formation, sparse vegetation and named plant adaptations. No animals beyond one bridging point.
2. 80 marks: "Examine the characteristics of one biome you have studied with reference to climate, soils and fauna." Model answer: tropical rainforest only: three aspects (equatorial climate and its cause; latosols and the nutrient cycle; fauna adaptations with named animals and biotic inter-relationships).
3. 80 marks: "Describe the climatic and soil characteristics of one biome and examine their impact on the development of vegetation." Model answer: tropical rainforest only, with vegetation layers and plant adaptations as the main body.
