## 1. Must cover (one bullet or block for every item; tick each off)

Why states have migration policies
- [ ] W1 Aims: control numbers; fill labour and skills shortages; meet humanitarian and legal duties to refugees; protect security; support integration. Policies balance economic need against public opinion.
- [ ] W2 Key distinctions: EU citizens (free movement) vs non-EU (third-country) nationals; economic migrants vs refugees and asylum seekers; refugee (1951 Refugee Convention definition: a well-founded fear of persecution for reasons of race, religion, nationality, membership of a particular social group or political opinion) vs asylum seeker (a person who has applied for protection and is awaiting a decision).
- [ ] W3 Why people seek asylum (exams ask for one): war and conflict; persecution for religion, ethnicity, political opinion or sexuality; human rights abuses by the state.
- [ ] W4 Why EU states attract asylum seekers and other migrants (pull factors to Europe): safety and the rule of law; respect for human rights; high living standards, jobs and welfare; existing family and diaspora communities; nearness to conflict zones in the Middle East and Africa.

Irish policy
- [ ] I1 The Common Travel Area with the UK: Irish and British citizens move, live and work freely between the two; reason Ireland is outside Schengen; kept after Brexit.
- [ ] I2 EU free movement: EU/EEA and Swiss citizens may live and work in Ireland without permits; Ireland's open labour market for the 2004 accession states (and a later, delayed opening for Bulgaria and Romania).
- [ ] I3 Employment permits for non-EU workers: a skills-based system; Critical Skills Employment Permit (for occupations in short supply, e.g. ICT, engineering, medicine; can lead to family reunification) and General Employment Permit; a list of ineligible occupations; a labour-market test. Purpose: fill gaps without displacing Irish/EU workers.
- [ ] I4 International protection (asylum): the Refugee Act 1996; the International Protection Act 2015 (a single application procedure). Applications rose sharply in 2022–23.
- [ ] I5 Direct provision: accommodation and meals in centres with a small weekly allowance, introduced in 2000; criticised for long stays, lack of privacy, unsuitable for children, isolation in rural centres, poor mental health; reforms (right to work after a waiting period from 2018); the 2021 White Paper promised to end it, but rising numbers stalled the plan; shortages of accommodation left some applicants unaccommodated.
- [ ] I6 Citizenship: the 2004 citizenship referendum ended automatic citizenship for anyone born in Ireland; naturalisation after a period of residence; citizenship ceremonies.
- [ ] I7 Refugee resettlement: the Irish Refugee Protection Programme (2015) to take refugees from Syria and elsewhere through EU relocation and UNHCR resettlement.
- [ ] I8 Temporary protection for people fleeing Ukraine (2022): immediate right to live, work and access services without an asylum claim; housing pressure; the contrast with international protection applicants.
- [ ] I9 Evaluation of Irish policy: open to EU and skilled workers, restrictive for low-skilled non-EU workers; asylum system slow and accommodation-based; tension between labour needs and housing pressure; anti-immigration protests (detail in `geo-population-migration-s5`).

EU policy
- [ ] E1 Free movement of workers (Treaty of Rome, 1957) and EU citizenship (Maastricht Treaty, 1992): EU citizens may live and work in any member state.
- [ ] E2 Schengen: internal border checks abolished; common external border and visa rules; Ireland not a member (Common Travel Area); temporary reintroduction of internal checks by some states after 2015.
- [ ] E3 The Dublin system: the member state where an asylum seeker first enters is responsible for the claim. Places most pressure on frontier states (Italy, Greece, Spain, Malta); "asylum shopping" is prevented in theory, but the rule broke down in 2015.
- [ ] E4 Frontex: the EU border and coast guard agency; patrols, returns, search and rescue role debated.
- [ ] E5 Legal migration: the EU Blue Card for highly skilled non-EU workers; seasonal worker rules; labour shortages and ageing populations (link to `geo-population-migration-s1`) as reasons to admit workers.
- [ ] E6 Recent trends driving policy (a 30-mark Higher question, asked twice): the 2015–16 migration crisis (war in Syria from 2011; Afghanistan, Iraq, Eritrea; arrivals by the Eastern Mediterranean route to Greece and the Balkan route, and by the Central Mediterranean route from Libya to Italy); deaths at sea; the Ukrainian refugee movement from 2022; continuing irregular arrivals.
- [ ] E7 Policy responses to those trends: Germany's open stance in 2015; the emergency relocation scheme (2015) and the refusal of Hungary, Poland and the Czech Republic to take part; Hungary's border fence (2015); the EU–Turkey statement (March 2016); externalisation (deals with Turkey and with Libya and other North African states); stronger Frontex; reintroduced internal border checks; first activation of the Temporary Protection Directive (2022); the Pact on Migration and Asylum (2024): faster border procedures and "mandatory solidarity" (states either accept relocated asylum seekers or pay into a fund).
- [ ] E8 Challenges for EU states from large numbers of asylum seekers (exams ask for two): cost of reception and housing; slow processing and backlogs; integration (language, jobs, schooling); unequal burden on frontier states; political division and the rise of anti-immigration parties; security screening; deaths at sea and smuggling gangs.
- [ ] E9 Evaluation: tension between humanitarian duty and border control; between solidarity and national sovereignty; Europe needs workers but faces public opposition.

## 3. Traps

- Ireland is in the EU but not in Schengen; the UK left the EU but the Common Travel Area continues. Don't say Ireland is in Schengen or that Brexit ended the Common Travel Area.
- Temporary protection (Ukrainians) is not the same as international protection (asylum). Don't count Ukrainians in asylum applications.
- The Dublin system is an EU asylum rule, not an Irish law; it was named after the city where the 1990 Convention was signed.
- Don't copy past-paper data (asylum applications by country of origin in 2016, migrants rescued by month); model answers include invented figures such as "Germany received 890,000 asylum applications in 2015" and "2.5 million applications in 2015" — do not use them. The checked EU figure is about 1.3 million applications in 2015.
- Don't claim the 2024 Pact is already fully in force in 2027 detail; say it applies from mid-2026, and describe its main ideas only.
- Labour shortages and ageing (why Europe needs migrants) are explained in `geo-population-migration-s1`; give one line here.
- Impacts of migration on Ireland belong to `geo-population-migration-s3`; racial and religious tensions to `-s5`. Refugees as a global human-rights issue belong to `geo-option-global-interdependence`.
