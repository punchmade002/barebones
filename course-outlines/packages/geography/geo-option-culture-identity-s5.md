# Package: geo-option-culture-identity-s5 — Nationality and the nation state

- **loId:** `geo-option-culture-identity-s5` · **chapterId:** `geo-option-culture-identity` · **syllabusRef:** 8.2
- **Syllabus:** Statement: "Nationality and the nation state are political entities placed on
  the physical and cultural landscape." Students should study the complex issues relating to
  "physical and political boundaries" (national setting: "The island of Ireland and
  partition."); "examples of cultural groups within nation states"; "examples of cultural
  groups without nationality"; "conflicts between political structures and cultural groups"

  "discuss nation states as political entities on the physical and cultural landscape";

## 1. Must cover (one bullet or block for every item; tick each off)

Concepts
- [ ] N1 Definitions: state (a sovereign political unit with territory, a permanent population, a government and recognition by other states); nation (a people who share a culture, history, and often language or religion, and see themselves as one community); nationality (membership of a nation, or legally, citizenship of a state); nation state (a state whose people largely form one nation, e.g. Iceland, Portugal, Japan); multinational state (several nations in one state, e.g. the UK, Belgium, Switzerland, Spain); stateless nation (a nation without its own state, e.g. the Kurds).
- [ ] N2 How nation states arose: in Europe from the 19th century nationalism (each nation should have its own state), the break-up of empires after the First World War and after 1989–91; elsewhere through decolonisation after 1945. Most states are not pure nation states.

Physical and political boundaries
- [ ] B1 Types of boundary: physical (following mountains, rivers, lakes, seas); geometric (straight lines, often lines of latitude); cultural (following language or religion); and by origin: antecedent (drawn before settlement), subsequent (drawn after, following cultural divisions), superimposed (imposed by outside powers regardless of culture, e.g. colonial Africa), relict (no longer functioning but still visible, e.g. the Berlin Wall's line).
- [ ] B2 Examples of physical boundaries: the Pyrenees (France–Spain), the Alps, the Rhine (part of the France–Germany border), the Rio Grande (USA–Mexico), the Himalayas. Physical boundaries often fit cultural divides but can also cut through a cultural group (Basques on both sides of the Pyrenees).
- [ ] B3 Political/geometric boundaries: the 49th parallel (USA–Canada); colonial borders in Africa drawn at and after the Berlin Conference that split ethnic groups and joined rival ones, a cause of later conflict and secession.
- [ ] B4 How boundaries affect identity (exams ask this): boundaries create a shared national identity within them (citizenship, schools, media, sport, symbols); split cultural groups and create minorities (partition, Kurds, Basques); can harden into barriers (Berlin Wall, Cyprus Green Line, Israel's West Bank barrier) or soften (Schengen, the open Irish border since 1998); boundary disputes can cause war; changing boundaries create new identities and new minorities.
- [ ] B5 The island of Ireland and partition (national setting): the island as a clear physical unit vs the political border; the 1920 Act, 1921 Treaty, 1925 Boundary Commission; the border follows old county boundaries, not physical features, and cuts through communities, farms, and the Erne and Foyle catchments; the six counties; the effects on identity (two states, two majorities, two minorities); Articles 2 and 3 and their 1998 change; the open border after 1998 and its importance after Brexit; Irish citizenship available to people born in Northern Ireland. Partition's detailed boundary history is also in `geo-region-european-s4`: keep facts identical and don't repeat at length.

Cultural groups within nation states
- [ ] W1 Why most states contain several cultural groups: historical conquest and union, boundaries drawn without regard to culture, and migration.
- [ ] W2 How states accommodate them (a range): federalism and autonomy (Belgium, Switzerland, Spain's autonomous communities); devolution (UK 1999); power-sharing (Northern Ireland, Lebanon); multiculturalism (Canada); assimilation (France); suppression (historically, Kurds in Turkey; today Uyghurs in China).
- [ ] W3 Spain: Basques and Catalans with their own languages, autonomy statutes and strong regional identities; independence movements (ETA; Catalonia 2017). Detailed facts here; the language policy is in s2.
- [ ] W4 The UK: England, Scotland, Wales and Northern Ireland; devolution in 1999; the Scottish independence referendum of 2014; Brexit referendum's different results in each part.
- [ ] W5 Canada and Quebec: French-speaking Quebec, the 1980 and 1995 referendums, recognition as a nation within Canada.
- [ ] W6 Belgium and Switzerland: name and point to s6 (full case studies there) and `geo-region-european-s3`; one bullet each.
- [ ] W7 Ireland: Irish Travellers as an indigenous ethnic minority (recognition 2017); new immigrant communities (Census 2022, from s1). One bullet each.
- [ ] W8 Indigenous peoples within states: the Sámi (Norway, Sweden, Finland, Russia) with their own parliaments; Aboriginal Australians and Māori (facts in s1).
- [ ] W9 China's minorities: Tibetans and Uyghurs (also cultural groups without nationality; see G4–G5).

Cultural groups without nationality (stateless nations)
- [ ] G1 Kurds (main case study): numbers and spread across Turkey, Iran, Iraq and Syria; the promise of the Treaty of Sèvres (1920) withdrawn at Lausanne (1923); suppression in Turkey and the PKK conflict, ending with the PKK's 2025 dissolution; Iraq (Halabja 1988, autonomous Kurdistan Region, the 2017 referendum); Syria (Kurdish-led forces against Islamic State, Kobani); identity (Kurdish language, Newroz festival).
- [ ] G2 Palestinians: a people without a fully sovereign state; 1948 and 1967 (facts in s3, use them, don't repeat); refugees in neighbouring states; partial self-rule; growing recognition of the State of Palestine (Ireland 2024).
- [ ] G3 Roma: Europe's largest ethnic minority, with no territory of their own; origins in India; spread across Europe; persecution (Holocaust) and continuing discrimination (segregated schooling).
- [ ] G4 Tibetans: Chinese control since 1950; the Dalai Lama in exile in India since 1959; restrictions on religion and language.
- [ ] G5 Uyghurs: Turkic-speaking Muslims in Xinjiang, China; mass detention since 2017; the UN's 2022 findings.
- [ ] G6 Basques and Catalans as nations without independent states that do have strong autonomy (contrast with the Kurds).
- [ ] G7 Responses of stateless groups: cultural survival (language, festivals, diaspora organisations), peaceful political campaigns, autonomy demands, armed struggle; outcomes range from autonomy (Iraqi Kurdistan, Catalonia) to repression (Tibet, Xinjiang).

Conflicts between political structures and cultural groups
- [ ] K1 General causes: boundaries that cut across cultural groups; a dominant group controlling the state; suppression of language and religion; economic inequality between regions or groups; competing claims to the same territory.
- [ ] K2 Northern Ireland (national setting; full case study): a state created around a unionist majority with a large nationalist minority; discrimination; civil rights; the Troubles; the Good Friday Agreement as a political structure redesigned for two cultural groups (power-sharing, consent principle, dual citizenship, cross-border bodies); Brexit's challenge to it. Use s3's Troubles facts and `geo-region-european-s3`'s Agreement facts; don't repeat them twice in the notes; frame them here as state vs cultural group.
- [ ] K3 The Basque Country: ETA's campaign (founding, deaths, ceasefire, dissolution) and Spain's political responses.
- [ ] K4 Catalonia: 2006 statute, the 2010 court ruling, the 2017 referendum and its aftermath (direct rule, trials, pardons, amnesty).
- [ ] K5 Former Yugoslavia: the break-up of a multinational state (details in s6; point there with one block here).
- [ ] K6 Other examples (one or two bullets each): Sri Lanka (Sinhalese state and Tamil separatists, 1983–2009); Chechnya (Russian wars 1994–96 and 1999–2009); Cyprus (division since 1974: facts in `geo-region-european-s4`); Kurds in Turkey (G1); Rwanda (s1).

In D.H. and Others v Czech Republic (2007) the European Court of Human Rights ruled that placing Roma children in special schools was discrimination. |

| G5 | Uyghurs: Turkic-speaking, mainly Muslim people of Xinjiang, north-west China. ## 3. Traps

- Nation ≠ state ≠ nation state. Ireland is close to a nation state but has a minority (Travellers) and new communities; the UK is a multinational state.
- Catalonia and the Basque Country are nations without independent states, but they have far more autonomy than the Kurds; don't equate their situations.
- Don't give a single precise Kurdish population; use "30 million or more". Don't use past-paper figures (e.g. "14 million in Turkey", "5 million in Iraq", "30–40 million", "1–1.5 billion people in stateless groups").
- Don't state current ceasefire or disarmament details for the PKK beyond the May 2025 dissolution announcement; don't predict outcomes.
- ETA killed "over 800"; don't give a more precise figure. Catalonia's 2017 result: always pair the 90% Yes with the 43% turnout.
- The Good Friday Agreement did not move the border. Don't say Catholics are a majority in Northern Ireland.
- The 2014 Scottish vote was No (about 55%); don't confuse it with the 2016 EU referendum.
- Overlaps: the partition boundary history and the Yugoslav, Cyprus and Polish boundary changes are in `geo-region-european-s4`; the present-day Northern Ireland interaction, Brexit and Belgium's interaction are in `geo-region-european-s3`. Here frame them as "political structures vs cultural groups" and keep facts identical. Belgium, Switzerland, the Balkans and the Celtic regions are full case studies in s6; one pointer bullet each here. Religious aspects of Northern Ireland and Israel–Palestine are in s3; don't repeat the event list, just use it.
- Keep the 2003 syllabus framing. Don't add the 2026 specification's "geopolitics" or "territorial disputes" (e.g. Russia–Ukraine, Crimea) as examples.
