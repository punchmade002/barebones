## 1. Must cover (one bullet or block for every item; tick each off)

What satellite imagery is
- [ ] B1 Remote sensing: gathering information about the earth's surface from a distance, without contact, by sensors on satellites or aircraft that record reflected or emitted energy (light, infrared, microwave/radar).
- [ ] B2 A satellite image is not a photograph in the ordinary sense: the sensor records energy in several wavebands as numbers for each pixel (picture element), and a computer turns them into an image. Resolution = the size of ground area one pixel covers; high resolution shows small features such as individual buildings, low resolution shows only large patterns such as cloud systems.
- [ ] B3 Geostationary satellites: orbit about 36,000 km above the equator at the same speed as the earth turns, so they stay over one point and view the same large area (a whole hemisphere) continuously; low detail; used for weather (e.g. Meteosat, operated by EUMETSAT, of which Ireland is a member).
- [ ] B4 Polar-orbiting satellites: much lower orbits (hundreds of km), passing near both poles while the earth turns beneath, so they cover the whole surface strip by strip over days; much greater detail; used for land use, mapping and environmental monitoring (e.g. Landsat, USA, since 1972; Sentinel satellites of the EU's Copernicus programme).
- [ ] B5 Image types, one bullet each: visible (reflected sunlight, like a photo; daytime only; thick cloud bright white); infrared (heat; works day and night; cold high cloud tops appear white, warm land and sea darker; used to show cloud height and sea temperature); false-colour (near-infrared shown as red, so healthy vegetation appears red and towns blue-grey; used for crops and forests); radar (sends microwaves and records the echo; sees through cloud and works at night, useful for floods in cloudy weather).
- [ ] B6 Satellite image vs aerial photograph: satellite images are taken from far higher (space vs an aircraft or drone), cover much larger areas, are repeated regularly on a schedule, and can record invisible wavebands; aerial photographs show more detail of a small area and are vertical or oblique. One comparison bullet pair. Aerial photo skills are in `geo-aerial-photographs-s1`.

Reading a satellite image (method)
- [ ] R1 Method steps: (1) read the caption, date and scale or area covered, and check whether it is true colour, false colour or infrared; (2) find north (usually the top) and any coastline or river to orient yourself; (3) identify features by colour/tone, texture, shape, size, pattern and association (what is next to what); (4) describe the distribution and pattern (e.g. linear, clustered, regular, along rivers or coasts); (5) explain using geographical knowledge.
- [ ] R2 Colour and tone in true colour: water dark blue to black (muddy or shallow water brown or turquoise); vegetation green (forest dark green, crops and grass lighter); bare ground and desert yellow, brown, orange or red; urban areas grey; snow, ice, cloud and salt flats white.
- [ ] R3 Telling cloud from snow and ice: cloud is fluffy, streaky or swirling and casts shadows, and moves between images; snow and ice follow the shape of the land (valleys, mountain ridges, coastline).

Recognition clues for features in matching questions (one bullet per feature; the clue only)
- [ ] C1 Depression / mid-latitude storm: a large comma-shaped or spiral band of white cloud with a long trailing cloud band (the front).
- [ ] C2 Hurricane (tropical cyclone): a huge circular spiral of thick white cloud with a clear, dark eye at the centre, over the ocean.
- [ ] C3 Anticyclone: a large cloud-free area (land and sea clearly visible).
- [ ] C4 Glacier: a long white or grey-blue river of ice in a valley between dark mountains, with dark stripes of moraine along it and cracked (crevassed) texture; may end in the sea or a lake with icebergs. Ice sheet: a vast, smooth white surface with no visible land.
- [ ] C5 Volcano and eruption: a circular cone with a crater at the summit, radial gullies down its sides; during an eruption a grey or white ash plume blown in one direction; dark lava flows spreading downslope.
- [ ] C6 Wildfire: grey or brown smoke plumes drifting downwind from bright red-orange fire fronts; afterwards a dark brown or black burn scar among green vegetation.
- [ ] C7 Flooding: brown, muddy water spread over land outside the river channel, covering fields and roads; compare with a before image.
- [ ] C8 Hot desert: yellow, orange or red-brown surface, no vegetation, sand dune patterns (ripples or ridges), dry river beds.
- [ ] C9 Irrigated agriculture in a dry region: bright green geometric fields (often perfect circles from centre-pivot irrigation, or rectangles) sharply set against brown desert; or a green strip along a river.
- [ ] C10 Commercial crop farming in temperate lowlands: a regular chequerboard of rectangular fields of different colours (crops at different stages), straight roads on a grid.
- [ ] C11 Forest: uniform dark green texture; deforestation shows as light-brown cleared patches, often in a "fishbone" pattern along new roads.
- [ ] C12 Urban area: grey or purple-grey patch with a fine texture, street grid, roads and railways converging; parks green; an airport shows as long straight runways (often crossing) with large paved areas and terminal buildings.
- [ ] C13 Coastal features: spits, tombolos and sandbars show as narrow pale strips of sand joining or extending from the coast; deltas as fan-shaped land with branching channels and sediment plumes in the sea; islands, bays and headlands by outline. Formation of coastal landforms is in `geo-sea-s1`; name the feature here.
- [ ] C14 Large landforms: river valleys and meanders; mountain ranges (ridges, shadows, snow); canyons (deep, branching, steep-sided valleys in bare rock); salt flats and salt pans (dazzling white geometric pans in deserts).
- [ ] C15 Night-time lights images: bright areas show cities and population density; dark areas are sparsely populated.

Uses of satellite imagery (the "state one use" question; each use one bullet with what it shows)
- [ ] U1 Weather forecasting: tracking depressions, fronts and cloud, issuing storm warnings; tracking hurricanes so coasts can be evacuated. Met Éireann uses satellite data alongside weather charts (`geo-weather-climate-s1`).
- [ ] U2 Mapping and updating maps of inaccessible or remote areas, and changes such as new roads and housing.
- [ ] U3 Monitoring agriculture: crop health and yields, irrigation, land use change, checking farm area claims. Linked to the GIS study of changing agricultural land use in `geo-weather-climate-s3`.
- [ ] U4 Monitoring environmental change: deforestation, desertification, shrinking lakes, glacier and ice-sheet retreat, sea ice extent, sea surface temperature.
- [ ] U5 Natural hazards: tracking volcanic ash plumes, mapping earthquake and flood damage to direct emergency relief, monitoring wildfires.
- [ ] U6 Pollution: oil spills at sea, smog, algal blooms.
- [ ] U7 Urban growth and planning: measuring urban sprawl over time (link to `geo-weather-climate-s3`).
- [ ] U8 Navigation and positioning (GPS/Galileo satellites) and communications: one bullet, noting these are not imaging satellites.
- [ ] U9 Military and security surveillance: one bullet.

Using satellite imagery with the core and elective units (syllabus requirement: one bullet each, saying what an image shows)
- [ ] L1 Physical landscape: plate boundaries and fold mountains (long parallel ranges), volcanoes, rift valleys, glaciated valleys and ice sheets, river basins and deltas, coastlines.
- [ ] L2 Regions: images of Ireland and Europe show regional contrasts, e.g. mountainous, wetter, greener west vs flatter, more cultivated east; cloud cover patterns. One bullet; regional detail is in the regional chapters.
- [ ] L3 Human landscape: settlement patterns, urban growth, transport routes, field patterns, land use change, deforestation.

Advantages and limitations
- [ ] A1 Advantages: cover very large areas in one image; regular repeat coverage shows change over time; reach remote and dangerous areas; record invisible wavebands (infrared, radar); fast, so useful in emergencies; archives go back decades.
- [ ] A2 Limitations: cloud blocks visible and infrared images; limited resolution on many images; need skilled interpretation and software; no names or labels; relief is hard to judge on a flat image unless shadows show it; some high-resolution imagery is costly.

## 3. Traps

- **Never use the images or captions from past papers** (the specific glaciers, volcanoes,
  hurricanes, deserts, farming areas, wildfires, floods and landmarks matched in exams). Teach
  the recognition clues; don't name the exam's examples.
- Geostationary = high, fixed over the equator, weather, low detail. Polar-orbiting = low,
  moving, detailed, land use. Students often swap them.
- In standard false colour, healthy vegetation is **red**, not green.
- White can be cloud, snow, ice or salt: use shape, texture and location to decide.
- A satellite image is not an aerial photograph: don't apply the oblique-photo nine-area grid or
  sketch-map rules from `geo-aerial-photographs-s1` unless the question asks for location on a
  vertical image (then use left/centre/right with top/centre/bottom or compass directions).
- GPS satellites are for positioning, not imaging.
- Don't give Aral Sea, Amazon or glacier-retreat percentages or areas: none are checked.
- How hurricanes and depressions form belongs to `geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s4`; how volcanoes,
  glaciers, deltas and coastal landforms form belongs to their own chapters. Here give only
  what the image shows.
- GIS (combining satellite images with other data layers) is `geo-weather-climate-s3`. Mention it
  in one sentence at most.

## 4. What exams ask (topics only)

- Ordinary Level matching questions: four or five satellite images matched to descriptions of a
  glacier, a hurricane, a volcano erupting, a desert, irrigated or crop farming, a wildfire, a
  flood, a coastal feature (e.g. a tombolo), a canyon, a famous built landmark, an airport
  runway, salt production, autumn forest colours, islands. Answers depend on the recognition
  clues in C1–C15.
- "State one use of satellite images", with expected answers such as weather forecasting,
  mapping, monitoring crops, deforestation or hazards.
- Follow-on true/false items about a feature shown (e.g. the process that forms it, or what
  irrigation is). Irrigation = the artificial supply of water to crops.
- Higher Level: satellite images used as a source alongside other questions (locating features on
  a satellite image beside an aerial photograph is taught in `geo-aerial-photographs-s1`).

## 5. Practice questions to write

1. 5 marks: "Match each described satellite image to the feature it shows." The writer invents
   five short image descriptions built from the clues (e.g. "a white spiral of cloud with a dark
   centre over the ocean"; "bright green circles in a brown landscape"; "a long white river of
   ice with dark stripes between mountains"; "a grey cone with a plume drifting east";
   "brown water spread across fields beside a river") and five features (hurricane, centre-pivot
   irrigation, glacier, erupting volcano, flood). Model answer: the five matches, each with the
   clue that decides it. Label the descriptions illustrative.
2. 6 marks: "Explain three uses of satellite imagery." Model answer: exactly three uses (weather
   forecasting; monitoring deforestation, using the Rondônia fishbone pattern; disaster response
   mapping), each with what is seen and why it helps; about 2 SRPs each.
3. 4 marks: "Distinguish between geostationary and polar-orbiting satellites." Model answer: the
   height, coverage, detail and main use of each.

Each model answer uses only section 2 facts or invented, labelled descriptions.
