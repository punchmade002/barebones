## 1. Must cover (one bullet or block for every item; tick each off)

Resources: the ideas
- [ ] R1 A resource is anything in the environment that people use to meet their needs (energy, minerals, water, soil, forests, fish, land, scenery).
- [ ] R2 Non-renewable (stock, finite) resources: formed over millions of years, so they are used up faster than they form and will run out: coal, oil, natural gas, peat, metal ores, uranium.
- [ ] R3 Renewable (flow) resources: replaced naturally and will not run out however much is used: wind, solar, hydro (running water), wave, tidal, geothermal heat.
- [ ] R4 Renewable only if managed sustainably: forests, fish stocks, soils, groundwater, biomass. Overuse turns them into exhausted resources (link: fish-stock depletion is taught in "Sustainable economic development").
- [ ] R5 Fossil fuels: coal, oil, natural gas and peat, formed from the remains of plants or marine organisms buried and compressed over millions of years; burning them releases the carbon they stored. One line on formation only (it is taught in "Human interaction with the rock cycle").
- [ ] R6 Peat is classed as non-renewable: a bog grows only about 1 mm a year, far slower than it is cut.
- [ ] R7 Nuclear energy (uranium) is an alternative to fossil fuels but is not renewable.
- [ ] R8 Primary energy (energy in its raw form: oil, gas, wind) vs electricity (a secondary form made from primary sources). Renewables give a far larger share of electricity than of total energy.
- [ ] R9 Why resources matter to the economy: energy powers industry, transport, farming and homes; a country that imports its energy pays a large import bill and is exposed to price shocks and supply cuts (energy security).

Studied area 1: Ireland's national energy resources (a full block for each source)
- [ ] I1 Overview: Ireland has few fossil fuels of its own and imports most of its energy (import dependence, section 2); oil is the largest single source of primary energy, then natural gas; renewables are growing.
- [ ] I2 Oil: all imported; used mainly for transport and home heating; Whitegate refinery.
- [ ] I3 Natural gas: Kinsale Head (now depleted) and Corrib; most gas now imported from Britain by interconnector pipeline; gas is the largest source of electricity.
- [ ] I4 Coal: small native coalfields long closed (Arigna, Castlecomer); imported coal burned at Moneypoint, now ended.
- [ ] I5 Peat: Bord na Móna, peat-fired power stations in the Midlands, closure of peat harvesting and of the peat stations; Edenderry moved to biomass; cutaway bogs reused (wind farms, wetlands, parks).
- [ ] I6 Hydroelectric power: Ardnacrusha (Shannon Scheme), other stations on the Liffey, Lee and Erne; Turlough Hill pumped storage; little potential left for large new schemes.
- [ ] I7 Wind: Ireland's main renewable; strong, frequent Atlantic winds, best on western coasts and uplands; onshore wind farms; offshore wind (Arklow Bank) and its planned growth.
- [ ] I8 Solar, wave, tidal, geothermal and biomass in Ireland: solar growing fast; wave-energy test site off Belmullet; geothermal limited to low-temperature heat pumps; biomass and biofuels.
- [ ] I9 Ireland's energy targets: the 2020 EU renewable target (16%) was missed and why; Climate Action Plan targets for 2030 (80% renewable electricity, offshore wind); Climate Act targets.
- [ ] I10 Reasons Ireland lagged behind other EU countries on renewables (exam topic): heavy dependence on cars and road freight; oil for home heating in a dispersed rural settlement pattern; slow planning and grid connection for wind; no large hydro potential left; late start on offshore wind; strong economic and population growth raising demand.
- [ ] I11 Interconnection and energy security: East–West Interconnector to Wales; Celtic Interconnector to France; gas pipelines from Scotland.
- [ ] I12 Consumption by sector in Ireland: transport the largest energy user, then heat in homes and businesses, then electricity generation for industry and services; data centres a fast-growing user of electricity.

Studied area 2: European energy production and consumption
- [ ] E1 The EU imports more than half of its energy (section 2); main suppliers of oil and gas; Norway (not an EU member) as a major gas supplier; the North Sea (UK and Norway) as Europe's main oil and gas province.
- [ ] E2 Russia and energy security: heavy EU dependence on Russian gas before 2022; the 2022 invasion of Ukraine, price spike and REPowerEU response (cut Russian imports, more LNG, more renewables, saving energy).
- [ ] E3 Contrasting national energy mixes (a short block each): France (nuclear), Germany (coal including lignite, the Energiewende, nuclear phase-out), Poland (coal), Denmark (wind), Norway (hydro), Iceland (geothermal and hydro).
- [ ] E4 EU renewable targets: 20% by 2020 (achieved), 42.5% by 2030; current share about a quarter.
- [ ] E5 Patterns of consumption by sector in the EU: transport, households and industry are each a large share (roughly a quarter to a third each); services smaller. Fossil fuels still supply most of the EU's energy.
- [ ] E6 Production vs consumption: core industrial countries (Germany, France) consume most; resource-rich countries (Norway) export; peripheral countries such as Ireland depend on imports.

## 3. Traps

- Peat is non-renewable. Biomass (wood, energy crops) is renewable. Never call peat renewable.
- Nuclear is an alternative, low-carbon source but not renewable (uranium is finite).
- Renewables' share of electricity (about two-fifths) is very different from their share of all energy (a little over a tenth). Never mix them up.
- Kinsale Head stopped producing in 2020. Do not describe it as a working gas field.
- Moneypoint burned coal, not peat; it stopped burning coal in 2025. Lanesborough and Shannonbridge were the peat stations.
- Ireland has no nuclear power station; the reasons belong in "Burning fossil fuels and using alternative energy sources".
- Norway and Iceland are not EU members. Say "European", not "EU", when using them.
- Do not use past-paper figures (SEAI charts of Ireland's energy mix, renewables in 2017, Iceland's percentage, the EU-28 energy breakdown). Use only the rounded figures above.
- Kinsale and Corrib formation and extraction are taught in `geo-rocks-weathering-s2`; give one line each here. Do not repeat the Corrib conflict, which belongs in "Conflicts between economic interests and environmental interests".
- Advantages and disadvantages of each alternative energy source and the impacts of burning fossil fuels belong in "Burning fossil fuels and using alternative energy sources". This subsection describes the resources and their place in the economy.
- Fishing as an economic activity belongs in `geo-economic-activities`; depletion of fish stocks belongs in "Sustainable economic development". Give fish only as an example of a renewable-if-managed resource.

## 4. What exams ask (topics only)

- Data questions (Ordinary, often Higher short questions): read an energy-mix chart for Ireland or the EU; which fuel is largest; the renewable share; add up the fossil-fuel share. Notes need the definitions and the general pattern so students can interpret such charts.
- Definitions: fossil fuel; renewable energy; non-renewable resource.
- Name two renewable energy sources.
- Explain why Ireland was furthest from its 2020 renewable target (Higher short question).
- Explain one reason for the growth of wind energy in Ireland (Atlantic winds, targets, falling costs, grants and supports, energy security).
- 30 marks: how renewable energy development is crucial for Ireland's environment and economy (shared with the next subsection: import bill, energy security, jobs, targets, emissions).
