# Package: geo-option-culture-identity-s4 — Everyday expressions of culture and identity

- **loId:** `geo-option-culture-identity-s4` · **chapterId:** `geo-option-culture-identity` · **syllabusRef:** 8.1
- **Syllabus:** Students should study "everyday expressions of culture and identity, e.g.
  sports, traditions, costume, food, music, art and festivals." National setting: "Irish
  examples." International setting: "Appropriate European examples."
- **Level and exam form:** Higher Level only option. 80-mark essays (about four aspects, about
  25–30 SRPs). "How people express their culture and identity in everyday life" is set often,
  once restricted to Irish examples, so the Irish material must carry a whole essay alone and the
  European material a second one.
- **Prepared by:** a higher model, 2026-10-20. The writer works only from this package and
  `course-outlines/NOTES_STANDARD.md`.

## 1. Must cover (one bullet or block for every item; tick each off)

Ideas
- [ ] X1 Culture (a group's shared, learned way of life: beliefs, customs, language, arts, food, dress) and identity (a person's or group's sense of who they are and where they belong). Everyday expressions are the visible, practised parts of culture that people choose, repeat and pass on.
- [ ] X2 Why everyday expressions matter: they mark belonging; they pass culture between generations; they assert identity when a culture is under pressure (e.g. Irish games and music during the Gaelic Revival); they attract tourists and income; they can also mark division (parades in Northern Ireland; football rivalries).
- [ ] X3 Pressures on everyday culture: globalisation and Americanisation (fast food, Hollywood, global sports and brands, Halloween customs returning from the USA in a commercial form), which can make cultures more alike; and the responses (revival movements, UNESCO listing of intangible cultural heritage, State support).
- [ ] X4 Migration brings new everyday expressions (food, festivals, religious celebrations, sports) and blends them with existing ones (multicultural festivals, fusion food).

Irish examples (full depth for each heading)
- [ ] S1 Sport: the GAA (founding, aims, amateur and parish/county structure, Croke Park, hurling's UNESCO status, camogie and ladies' football); the GAA's historic ban rules and their removal; Croke Park's 1920 Bloody Sunday and its 2007 opening to rugby and soccer; sport and identity elsewhere (the all-island rugby team; Northern Ireland's divided sporting loyalties).
- [ ] S2 Music and dance: traditional music (fiddle, uilleann pipes, tin whistle, bodhrán; sessions); sean-nós singing and dancing; Comhaltas Ceoltóirí Éireann and the Fleadh Cheoil; Riverdance (1994) and the global popularity of Irish dance; uilleann piping's UNESCO status; Irish popular music with traditional roots.
- [ ] S3 Festivals and traditions: St Patrick's Day (a global expression of Irish identity in the diaspora); Brigid's Day (public holiday since 2023); Samhain and Halloween; Puck Fair; Lisdoonvarna matchmaking festival; Rose of Tralee; Bloomsday; Ballinasloe Horse Fair (also important to Travellers); arts festivals (Galway, Wexford Opera); wakes, holy wells and pattern days as older traditions.
- [ ] S4 Costume and craft: the Aran sweater; Irish dancing costumes; the Claddagh ring; GAA jerseys as everyday dress showing county identity.
- [ ] S5 Food and drink: traditional dishes (soda bread, Irish stew, colcannon, boxty, coddle, the full Irish breakfast); Guinness; changes through immigration and globalisation.
- [ ] S6 Art and literature: early Christian art (Book of Kells, high crosses); Newgrange; Irish writers and Nobel laureates; the Irish language in literature and song.
- [ ] S7 Irish Travellers as a group with distinct everyday culture: nomadism, horse fairs, their own language (Cant/Gammon/Shelta), extended family ties; ethnic recognition 2017 (fact in s1).
- [ ] S8 Northern Ireland: parades (Orange Order marching season, bonfires on 11 July) and murals as expressions of unionist and nationalist identity; flags and kerb painting marking territory. Brief: the conflict itself is in s3.

European examples (each with facts)
- [ ] E1 Spain: festivals (San Fermín, La Tomatina, Semana Santa); flamenco (Andalusia); Catalan castells (human towers) and regional identity; football and identity (FC Barcelona and Catalonia; Athletic Bilbao's Basque-only player policy); bullfighting and its bans; food (tapas, paella).
- [ ] E2 Scotland: kilts and tartan, bagpipes, Highland Games, Burns Night, Hogmanay, haggis; Celtic and Rangers as sporting expressions of religious/national identity in Glasgow.
- [ ] E3 Germany: Oktoberfest (Munich), lederhosen and dirndl, Christmas markets; Turkish immigrants' influence (döner kebab).
- [ ] E4 France: Bastille Day, the Tour de France, food (the French gastronomic meal on UNESCO's list), the Cannes Film Festival, laïcité affecting religious dress (link to s3).
- [ ] E5 Italy: the Palio di Siena, the Venice Carnival, regional food (pizza from Naples, pasta), football.
- [ ] E6 Others to choose from (one bullet each): Norway's Constitution Day and the bunad costume; the Netherlands' King's Day in orange; Brittany's Festival Interceltique de Lorient and fest-noz; Wales's Eisteddfod; Switzerland's and the Balkans' festivals are in s6 (point there).
- [ ] E7 The Eurovision Song Contest as a European-wide cultural event that also expresses national identity and politics (Ireland's seven wins).

## 2. Checked facts and examples (the only named places, dates and figures you may use)

| X3 | McDonald's opened its first Irish restaurant on Grafton Street, Dublin, in 1977. UNESCO's list of the Intangible Cultural Heritage of Humanity (convention of 2003) records living traditions; Irish entries include uilleann piping (2017), hurling (2018) and Irish harping (2019). |
| S1 | Gaelic Athletic Association founded on 1 November 1884 in Hayes' Hotel, Thurles, Co. Tipperary, by Michael Cusack and others (Maurice Davin was first president), to preserve and promote native Irish games. Players are amateurs; clubs are based on parishes and teams on counties, so the GAA reinforces local and county identity. The GAA is organised on an all-island basis and has clubs among the diaspora. Croke Park, Dublin, holds about 82,300, one of Europe's largest stadiums. Bloody Sunday, 21 November 1920: British forces opened fire at a Gaelic football match in Croke Park, killing 14 people. The ban on GAA members playing "foreign games" (soccer, rugby, cricket, hockey) was removed in 1971; the rule barring members of the British security forces was removed in 2001. Croke Park hosted rugby and soccer internationals from 2007 while Lansdowne Road was rebuilt; England played Ireland in rugby there in February 2007. Camogie Association founded 1904. The Ireland rugby team represents the whole island. |
| S2 | Comhaltas Ceoltóirí Éireann founded 1951 to promote traditional music; the first Fleadh Cheoil na hÉireann was held in Mullingar in 1951 and it is now held in a different town each August, drawing hundreds of thousands of visitors. Riverdance began as the interval act of the Eurovision Song Contest in Dublin on 30 April 1994 and became a worldwide touring show. Sean-nós ("old style"): unaccompanied traditional singing in Irish, strongest in Conamara. Traditional instruments: fiddle, uilleann pipes, tin whistle, flute, concertina, accordion, bodhrán, harp. The harp is Ireland's national symbol (on coins and the presidential seal). |
| S3 | St Patrick's Day, 17 March; Dublin's St Patrick's Festival began in 1996; parades are held across the world, e.g. New York, and landmarks are lit green. St Brigid's Day (Lá Fhéile Bríde, around 1 February) became a public holiday in 2023, the first named after a woman. Samhain (31 October–1 November) is the Celtic origin of Halloween. Puck Fair, Killorglin, Co. Kerry: every August (10–12 August), a wild goat is crowned king. Lisdoonvarna Matchmaking Festival, Co. Clare: every September. Rose of Tralee, Co. Kerry: since 1959, open to women of Irish birth or descent worldwide. Bloomsday, 16 June: celebrates James Joyce's Ulysses, set on 16 June 1904. Ballinasloe Horse Fair, Co. | S4 | The Aran sweater: knitted wool jumper from the Aran Islands, Co. Galway. The Claddagh ring (hands, heart and crown) is named after the Claddagh area of Galway city. |
| S5 | Guinness: brewed at St James's Gate, Dublin, since 1759. Coddle is a Dublin dish; boxty a potato pancake associated with the north midlands and Connacht. Census 2022: Polish is the most common home language after English and Irish, and Polish shops and food are now common in Irish towns. |

Meath): passage tomb built around 3200 BC, a UNESCO World Heritage Site; sunlight enters its chamber at the winter solstice. Irish Nobel Prize winners for Literature: W. B. | S7 | Travellers recognised as an ethnic minority on 1 March 2017; about 33,000 Travellers (Census 2022). Their own language is known as Cant, Gammon or Shelta. |
| S8 | The Orange Order parades on 12 July to commemorate the Battle of the Boyne (1690); bonfires are lit on the night of 11 July in loyalist areas. Painted gable-end murals in Belfast and Derry show loyalist and republican identities; kerbstones are painted red, white and blue or green, white and orange to mark territory. |
| E1 | San Fermín, Pamplona, Navarre: 6–14 July, with the running of the bulls through the streets. La Tomatina, Buñol, near Valencia: a tomato fight on the last Wednesday of August, since 1945. Semana Santa (Holy Week): religious processions, especially in Seville. Flamenco (song, guitar and dance from Andalusia): on UNESCO's list since 2010. Castells (human towers), Catalonia: on UNESCO's list since 2010. FC Barcelona's motto "Més que un club" ("More than a club") reflects its role as a symbol of Catalan identity, especially under Franco. Athletic Bilbao fields only players born or raised in the Basque Country (or trained in Basque clubs). Catalonia's parliament banned bullfighting in 2010 (in force 2012); Spain's Constitutional Court overturned the ban in 2016. Paella comes from Valencia. |

Highland Games: traditional sports (caber toss, hammer throw) with piping and Highland dancing. Celtic FC (founded 1887 in Glasgow's East End by Brother Walfrid, a Marist brother, to raise money for poor Irish Catholic immigrants) and Rangers (founded 1872, traditionally supported by Protestants/unionists): their rivalry, the "Old Firm", carries religious and Irish/British identity. |
| E3 | Oktoberfest, Munich, Bavaria: held since 1810 (it began with a royal wedding); visitors wear lederhosen and dirndl. Christmas markets in German cities. The döner kebab became popular in Germany through Turkish immigrants. |

| E5 | Palio di Siena: a horse race around the Piazza del Campo between the city's districts (contrade), held on 2 July and 16 August. Venice Carnival, with its masks, before Lent. Neapolitan pizza-making was added to UNESCO's list in 2017. |
| E6 | Norway's Constitution Day, 17 May (constitution of 1814): children's parades and the bunad (regional folk costume). ## 3. Traps

- Don't call hurling "Ireland's national sport" by law; it is a cultural symbol. The GAA's 1971 and 2001 rule changes are the only rule-change dates checked; don't add Rule 42 details.
- Bloody Sunday 1920 (Croke Park, 14 killed) ≠ Bloody Sunday 1972 (Derry, 13 killed, in s3). Keep them separate.
- Don't present Halloween as an American invention; it comes from the Irish/Celtic Samhain, and modern commercial customs returned from the USA.
- Eurovision: give only Ireland's seven wins and the 1992–94 run; don't claim Ireland holds the record outright (Sweden has also won seven times).
- Don't give visitor numbers or economic values for any festival (none checked), and don't take any from past-paper answers (e.g. "cultural tourism ~20%+ of earnings").
- Don't use unchecked costume claims such as Scottish clan tartans dating from ancient times.
- Overlaps: language as an expression of identity is s2; religious practice, diet and dress rules belong to s3 (one cross-reference bullet only here); Swiss, Balkan, Welsh, Breton and Belgian festivals and music are part of the case studies in s6, so only name them here. Travellers' ethnic recognition is stated in s1.

## 4. What exams ask (topics only)

- 80 marks (set repeatedly): how people express their culture and identity in everyday life; once restricted to Irish examples. Expected aspects: sport (GAA in depth), music and dance, festivals and traditions, food and costume, plus art; with Irish and European examples and comment on globalisation and migration.
- Ordinary-level regional questions ask how religion, language, sport and music show the culture of a region; the same material serves.

## 5. Practice questions to write

1. 80 marks, Higher: "Examine how people express their culture and identity in everyday life, with reference to Irish examples." Model answer: four aspects: sport (S1), music and dance (S2), festivals and traditions (S3), and costume, food and art (S4–S6); one closing aspect point on globalisation or Travellers. About 25–30 SRPs. Irish examples only.
2. 80 marks, Higher: "Examine how people express their culture and identity in everyday life, with reference to European examples." Model answer: Spain (festivals, flamenco, castells, football and regional identity) and Scotland (dress, festivals, the Old Firm), with Germany or France as a third; one point on globalisation. About 25–30 SRPs.
3. 20 marks: "Explain how sport can express cultural identity, with reference to examples." Model answer: GAA (founding aims, parish and county identity, Bloody Sunday 1920, the 1971 and 2001 changes), FC Barcelona and Athletic Bilbao, Celtic and Rangers.
