## 1. Must cover (one bullet or block for every item; tick each off)

Site and situation of urban settlements
- [ ] U1 Site characteristics of towns: bridging point or ford; route focus (several roads meet); river confluence; sheltered harbour or estuary head; defensive site (hill, river loop, island); dry point; resources. Situation: centre of a fertile farming area, on a coast facing trade partners, at a gap through uplands. One block, each with a checked Irish example where one exists. (The historic reasons for sites are in s1; don't repeat their history.)
- [ ] U2 Urban functions: residential, market and retail, services (health, education, administration), industrial, port, transport, tourism/recreation, ecclesiastical, defence/garrison, commuter (dormitory) towns. Most towns are multi-functional.

Urban hierarchy
- [ ] H1 Definition: settlements ranked by size and by the number and range of their functions/services: hamlet → village → small town → large town → city → capital (primate) city. Few large centres, many small ones (pyramid).
- [ ] H2 Ireland's urban hierarchy with Census 2022 figures: Dublin; the regional cities Cork, Limerick, Galway, Waterford; the largest towns (Drogheda, Dundalk, Swords); many smaller county and market towns; villages.
- [ ] H3 Primate city: a city much larger than the second city and dominant in functions. Dublin is a primate city: about six times the size of Cork. The rank-size rule (the nth city is 1/n the size of the largest) does not fit Ireland. Causes of Dublin's primacy: capital, port, routeway focus, colonial administration, multinationals, services; NSS and NPF try to balance it (point to s6).
- [ ] H4 Order of goods and services: low-order (convenience: bread, newspapers; found in every village, bought often, low threshold) vs high-order (comparison goods and specialised services: jewellers, department stores, hospitals, universities; high threshold, found only in large centres).

Hinterland
- [ ] N1 Hinterland (sphere of influence, catchment area, umland): the surrounding area a town serves and draws people from. Size depends on the town's size and functions, transport, competition from nearby centres.
- [ ] N2 How a hinterland is measured: newspaper circulation, bus routes and timetables, school or hospital catchments, shop customer surveys, delivery areas, commuter flows, sports club catchments.
- [ ] N3 Hinterlands overlap and change (cars, motorways and online shopping widen them; bypasses and out-of-town centres change them). Example: the large hinterland of Galway city across the West.

Central place theory
- [ ] C1 Walter Christaller (1933), from a study of southern Germany. A central place is a settlement providing goods and services to people in its surrounding area.
- [ ] C2 Threshold (the minimum number of customers needed to keep a service in business) and range (the maximum distance people will travel to obtain it). A service locates where its threshold falls within its range.
- [ ] C3 Assumptions: a flat, uniform (isotropic) plain; evenly spread population with equal purchasing power; equal ease of transport in all directions; consumers buy at the nearest centre; no excess profits.
- [ ] C4 Predictions: hexagonal market areas (circles overlap or leave gaps, so hexagons fit); a hierarchy of centres: many small, closely spaced low-order centres; fewer, more widely spaced high-order centres; each higher-order centre also supplies all lower-order goods. Nesting (k = 3 marketing principle; k = 4 transport, k = 7 administrative, one bullet).
- [ ] C5 Evidence that supports it: the regular spacing of market towns serving farming areas in parts of the Irish Midlands (describe, no distances); the Dutch polders as an almost perfect planned example (Noordoostpolder: Emmeloord and its ring of villages, a planned two-level hierarchy).
- [ ] C6 Limitations and criticisms: relief, rivers and coasts are not uniform; population is unevenly spread; transport routes are linear, not equal in all directions; people shop on the way to work or multi-purpose trips, not always at the nearest centre; car ownership, motorways, out-of-town shopping centres and online shopping change ranges and thresholds; government decisions (e.g. county towns, new towns) and history (Viking and Norman towns) fix locations; Dublin's primacy breaks the regular hierarchy; theory designed for market (service) towns, not industrial, port or tourist towns.
- [ ] C7 Applying it to Ireland: Dublin at the top, regional cities next, county/market towns, villages; the Irish hierarchy is broadly hierarchical but distorted by primacy and the coastline.

How functions and services change over time (case studies; write each as its own block)
- [ ] F1 General trends: defence functions lost; market and fair functions declined (marts, supermarkets); industrial functions shifted from inner-city to suburbs or closed; growth of services, offices, tourism, education, health; many towns near cities became commuter towns; village services (post office, Garda station, pub, shop, school) closing.
- [ ] F2 Case study: **Galway** (main case study): from Norman walled port and merchant trading town, through decline, to a university city, high-tech manufacturing (medical devices), tourism and culture centre and the regional capital of the West.
- [ ] F3 Case study: **Limerick**: Viking settlement, Norman castle and walled city, Georgian commercial port, industrial city (port, flour milling, then multinationals linked to Shannon), decline of older industry and the Dell closure, education (University of Limerick), regeneration and culture.
- [ ] F4 Case study: **Dublin** in summary only (Viking trading port → administrative capital → Georgian city → port and industry → services and tech in the Docklands). Point to `geo-region-ireland-s1` for detail; don't repeat it.
- [ ] F5 Small-town example: market town turning into a commuter town (describe; commuter towns of the GDA named in `geo-region-ireland-s1`, e.g. Navan, Naas).

Reasons for the growth of an urban area (exam asks this as a stand-alone Ordinary 30-mark question)
- [ ] G1 A short checklist that uses the case studies: site and situation, route focus and port, administrative role, industry and multinationals, services and education, tourism, government policy (NSS gateways, NPF), in-migration. Point students to Galway or Limerick here, and to Dublin in `geo-region-ireland-s1`.

## 3. Traps

- **Threshold ≠ range.** Threshold is a number of people (minimum demand); range is a distance. Students swap them.
- Christaller, **1933**, **southern Germany**. Don't attribute the theory to Burgess, Hoyt or Lösch (Lösch modified it; one mention at most).
- Hexagons, not circles, are the predicted market areas.
- Drogheda is Ireland's largest **town**; Galway, Limerick, Cork and Waterford are **cities**. Swords is a town in Fingal, not a separate city.
- Use Census 2022 "city and suburbs" figures as given; don't give city council area figures for Cork, Limerick or Galway (boundaries changed), and don't copy any town table from a past paper.
- Don't give the number of Galway or Limerick jobs at any company except the checked Dell figure.
- Site reasons for a town on a past-paper OS map or aerial photograph (Clonmel, Bantry, Foxford, Sligo) are map skills (`geo-mapwork-s1`, `geo-aerial-photographs-s1`): don't use those towns here.
- Historic phases of settlement belong to s1; urban land-use zones and models to s4; the NSS and NPF in full to s6. Dublin's development as "one urban area" is taught in `geo-region-ireland-s1`; India's Mumbai in `geo-region-continental-s1`; Paris in `geo-region-european-s1`: point to them for "an urban area in a region" questions.
- Don't use Census 2022 urban/rural percentages (not checked); Census 2016 (about 63% urban) is in s2.
