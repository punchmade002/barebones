# Package: geo-region-ireland-s2 — A Peripheral Irish Region: the West of Ireland (Western Region)

- **loId:** `geo-region-ireland-s2` · **chapterId:** `geo-region-ireland` · **syllabusRef:** 2.2
- **Syllabus:** "two contrasting Irish regions: the study of each region should include –
  physical processes, e.g. climate, soils, relief, drainage – economic processes - primary
  activities e.g. agriculture, forestry, fishing, mining/energy - secondary activities e.g.
  patterns in manufacturing activities - tertiary activities e.g. tourism and transport – human
  processes, e.g. language, religion, urban and rural development, population dynamics."
  Statement: "The study of regions show how economic, human, and physical processes interact in
  a particular area." This subsection is the **peripheral** region of the pair; contrast it with
  the Greater Dublin Area in `geo-region-ireland-s1`.
- **Region used:** the West Region = Counties **Galway (including Galway city), Mayo and
  Roscommon**. This is the standard textbook "West of Ireland" region (and the State's West
  regional unit for statistics). See Traps for the wider seven-county Western Development
  Commission area.
- **Exam weight:** Higher Level 30-mark questions are set on every heading (one primary activity,
  secondary activity, one tertiary activity, one urban area, population distribution, physical
  factors and agriculture), each answered with about 15 SRPs. Every heading below therefore needs
  at least 15 distinct, specific points. Ordinary Level 40-mark questions ask: name one
  primary/secondary/tertiary activity, explain the region's advantages for it, describe one
  problem or challenge it faces. A 20/30-mark sketch map of the region is also common.
- **Prepared by:** a higher model, 2026-10-20. The writer works only from this package and
  `course-outlines/NOTES_STANDARD.md`.

## 1. Must cover (one bullet or block for every item; tick each off)

The region
- [ ] R1 Definition and location: Galway, Mayo and Roscommon, on the Atlantic coast, far from Dublin and from European markets.
- [ ] R2 Why it is a peripheral region: remote location; difficult physical conditions (high rainfall, poor soils, rugged relief); small, scattered, ageing population with a long history of out-migration; dependence on primary activities and the public sector; lower incomes; weaker infrastructure; Galway city as the exception (a growth centre). One short block; the general core–periphery theory is in `geo-region-concept-s4`.
- [ ] R3 Sketch-map items: outline of Galway, Mayo and Roscommon; River Corrib (also Moy, Shannon on the eastern edge); Galway city and Castlebar (also Westport, Ballina, Roscommon town); relief: the Twelve Bens (Connemara), Croagh Patrick or Mweelrea; lowland: the limestone lowland of east Galway and Roscommon. Short "what to draw" bullet list, no grid references.

Physical processes
- [ ] P1 Climate: cool temperate oceanic, strongly influenced by the Atlantic; prevailing south-westerly winds bring frequent depressions and relief rainfall as moist air rises over the western mountains; heavy rainfall on many days; mild winters (North Atlantic Drift) and cool summers, so a small annual temperature range; frost and snow rare at the coast; cloudier and less sunny than the east; frequent gales and Atlantic storms. Effects: grass grows well but land is waterlogged, tillage is difficult, hay saving is hard, storms damage forests and power lines.
- [ ] P2 Relief: rugged uplands in the west (Connemara: the Twelve Bens and Maumturks; west Mayo: Mweelrea, Croagh Patrick, Nephin and the Nephin Beg range), mainly of hard Caledonian rocks (quartzite, granite, schist, marble); ice-scoured Connemara lowland dotted with small lakes and bare rock; low, flat to rolling limestone lowland in east Galway, east Mayo and Roscommon; drumlins (Clew Bay drowned drumlins); a heavily indented Atlantic coastline (Killary Harbour fjord, Clew Bay, Achill Island, the limestone Aran Islands, a continuation of the Burren). Effects on farming, settlement and tourism.
- [ ] P3 Soils: blanket bog (peat) over much of west Mayo and Connemara, formed under high rainfall and waterlogging; leached, acidic podzols with an iron pan on uplands; heavy, poorly drained gley soils on drumlins and clay lowlands; better-drained, more fertile brown earths on limestone in east Galway and Roscommon; thin, rocky soils on limestone pavement and the Aran Islands, where islanders made soil from sand and seaweed. Effects: most of the region is suited only to grazing.
- [ ] P4 Drainage: River Corrib (very short, from Lough Corrib through Galway city to Galway Bay); Lough Corrib, Lough Mask, Lough Conn and Lough Carra; River Moy (to Killala Bay at Ballina); River Shannon and Lough Ree along Roscommon's eastern edge, with its tributary the River Suck; underground drainage and turloughs on limestone (Mask–Corrib underground link, Rahasane turlough); poor natural drainage and flooding of the Shannon callows; the Moy arterial drainage scheme. Reuse the karst facts from `geo-rocks-weathering-s4`; don't re-teach formation.

Primary activities
- [ ] A1 Agriculture, physical factors: high rainfall, waterlogged and leached soils, steep uplands and exposure give a short effective grazing season and limit tillage; better land in east Galway and Roscommon.
- [ ] A2 Agriculture, human/economic factors: small, often fragmented farms (a legacy of the pre-Famine rundale system and population pressure); the Congested Districts Board (1891) and later the Land Commission consolidated holdings; older farmers; low incomes; part-time farming with off-farm jobs; distance from markets and ports; dependence on EU CAP payments.
- [ ] A3 Agriculture, farm types: cattle rearing (suckler herds; young "store" cattle sold through local marts to farmers in the east for fattening); sheep on hill commonage in Connemara and Mayo; little tillage or dairying compared with the south and east; Ballinasloe October fair (horses); Connemara ponies.
- [ ] A4 Agriculture problems and responses: low incomes, ageing farmers, few young successors, land abandonment; overgrazing of hill commonage after headage payments (facts from `geo-rocks-weathering-s7`); REPS and area-based payments; diversification (agri-tourism, forestry).
- [ ] A5 Forestry: conifer plantations (mainly Sitka spruce) on marginal land and blanket bog where farming pays poorly; planting grants and premiums; Coillte; benefits (income, timber, jobs, carbon storage); problems (acidification of streams, monoculture, landscape impact, peat slides on forested bog such as Derrybrien, storm damage).
- [ ] A6 Fishing and aquaculture: Rossaveel, Co. Galway, a State fishery harbour centre; ferries to the Aran Islands from Rossaveel; aquaculture in sheltered bays: mussel rafts in Killary Harbour, salmon farms in Connemara bays (sea-lice and pollution concerns); seaweed harvesting (Arramara Teoranta, Kilkieran); inland angling: salmon on the River Moy at Ballina, brown trout on Loughs Corrib, Mask and Conn. Problems: EU quotas, Brexit quota loss, small boats, distance from markets.
- [ ] A7 Mining and energy: Corrib gas field and the Bellanaboy terminal, with the "Shell to Sea" conflict (from `geo-rocks-weathering-s2`); peat-fired electricity at Bellacorick (closed 2003) replaced by wind energy on cutaway bog (Bellacorick wind farm, Ireland's first commercial wind farm, 1992; Oweninny wind farm); strong Atlantic winds and waves give great renewable potential (wave-energy test site off Belmullet); former coal mining at Arigna, Co. Roscommon (closed 1990, now the Arigna Mining Experience); Connemara marble quarrying; gold was found in Croagh Patrick but mining was not permitted after public opposition; Derrybrien wind-farm peat slide (from `geo-rocks-weathering-s7`).

Secondary activities
- [ ] S1 Pattern: manufacturing is limited and concentrated in Galway city and a few towns (Castlebar, Westport, Ballina); few heavy industries; most rural areas have little manufacturing apart from small food-processing and craft firms and Údarás na Gaeltachta enterprises.
- [ ] S2 Galway medical-devices cluster (case study): Digital Equipment Corporation (DEC) closed its Galway plant in 1993, leaving a skilled workforce; Boston Scientific (1994) and Medtronic followed; suppliers and start-ups clustered around them; links with the University of Galway; Galway is now one of Europe's leading medtech clusters.
- [ ] S3 Mayo multinationals: Baxter Healthcare (Castlebar), Allergan/AbbVie (Westport), Hollister (Ballina), Coca-Cola (Ballina).
- [ ] S4 Factors (each a full block of points, as exams ask any two): **government policy** (IDA Ireland and regional incentives, Údarás na Gaeltachta grants, EU Structural Funds for roads and broadband, the Western Development Commission, the National Spatial Strategy (2002) gateways and the National Planning Framework (2018) naming Galway for growth); **labour** (young graduates from the University of Galway and ATU; but out-migration of skilled young people elsewhere); **markets** (small, scattered local market; exports must travel far to ports and airports); **infrastructure** (M6 and M17/M18 motorways, rail to Dublin, broadband; weak elsewhere); **raw materials** (local farm and fish produce for food processing; medtech imports its materials).
- [ ] S5 Problems: peripheral location and transport costs, small labour pool outside Galway, dependence on a few multinationals, closures (DEC), poor rural infrastructure, difficulty attracting investment away from Galway.

Tertiary activities
- [ ] T1 Tourism attractions and factors: spectacular physical landscape (Connemara, Killary Harbour, Croagh Patrick, Achill Island, Clew Bay, the Aran Islands' limestone and Dún Aonghasa); Atlantic coast and beaches for surfing and water sports; Gaeltacht culture, language and Irish summer colleges; heritage (Céide Fields, Kylemore Abbey, Westport House, Strokestown Park and the National Famine Museum, Rathcroghan); religious tourism (Knock Shrine, Croagh Patrick pilgrimage); Galway city's festivals, nightlife and the Galway Races; angling; walking and cycling (the Great Western Greenway); Fáilte Ireland's Wild Atlantic Way (2014); Galway European Capital of Culture 2020; access via Ireland West Airport Knock and the M6.
- [ ] T2 Tourism challenges: short, weather-dependent season; seasonal and low-paid jobs; distance and weaker access; pressure on fragile landscapes and the Irish language; over-reliance on Galway city.
- [ ] T3 Transport: improvements (M6 Dublin–Galway motorway, 2009; M17/M18 Galway–Tuam, 2017; Ireland West Airport Knock, 1986; rail lines from Galway, Westport and Ballina to Dublin; Western Rail Corridor reopened Ennis–Athenry, 2010; ferries to the islands); problems (low population density makes public transport uneconomic; poor minor roads; car dependence; congestion in Galway city with no ring road built; long journey times to Dublin Port); importance of transport for tourism, industry and reducing peripherality.
- [ ] T4 Other services: University of Galway and ATU; Galway University Hospital as a regional centre; public-sector jobs and decentralised offices; remote working and digital hubs.

Human processes
- [ ] H1 Population size and distribution (Census 2022 figures in section 2): about 486,000, under a tenth of the State; low density. Exams ask to "account for the distribution", so explain each pattern: concentration in Galway city and its suburbs and satellite towns (jobs, services, transport); moderate densities in the east Galway, Roscommon and east Mayo lowlands and in towns (better land, roads); very low densities on the uplands and blanket bogs of west Mayo (Erris, Nephin Beg) and Connemara (poor soils, steep slopes, exposure); coastal and island settlement linked to fishing and the Gaeltacht.
- [ ] H2 Population dynamics: the Great Famine of the late 1840s hit the West hardest; a long history of emigration has left populations far below pre-Famine levels; continuing out-migration of young adults to Dublin and abroad (brain drain); ageing population, high old-age dependency, fewer births, empty houses; recent growth from return migration, immigration and Galway's expansion; every county grew between 2016 and 2022.
- [ ] H3 Urban development of Galway city (the urban area to use for "one urban area" questions): site at the lowest crossing of the River Corrib on Galway Bay; Anglo-Norman walled town; medieval trading port ("City of the Tribes", 14 merchant families; the Spanish Arch); university since 1845; industrial growth (DEC, then medtech); designated a gateway (2002) and a city for at least 50% growth by 2040 (National Planning Framework, 2018); suburban sprawl and satellite growth (Oranmore); problems: traffic congestion, housing costs, lack of a ring road.
- [ ] H4 Other towns: Castlebar (county town and largest town of Mayo), Westport (an 18th-century planned estate town of the Browne family, with the Octagon and the Mall along the Carrowbeg River), Ballina, Tuam (cathedral town), Ballinasloe, Roscommon town (county town), Boyle. Small towns, weak urban hierarchy.
- [ ] H5 Rural development: traditional dispersed settlement and clachans (pre-Famine rundale); later ribbon development and one-off housing; rural decline (depopulation, ageing, closure of schools, post offices and Garda stations, island depopulation); responses: Congested Districts Board (1891), CLÁR programme (2001), LEADER, the National Broadband Plan, remote-working hubs, Our Rural Future (2021), Western Development Commission.
- [ ] H6 Language: the region holds Ireland's largest Gaeltacht areas: Galway (Connemara, Cois Fharraige, the Aran Islands) and Mayo (Erris, Achill, Tourmakeady); Roscommon has none. Institutions: Údarás na Gaeltachta (HQ Na Forbacha), TG4 (Baile na hAbhann), RTÉ Raidió na Gaeltachta (Casla). Decline of daily speakers: emigration, English-language media, tourism and in-migration of English speakers, English in the home. Strategies: Gaeltacht Act 2012 language-planning areas, the 20-Year Strategy for the Irish Language 2010–2030, Irish-medium schools and summer colleges, planning conditions reserving new houses for Irish speakers, Údarás jobs to keep young people. (Changes in Gaeltacht boundaries since 1850 belong to `geo-region-european-s4`; one sentence at most.)
- [ ] H7 Religion: predominantly Roman Catholic, with a higher Catholic share than Dublin; religious sites and tourism (Knock Shrine, Croagh Patrick pilgrimage on Reek Sunday, Ballintubber Abbey); Tuam as the seat of the Catholic Archdiocese of Tuam; declining practice and vocations, but religion remains important in community life.

## 3. Traps

- Define the region once and stick to it: Galway, Mayo, Roscommon. The Western Development Commission's "Western Region" is seven counties (adds Donegal, Sligo, Leitrim, Clare), and the Northern and Western Regional Assembly area is larger again (`geo-region-concept-s4` uses it as its general peripheral example). Students may say their region is part of these wider areas, but all facts here are for Galway, Mayo and Roscommon. Don't mix them: Killybegs (Donegal), the Burren and Cliffs of Moher (Clare) and Sligo town are outside this region; name them only as outside it.
- Croagh Patrick is quartzite, not limestone or granite. The Aran Islands are limestone, not granite.
- Mweelrea (814 m), not Croagh Patrick, is the highest peak in Connacht. Carrauntoohil is in Kerry.
- Lough Corrib is the largest lake in the Republic; Lough Neagh (Northern Ireland) is the largest on the island. Don't call Lough Corrib "Ireland's largest lake".
- Galway Airport has no checked current status; don't name it as a working airport. Use Ireland West Airport Knock.
- The Western Rail Corridor north of Athenry is not open; don't say it is.
- Don't give lengths for the Corrib or Moy, visitor numbers for Knock or the Wild Atlantic Way, or medtech job numbers: none are checked.
- Never copy figures from past-paper data (daily-speaker counts by Gaeltacht area, forestry hectares by county, seafood-sector tables, IDA job tables, tourist percentages).
- Karst, glacial, mass-movement and Corrib gas facts are taught in chapters 1–6; reuse their checked facts in one or two clauses and don't re-teach formation.
- Gaeltacht boundary changes since 1850 belong to `geo-region-european-s4`; general core–periphery theory to `geo-region-concept-s4`; migration theory to `geo-population-migration`; rural settlement patterns in general to `geo-urban-s2`.
- Keep Dublin's facts (Intel, Dublin Port, Luas) out except as a one-line contrast.

## 4. What exams ask (topics only)

- Sketch map (20 marks Higher, 30 marks Ordinary): outline of the region, one river, one/two urban centres, one relief feature (upland or lowland).
- 30 marks Higher: physical factors influencing one primary activity (agriculture: climate, relief, soils, drainage); agriculture with any two of relief, climate, markets; physical factors for agriculture in two contrasting Irish regions (this region gives the "difficult" half).
- 30 marks Higher: two factors influencing one primary activity (agriculture, forestry, fishing or energy); two factors influencing secondary activity (government policy, labour, markets, infrastructure, raw materials); two factors influencing tourism or transport.
- 30 marks Higher: factors influencing one urban area (Galway city); account for population distribution in the region.
- 10 marks Higher: one strategy to address the decline in daily Irish speakers in Gaeltacht areas (expected: language planning under the Gaeltacht Act 2012, Údarás jobs, Irish-medium education and summer colleges, housing conditions, TG4/Raidió na Gaeltachta).
- Ordinary 40 marks: name a primary/secondary/tertiary activity; explain the region's advantages; describe one problem or challenge (e.g. tourism: advantages = landscape, culture, Wild Atlantic Way; challenge = seasonality).
- Ordinary 15 marks: name a peripheral region and explain why it is peripheral. Short questions: why the west is wetter and cloudier than the east.

## 5. Practice questions to write

1. 30 marks, Higher: "Examine the physical factors that influence the development of agriculture in a peripheral Irish region you have studied." Model answer: West Region; climate, relief, soils and drainage only, each with effects on farm type and named places from section 2. About 15 SRPs. No human factors.
2. 30 marks, Higher: "Examine two factors that have influenced the development of tourism in a peripheral Irish region you have studied." Model answer: physical landscape and culture/heritage (Gaeltacht, religious and historic sites) only, about 7–8 SRPs each, with the Wild Atlantic Way and named attractions.
3. 30 marks, Higher: "Account for the distribution of population in a peripheral Irish region you have studied." Model answer: high density in Galway city and suburbs, moderate in the eastern lowlands and towns, very low in the western uplands and bogs and on islands, each with physical and economic reasons, plus historic out-migration. About 15 SRPs.

Each model answer covers exactly what its question asks, nothing appended.
