# Package: geo-weather-climate-s1 — Weather maps and weather data

- **loId:** `geo-weather-climate-s1` · **chapterId:** `geo-weather-climate` · **syllabusRef:** 3 (Skills)
- **Syllabus:** Higher Level, "5. Weather maps and weather data. Students should study daily
  weather maps and examine the information shown and symbols used. Students should examine
  media weather reports and interpret synoptic weather maps." Ordinary Level: "Students should
  study daily weather maps, the information shown, and the symbols used. Students should examine
  media weather reports and synoptic weather maps." The general aims list "synoptic weather
  maps" as an information-gathering skill.
- **This is a skills subsection.** It teaches how to read weather charts and weather data, with
  worked examples that use **invented, labelled illustrative values**. Never take pressures,
  rainfall totals, temperatures, station names or chart layouts from past papers.
- **Boundary:** depressions, anticyclones, fronts and air masses **in depth** (how they form, the
  full sequence of weather as a depression passes, the general circulation, the Coriolis force,
  cloud types, how instruments work) belong to the Atmosphere–Ocean option,
  `geo-option-atmosphere-ocean` (s1 instruments, s3 clouds and precipitation, s4 depressions
  and anticyclones). Here give only what is needed to read a synoptic chart: one or two bullets
  per system, then refer to that chapter.
- **Prepared by:** a higher model, 2026-10-20. The writer works only from this package and
  `course-outlines/NOTES_STANDARD.md`.

## 1. Must cover (one bullet or block for every item; tick each off)

What a weather map is
- [ ] M1 Weather vs climate: weather is the state of the atmosphere at a place at a given time (hours, days); climate is the average weather of a place over a long period (usually 30 years). One bullet each.
- [ ] M2 Synoptic chart: a map summarising the weather over a large area at one moment in time, drawn from simultaneous observations at many weather stations, ships, buoys and satellites. "Synoptic" means a general view of the whole. Surface analysis charts show the observed situation; forecast charts show the expected situation at a later time. Charts are issued at fixed times in UTC.
- [ ] M3 Who produces Irish weather maps: Met Éireann, Ireland's national meteorological service, from a network of weather stations (e.g. Valentia Observatory, Malin Head, Belmullet, Dublin Airport, Shannon Airport), plus satellite and radar data.
- [ ] M4 Units: pressure in hectopascals (hPa), numerically equal to millibars (mb); temperature in °C; rainfall in mm; wind speed in km/h or knots; sunshine in hours; cloud cover in oktas (eighths of the sky).

Isolines (lines joining places of equal value). Exams ask for each name; give each its own bullet.
- [ ] I1 Isobars: equal atmospheric pressure. Usually drawn at fixed intervals (commonly every 4 hPa); read the labels on the chart to find the interval.
- [ ] I2 Isotherms: equal temperature.
- [ ] I3 Isohyets: equal rainfall.
- [ ] I4 Isohels: equal sunshine.
- [ ] I5 Isonephs: equal cloud cover. One bullet only.
- [ ] I6 Reading a value between isolines: find the two lines either side, note the interval, estimate in proportion to distance; a value at a closed centre is the stated figure (e.g. "L 976") or lies beyond the innermost isobar. Worked example W1.
- [ ] I7 Shaded or banded maps (rainfall, sunshine, UV, temperature anomaly maps with a colour key): read the class from the key and give the range (e.g. "50–74 mm") when the map shows a band rather than a single value.

Pressure systems on a chart (chart-reading level only; detail in `geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s4`)
- [ ] P1 Average sea-level pressure is about 1013 hPa. "High" and "low" are relative to the surrounding air, not fixed numbers.
- [ ] P2 Depression (low-pressure system, cyclone, "low"): marked L or "Low" with a pressure figure; closed isobars with pressure falling towards the centre; usually tightly packed isobars; usually has fronts attached. Air rises, cools and condenses, giving cloud, rain and strong winds. Rainfall is associated with depressions, not anticyclones. Another name for a low = depression.
- [ ] P3 Anticyclone (high-pressure system, "high"): marked H or "High"; closed isobars with pressure rising towards the centre; widely spaced isobars; no fronts. Air sinks and warms, so clouds rarely form: calm, dry, settled weather. Summer: warm, sunny days. Winter: cold, clear nights with frost and fog. Another name for a high = anticyclone.
- [ ] P4 Other pressure features, one bullet each: trough (an elongated area of low pressure, often unsettled, sometimes drawn as a line); ridge (an elongated extension of high pressure, a short settled spell); col (a slack area between two highs and two lows; light winds, weather uncertain, fog or thunderstorms possible).
- [ ] P5 Air masses: a large body of air with fairly uniform temperature and humidity, named from its source region (polar or tropical; maritime or continental). One bullet; Ireland's usual air mass is maritime (from the Atlantic). Full treatment in `geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s4`.

Wind on a chart
- [ ] V1 Wind direction is named by the direction the wind blows **from** (a westerly blows from the west).
- [ ] V2 Pressure gradient: air moves from high to low pressure. Close isobars = steep pressure gradient = strong winds; widely spaced isobars = light winds or calm. This is the rule used to judge statements like "winds are stronger over X than over Y".
- [ ] V3 Direction around systems in the northern hemisphere: anticlockwise around a depression, clockwise around an anticyclone; near the surface the wind blows nearly parallel to the isobars, angled slightly in towards the low and out from the high. Buys Ballot's law: stand with your back to the wind and low pressure is on your left (northern hemisphere). One bullet; the cause (Coriolis force) is in `geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s4`.
- [ ] V4 Prevailing wind: the wind that blows most frequently over an area. In Ireland it is south-westerly, from the Atlantic.
- [ ] V5 Beaufort scale: wind force from 0 (calm) to 12 (hurricane force), based on observed effects. One bullet.

Fronts and their symbols (exams ask students to draw and identify them)
- [ ] F1 A front is the boundary between two air masses of different temperature and humidity. Frontal symbols point in the direction the front is moving.
- [ ] F2 Warm front: line with semicircles on the side towards which it moves (red on colour charts). Warm air rising gently over cold air: long period of steady rain, thickening cloud, then rising temperature after it passes.
- [ ] F3 Cold front: line with triangles on the side towards which it moves (blue on colour charts). Cold air undercutting warm air: short heavy showers, possible thunder, falling temperature, clearer and brighter showery weather after it passes.
- [ ] F4 Occluded front: line with triangles and semicircles alternating on the **same** side (purple on colour charts). Forms where a cold front catches up with a warm front, lifting the warm air off the ground; gives cloud and rain. It is found near the centre of a mature depression.
- [ ] F5 Stationary (quasi-stationary) front: triangles and semicircles alternating on **opposite** sides. One bullet.
- [ ] F6 Diagram cue: the writer describes a labelled diagram of the four front symbols, and a plan of a depression with warm front ahead, warm sector between, cold front behind, occlusion near the centre, isobars kinked at the fronts. The full weather sequence of a passing depression is in `geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s4`; give one sentence pointing there.

Station models and weather symbols
- [ ] S1 Station model: the cluster of symbols plotted at each weather station on a synoptic chart. A circle shows cloud cover in oktas by how much of it is shaded (empty = clear sky; fully shaded = 8 oktas, overcast).
- [ ] S2 Wind arrow (shaft and barbs): the shaft points from the station into the direction the wind is coming **from**. Barbs (feathers) give speed: a half barb = 5 knots, a full barb = 10 knots, a pennant (filled triangle) = 50 knots; add them up. A circle around the station with no shaft = calm.
- [ ] S3 Other values plotted around the circle: temperature (°C), dew point, pressure, and a present-weather symbol (rain, drizzle, showers, snow, fog, thunderstorm). One bullet; students read the values from the chart's key.
- [ ] S4 Media symbols used on TV, websites and apps: sun, cloud, sun-and-cloud, rain drops, showers, snow, thunder (lightning bolt), fog; wind arrows with speed in km/h; temperatures in coloured boxes; rainfall radar colours (heavier rain darker or warmer colours). Matching symbols to features (H, L, warm front, cold front, occluded front) is a regular short question.
- [ ] S5 Worked example W2 (station model) and W3 (front symbols).

Media weather reports
- [ ] R1 Sources: Met Éireann forecasts on TV, radio, the website and app; national, regional and city forecasts; sea area and coastal forecasts (wind and sea state for shipping and fishing); farming forecasts; rainfall radar; UV and pollen forecasts; weather warnings.
- [ ] R2 Content of a forecast: the synoptic situation (what pressure systems and fronts are doing), expected rain, temperature (highs and lows), wind direction and speed, cloud and sunshine, and how conditions change across the country and through the day.
- [ ] R3 Weather warnings: colour-coded Yellow, Orange and Red by the expected impact (Red is the most severe; people should act to protect themselves and property). Types include wind, rain, snow-ice, low temperature, high temperature, fog and thunderstorm.
- [ ] R4 Storm naming: since 2015 Met Éireann names storms jointly with the UK Met Office (and, since 2019, the Dutch service KNMI). Names make warnings easier to communicate. Use the checked storms in section 2 only.
- [ ] R5 Rainfall radar and forecast sequences: a series of maps at set times shows rain moving across the country. Method: compare the position of the rain band on successive maps to give its direction of movement (in Ireland most rain bands arrive from the west or south-west with Atlantic depressions, but read each map, don't assume).
- [ ] R6 UV index: a measure of the strength of the sun's ultraviolet radiation. Categories (World Health Organization scale): Low 1–2, Moderate 3–5, High 6–7, Very high 8–10, Extreme 11+. UV maps are read with the isolines or colour key like any other isoline map.

Weather data (tables, graphs and station readings). Exams set a table or graph of a station's daily, hourly or monthly figures.
- [ ] D1 Instruments, one bullet each: rain gauge (rainfall, mm); thermometer (temperature, °C); maximum–minimum thermometer (highest and lowest temperature in a period); barometer (pressure, hPa) and barograph (continuous pressure record); anemometer (wind speed); wind vane (wind direction); hygrometer or wet-and-dry bulb thermometer (humidity); sunshine recorder (hours of sunshine); thermometers are kept in a Stevenson screen (white, louvred box, about 1.25 m above the ground) so they measure shaded air temperature. How each instrument works is in `geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s1`; here name and purpose only.
- [ ] D2 Calculations, each with a worked example (W4): total (add the values, e.g. rainfall over three days or three months); mean (total ÷ number of values); mean daily temperature = (maximum + minimum) ÷ 2; temperature range = highest − lowest (daily or annual range); difference between two places; counting how many months or days pass a threshold ("more than 100 mm", "greater than 9 °C"); identifying the highest or lowest month. Always give units.
- [ ] D3 Reading hourly graphs (temperature line, rainfall bars, wind speed) for one day: read times on the horizontal axis carefully (00:00 = midnight), read the value at a given hour, count hours with rain.
- [ ] D4 Climate graphs (temperature line plus rainfall bars by month) at an Irish station: describe the pattern (mild winters, cool summers, rain all year, wettest months usually in autumn and winter). One bullet linking to the cool temperate oceanic climate in `geo-region-concept`; the graph-drawing method itself is in `geo-graph-skills-s1`.

Explaining patterns on Irish weather and climate maps (the "state one reason" 2-mark questions)
- [ ] E1 West wetter than east: moist south-westerly winds off the Atlantic meet the western mountains, are forced to rise, cool and condense, giving relief (orographic) rain. One bullet; the process belongs to `geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s3`.
- [ ] E2 East and south-east sunnier and drier: they lie in the rain shadow of the western and central uplands; descending air warms, cloud evaporates, so there is less cloud and more sunshine. The south-east is the sunniest part of Ireland.
- [ ] E3 Coasts milder in winter than the interior: the sea (and the North Atlantic Drift) warms coastal areas in winter; inland areas have a larger temperature range. One bullet; the North Atlantic Drift belongs to `geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s4`.
- [ ] E4 Highest rainfall totals are on mountains near the west coast; lowest around Dublin and the east. Use the checked long-term figures in section 2 only.

## 3. Traps

- **Never use past-paper charts or figures**: no real chart pressures, rainfall totals, station
  readings, UV values, county sunshine totals or named-station tables from exam papers. Use
  only the illustrative values above.
- Isohyets = rainfall; isohels = sunshine; isotherms = temperature; isobars = pressure. Exams
  set true/false items that swap them (e.g. calling isobars isohyets). Spell "isohel" and
  "isohyet" correctly.
- Wind direction is where the wind comes **from**. A wind arrow's shaft points to where the wind
  comes from, not where it is going.
- Close isobars mean **strong** winds, not weak ones.
- Rain is associated with **depressions**; anticyclones bring dry, settled weather (but winter
  anticyclones can bring fog and frost, not warmth).
- Frontal symbols: triangles = cold, semicircles = warm. They sit on the side the front is
  moving **towards**. Occluded = both on the same side; stationary = opposite sides.
- Another name for a low is a **depression** (or cyclone); for a high, an **anticyclone**.
  Don't call an anticyclone a "cyclone".
- Hectopascals and millibars are the same value; don't convert between them.
- Don't state a fixed pressure value as the boundary between "high" and "low".
- Don't give storm wind speeds, damage figures or rainfall totals for named storms: none are
  checked. Don't say a hurricane "forms from a front"; describe Ophelia only as in section 2.
- Hurricane formation, the full depression life cycle, air mass types and how instruments work
  belong to `geo-option-atmosphere-ocean`. Keep each to one pointing bullet here.
- Drawing graphs (bar, line, climate graphs) belongs to `geo-graph-skills-s1`. Here, read data
  and calculate only.
- Satellite images of clouds and hurricanes belong to `geo-weather-climate-s2`; give one
  sentence saying forecasters combine charts with satellite and radar data.
- Climate change projections and renewable energy are not this subsection: renewable energy is
  `geo-economic-environment`; climate change is `geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s5` and
  `geo-economic-environment-s3`.

## 4. What exams ask (topics only)

- Matching letters on a chart to H, L, warm front, cold front, occluded front; naming the front
  type at a labelled position or around a named storm (often occluded near a mature low's
  centre).
- Drawing the symbol for a cold front or a warm front.
- Reading the pressure at a low's centre, at a high's centre or at a marked point, in hPa/mb.
- What H and L mean; another name for a high (anticyclone) or low (depression).
- Names of isolines: isobars, isotherms, isohyets, isohels; true/false items that swap them.
- Wind strength comparisons between two places from isobar spacing; whether an area has calm or
  windy conditions; whether a region is wet and windy or calm and dry.
- Whether rainfall is more likely in a depression or an anticyclone.
- The term for the wind that blows most frequently (prevailing wind).
- Reading rainfall, sunshine, temperature and UV maps with a key: the value at a marked area;
  which province or county gets least or most; how many counties exceed a value; differences.
- True/false on rain spreading from a direction on a timed forecast sequence; whether relief rain
  explains a rainfall pattern; why the east is sunnier than the west.
- Station data tables and graphs (monthly, daily, hourly): highest/lowest value, value for a
  month, totals over three months or days, ranges, means, counts above a threshold.
- Naming the instrument for rainfall (rain gauge) or temperature (thermometer).
