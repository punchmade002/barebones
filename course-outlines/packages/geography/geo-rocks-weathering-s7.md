## 1. Must cover (one bullet or block for every item; tick each off)

The idea
- [ ] H1 Humans make mass movement more frequent and faster by removing the vegetation that binds soil and absorbs water, by adding water or weight to slopes, and by removing the support at the base of slopes. They can also reduce it by managing slopes.
- [ ] H2 Soil erosion: when vegetation is removed, rain splash, sheet wash, gullying and wind strip the soil, and slopes become prone to creep, slides and mudflows. Exams accept both soil erosion and mass movement in answers on this option, as long as the link to slopes and gravity is made.

Overgrazing (block: how, effects, Irish case study, international example, management)
- [ ] OG1 How: too many animals for the land's carrying capacity eat the vegetation down to the roots and trample the ground; hooves compact the soil and break the surface of peat; bare patches appear.
- [ ] OG2 Effects on mass movement: without roots, soil and peat lose cohesion; rain runs off the compacted surface or saturates the broken peat; sheet and gully erosion, soil creep speeds up (terracettes deepen), peat slides; sediment silts up rivers and damages fish spawning beds.
- [ ] OG3 Irish case study: sheep overgrazing on western mountain commonage after EU headage payments (facts in section 2).
- [ ] OG4 International example: the Sahel (overgrazing near wells and boreholes, combined with overcropping and fuelwood cutting, leading to desertification). Facts in section 2.
- [ ] OG5 Management: reduced stocking (destocking), commonage plans, grazing rotation, fencing to let vegetation recover, agri-environment schemes paying farmers to manage land sustainably.

Overcropping (block: how, effects, case study, management)
- [ ] OC1 How: the same land is cropped year after year without fallow periods or fertiliser, so soil nutrients and organic matter are used up; the soil structure breaks down, crops grow poorly and the ground is left bare between harvests; marginal and sloping land is ploughed.
- [ ] OC2 Effects: loose, dry, structureless soil is easily washed downslope by rain or blown away by wind; rills and gullies; on slopes, ploughing up and down the slope channels water and soil downhill.
- [ ] OC3 Case study: the Dust Bowl (facts in section 2), with the link to wind erosion.
- [ ] OC4 Sahel: overcropping as fallow periods were shortened and cash crops expanded (one or two bullets, shared with OG4).
- [ ] OC5 Management: crop rotation, fallow periods, contour ploughing (along the slope, not up and down), strip cropping, terracing of steep slopes, shelter belts against wind, mulching and cover crops.

Deforestation (block: how, effects, Irish and international case studies, management)
- [ ] DF1 How: trees removed for farmland, timber, fuelwood, roads and building. Tree roots bind soil and anchor it to bedrock; the canopy breaks the force of rain; trees take up water (interception and transpiration).
- [ ] DF2 Effects on mass movement: more rain reaches and saturates the soil; roots rot and no longer hold the slope; landslides and mudflows become more frequent, especially in heavy tropical rain; more sediment and faster run-off reach rivers.
- [ ] DF3 International case studies: Haiti (Gonaïves, 2004) and Central America (Hurricane Mitch, 1998). Facts in section 2.
- [ ] DF4 Ireland: historic forest clearance left Ireland with very little woodland; today clear-felling of conifer plantations on upland peat and building forestry and wind-farm roads can destabilise peat slopes.
- [ ] DF5 Management: afforestation and reforestation of steep slopes; leaving buffer strips of trees beside rivers; selective felling instead of clear-felling; restricting felling on steep or peaty slopes.

Other human causes (exams accept them; one block)
- [ ] OH1 Construction on peat: wind farms and roads on upland blanket bog (excavation, dumping of spoil on the bog surface, interference with drainage). Irish case studies: Derrybrien (2003) and Meenbog (2020). Facts in section 2.
- [ ] OH2 Road and rail cuttings that steepen slopes or remove support at their base; building weight on top of slopes; leaking pipes, drainage and irrigation saturating slopes; reservoirs saturating valley sides (Vajont, in `geo-rocks-weathering-s5`; name only); mining spoil heaps (Aberfan, in `geo-rocks-weathering-s2`; name only).

Reducing the risk (one block of engineering responses)
- [ ] R1 Slope engineering: retaining walls, gabions (wire cages of stones), rock bolts, wire netting and rock-catch fences on cliffs, regrading slopes to a gentler angle, drainage pipes to lower water content.
- [ ] R2 Planning: hazard mapping, avoiding building on unstable slopes, environmental impact assessments before building on peat, monitoring and warning systems.

## 2. Checked facts and examples (the only named places, dates and figures you may use)

Do not name any place, date or figure that is not on this list. Give no sheep numbers,
grant rates, rainfall totals or soil-loss figures: none are checked.

| Item | Example(s) |
|---|---|
| OG3 | From the 1980s, EU (CAP) headage payments paid farmers per animal, so sheep numbers rose sharply on hill commonage in the west of Ireland, including Connemara, Co. Galway, and the mountains of Co. Mayo, Co. Donegal and Co. Kerry. Overgrazing stripped heather and grass, exposed and eroded blanket peat, and sent silt into rivers, harming salmon and trout spawning grounds. Responses: payments were changed from per-head to per-area; Commonage Framework Plans (from the early 2000s) set lower stocking rates; REPS (the Rural Environment Protection Scheme, introduced in 1994) paid farmers to farm in environmentally friendly ways. |
| OG4, OC4 | The Sahel, the semi-arid belt along the southern edge of the Sahara: population growth led to more cattle, goats and sheep concentrated around wells and boreholes, shorter fallow periods, more cash crops and more fuelwood cutting; with droughts (notably in the 1970s and 1980s), the bare soil was eroded by wind and by the heavy rain of the short wet season, a process called desertification. |
| OC3 | The Dust Bowl, Great Plains of the USA (including Oklahoma and Kansas), 1930s: the natural grassland was ploughed up for wheat; severe drought then left bare, dry soil that strong winds blew away in huge dust storms; many farming families were forced to leave. The US Soil Conservation Service was set up in 1935; shelter belts of trees, contour ploughing and crop rotation were promoted. |
| OC5 | Rice terraces of Banaue, Luzon, Philippines: terracing of steep slopes, over 2,000 years old. |
| DF3 Haiti | Haiti has lost almost all its natural forest, mainly for farmland and charcoal; neighbouring Dominican Republic has far more forest cover. In September 2004 Tropical Storm Jeanne caused floods and mudslides off the bare hills around Gonaïves, killing about 3,000 people. |
| DF3 Mitch | Hurricane Mitch, October 1998: torrential rain over Honduras and Nicaragua caused floods and landslides, made worse by deforested slopes; about 11,000 people were killed. A mudflow from the Casita volcano, Nicaragua, buried villages. |
| DF4 | About 1% of Ireland was forested around 1900; forest cover is now about 11%, mostly conifer plantations. |
| OH1 Derrybrien | Derrybrien, Slieve Aughty Mountains, Co. Galway, 16 October 2003: during construction of a wind farm on a forested blanket-bog hillside, a peat slide of about 450,000 cubic metres of peat moved down the slope and into the Owendalulleegh River, killing large numbers of fish. In 2019 the EU Court of Justice fined Ireland (a lump sum of €5 million plus €15,000 a day) because no proper environmental impact assessment had been carried out. |
| OH1 Meenbog | Meenbog wind farm, near Ballybofey, Co. Donegal, November 2020: a peat slide during construction flowed into the Mourne Beg River and crossed into Northern Ireland, polluting a river system important for salmon. |

## 3. Traps (known errors)

- The "Cummeragh Mountain bogburst in Donegal (2003)" in a marking-scheme answer is not a checked event. Do not use it.
- Do not claim overgrazing caused the Pollatomish landslides (2003): they were triggered by an intense storm. Pollatomish belongs to `geo-rocks-weathering-s5`.
- Do not claim that urban construction in Dublin or Cork causes landslides, as one marking-scheme answer does; no checked Irish urban example exists.
- Do not say Himalayan deforestation causes Bangladesh's floods: the link is disputed. No checked Nepal example: do not name one.
- Derrybrien is in Co. Galway (Slieve Aughty), not Co. Clare. Meenbog is in Co. Donegal.
- The mass movement processes themselves (creep, slump, mudflow, bog burst) are taught in `geo-rocks-weathering-s5`. Use the terms here without redefining them.
- The Dust Bowl's wind processes (deflation) are in `geo-rocks-weathering-s6`; here focus on overcropping as the human cause.
- Hydro-electric dams, canalisation and flood control belong to `geo-rivers-s4`; coastal defences and recreation to `geo-sea-s2`. Leave them out.
- Keep the three syllabus activities (overgrazing, overcropping, deforestation) as separate blocks: a student must be able to answer on any one.

## 4. What exams ask (topics only)

- Higher and Ordinary 30 marks: "Examine how humans interact with one of the following surface processes: river processes, coastal processes, mass movement." Expected answer for mass movement: overgrazing, overcropping and/or deforestation (and accepted others, e.g. wind farms on peat), each with how it affects slope stability, named examples with specific facts, and management.
- Questions on a news article about a bog burst at a wind farm: county, cause (construction activity), impacts (river pollution, fish kills).
