# Package: geo-economic-activities-s1 — Case study of a developed economy: Ireland

- **loId:** `geo-economic-activities-s1` · **chapterId:** `geo-economic-activities` · **syllabusRef:** 4.2
- **Syllabus:** Statement: "Levels of economic development show major spatial variations and
  can change over time. Levels of economic development evolve through the complex interaction of
  factors including physical, social, cultural, and political." Students should study "a case
  study from developed economies. This case study should include regions dominated by service
  and footloose industries, financial services and/or mass tourism regions. The case study
  should also, where appropriate, examine evidence of industrial decline." National setting:
  "Ireland." International settings: "Appropriate relevant European and global examples."
- Elective Unit 4 is one of two electives, examined at both levels. Write in full Higher Level
  detail. The syllabus says "and/or", but write out **all three** region types (service and
  footloose industries; financial services; mass tourism) so students can choose.
- **Prepared by:** a higher model, 2026-10-20. The writer works only from this package and
  `course-outlines/NOTES_STANDARD.md`.

Ireland is the case study. The European and global examples (Silicon Valley; the City of
London; Spain's mass-tourism coasts) are shorter comparison blocks. The 30-mark answers this
subsection must support are: the development of tourism, financial services, or service and
footloose industries in a developed economy; evidence and causes of industrial decline; and the
data-response short questions on Irish agriculture, fishing, industry, tourism and employment.

## 1. Must cover (one bullet or block for every item; tick each off)

Economic activity types and the Irish economy
- [ ] E1 Primary (extracting raw materials from nature: farming, fishing, forestry, mining, quarrying, energy extraction), secondary (processing raw materials or assembling components into goods: manufacturing, food processing, construction) and tertiary (services: retail, banking, tourism, teaching, health, transport, IT). Exams ask students to classify jobs (miner, teacher, banker, builder, forester, tour guide) and photos. Construction is secondary. Tourism, banking and transport are tertiary. Some writers add quaternary (research, information); one clause only.
- [ ] E2 Sectoral shift in a developed economy: employment moves from primary to secondary and then to tertiary as an economy develops. Ireland today: services employ about three-quarters of workers; agriculture, forestry and fishing a small share (section 2). Contrast a developing economy, where primary employment is high (one clause; developing economies belong to `geo-economic-development`).
- [ ] E3 How Ireland became a developed economy, as a timeline of policy (section 2): protectionism (1930s–1950s); the turn to free trade and foreign investment from 1958; EEC membership 1973; free second-level education 1967 and a growing educated workforce; the Celtic Tiger (mid-1990s to 2007); the 2008 crash; recovery after 2013. For each stage give cause and effect.
- [ ] E4 The crash and recovery: property bubble and bank lending; the bank guarantee (2008); bank nationalisation and NAMA; the EU–IMF programme (2010–2013); unemployment and emigration peak; why Ireland's government debt rose so much (borrowing to rescue banks and cover a collapsed tax base); recovery and near-full employment by the 2020s.
- [ ] E5 Benefits of high employment to the economy (exams ask this): more income-tax and VAT revenue, lower welfare spending, more consumer spending supporting other jobs, less emigration, government can invest in infrastructure and services.
- [ ] E6 GDP distortion by multinationals: Ireland's GDP is inflated by multinational profits and assets, so the CSO created modified GNI (GNI*) in 2017. One or two bullets; GNP/HDI as measures belong to `geo-economic-development`.

Primary activities in Ireland's economy (data-response support; regional depth is in `geo-region-ireland`)
- [ ] P1 Agriculture: grass-based livestock farming dominates because of the mild, wet climate (long grass-growing season); tillage concentrated on drier, flatter land in the south and east. Name crops (barley, wheat, oats, potatoes) and livestock (beef and dairy cattle, sheep, pigs). Pastoral = livestock; arable/tillage = crops (exams use true/false on this).
- [ ] P2 Dairy expansion after the end of EU milk quotas (2015); most beef and dairy output is exported. Benefits of a large cattle herd (exports, food-processing jobs, rural incomes). Environmental problems of large livestock numbers (methane and greenhouse gas emissions; nitrate and phosphate runoff into rivers and lakes; ammonia).
- [ ] P3 Agri-food processing and named Irish food and drink products and companies (section 2): exams ask for one food product and one beverage.
- [ ] P4 Fishing: advantages Ireland has (long, indented coastline with sheltered harbours; wide continental shelf; plankton-rich waters; North Atlantic Drift; productive pelagic stocks such as mackerel); the six State Fishery Harbour Centres (exams ask for two fishing ports); the EU Common Fisheries Policy quotas and Brexit reductions as problems (CFP detail is in `geo-economic-eu`). Value of fishing to the economy (exports, jobs in remote coastal communities, processing).
- [ ] P5 Forestry and wood processing: low forest cover; Coillte; why wood processing employs few people (small forest area, young plantations, highly mechanised sawmills and board mills, timber partly exported unprocessed).
- [ ] P6 Mining and energy extraction, one bullet each: Tara Mines; Corrib gas; the end of peat-fired electricity.

Secondary activity in Ireland
- [ ] S1 Foreign direct investment (FDI) as the engine of manufacturing: why multinationals come (low corporation tax; IDA grants and serviced sites; young, English-speaking, educated workforce; access to the EU single market; clusters of similar firms; time zone between the USA and Asia). These double as "factors that attract industries to Ireland" in short questions.
- [ ] S2 Main manufacturing sectors and their locations (section 2): pharmaceuticals and chemicals (Cork Harbour); medical devices (Galway); ICT and electronics (Intel Leixlip; Apple Cork); food and drink (indigenous firms). Pharmaceuticals and chemicals make up the largest share of industrial output and goods exports.
- [ ] S3 Indigenous industry and Enterprise Ireland: Irish-owned firms, many in food processing and building materials, that grew into multinationals (section 2). Dual economy: a highly productive foreign-owned sector beside a smaller, lower-productivity Irish-owned sector.
- [ ] S4 Challenges facing Irish industry (exams ask for two): dependence on a few large US multinationals; global minimum corporation tax (15% for large groups from 2024); US trade and tax policy changes; Brexit (trade, supply chains, UK market); high housing and living costs making staff hard to attract; electricity grid and water constraints; high energy costs; competition from lower-cost countries.

Region dominated by service and footloose industries
- [ ] F1 Footloose industry: one that is not tied to a location by raw materials or power, so it can locate almost anywhere; it chooses places with skilled labour, good communications, quality of life and government incentives. Services such as software, internet and data processing are the most footloose.
- [ ] F2 Case study: the Dublin region and "Silicon Docks" (Grand Canal Dock) as a region dominated by services and footloose industries: named firms and dates (section 2); why they cluster (skilled graduates, universities, EU-wide recruitment, airport, fibre connectivity, tax, agglomeration); effects (high-paid jobs; office and housing demand, rising rents, congestion).
- [ ] F3 Data centres as a newer footloose-service activity: why they come (cool climate reduces cooling costs, fibre links, clusters, tax); the problem of their electricity demand (section 2) and the EirGrid restrictions on new Dublin connections.
- [ ] F4 Shannon Free Zone as an early planned zone for footloose industry: the world's first duty-free airport shop (1947), the Free Zone (1959), its tax advantages; aircraft leasing grew from Shannon (GPA, 1975).
- [ ] F5 Global comparison: Silicon Valley, California, as the original region of footloose high-technology and service industry (section 2): origins around Stanford University; named firms; why it grew; problems (housing costs, congestion, inequality).

Financial services
- [ ] B1 Financial services defined: banking, insurance, fund administration, asset management, aircraft leasing, payments and fintech. A highly footloose, high-value tertiary activity.
- [ ] B2 Case study: the International Financial Services Centre (IFSC), Dublin (section 2): set up 1987 on derelict Custom House Docks; reasons (low tax rate, government incentives, educated English-speaking workforce, EU access, time zone, regeneration of decline); growth into one of the world's main centres for investment funds and for aircraft leasing; effects (jobs, regeneration of the north docklands, Luas Red Line and new housing nearby).
- [ ] B3 Problems and risks: the 2008 banking crisis (Anglo Irish Bank nationalised 2009; NAMA 2009); reputation and regulation; Brexit as an opportunity: banks moved EU operations to Dublin (section 2).
- [ ] B4 Global comparison: the City of London, the "Big Bang" deregulation of 1986, Canary Wharf, and Brexit moving some EU activity to Dublin, Frankfurt and Paris.

Mass tourism and tourism in Ireland
- [ ] T1 Factors influencing the development of tourism in an area (SimpleStudy's "factors" topic sits here): physical attractions (climate, coast, scenery, mountains, lakes); cultural and historic attractions (heritage sites, language, music, festivals, sport); access (airports, ports, roads, low-cost airlines); accommodation and services; marketing and government agencies; cost and exchange rates; safety and political stability.
- [ ] T2 Tourism's importance to Ireland: overseas visitors and jobs (section 2); the agencies Fáilte Ireland and Tourism Ireland; marketing brands (Wild Atlantic Way, Ireland's Ancient East); the COVID-19 collapse in 2020–2021 and its effects (job losses, closed hotels, lost revenue for remote areas). Name Irish international airports and ferry ports.
- [ ] T3 Why the east coast gets most visitors (exams ask this): Dublin Airport and Dublin Port are the main entry points; Dublin is the capital with the most attractions, hotels and events; short trips (city breaks); good motorway and rail links from Dublin.
- [ ] T4 Irish tourism region case study: Co. Kerry (Killarney and the Ring of Kerry) as a mass-tourism region (section 2): attractions, access, accommodation, history of tourism; positive impacts (jobs, income in a peripheral region, services kept open, heritage protected); negative impacts (seasonality, congestion on narrow roads, litter and erosion on popular trails, holiday homes raising house prices).
- [ ] T5 European mass-tourism case study: Spain's Mediterranean coasts and islands (Costa del Sol, Costa Blanca/Benidorm, the Balearic Islands) (section 2): causes (sun, beaches, package holidays and cheap flights from the 1960s, government promotion, low costs, proximity to northern Europe); effects (jobs, foreign earnings, infrastructure); problems (high-rise coastal overdevelopment, water shortages and drought, seasonal low-paid jobs, overtourism protests and housing pressure); responses (tourist taxes, limits on tourist apartments in Barcelona).
- [ ] T6 Exams also ask for two named Irish tourist attractions: use section 2 only.

Evidence of industrial decline
- [ ] D1 Industrial decline defined: the long-term fall in the number of firms and jobs in a traditional industry or region. Evidence: plant closures, derelict sites and buildings, high unemployment, emigration or out-migration, falling population in industrial towns, social problems.
- [ ] D2 Causes in Ireland: the end of protection and the opening to free trade (Anglo-Irish Free Trade Agreement 1965, EEC 1973), exposing small, inefficient protected firms; competition from low-wage countries; recessions (1980s, 2008); new technology replacing labour; EU policy reform; corporate decisions to move production (link to s2).
- [ ] D3 Irish examples with dates (section 2): Cork's 1980s closures (Ford, Dunlop, Verolme Dockyard); Irish Steel at Haulbowline (2001); Irish sugar factories (2005–2006); Waterford Crystal (2009); Dell manufacturing in Limerick (2009; case study in `geo-economic-globalisation-s2`); peat-fired power stations and coal at Moneypoint (energy transition).
- [ ] D4 Effects: 1980s unemployment and emigration (section 2); derelict docklands; loss of skills and spending in local towns.
- [ ] D5 Responses: IDA and Enterprise Ireland replacement investment; regeneration (Dublin Docklands and IFSC; Cork Docklands); heritage tourism reuse (the House of Waterford Crystal); retraining; the EU Just Transition Fund for the Midlands after peat.
- [ ] D6 European regions of industrial decline (the Ruhr, Sambre–Meuse, South Wales) are taught in `geo-region-concept-s4`: one pointer only.

Regional variation within Ireland (one block; core–periphery theory is in `geo-region-concept-s4`)
- [ ] V1 Ireland's economic activity is uneven: the Greater Dublin Area and other cities (Cork, Galway, Limerick) hold most multinational and service jobs; the Border, Midlands and West have more small farms, fewer FDI jobs and lower incomes. The BMW / Southern and Eastern split (1999) for EU funding. National Planning Framework (2018) aims to rebalance growth. Keep to two or three bullets.

## 3. Traps

- Construction is **secondary**, not tertiary. Forestry and mining are primary. Tourism, banking, teaching and transport are tertiary. Pastoral farming is livestock, not crops.
- Do not copy any figure from past-paper charts or infographics (sector employment percentages, airport passenger shares, crop tonnages, tourist attraction visitor counts, fish export values, visitor spend, unemployment by year). They are data-response material. The figures in section 2 come from other sources and must be quoted with their year.
- The IFSC tax rate was **10%**, not 12.5%; 12.5% is the general rate from 2003. Don't say Ireland's corporation tax is "10%" today.
- Shannon's duty-free shop (1947) came **before** the Free Zone (1959). Don't merge the dates.
- Intel opened in Leixlip in **1989**, Apple in Cork in **1980**, Dell in Limerick in **1990**. Don't swap them. Give no Intel job number.
- Dell's 2009 move to Poland is the standard **product life cycle / corporate strategy** case study in `geo-economic-globalisation-s2`. Here it is one bullet of evidence of decline; don't explain the mechanism.
- DEC's Galway closure (1993) and Boston Scientific (1994) are in `geo-region-ireland-s2`; mention DEC only in s2 of this chapter if at all.
- The Ruhr, Sambre–Meuse and South Wales coalfield are taught in `geo-region-concept-s4`. Don't re-teach them here.
- Core and peripheral region theory and the 30-mark "core vs peripheral economic activity" answer belong to `geo-region-concept-s4`. Keep V1 to a few bullets.
- The Mezzogiorno's tourism is in `geo-region-european-s2` and the Paris Basin's in `geo-region-european-s1`. Use Spain for mass tourism here; don't repeat Italy or France.
- GNP, GDP and HDI as measures, and developing economies (India, colonialism), belong to `geo-economic-development`. Multinational structure, sourcing and markets, the trading triad and the international division of labour belong to `geo-economic-globalisation`. CAP, CFP and EU regional and social funds belong to `geo-economic-eu`. Renewable energy, pollution and sustainability belong to `geo-economic-environment`. Mention these only as one-clause links.
- Location factors for individual plants (raw materials, labour, markets, transport) and mapwork siting questions (hotel, factory, fish plant) are in s2. Here, describe why **regions** developed their activities.
- Kinsale Head gas field: no checked dates. Don't use it.
- Don't call Benidorm, Torremolinos or Magaluf "in the Canary Islands". The Canaries are in the Atlantic off Africa; the Balearics are in the Mediterranean.
- Don't say Killarney National Park is a UNESCO site. Skellig Michael is.
- Silicon Docks is Grand Canal Dock, south of the Liffey; the IFSC is on the north bank. Don't mix them.
- Don't state the Brexit quota cut, the US tariff rate, the size of the Apple payment beyond "about €13 billion plus interest", or any Bord na Móna date.

## 4. What exams ask (topics only)

- 30 marks (Ordinary, sometimes Higher): describe and explain the development of one of tourism, financial services, or service and footloose industries in a developed economy. Expected answer: one activity only, with Irish dated examples (IFSC 1987, Silicon Docks, Kerry) and reasons, effects and problems, about 15 SRPs.
- Classify jobs or photos as primary, secondary or tertiary (miner, teacher, banking, construction, forestry, manufacturing, tourism); name another primary or tertiary activity.
- Employment by sector across countries: which country is most developed (highest tertiary share), and why primary employment falls as countries develop.
- Data-response on Irish agriculture: name two crops; one food and one beverage product; benefit of a large cattle herd; one environmental problem of livestock; why wood processing employs few; pastoral vs arable true/false.
- Data-response on Irish fishing: name two fishing ports; advantages Ireland has for fishing; why most fish exports go to the EU; one advantage of fishing to the economy; why the EU fishing fleet has shrunk (quotas, overfishing, decommissioning).
- Data-response on tourism: name two attractions; one advantage of tourism; why the east coast attracts more visitors; why visitor numbers fell in 2020–2021; one challenge caused by fewer tourists; name an Irish airport and ferry port; positive and negative impacts of tourism in a region.
- Data-response on industry: one factor attracting industries to Ireland; two challenges facing Irish industries; the dominance of pharmaceuticals and electronics.
- Short questions on the economy: one benefit of high employment; why the Irish government has a large debt.
- Evidence and causes of industrial decline (Higher 30 marks, sometimes combined with a named region).

## 5. Practice questions to write

1. 30 marks, Higher: "Describe and explain the development of financial services in a developed economy you have studied." Model answer: the IFSC, Dublin, only: origins in 1987 on the Custom House Docks, reasons (tax, incentives, workforce, EU access, time zone), growth in funds and aircraft leasing (Shannon/GPA link), Brexit relocations, effects on the docklands, risks shown by 2008. About 15 SRPs, all from section 2.
2. 30 marks, Higher: "Examine the evidence of industrial decline in a developed economy you have studied." Model answer: Ireland: definition; causes (end of protection, free trade, competition, recession, corporate relocation); dated examples (Cork 1980s closures, Irish Steel 2001, sugar 2005–2006, Waterford Crystal 2009, Dell 2009, peat stations 2020); effects (1980s unemployment about 17%, emigration, dereliction); responses (Docklands, IDA, Just Transition). About 15 SRPs.
3. 40 marks, Ordinary: "(i) Name one tertiary activity in a developed economy you have studied. (ii) Explain the advantages this economy has for it. (iii) Describe one challenge it faces." Model answer: tourism in Ireland, using Kerry and Dublin examples; advantages (scenery, heritage, access, marketing, English language); one challenge only (seasonality, or the COVID-19 collapse), explained in about 4 SRPs.

Each model answer covers exactly what its question asks, nothing appended.
