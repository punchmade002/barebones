# Package: geo-graph-skills-s1 — Figure interpretation

- **loId:** `geo-graph-skills-s1` · **chapterId:** `geo-graph-skills` · **syllabusRef:** 3 (Skills)
- **Syllabus:** Higher Level, "3. Figure interpretation. Students should analyse and evaluate
  information in the form of figures, graphs, and tables." Ordinary Level: "Students should
  represent and understand information in the form of figures, graphs, and tables." The skills
  listed for every unit include "figure interpretation", "statistical analysis" and "figure
  drawing"; the general skills list includes "figures (line graphs, bar graphs, pie charts,

## 1. Must cover (one bullet or block for every item; tick each off)

Reading any figure or table
- [ ] R1 First read the title, the axis labels, the key/legend, the units (%, thousands, millions, €m, hectares, mm, °C, per 1,000) and the dates or years shown, and any source line. Most lost marks come from misread units or the wrong year or column.
- [ ] R2 Reading values: for a bar or line, run a ruler from the top of the bar or point across to the vertical axis; estimate between gridlines; quote the value with its unit. For a table, find the row and the column and read the cell where they meet.
- [ ] R3 Finding highest/lowest, ranking, "how many were above the average line", "which had twice as much as …" (compare values; double one and look for the match).
- [ ] R4 Tables: row and column headings; totals rows; comparing two years in neighbouring columns; spotting the one that did not change.
- [ ] R5 Infographics (the format of many census and CSO figures): headline numbers, icons, small charts and county maps combined on one page. Read each panel's own label and unit; headline figures are often totals, panels may be percentages.

Describing and explaining trends
- [ ] T1 Trend vocabulary: increase/rise/growth; decrease/fall/decline; steady/constant/no change; fluctuate; peak (highest point) and trough (lowest point); gradual vs rapid/sharp; levelling off; overall (long-term) trend vs short-term change.
- [ ] T2 Describing a trend in an answer: state the overall direction; give start and end values with years and units; name the period of fastest change and any peak, trough or reversal; quantify the change (difference and/or percentage change); point out anomalies (values that break the pattern).
- [ ] T3 Comparing two series (multiple line or multiple bar): which is higher, when lines cross, whether the gap widens or narrows, the difference in a given year (e.g. net migration = immigration − emigration).
- [ ] T4 Explaining a trend: link the pattern to a cause from the relevant chapter (one clause). Describe = what the figure shows; explain = why. The data questions add a short "explain one reason/benefit" part; that content belongs to the chapter the data is about, not here.
- [ ] T5 Evaluating a figure (Higher: "analyse and evaluate"): is the scale misleading (axis not starting at zero, uneven intervals); are the dates far apart or unevenly spaced; is the sample small; is the source reliable and recent; are percentages hiding small totals; does correlation prove cause (it does not).

Statistical terms and calculations (each with a worked example from section 2)
- [ ] S1 Total (sum) and difference (subtract the smaller from the larger; say which is larger).
- [ ] S2 Mean (average) = total of the values ÷ number of values. Round sensibly and keep the unit.
- [ ] S3 Median (middle value when values are put in order; average of the middle two for an even number) and mode (most frequent value). One bullet each.
- [ ] S4 Range = highest value − lowest value.
- [ ] S5 Percentage of a total = part ÷ total × 100.
- [ ] S6 Percentage change = (new value − old value) ÷ old value × 100; positive = increase, negative = decrease. Always divide by the OLD (earlier) value.
- [ ] S7 Percentage change vs percentage-point difference: a rise from 20% to 30% is a 10 percentage-point increase but a 50% increase. When both values are already percentages and a question asks "by what percentage did X increase", the subtraction is the usual reading: give the answer in percentage points, and add the percentage change if unsure. Use S6 when the values are numbers rather than percentages.
- [ ] S8 Calculating a missing value X: parts of a whole add up to 100% (pie, percentage table) or to a stated total (stacked bar, total row); X = total − sum of the known parts. Finding a number from a percentage: number = percentage ÷ 100 × total.
- [ ] S9 Rates and ratios: rate per 1,000 (e.g. birth rate = births ÷ population × 1,000); density = number ÷ area (people per km²); "twice as much", "half", ratio (e.g. 2 : 1). One bullet each.
- [ ] S10 Rounding and units: round to the precision the data uses (or one decimal place); state units and the multiplier (thousands, millions) in every answer; show the working in calculation questions (a correct answer with working is safe; working can earn marks if the answer slips).

Drawing a graph: the rules (marks are given for each)
- [ ] D1 Use graph paper and a pencil and ruler; draw the graph large enough to fill most of the space.
- [ ] D2 Title: says what the data is, where and when (taken from the question's own wording).
- [ ] D3 Axes: horizontal (x) axis for the categories or time; vertical (y) axis for the values. Label both axes, with units.
- [ ] D4 Scale: starts at zero, evenly divided, with intervals chosen so the largest value fits (e.g. every 5 or 10 units). Time intervals on a line graph must be evenly spaced in proportion to the years.
- [ ] D5 Accurate plotting of every value; bars of equal width with equal gaps; points joined with ruled straight lines.
- [ ] D6 Key/legend whenever more than one colour or shading is used; shade or colour each category distinctly.
- [ ] D7 Choosing a suitable graph (the "draw a suitable graph" question): one value for separate categories → bar chart; change over time → line graph (a bar chart is usually also accepted); parts of a whole/percentage shares adding to 100% → pie chart (or divided bar); two related variables for the same places → scatter graph; three components adding to 100% → triangular graph; age and sex structure → population pyramid; monthly temperature and rainfall → climate graph; values for areas on a map → choropleth map. One sentence of justification earns the choice.

Graph types: what each shows, how to draw it, how to read it (each a short block)
- [ ] G1 Simple bar chart: separate categories, one bar per category, equal widths and gaps, vertical or horizontal.
- [ ] G2 Multiple (clustered/comparative) bar chart: two or more bars side by side per category (e.g. two years, males and females); needs a key.
- [ ] G3 Compound (stacked/divided) bar chart: each bar split into components; the top of the bar is the total; read a component as top of its segment − bottom of its segment. Percentage compound bar: every bar reaches 100%.
- [ ] G4 Histogram: like a bar chart but for continuous data in classes (e.g. age groups, rainfall amounts), with no gaps between bars. One bullet distinguishing it from a bar chart.
- [ ] G5 Line graph (trend graph): one variable over time; plot points at the correct years and join with ruled lines; multiple line graph for two or more series with a key. Read the gradient: steeper = faster change.
- [ ] G6 Pie chart: parts of a whole. Degrees = percentage × 3.6 (because 360° ÷ 100 = 3.6); for raw numbers, degrees = value ÷ total × 360. Draw a circle with a compass, draw a radius to 12 o'clock, measure each angle clockwise with a protractor (largest segment first is the usual convention), shade, key or label each segment with its percentage. Check the angles add to 360°. Two pie charts compare shares between years, not totals (unless the circles are drawn in proportion to the totals).
- [ ] G7 Divided (percentage) bar: a single bar split into shares adding to 100%; an alternative to a pie chart. One bullet.
- [ ] G8 Pictograms and infographics: icons stand for a fixed quantity (check the key; part-icons are fractions).
- [ ] G9 Population pyramid (age-sex pyramid): back-to-back horizontal bar chart (a histogram on its side); age groups (usually 5-year bands, 0–4 at the bottom, oldest at the top) on the central vertical axis; males on the left, females on the right; horizontal axis is % of the total population (or number), scaled outward from zero on both sides. Reading it: wide base = high birth rate; narrow top = low life expectancy; a narrowing base = falling birth rate; bulges and indentations = baby booms, migration, war or famine. Describe dependent (young and old) and working-age groups. The shapes and what they say about stages of demographic transition belong to `geo-population-migration`; give one sentence linking there.
- [ ] G10 Climate graph: months J to D along the bottom; rainfall as bars against a left-hand axis in mm; temperature as a line (plotted at the middle of each month) against a right-hand axis in °C. Reading it: hottest and coldest months; annual temperature range (max − min mean monthly temperature); mean annual temperature (sum of 12 months ÷ 12); total annual rainfall (sum of 12 bars); wettest and driest months; seasonal pattern (rain all year vs a wet and dry season). Identifying the climate type from the graph is a link to `geo-region-concept-s1` and `geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s5`; one bullet.
- [ ] G11 Scatter graph: two variables for the same set of places; independent variable on the x-axis; plot each place as a point (do not join the points); draw a line of best fit by eye. Positive correlation (both rise together), negative correlation (one rises as the other falls), no correlation; strong vs weak; outliers (anomalies). Correlation does not prove cause.
- [ ] G12 Triangular graph: shows three components that add up to 100% (e.g. % employed in primary, secondary and tertiary sectors; sand, silt and clay in a soil). Each side is a 0–100% scale; read each component along the gridlines parallel to the side where its 0 is, following the direction the scale's numbers run; check the three readings add to 100. Plotting: find the first value on its scale, follow the gridline in, find where it meets the second value's gridline, and check with the third. Points clustered near a corner are dominated by that component.
- [ ] G13 Choropleth map: areas (counties, regions, countries) shaded by value class; darker shading = higher value; classes must not overlap and must cover all values (e.g. 0–49, 50–99, 100–149, 150+); a key is essential. Good for densities and percentages; weakness: implies the value is even across each area and hides variation inside it, and boundaries look like sudden changes.
- [ ] G14 Other statistical maps, one bullet each: dot map (each dot = a fixed number, shows distribution); isoline map (lines joining equal values, e.g. isobars, isotherms, isohyets, contours); flow-line map (arrow width proportional to the amount moving, e.g. migration, traffic, trade); proportional symbols (circles or bars sized in proportion to the value, placed on the map).
- [ ] G15 Tables as figures: when to use a table rather than a graph (many exact values); a table cannot show a trend at a glance.

Using figures in the Geographical Investigation and other units
- [ ] U1 One bullet: graphs, maps and tables are expected in the investigation report's presentation of results; the report's structure is in `geo-mapwork-s3`.

### Worked-example specs (all invented; the writer must say "illustrative" each time)

- **W1 Simple bar chart (D1–D7, G1).** Illustrative population of six towns, in thousands:
  Town A 18, Town B 32, Town C 25, Town D 41, Town E 12, Town F 29. Graph choice: bar chart
  (one value for separate categories). Title "Population of six towns (thousands),
  illustrative"; x-axis "Town"; y-axis "Population (thousands)" from 0 to 45 in steps of 5;
  six equal bars with equal gaps.
- **W2 Statistics on W1 data (S1–S5).** Total = 157 thousand. Mean = 157 ÷ 6 = 26.2 thousand
  (to one decimal place). Ordered: 12, 18, 25, 29, 32, 41; median = (25 + 29) ÷ 2 = 27
  thousand. No mode (no value repeats). Range = 41 − 12 = 29 thousand. Town D's share of the
  total = 41 ÷ 157 × 100 = 26.1%. Town D is more than three times the size of Town E.
- **W3 Line graph and trend (G5, T1–T2, S6–S7).** Illustrative percentage of households in
  Country P with broadband: 2000 20%, 2005 38%, 2010 61%, 2015 74%, 2020 86%, 2025 90%.
  Trend: a continuous rise; fastest 2005–2010 (+23 percentage points); slowing 2020–2025 (+4
  points), levelling off. Change 2000–2025 = +70 percentage points; percentage change =
  (90 − 20) ÷ 20 × 100 = 350%. Use this to show the S7 difference.
- **W4 Pie chart (G6, S8).** Illustrative electricity sources in Country Q, %: gas 40, wind 25,
  solar 10, coal 5, hydro 5, other 15 (total 100). Degrees: 144°, 90°, 36°, 18°, 18°, 54°
  (total 360°). Missing-value version: give all but "other" and show X = 100 − 85 = 15%.
- **W5 Climate graph (G10).** Illustrative Station M. Mean monthly temperature (°C) J–D: 5, 5,
  7, 9, 12, 14, 16, 16, 14, 11, 8, 6. Rainfall (mm) J–D: 110, 80, 85, 60, 65, 70, 70, 85, 90,
  115, 110, 120. Results: warmest July and August (16°C); coldest January and February (5°C);
  annual range 11°C; mean annual temperature 123 ÷ 12 = 10.3°C; total rainfall 1,060 mm;
  wettest December (120 mm); driest April (60 mm); rain in every month with a winter maximum,
  mild winters and cool summers. The writer may say this pattern resembles a cool temperate
  oceanic climate, without naming a real station.
- **W6 Scatter graph (G11).** Illustrative distance from a city centre (km) and land value
  (€ per m²) at eight sites: 1 km 900; 2 km 700; 3 km 620; 4 km 450; 5 km 400; 6 km 250;
  7 km 260; 8 km 150. Distance on the x-axis (independent variable). Strong negative
  correlation; the 7 km site is a slight anomaly (higher than the 6 km site). Explanation link
  in one clause: accessibility falls with distance from the centre.
- **W7 Triangular graph (G12).** Illustrative employment structure: Country X primary 10%,
  secondary 25%, tertiary 65%; Country Y primary 60%, secondary 15%, tertiary 25%. Each total
  100%. Plot both; X sits near the tertiary corner (typical of a developed economy), Y near
  the primary corner (typical of a less developed economy).
- **W8 Compound bar and missing value (G3, S8).** Illustrative tree planting in Region R in one
  year: total 2,000 hectares, of which conifers 1,350 ha; broadleaf X = 2,000 − 1,350 = 650 ha;
  broadleaf share = 650 ÷ 2,000 × 100 = 32.5%.
- **W9 Choropleth (G13).** Illustrative population densities of five regions (people per km²):
  North 35, East 160, South 85, West 40, Midlands 60. Classes 0–49, 50–99, 100–149, 150+,
  shaded lightest to darkest; North and West lightest, East darkest; key required. Note the
  empty 100–149 class still appears in the key.
- **W10 Population pyramid method (G9).** Method only, with one illustrative band: in Country Z,
  males aged 20–24 are 3.5% and females aged 20–24 are 3.4% of the total population; draw a bar
  3.5 units long to the left of the centre axis and 3.4 to the right at the 20–24 level. No full
  invented pyramid; interpretation of real pyramids is in `geo-population-migration`.
- **W11 Choosing a graph (D7).** A table of five illustrative datasets with the right graph and a
  one-line reason each: population of towns → bar; % urban population 1980–2020 → line;
  car sales by fuel type in one year (%) → pie; GNI and birth rate for 20 countries → scatter;
  % of soil that is sand, silt and clay at five sites → triangular.

## 3. Traps

- **Never use real data or figures from past papers** (no real countries, counties, years or
  values attached to the worked examples). Use the invented labels and values above.
- Percentage change divides by the OLD value, not the new one; and a change in a percentage is
  in percentage points. Students mix these up (see W3).
- The vertical axis on a bar chart drawn in an exam must start at 0 and be evenly scaled; uneven
  scales and missing zeros lose marks.
- A line graph's time axis must be spaced in proportion to the years: if data is for 1990, 2000,
  2010, 2015, the last gap is half the others.
- Bar chart ≠ histogram: bar charts have gaps (separate categories); histograms have none
  (continuous classes).
- Pie chart: × 3.6 for percentages, not × 3.6 for raw numbers (use value ÷ total × 360). Angles
  must total 360°.
- Scatter graph points are not joined. Correlation is not causation.
- Population pyramid: males left, females right; horizontal axis is percentage (or number), not
  age. Don't teach the demographic transition model or interpret pyramid shapes in depth: that is
  `geo-population-migration-s1`.
- Climate graph: temperature is a line, rainfall is bars; two different vertical axes. Climate
  types themselves belong to `geo-region-concept-s1` and `geo-option-atmosphere-ocean-s5`.
- Daily weather maps, station models, synoptic charts, weather instruments and wind roses belong
  to `geo-weather-climate-s1`; satellite image matching belongs to `geo-weather-climate-s2`.
  Leave them out (isobars may be named once as an example of an isoline).
- OS map skills (grid references, scale, gradient, cross-sections) are in `geo-mapwork-s1`.
  Statistical maps (choropleth, dot, isoline, flow-line, proportional symbols) are taught here.
- Census content (what the census is, its dates, demographic structure, local-area study) is in
  `geo-graph-skills-s2`. Here, census figures appear only as a type of data to graph.
- The short "explain one reason/benefit" add-ons in data questions (forestry, renewable energy,
  migration, employment) are taught in their own chapters. Don't teach those topics here.

## 4. What exams ask (topics only)

- Short questions (2 marks each, Higher and Ordinary Paper 1 style): read a value from a bar
  chart, line graph, pie chart, table or infographic; identify the highest/lowest; count how many
  items are above an average line; find an item that is twice another; identify the year of no
  change; tick whether the trend is increasing or decreasing; calculate a difference, a mean, a
  percentage of a total, a percentage change, or a missing value X (from 100% or a total row).
- Data-response parts of long questions (Higher 20 marks, Ordinary 30 marks): a chart or table of
  social or economic data (migration, asylum applications, energy sources, exports, farm sizes,
  employment, population, life expectancy, urbanisation), with several reading and calculation
  parts and one or two short "explain" parts.
- Drawing a graph on graph paper from a small data table: the suitable graph has been a bar chart
  (values for separate countries or towns), a line graph (change over time) and a pie chart
  (percentage shares of one total). Marks for: title, labelled axes, evenly divided scale from
  zero, accurate plotting, equal bars, key/shading.
- Higher Level: evaluating and analysing data (trends, comparisons, anomalies).
