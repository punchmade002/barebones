# Package: geo-economic-eu-s2 — Irish trading patterns with the EU

- **loId:** `geo-economic-eu-s2` · **chapterId:** `geo-economic-eu` · **syllabusRef:** 4.4
- **Syllabus:** Statement: "Ireland as a member of the EU is part of a major trading bloc
  within the global economy." Students should study "Irish trading patterns with the EU and
  how the EU influences the Irish economy" (national setting: "Appropriate relevant national
  examples"). This subsection takes the first half of that bullet; the EU's influence (CAP, CFP,
  regional and social funds) is `geo-economic-eu-s3`.
- **Prepared by:** a higher model, 2026-10-20. The writer works only from this package and
  `course-outlines/NOTES_STANDARD.md`.
- **Scale:** must carry a 30-mark Higher answer (about 15 SRPs) on how and why Ireland's trade

## 1. Must cover (one bullet or block for every item; tick each off)

Before membership
- [ ] P1 Before 1973 Ireland's trade depended on the UK: most exports went there, mainly live cattle and other farm produce; trade was governed by the Anglo-Irish Free Trade Agreement (1965). Economic protectionism ended from the late 1950s.
- [ ] P2 Ireland joined the EEC on 1 January 1973 (with the UK and Denmark), after a referendum in May 1972.

Changing pattern since 1973
- [ ] C1 Diversification of markets: the UK's share of Irish exports fell steadily; the rest of the EU and the USA grew in importance.
- [ ] C2 Change in what is exported: from mainly agricultural goods and live animals to high-value manufactured goods (pharmaceuticals and chemicals, medical devices, computers and electronics in the 1990s–2000s) and services; processed food and drink rather than live animals.
- [ ] C3 Exports far exceed imports: Ireland has a very large goods trade surplus, mainly because of foreign multinationals that export most of what they make.

Ireland's goods trade today
- [ ] T1 Total goods exports and imports, and the surplus (2024).
- [ ] T2 Export markets: the EU 27 is the largest bloc (about two-fifths); the USA is the largest single country (about one-third). Main EU markets: the Netherlands, Germany, Belgium, France.
- [ ] T3 Import sources: the EU 27 is the largest bloc; the USA and the UK are the largest single countries; Asia (especially China) is growing.
- [ ] T4 Main exports: medical and pharmaceutical products (the largest group, about half), organic chemicals, medical devices, food and drink.
- [ ] T5 Main imports: fuels (Ireland imports most of its energy), machinery and transport equipment (including aircraft for the leasing industry), chemicals, consumer goods, food.
- [ ] T6 2025 surge: front-loading of pharmaceutical exports to the USA ahead of US tariffs shows how dependent the totals are on a few products and one market.

Services trade
- [ ] V1 Ireland is a major exporter of services, especially computer and software services from multinational firms (e.g. Google, Microsoft, Meta in Dublin), plus financial services (IFSC) and aircraft leasing. Much of this is sold into EU markets from an Irish base. No figures.

Agri-food trade with the EU
- [ ] A1 Ireland exports about 90% of its beef and dairy output; it produces far more food than its population eats.
- [ ] A2 Food, drink and horticulture exports (2024): about €17 billion, split roughly a third each between the EU, the UK and international markets. Dairy is the largest category.
- [ ] A3 The UK is still the largest single market for Irish food; meat goes almost equally to the EU and the UK.
- [ ] A4 Brands and products: Kerrygold butter (Ornua) in Germany and other EU markets; Irish beef in EU retailers; whiskey and Baileys. No sales figures.

Why Ireland trades so heavily with the EU (factors)
- [ ] F1 Single market: tariff-free, barrier-free access to about 450 million consumers.
- [ ] F2 Foreign direct investment: US and other multinationals locate in Ireland to supply the EU market from inside it (English-speaking, educated workforce, 12.5%/15% corporation tax, IDA Ireland). Examples: Intel (Leixlip), Apple (Cork), Pfizer. Details of these firms are in `geo-economic-activities` / `geo-economic-globalisation`; one bullet here.
- [ ] F3 The euro: no exchange-rate risk when trading with eurozone countries; sterling's fluctuations make UK trade riskier (e.g. the 2016 fall in sterling hurt Irish food exporters to the UK).
- [ ] F4 CAP access and common standards opened EU food markets to Irish farm produce (detail in s3).
- [ ] F5 Transport: Ireland is an island on the periphery; most trade goes by sea through Dublin Port, Rosslare Europort, Cork and Shannon Foynes, and high-value, light goods (pharmaceuticals, electronics) by air.

Brexit and Irish trade with the EU
- [ ] B1 The UK left the EU (31 January 2020; transition ended 31 December 2020): Irish trade with Great Britain now crosses an EU external border, with customs declarations and checks on food and animals.
- [ ] B2 The UK "landbridge" (trucks crossing Britain to reach the continent) became slower; direct ferry routes from Ireland to the continent expanded (Rosslare–Dunkirk, Rosslare–Cherbourg, Rosslare–Bilbao and others).
- [ ] B3 Imports from Great Britain fell after 2021 and some importers switched to EU suppliers; cross-border trade with Northern Ireland grew, as Northern Ireland stays in the EU single market for goods under the Windsor Framework (point to `geo-region-european-s3`).
- [ ] B4 Brexit raised the share of Ireland's trade with the rest of the EU and reduced its dependence on the UK further.

Issues
- [ ] X1 Over-reliance on a few multinationals, one sector (pharmaceuticals) and one country (the USA): exposure to US tariffs and to corporate decisions.
- [ ] X2 Peripheral location adds transport costs; sea links to the continent are vital.

## 3. Traps

- The USA, not the UK or Germany, is Ireland's largest single export market (2024). The EU 27 together take more than the USA.
- Don't say the UK is Ireland's largest export market today. It remains the largest single market for Irish **food**, not for goods overall.
- Belgium's high figure reflects pharmaceutical and chemical flows; don't explain it as Belgian consumer demand.
- Ireland's export totals are inflated by multinational pharmaceutical shipments; 2025 is unusual (tariff front-loading). Use 2024 as the typical year and say so.
- Past-paper model answers include unchecked figures (e.g. "EU trade is about 55% of Ireland's total trade", "farming employs about 7%"). Use only section 2 figures.
- No checked figure for 1973 trade shares or for Irish services trade: describe the trend without numbers.
- The Northern Ireland Protocol / Windsor Framework and the Common Travel Area are taught in `geo-region-european-s3`. One cross-reference bullet only.
- MNC case studies (Intel, Apple, Pfizer structures, product life cycle) belong to `geo-economic-globalisation`; the Irish developed-economy case study (footloose industry, financial services) to `geo-economic-activities`. Name the firms only as reasons for trade.
- CAP, CFP and EU funds belong to `geo-economic-eu-s3`. EU-wide trading patterns belong to `geo-economic-eu-s1`.
- Don't give a Kerrygold market-share ranking or a beef "largest exporter" ranking: not checked.

## 4. What exams ask (topics only)

- Describe and explain Irish trading patterns with the EU: how they changed after 1973 (diversification away from the UK, from farm produce to high-tech goods) and why (single market, FDI, euro, CAP).
- Advantages of EU membership for Ireland's trade and for Irish farmers (tariff-free access to a large market; most beef and dairy output exported).
- Effects of Brexit on Irish trade (landbridge, direct ferries, customs checks, food exports to the UK).
- Data questions on tables of trade or farm output: read values, calculate a missing percentage.
