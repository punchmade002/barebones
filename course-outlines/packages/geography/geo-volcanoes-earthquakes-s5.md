## 1. Must cover (one bullet or block for every item; tick each off)

Stress and deformation
- [ ] D1 Three kinds of stress, matched to their structures (exams ask this as a table): compression (pushing together) causes folding and reverse/thrust faults; tension (pulling apart) causes normal faults; shearing (sliding past) causes tear (strike-slip) faults. One sentence linking each to plate movement; the boundary types themselves are in `geo-plate-tectonics-s3`.
- [ ] D2 Rocks bend (fold) when compressed slowly, especially when deep, hot and under pressure; they break (fault) when the stress is sudden or the rock is brittle.

Folding
- [ ] F1 Compression (as it relates to folding): horizontal pressure squeezes layers of rock into a shorter distance, so they buckle into folds. Exams ask for a short definition.
- [ ] F2 Parts of a fold: anticline (upfold, layers pushed upwards into an arch); syncline (downfold, layers pushed downwards into a trough); limbs (the sides); axis (the line along the crest or trough). Exams label A = anticline, B = syncline.
- [ ] F3 Types of fold, increasing compression, with a diagram cue for each (exams match diagrams): simple (symmetrical) fold; asymmetrical fold (one limb steeper); overfold (one limb pushed over the other); recumbent fold (lying almost flat); overthrust fold or nappe (the fold breaks along a thrust fault and is pushed far forward over other rocks).
- [ ] F4 Fold mountains as landforms, in numbered stages: sediments deposited in a sea between two plates; plates converge (refer to `geo-plate-tectonics-s3` for the boundary in one sentence); the sediments are compressed and folded into anticlines and synclines; continued pressure produces overfolds, recumbent folds and nappes; the crust is thickened and uplifted into long, high ranges parallel to the plate edge. Diagram cue: before-and-after cross-section. This is the answer to "how are fold mountains formed".
- [ ] F5 Young vs old fold mountains: young ranges (Alpine) are high, steep and jagged; old ranges have been worn down by hundreds of millions of years of weathering and erosion into lower, rounded mountains. Ireland's mountains are old fold mountains. Name the Caledonian and Armorican periods only; their dates, causes and trends are in `geo-plate-tectonics-s2`.
- [ ] F6 Differential erosion in folded rock: the landform depends on which rocks are exposed; in Munster the resistant sandstone survives on the anticlines as ridges and the weaker limestone survives in the synclines as valleys.
- [ ] F7 Folding and drainage: rivers follow the weak rock along the synclines and cut across the ridges where they break through, giving trellised drainage and sharp right-angled bends.

Settings for folding
- [ ] S1 South Ireland ridge-and-valley province (Munster), in full: Armorican folding; east–west ridges of Old Red Sandstone and valleys of Carboniferous limestone; named ridges and valleys; rivers flowing east then turning south; the drowned valleys at the coast are rias (one clause; rias are in `geo-glaciation-s2`).
- [ ] S2 The Armorican (Variscan) front: the northern limit of strong Armorican folding running east–west across Munster; to the south, rocks were intensely folded and pushed (thrust) northwards; to the north, folding is gentle.
- [ ] S3 The Alps: young fold mountains from the Alpine folding; recumbent folds and nappes; high jagged peaks.
- [ ] S4 The Appalachians: old fold mountains in eastern North America, folded in the same mountain-building episodes as Ireland's older mountains when the two continents were joined; the Ridge-and-Valley Appalachians show long parallel folded ridges and valleys with trellised drainage.

Doming
- [ ] M1 Doming: layers of rock pushed upwards from below into a dome (a circular or oval upfold), by compression or by rising magma. Exams ask true/false.
- [ ] M2 Erosion of a dome: the top is worn away first, exposing older rocks in the centre; resistant layers form inward-facing escarpments in rings around it; drainage flows outwards (radial).
- [ ] M3 The Weald, south-east England, in full (section 2).

Faulting
- [ ] T1 A fault is a fracture in the crust along which rocks have moved. Vertical displacement (rocks move up or down) vs horizontal displacement (rocks move sideways).
- [ ] T2 Normal fault: tension; one block slides down the fault plane relative to the other; the crust is lengthened.
- [ ] T3 Reverse fault: compression; one block is pushed up the fault plane over the other; the crust is shortened. Thrust fault: a low-angle reverse fault that can push rocks a long way forward (linked to nappes, F3).
- [ ] T4 Tear (strike-slip) fault: shearing; blocks move horizontally past each other. A transform fault is a tear fault at a plate boundary.
- [ ] T5 Fault scarp: a steep slope or cliff where one block has been raised above the other.
- [ ] T6 Horst (block mountain): a block of land raised between two parallel faults, or left standing as the land on either side sinks.
- [ ] T7 Graben (rift valley): a block of land that sinks between two parallel normal faults, forming a long, flat-floored valley with steep walls; often holds lakes. Formation in numbered stages with a diagram cue showing horst and graben together. Exams ask true/false that horsts and grabens are associated with faulting, and to name two landforms of faulting.
- [ ] T8 Landforms of tear faults: straight valleys eroded along the shattered rock of the fault zone; features (ridges, rivers) offset sideways across the fault.
- [ ] T9 Donegal tear faults (national setting): north-east to south-west faults in Donegal along which rivers and glaciers have eroded long, straight valleys.
- [ ] T10 Faults also guide coastlines and drainage, and earthquakes occur along active faults (one bullet; earthquakes are in s2).

## 3. Traps (known errors)

- Normal fault = tension; reverse fault = compression; tear (and transform) fault = shearing. Exams test exactly this; never swap normal and reverse.
- Anticline = up (arch, "A" for apex); syncline = down (trough). In Munster the ridges are anticlines and the valleys synclines; don't reverse them.
- A horst goes up; a graben goes down. A rift valley is a graben, not a river valley.
- The Weald is a dome (eroded anticline), not a basin; the Downs' escarpments face **inwards** towards the Weald. The Paris Basin (s4) is the opposite shape.
- The Alpine folding did not build Ireland's mountains. Ireland's two periods are Caledonian and Armorican.
- The Mourne Mountains are granite intrusions (s3), not fold mountains.
- Carrauntoohil is in Macgillycuddy's Reeks; the glaciation package uses it as a pyramidal peak. Don't describe its glacial features here.
- Don't give heights, fault displacements or dates not in section 2.
- Plate boundary processes (subduction, collision, the dates and causes of the Caledonian and Armorican periods, the closing of the Iapetus Ocean) are in `geo-plate-tectonics-s2` and `-s3`. Here one sentence of cause at most; the structures and landforms are the focus.
- Rias are explained in `geo-glaciation-s2`; Munster rock types (sandstone, limestone) form in `geo-rocks-weathering-s1`. Name only.
- Bedding planes, joints, escarpments on dipping strata and the Paris Basin belong to `geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s4`.
