## 1. Must cover (one bullet or block for every item; tick each off)

A. Changes in the boundaries and extent of language regions: the Gaeltacht since 1850
- [ ] G1 Irish in 1851: the first census to count Irish speakers; numbers and share; Irish-speaking areas then covered much of the west and south.
- [ ] G2 Causes of decline after 1850: the Great Famine (1845–49) and emigration hit the poor, western, Irish-speaking areas hardest; national schools teaching through English (from 1831); English as the language of law, trade, politics, the Church and emigration; parents seeing English as the route to work. 1891 figures show the decline.
- [ ] G3 Revival efforts: the Gaelic League (1893); the new state's language policy (Irish compulsory in schools; Irish required for some public jobs).
- [ ] G4 First official Gaeltacht boundaries: Coimisiún na Gaeltachta (report 1926): Fíor-Ghaeltacht and Breac-Ghaeltacht definitions; a large area across many western and southern counties.
- [ ] G5 1956 redrawing: Gaeltacht Areas Order 1956 shrank the official Gaeltacht to areas where Irish was still the community language; Department of the Gaeltacht (1956); Gaeltarra Éireann (1957). Later small extensions by order.
- [ ] G6 The Gaeltacht today: seven counties; the Meath Gaeltacht (planned colonies, 1935); population and daily speakers (Census 2022); the biggest Gaeltacht areas in Galway and Donegal.
- [ ] G7 Supports: Údarás na Gaeltachta (1980) and Gaeltacht industry (Gaoth Dobhair estate); Raidió na Gaeltachta (1972); TG4 (1996); Official Languages Act 2003; Irish as a full EU official language (2022); Gaeltacht summer colleges; the 20-Year Strategy (2010–2030).
- [ ] G8 Gaeltacht Act 2012: language-based (not map-based) definition: 26 Language Planning Areas, Gaeltacht Service Towns and Irish Language Networks, each with a language plan. Boundaries now depend on language use.
- [ ] G9 Continuing decline and pressures: the 2007 linguistic study (67% daily-speaker threshold) and its 2015 update; in-migration of English speakers, suburban growth (e.g. Galway city's spread west), tourism, emigration of young people, English-language media.
- [ ] G10 Strategies to address decline in daily speakers (exams ask for one): housing language conditions, jobs through Údarás, Irish-medium schooling and preschools, language plans, family supports, media. One block.
- [ ] G11 A brief international comparison (syllabus: "appropriate examples"): Belgium's language border fixed in law in 1962–63 and federalisation (1970 onwards; federal state 1993) as a language-region boundary made official. Keep short; Belgium's present-day interaction is in s3.

B. Urban growth and the expansion of city regions: Dublin council structures
- [ ] U1 Dublin's growth: suburban expansion after 1950s/60s; the Myles Wright plan (1967) and the western new towns (Tallaght, Clondalkin, Lucan, Blanchardstown); Ballymun; sprawl into the commuter counties of the Greater Dublin Area; Census 2022 figures.
- [ ] U2 City boundary extensions: Dublin city absorbed Rathmines and Pembroke in 1930.
- [ ] U3 Dublin council structures: Dublin County Council split on 1 January 1994 into Fingal, South Dublin and Dún Laoghaire–Rathdown; Dublin Corporation renamed Dublin City Council (2002); four local authorities in County Dublin and their populations (Census 2022). Why: the county had grown too large to govern as one unit, and local government needed to be closer to the people.
- [ ] U4 Regional structures: the Dublin Regional Authority (1994) replaced in 2015 by the Eastern and Midland Regional Assembly; the Greater Dublin Area (Dublin, Meath, Kildare, Wicklow).
- [ ] U5 Problems and planning for the expanding city region: sprawl, long commutes, congestion, housing costs, one-off and dormitory growth; the National Planning Framework (2018); transport (M50, Luas, DART); regeneration (Docklands, IFSC, Ballymun); the Citizens' Assembly on a directly elected mayor for Dublin (2023 recommendation; Limerick's directly elected mayor from 2024 as the first).
- [ ] U6 Other Irish city/administrative boundary changes: Cork city boundary extension (2019); Limerick and Waterford city and county councils merged (2014).
- [ ] U7 Irish economic regions (syllabus setting): the 1999/2000 split of Ireland into two NUTS 2 regions for EU funding (Border, Midland and Western with Objective 1 status; Southern and Eastern); the three regional assemblies since 2015. Shows regions redrawn for economic purposes.

C. European Union development and expansion
- [ ] E1 Post-war origins: the reasons (peace after two world wars, rebuilding, coal and steel under shared control, economic growth, the Cold War); Schuman Declaration (1950); ECSC (1951); Treaty of Rome (1957) and the EEC's six founders.
- [ ] E2 Deepening: Single European Act; single market (1993); Maastricht (1992, EU created); euro; Lisbon (2009). Dates only here; the issues are in s3.
- [ ] E3 Widening: each enlargement with dates and countries (1973, 1981, 1986, 1990 German reunification, 1995, 2004, 2007, 2013); the UK's departure (2020); 27 members.
- [ ] E4 Eastern European expansion: after the fall of communism (Berlin Wall 1989); the Copenhagen criteria (1993); the 2004 and 2007 enlargements; effects on the newer members (growth, EU funds, emigration) and on older members.
- [ ] E5 Impact of enlargement on Ireland: advantages (larger market, labour supply in the Celtic Tiger, cultural diversity, export opportunities) and disadvantages (competition for FDI from lower-cost states, e.g. Dell's move from Limerick to Poland; less EU regional funding for Ireland; pressure on wages and services). Ireland opened its labour market at once in 2004; the Polish community as the main example.
- [ ] E6 Future expansion: current candidates (list in s3's F5; refer to it, don't repeat), and benefits of further expansion.

D. Changes in political boundaries and their impact on cultural groups
- [ ] D1 Partition of Ireland: Government of Ireland Act 1920; Northern Ireland 1921; Anglo-Irish Treaty 1921; Boundary Commission 1925 (border left unchanged); the six counties; Ulster divided; a nationalist minority in the north, a small Protestant minority in the south; impact (discrimination, civil rights movement, the Troubles; communities, parishes and farms divided). The present-day interaction (Good Friday Agreement, Brexit) is in s3; one cross-reference bullet only.
- [ ] D2 Break-up of Yugoslavia: a multi-ethnic federal state; independence of Slovenia and Croatia (1991) and Bosnia and Herzegovina (1992); new borders followed old internal republic lines, leaving Serb, Croat and Bosniak minorities on the "wrong" side; war, ethnic cleansing, Srebrenica (1995); Dayton Agreement (1995) dividing Bosnia into two entities; Montenegro (2006) and Kosovo (2008).
- [ ] D3 Post-war Poland and Germany: Poland's borders moved west in 1945 (the Oder–Neisse line); millions of Germans expelled and Poles resettled; Germany divided (Berlin Wall 1961–1989) and reunified 1990.
- [ ] D4 Break-up of the USSR (1991) and Czechoslovakia (1993): Russian minorities left in the Baltic states; the peaceful "Velvet Divorce".
- [ ] D5 Cyprus: divided since 1974 between Greek Cypriots in the south and Turkish Cypriots in the north; the 2004 Annan Plan referendum; the island joined the EU in 2004 but EU law is suspended in the north.
- [ ] D6 Partition of India (1947), as a non-European example linked to the continental region studied: India and Pakistan created along religious lines; mass migration and violence.
- [ ] D7 General conclusions: boundaries drawn across cultural groups create minorities, displacement and conflict; boundaries drawn around groups (Belgium) can reduce conflict but harden division; institutions (power-sharing, EU membership) can soften the effect of a border.

## 3. Traps

- Keep the split with s3. s3 covers the Good Friday Agreement, Brexit and the Protocol/Windsor Framework, present-day Belgium and the EU's future (political union, economic union, sovereignty, candidates). Here: how boundaries changed and the effects on cultural groups. One cross-reference bullet each, no repetition.
- 1851 is the first census to count Irish speakers; don't give Irish-speaker figures for before 1851.
- The Fíor-/Breac-Ghaeltacht thresholds are 80% and 25%. The 67% figure is from the 2007 study, not 1926. Don't mix them.
- Don't give a Gaeltacht land area or a 1926 Gaeltacht population: not checked.
- Don't state that Dublin has a directly elected mayor or give a date for one: only the 2023 recommendation is checked.
- South Dublin is a county council area; don't confuse it with "Dublin South" constituencies. Dún Laoghaire–Rathdown takes an en dash.
- Croatia (2013) is the most recent member. Bulgaria and Romania joined in 2007, not 2004. East Germany joined in 1990 by reunification, not as a new member state.
- Ireland joined in 1973 with the UK and Denmark. Norway signed but voted No (don't add it to any list).
- Don't use Russia's annexation of Crimea or the war in Ukraine as boundary-change examples; they are framed as "territorial disputes" in the 2026 specification. Ukraine appears only as an EU candidate (s3).
- Don't give a Srebrenica figure other than "about 8,000", or a total death toll for the Yugoslav wars (sources vary).
- EU funding effects on Ireland (CAP, CFP, regional and social funds) belong to `geo-economic-eu`; only mention reduced regional funding as an effect of enlargement.
- Past-paper model answers for this chapter contain unchecked claims (e.g. Brussels "85% French-speaking", "1.4 million Serbs outside Serbia", "two million displaced"). Use none of them.
