# Package: geo-option-culture-identity-s2 — Language as a cultural indicator

- **loId:** `geo-option-culture-identity-s2` · **chapterId:** `geo-option-culture-identity` · **syllabusRef:** 8.1
- **Syllabus:** Students should study "language as a cultural indicator and include – major
  language groups – European languages - influence of the mass-media – minority languages -
  policies for survival". National setting: "Irish language Gaeltacht areas." International
  setting: "Appropriate examples, e.g. Welsh." Outcome: "examine language as a cultural
  indicator."
- **Level and exam form:** Higher Level only option. 80-mark essays (about four aspects, about
  25–30 SRPs). Language is the most often examined topic in this option, as both "the
  importance of language as a cultural indicator" and "strategies for the survival of minority
  languages", so the Irish, Welsh and one or two further minority-language studies each need
  full depth.
- **Prepared by:** a higher model, 2026-10-20. The writer works only from this package and
  `course-outlines/NOTES_STANDARD.md`.

## 1. Must cover (one bullet or block for every item; tick each off)

Language as a cultural indicator
- [ ] I1 Why language is a key cultural indicator: it carries a group's history, literature, songs, place names and ways of thinking; it marks who belongs to a group; it is easy to map (census questions on language), so it is used to define cultural regions.
- [ ] I2 Language and nationalism: many nation states were built around a language; language revival was part of the Irish independence movement (the Gaelic League); suppressing a language has been used to weaken a group (Franco's Spain, the "Welsh Not", Turkey's past ban on Kurdish).
- [ ] I3 Language and power: colonial languages became official languages of former colonies (English in India, Nigeria; French in West Africa; Spanish and Portuguese in Latin America); the language of the powerful brings jobs and status, so speakers of minority languages shift to it.
- [ ] I4 Place names as evidence of language and history: Irish place-name elements (baile, cill, dún, inis, cluain) show the Irish-speaking past of areas that are now English-speaking.
- [ ] I5 Language can unite (a shared national language) or divide (Belgium; language borders), and can cross borders (French in France, Belgium, Switzerland, Quebec; Basque in Spain and France).

Major language groups
- [ ] L1 Language family (languages descended from a common ancestor) vs branch vs language vs dialect. About 7,000 languages are spoken today.
- [ ] L2 The main world families with their main languages and regions: Indo-European (Europe, the Americas, Iran, South Asia: the largest); Sino-Tibetan (Mandarin and other Chinese languages, Tibetan, Burmese); Afro-Asiatic (Arabic, Hebrew, Amharic, Hausa); Niger-Congo (most of sub-Saharan Africa, e.g. Swahili, Yoruba, Zulu); Austronesian (Malay/Indonesian, Filipino, Malagasy, Māori, Hawaiian); Dravidian (southern India: Tamil, Telugu); Turkic; Uralic.
- [ ] L3 The most spoken languages: Mandarin (most native speakers) and English (most speakers in total, counting second-language speakers); Spanish, Hindi and Arabic also very widely spoken.
- [ ] L4 How languages spread: migration, conquest and empire (Latin with Rome; English, Spanish, French and Portuguese with colonialism), trade, religion (Arabic with Islam), and today globalisation and the media.

European languages
- [ ] E1 The European branches of Indo-European: Germanic, Romance (from Latin), Slavic, Celtic (Goidelic and Brythonic), Hellenic (Greek), Baltic, Albanian. Non-Indo-European languages: Finnish, Estonian, Hungarian (Uralic/Finno-Ugric); Basque (an isolate); Maltese (Semitic, Afro-Asiatic); Turkish (Turkic). A sketch map of the branches is expected.
- [ ] E2 The EU's 24 official languages; Irish an official EU language (2007, full status 2022). Language learning (e.g. Erasmus exchanges) and English as Europe's main second language.

European languages and the influence of the mass media
- [ ] D1 How mass media spread dominant languages: television, film, music, the internet, social media and streaming are dominated by English (Hollywood, US and UK music, about half of all websites in English); young people adopt English words and English-language culture; minority-language speakers consume most media in the majority language at home.
- [ ] D2 Effects on European languages: English loanwords ("Franglais", e.g. le weekend), English as the language of business, science and the internet; pressure on small national languages as well as minority languages.
- [ ] D3 Responses of states to the media's influence: France (Académie française; the 1994 Toubon Law; the radio quota for French-language songs); Quebec's Charter of the French Language (Bill 101, 1977); Iceland's creation of new Icelandic words for new things.
- [ ] D4 Media used to protect minority languages: TG4 and Raidió na Gaeltachta (Ireland); S4C (Wales); BBC Alba (Scottish Gaelic); EITB (Basque Country). Social media and online content also let small languages reach young people.

Minority languages and policies for their survival
- [ ] P1 Minority language: a language spoken by a minority of a state's population (e.g. Irish, Welsh, Breton, Basque, Scottish Gaelic, Sámi). Endangered and extinct languages: about 40% of the world's languages are endangered; extinct examples (Manx's last native speaker died 1974; Cornish's last traditional speaker 1777), and their revivals.
- [ ] P2 Why minority languages decline (causes): conquest and official suppression; a dominant language needed for jobs, education and emigration; famine and emigration; in-migration of majority-language speakers; urbanisation; mass media and globalisation; parents not passing the language on (inter-generational transmission breaks).
- [ ] P3 Strategies for survival (exams ask for these by heading): official status in law; education through the language (immersion and medium schools, compulsory study); media; jobs and economic development in the language's heartland; planning and housing controls; language commissioners and public-service rights; community and family supports; culture and festivals; technology and online content; cross-border and EU recognition. Exams accept strategies "that have been or could be implemented", so include possible future measures.
- [ ] P4 Irish and the Gaeltacht (national setting; full case study): status in the Constitution; decline and current figures (Census 2022); State bodies and laws (Údarás na Gaeltachta, the Official Languages Act, An Coimisinéir Teanga, Foras na Gaeilge); education (compulsory Irish, Gaelscoileanna, Gaeltacht schools, summer colleges); media (Raidió na Gaeltachta, TG4); the Gaeltacht Act 2012 and language plans; the 20-Year Strategy; EU status; Northern Ireland's 2022 Act; evaluation (high ability, low daily use; Gaeltacht decline from the 2007 study). The history of Gaeltacht boundaries since 1850 is taught in `geo-region-european-s4`; one pointer bullet only.
- [ ] P5 Welsh (international setting; full case study): history of suppression (Laws in Wales Acts, the "Welsh Not", the 1847 Blue Books); decline through industrial in-migration; activism (Cymdeithas yr Iaith, Tryweryn, the S4C campaign); laws (1967, 1993, 2011 Measure, Welsh Language Commissioner); Welsh-medium and compulsory Welsh education; S4C; Cymraeg 2050; Census 2021 figures and the north-west heartland; threats from second homes and in-migration and the 2023 council-tax response; evaluation (the most successful Celtic-language revival).
- [ ] P6 Basque (Euskara): suppression under Franco; autonomy (1979) and co-official status; the school models (Basque-medium model D now the most chosen); the ikastola movement; Basque television; standard Basque (Batua); evaluation (rising use among the young).
- [ ] P7 Breton, as a contrast where state support is weak: France's constitution makes French the language of the Republic; France has not ratified the European Charter for Regional or Minority Languages; Diwan immersion schools rely on voluntary effort; the language has declined sharply and most speakers are elderly.
- [ ] P8 One brief non-European example of revival: Māori in New Zealand (official status 1987, kōhanga reo "language nests") or Hebrew (revived as an everyday language and now Israel's main language). One block maximum.
- [ ] P9 European measures: the Council of Europe's European Charter for Regional or Minority Languages (1992); Irish's EU status.
- [ ] P10 Skill (from an Ordinary-level data question): reading a bar chart of languages spoken by age group (which group speaks most, adding percentages, a promotion idea, an advantage of an extra language). The writer must invent clearly labelled illustrative data.

Second homes and English in-migration push up house prices in Welsh-speaking rural areas; from April 2023 Welsh councils may charge up to a 300% council-tax premium on second homes. ## 3. Traps

- Don't call Flemish a separate language from Dutch; it is the Dutch spoken in Belgium.
- Basque is not a Romance or Celtic language; it is unrelated to any other. Finnish and Hungarian are not Indo-European. Maltese is Semitic. Don't misplace them on the language map.
- Ireland: Irish is the first official language under Article 8; don't call English the first official language. "Can speak Irish" (about 1.87 million) and "speaks Irish daily outside education" (about 72,000) are different measures; always pair them correctly. Census 2016 figures from past-paper answers must not be used.
- The 67% viability threshold is from the 2007 study; the 80%/25% Fíor-/Breac-Ghaeltacht thresholds are from 1926 and belong to `geo-region-european-s4`. Don't mix them.
- Irish became an EU official language in 2007; full status only in 2022. Don't say 1973.
- Don't repeat the myth that the Inuit (or Finnish) have "100 words for snow", or present the Sapir–Whorf hypothesis as proven; a past-paper model answer does both.
- Don't use unchecked statistics from past-paper answers (e.g. "90% of the world's languages face extinction", "over 50% of Basque students", "about 75% of Catalans speak Catalan as a first language", "7,000 Údarás jobs"). Don't give a Basque, Breton, Catalan or Scottish Gaelic speaker number: none checked.
- Don't state the outcome of Ireland's planned public-service Irish targets beyond the 2021 target itself.
- Overlaps: the Gaeltacht's boundary changes since 1850 belong to `geo-region-european-s4` and the Gaeltacht as a cultural region to `geo-region-concept-s3`; here give one pointer bullet and focus on survival policy. Belgium's language border belongs to `geo-region-concept-s3`/`geo-region-european-s4` (and Belgian identity to s6). Switzerland's four languages are taught in s6; mention them once only here. Language as part of a regional identity case study belongs to s6.
- Welsh political history (conquest, devolution, the Senedd) belongs to s6's Celtic-region case study; here keep to language policy.

## 4. What exams ask (topics only)

- 80 marks (set repeatedly): the importance of language as a cultural indicator. Expected aspects: identity and belonging; nationalism and the nation state; language and power/colonialism; language regions and conflict (Belgium); decline and revival (Irish, Welsh); the influence of the mass media.
- 80 marks (set repeatedly): strategies that have been or could be implemented to aid the survival of minority languages. Expected: Irish (laws, education, media, Údarás, Gaeltacht Act, language plans), Welsh (Measure 2011, education, S4C, Cymraeg 2050, second homes), Basque or Breton as a comparison, plus possible future measures; evaluation of success.
- Ordinary data question: a chart of languages spoken in Ireland by age group; reading values, adding percentages, a way to promote Irish, an advantage of speaking another language.

## 5. Practice questions to write

1. 80 marks, Higher: "Examine the strategies that have been or could be implemented to aid the survival of minority languages." Model answer: four aspects (legal status; education; media; economic and community planning), each illustrated with Irish and Welsh facts and one Basque or Breton contrast; about 25–30 SRPs; ends with one or two possible future strategies (e.g. housing language conditions in the Gaeltacht, more online content).
2. 80 marks, Higher: "Examine the importance of language as a cultural indicator." Model answer: identity and place names; nationalism and suppression; language families and colonial spread; mass media and English; minority-language revival as an assertion of identity. About 25–30 SRPs. Don't turn it into a strategy list.
3. 15 marks (data skill): an invented bar chart, clearly labelled "illustrative data", of the percentage of three age groups speaking Irish, French and Polish. Model answer: read the highest value, add one language across the groups, give one way to promote Irish (from P4), and one advantage of another language (jobs with multinationals, travel, understanding another culture).
