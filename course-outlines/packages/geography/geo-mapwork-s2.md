# Package: geo-mapwork-s2 — Textual sources

- **loId:** `geo-mapwork-s2` · **chapterId:** `geo-mapwork` · **syllabusRef:** 3 (Skills)
- **Syllabus:** Higher Level, "6. Textual sources. Students should understand and use, where
  possible, a range of textual and secondary sources of information (from both the past and
  present), particularly in relation to their geographical investigation." Ordinary Level has
  the same wording without "a range of". The general aims list "textual sources with
  geographical terminology" as an information-gathering skill, and the Core Unit 3 skills list
  includes "the use of documentary sources". The evaluation skills in the aims include
  "distinguish fact from opinion, draw conclusions".
- **This is a skills subsection.** It teaches what textual and secondary sources are, how to
  use and evaluate them, and how past sources (especially historical maps) show change. Worked
  examples use the invented town of **Rivertown** from `geo-mapwork-s1`, labelled
  "illustrative". Name no real document, newspaper article, website page or past-paper extract.
- **Prepared by:** a higher model, 2026-10-20. The writer works only from this package and
  `course-outlines/NOTES_STANDARD.md`.

## 1. Must cover (one bullet or block for every item; tick each off)

What the sources are
- [ ] T1 Primary data: information the student collects first-hand (measurements, observations, questionnaires, counts, field sketches, own photographs). Secondary data: information already collected or published by someone else. Textual sources are written secondary sources. One bullet each; the 60:40 ratio for the investigation is in `geo-mapwork-s3`.
- [ ] T2 Types of textual and secondary source, present day: textbooks and reference books; newspapers and magazines; official statistics and reports from state bodies (census reports, weather and climate records, environmental reports); local authority documents (county or city development plans, planning notices); tourist brochures and guides; websites and online databases; academic articles; current maps, aerial photographs and satellite images.
- [ ] T3 Types of source from the past: historical maps (earlier editions of OS maps, old town plans, estate maps); old photographs and postcards; old newspapers; earlier census returns; local histories and parish or school records; directories and travel accounts; archive documents in libraries and county archives.
- [ ] T4 Who produces official data in Ireland: the Central Statistics Office (CSO) runs the census and publishes statistics; Met Éireann is the national meteorological service (weather data); the Environmental Protection Agency (EPA) reports on water and air quality; Tailte Éireann now publishes the Ordnance Survey maps (Ordnance Survey Ireland became part of Tailte Éireann in 2023); local authorities publish development plans. One bullet each, naming only these.

Using textual sources
- [ ] U1 Reading for geography: identify the main idea, pick out facts, figures, places and dates, and translate everyday language into geographical terms (e.g. "the town keeps spreading into the fields" = urban sprawl).
- [ ] U2 Geographical terminology: use the correct term in answers and reports (e.g. hinterland, nucleated settlement, discharge, prevailing wind, dependency ratio); exam stimulus texts often contain the clue term.
- [ ] U3 Extracting information into a usable form: note-taking under headings matching the objectives; summarising in bullet points; turning figures in a text into a table or graph; plotting places named in a text on a sketch map.
- [ ] U4 Comparing sources: two sources on the same place or topic may agree, disagree or cover different dates; cross-checking with a second source, or with primary data, increases reliability.

Evaluating sources
- [ ] E1 Fact vs opinion: a fact can be checked (a measurement, a date, a statistic); an opinion is a view or judgement ("the bypass ruined the town"). Identify each in a passage.
- [ ] E2 Bias: who wrote it and why (a developer's brochure, a campaign group, a political leaflet, an advertisement); bias shows in one-sided arguments, emotive language, selective figures.
- [ ] E3 Reliability checklist: author or organisation (official body, expert, anonymous); date (is it current, or a past source for comparison?); purpose (inform, persuade, sell); evidence (sources cited, data given); scale and area covered (national figures may not apply to the local area); agreement with other sources.
- [ ] E4 Limitations of secondary data: may be out of date; collected for another purpose; may not cover the exact study area; definitions may differ between sources or dates; online sources may be unverified.
- [ ] E5 Acknowledging sources: every secondary source used in a report is listed (author or organisation, title, date, and web address and date accessed for online sources); never copy text as one's own; authenticity of the investigation is verified by teacher and principal.

Sources from the past: showing change
- [ ] C1 Why past sources matter: comparing an old map, photograph or record with a present-day one shows change in land use, settlement, transport, the coastline or a river course over time.
- [ ] C2 Historical maps: the first Ordnance Survey of Ireland mapped the whole country at six inches to one mile in the 1830s and 1840s; these and later editions show earlier field patterns, town plans, mills, demesnes, railways, and features since lost. Old maps may use different scales, symbols and place-name spellings, and can be inaccurate.
- [ ] C3 Method for comparing an old and a new map: match the scale and orientation; find fixed points (bridge, church, castle, main street); list what has appeared, disappeared and stayed; describe the change (e.g. growth along approach roads, a new bypass, loss of a mill, reclaimed land, a straightened river); explain it.
- [ ] C4 Sketch map of a historical map (exam: Higher 20 marks): the same rules as an OS sketch map (`geo-mapwork-s1` K1–K6): ruled frame of the same shape; show and label only the features asked for (e.g. the harbour or shoreline, two connecting streets drawn meeting, a named building such as the school or court house) in the correct position; title and north arrow. Historical maps usually have no grid, so divide the map and frame into a light pencil grid of equal sections to place features.
- [ ] C5 Old photographs and written accounts: describe what has changed between the old image or description and today; note limits (a single moment, chosen viewpoint, the writer's own view).

Textual sources in the written exam
- [ ] X1 Exam questions use stimulus material (a short text, a table, a cartoon, a newspaper-style extract) and ask students to extract information, identify the issue, distinguish fact from opinion, or use the information in an answer. Method: read the question first, underline key terms and figures in the text, answer using the text's evidence plus own knowledge.
- [ ] X2 Cartoons and pictures as sources: identify the message, the issue, who is shown and the viewpoint (the syllabus lists "pictures, including cartoons" as an information source). One bullet.

### Worked-example specs (all invented; the writer must say "illustrative" each time)

- **W1 Fact and opinion (E1–E2).** An invented two-sentence extract from a Rivertown community
  newsletter: "Traffic on Main Street has doubled since the new industrial estate opened in
  2015. The council's plan for a relief road is a disaster for the town." First sentence =
  fact (checkable by traffic counts or council data); second = opinion (emotive "disaster").
  Note the date 2015 is invented.
- **W2 Evaluating a source (E3–E4).** Apply the reliability checklist to an invented property
  developer's brochure praising a new Rivertown housing estate: purpose to sell; one-sided;
  figures not sourced; compare with the county development plan and own land-use survey.
- **W3 Old and new map (C1–C3).** An invented Rivertown map of the 1840s compared with today's
  OS map (illustrative): then, a mill and weir on the River Dallow, a market square, the castle,
  fields to the north; now, the mill gone, housing estates on those northern fields, the
  railway and station, an industrial estate on the N99, the GAA pitch. Describe three changes
  and explain each (growth after the railway, suburban expansion, decline of water-powered
  industry).
- **W4 Sketch of a historical map (C4).** Invented old Rivertown town plan, 20 cm × 15 cm.
  Requested: the River Dallow; two connecting streets; the market house; the church. Describe
  the frame, the pencil grid of equal sections, and how each feature is drawn and labelled.

## 3. Traps

- Do not name or quote real newspaper articles, reports, websites or past-paper extracts. The
  only named organisations are those in Checked facts.
- Do not give census figures or weather statistics here: census use is `geo-graph-skills-s2`;
  weather data is `geo-weather-climate`.
- Primary vs secondary: a questionnaire the student designs and carries out is primary; a
  published survey is secondary. Old maps and the census are secondary.
- "Secondary" does not mean less useful: secondary sources give background, past data and
  comparisons that fieldwork cannot.
- Don't re-teach the investigation's stages, 60:40 ratio detail, or report layout; they are in
  `geo-mapwork-s3`. Here, one line linking sources to the investigation's background and
  conclusion (comparing findings with published studies and theory).
- OS map-reading skills (grid references, scale, symbols, sketch-map rules) are in
  `geo-mapwork-s1`; refer to them, don't repeat them.
- GIS studies combining maps, census and the Record of Monuments and Places are in
  `geo-weather-climate`. Leave them out.
- An old map can be wrong or out of date: say so as a limitation rather than treating it as
  fully accurate.

## 4. What exams ask (topics only)

- A Higher sketch map (20 marks) of a historical map printed on the paper, showing a harbour or
  shoreline, two connecting streets, and named buildings (e.g. a school, a court house). Expected:
  ruled frame, features correctly placed and labelled, title.
- Stimulus texts, tables and cartoons appear across the paper; questions ask students to pick
  out information, use the correct terms, and support answers with the evidence given.
- The use of documentary and secondary sources is assessed mainly through the Geographical
  Investigation report (`geo-mapwork-s3`).
