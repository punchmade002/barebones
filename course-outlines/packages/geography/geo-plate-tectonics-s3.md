## 1. Must cover (one bullet or block for every item; tick each off)

Constructive (divergent, separating) boundaries: crust is created
- [ ] C1 Process: convection currents carry plates apart; pressure drops, the mantle partly melts, and magma rises into the gap, cooling to form new basaltic oceanic crust. Crust is **created**.
- [ ] C2 Oceanic example: mid-ocean ridge, with a rift valley along its crest, frequent shallow earthquakes, volcanic activity, and transform faults that cut across the ridge and offset it in sections. Where the ridge rises above sea level it forms volcanic islands. Diagram cue: cross-section of a ridge with convection currents, rising magma, rift valley and arrows apart.
- [ ] C3 Continental example: where a continent starts to split, the crust stretches and a block drops between parallel faults to form a rift valley, with volcanoes nearby. If spreading continues, the sea floods in and a new ocean begins (the Red Sea is at this stage).
- [ ] C4 Landforms and activity to name at constructive boundaries: mid-ocean ridge, rift valley, volcanic islands, fissure eruptions of runny basalt lava, shallow earthquakes. Name only; volcano types are in `geo-volcanoes-earthquakes`.

Destructive (convergent, colliding) boundaries: crust is destroyed
- [ ] X1 Why one plate sinks: oceanic crust is denser than continental crust, so where they meet the oceanic plate is forced down into the mantle. This is **subduction**; the zone where it sinks is the subduction zone. Crust is **destroyed** as the plate melts in the hot mantle.
- [ ] X2 Oceanic–continental: an ocean trench forms where the plate bends down; sediments are scraped off and crumpled against the continent into fold mountains; melting of the sinking plate produces magma that rises to form a chain of volcanoes on the continent; earthquakes occur along the sinking plate, shallow near the trench and deeper inland (the Benioff zone). Diagram cue: cross-section with ocean trench, subducting plate, melting zone, rising magma, volcano, fold mountains, earthquake foci.
- [ ] X3 Oceanic–oceanic: the older, denser plate subducts; an ocean trench and a curved chain of volcanic islands (an island arc) form; earthquakes are frequent.
- [ ] X4 Continental–continental (collision): both plates are too light to subduct, so the crust between them, including sediments from the ocean that once separated them, is crushed, folded and pushed up into high fold mountains; earthquakes are common but there is little or no volcanic activity. Crust is **modified** (thickened and folded) rather than destroyed.
- [ ] X5 Impacts of destructive boundaries (exams ask for a 30-mark discussion with examples): landforms (trenches, fold mountains, volcanic mountain chains, island arcs) and hazards (powerful earthquakes, tsunamis from undersea earthquakes, explosive volcanic eruptions), each with a named example from section 2. Give each hazard event in one or two points; full earthquake and volcano case studies belong to `geo-volcanoes-earthquakes`.

Passive (conservative, transform) boundaries: crust is neither created nor destroyed
- [ ] T1 Process: two plates slide past each other horizontally along a transform fault; friction locks them until stress builds up and is released suddenly as an earthquake.
- [ ] T2 No volcanic activity (no magma is produced) and no crust is created or destroyed. Diagram cue: plan view of two plates with arrows in opposite directions along a fault line.
- [ ] T3 Plates may move in the same direction at different speeds, or in opposite directions.

The tectonic cycle and global patterns
- [ ] G1 The tectonic cycle: crust is created at ridges, carried across the ocean floor, and destroyed at subduction zones, so the ocean floor is continually recycled; continental crust is too light to be subducted, which is why it is so much older. Oceans open and close over time (e.g. the Iapetus closed and the Atlantic opened; refer to s2 without repeating its detail).
- [ ] G2 Summary table cue: for each boundary type, the movement, what happens to crust (created/destroyed/modified/conserved), landforms and activity, and an example.
- [ ] G3 The Pacific Ring of Fire: the belt of destructive (and some transform) boundaries around the edge of the Pacific plate and its neighbours, with most of the world's active volcanoes and earthquakes.
- [ ] G4 How plate tectonics explains the global distribution of earthquakes, volcanoes and fold mountains: all three are concentrated in narrow belts along plate boundaries (ridges, subduction zones, collision zones, transform faults), with very few in plate interiors. Fold mountains are found specifically at convergent boundaries, as long chains along the plate edge. Exams set a 30-mark question on the distribution of one of the three; this package covers the boundary explanation and fold mountains; the distribution and prediction of volcanoes and earthquakes in detail is in `geo-volcanoes-earthquakes`.

## 3. Traps

- Use "constructive / divergent / separating", "destructive / convergent / colliding" and "passive / conservative / transform" as synonyms, and state the synonyms once. Exams use all of them.
- The Himalayas are a **collision** (continental–continental) boundary: no subduction and almost no volcanoes. Don't put volcanoes in the Himalayas.
- The Andes are oceanic–continental (Nazca under South American). The Nazca plate is oceanic; South America is continental.
- Iceland is on a **constructive** boundary (exams ask "colliding or separating?": separating). Some existing app text wrongly calls Iceland a convergent zone; don't repeat that. Iceland also sits over a hotspot, but hotspots (e.g. Hawaii) belong to `geo-volcanoes-earthquakes`; leave them out here.
- The San Andreas Fault is a transform boundary: no volcanoes.
- Don't call the transform boundary "destructive": no crust is destroyed.
- Rift valleys at constructive boundaries are named here as features of the boundary only. The formation of a rift valley by faulting (with horst and graben), fault types, and fold structures (anticline, syncline) belong to `geo-volcanoes-earthquakes` (subsection 5). Don't explain normal/reverse faults here.
- Volcano types, volcanic and plutonic landforms, eruption products, earthquake measurement (Richter, moment magnitude), focus and epicentre, and the prediction of volcanoes and earthquakes all belong to `geo-volcanoes-earthquakes`. Use hazard events here only to show the impact of a boundary type, in one or two points each.
- Don't give convergence or spreading speeds, trench depths other than the Mariana figure, or volcano heights: none others are checked. Don't copy plate speeds or directions from past-paper maps or texts.
- The layers of the earth and convection currents are in s1; plates, plate names and continental drift are in s2. Use those terms here without redefining them.

- From a world map: whether plates are colliding or separating at a boundary; the type of boundary; the two plates either side (e.g. Nazca and South American; Indian and Eurasian; North American and Eurasian); the ridge along the Atlantic (Mid-Atlantic Ridge); the volcanic island on it (Iceland).
- Labelled diagrams of a destructive boundary: convection current, subducting plate, continental crust, ocean trench, volcanic activity, fold mountains, earthquake focus; name the process where one plate sinks (subduction) and the feature where they meet (ocean trench).
- Matching movements (pushing together, sliding past, pulling apart) to boundary types; at which boundary ocean ridges and fold mountains form; an example of each.
- The two plates that converged to form the Andes; the name of the earthquake and volcano belt around the Pacific (Ring of Fire).
- A landform at colliding boundaries with an example (fold mountains, e.g. the Himalayas or the Andes).
- 30 marks: describe and explain constructive boundaries; describe and explain destructive boundaries; constructive and destructive together (Ordinary); what happens at plate boundaries (Ordinary, with two named plates and two boundary types).
- 30 marks, Higher: discuss the impacts of destructive plate boundaries, with examples.
- 30 marks, Higher: how plate tectonics explains the global distribution of earthquakes, volcanoes or fold mountains.

## 5. Practice questions to write

1. 30 marks, Higher: "Describe and explain constructive plate boundaries." Model answer: process (convection, rising magma, new basalt crust); mid-ocean ridge with rift valley, transform faults, shallow earthquakes and volcanic islands, using the Mid-Atlantic Ridge, Iceland, Þingvellir and Surtsey; continental rifting using the East African Rift Valley and the Red Sea; evidence from rock age and magnetic striping in one point. Constructive boundaries **only**. About 15 SRPs.
2. 30 marks, Higher: "Describe and explain destructive plate boundaries." Model answer: subduction and why it happens; the three types, each with its landforms and one example (Nazca/South American and the Andes; Pacific/Philippine and the Mariana Trench and islands; Indian/Eurasian and the Himalayas); earthquakes and volcanoes at each.
3. 30 marks, Higher: "Discuss the impacts of destructive plate boundaries, with reference to examples you have studied." Model answer: landform impacts (trenches, fold mountains, volcanic chains, island arcs) and hazard impacts (Tōhoku 2011 earthquake and tsunami, Mount St Helens 1980, Nepal 2015), two or three points per example; no prediction or measurement detail.
