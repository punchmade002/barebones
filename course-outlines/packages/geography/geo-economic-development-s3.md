# Package: geo-economic-development-s3 — Case study of a developing economy

- **loId:** `geo-economic-development-s3` · **chapterId:** `geo-economic-development` · **syllabusRef:** 4.2
- **Syllabus:** Students should study "a case study of a developing economy outlining the impact
  of colonialism, and adjustments to a global economy." Statement: "Levels of economic
  development show major spatial variations and can change over time." The syllabus names no
  setting for this item; teachers choose.
- **Prepared by:** a higher model, 2026-10-20. The writer works only from this package and
  `course-outlines/NOTES_STANDARD.md`.
- **Three case studies, written out in full so students can pick one:** **India** (SimpleStudy's
  choice, and it links to the continental region in `geo-region-continental-s1`), **Brazil** and
  **Sudan** (both standard Irish textbook case studies). Each needs two full blocks, "impact of
  colonialism" and "adjustments to a global economy", each carrying a 30-mark Higher answer
  (about 15 SRPs). Exams set "Examine the impact of colonialism on a developing economy you have
  studied" (30 marks, Higher) and "Name one developing economy that was colonised; describe and
  explain the effects of colonialism on it" (Ordinary).
- **Order:** a short general block on colonialism first, then India, Brazil, Sudan. Each country
  is self-contained: a student who learns only one must have a complete answer.

## 1. Must cover (one bullet or block for every item; tick each off)

Colonialism in general (one block, about 8 bullets)
- [ ] K1 Definition: colonialism is the control of one territory and its people by a foreign power, which rules it, settles it and/or exploits its resources for the benefit of the colonising country. Imperialism is the wider policy of building an empire.
- [ ] K2 The main European colonial powers (Britain, France, Spain, Portugal, the Netherlands, Belgium, Germany) and the main periods: the Americas from the 1490s–1500s; India and Asia from the 1600s–1700s; Africa in the "Scramble for Africa" after the Berlin Conference (1884–85).
- [ ] K3 The colonial economy: colonies supplied cheap raw materials (cotton, sugar, rubber, minerals) to the colonial power and bought its manufactured goods; local industries that competed were destroyed; plantations and mines used forced or slave labour; food crops were replaced by cash crops for export (monoculture).
- [ ] K4 Colonial infrastructure ran from the interior to the ports (railways and roads built to export raw materials, not to link regions); ports and capitals grew as the main cities.
- [ ] K5 Political legacies: artificial borders drawn in Europe that split some ethnic groups and grouped rival ones together; "divide and rule"; weak institutions handed over at independence; conflict and coups after independence.
- [ ] K6 Social and cultural legacies: European languages and religions, education for a small elite only, racial hierarchies, land owned by a few.
- [ ] K7 Positive legacies are debated (one bullet): railways, ports, some irrigation, legal and administrative systems, a world language. Most were built to serve the colonial power.
- [ ] K8 Neo-colonialism and dependency: after independence, former colonies often stayed dependent on exporting a few raw materials to rich countries at low and unstable prices, and on foreign loans and companies. One bullet; dependency theory as a model belongs to the Global Interdependence option.
- [ ] K9 Adjusting to a global economy (generic stages, one bullet each, so the country blocks can refer to them): import substitution industrialisation (protecting new home industries with tariffs) after independence; debt crises and IMF/World Bank structural adjustment programmes in the 1980s–90s; liberalisation (opening to trade and foreign investment, privatisation); export diversification; joining the WTO and trading blocs; growth of services and remittances.

India
- [ ] I1 Colonial timeline: the East India Company (1600); trading posts; the Battle of Plassey (1757) began Company rule in Bengal; the 1857 rebellion; direct British Crown rule (the Raj) from 1858; independence and Partition, 15 August 1947.
- [ ] I2 Deindustrialisation: India's large handloom textile industry (Bengal's fine cottons and muslins) was destroyed by cheap machine-made cloth from Lancashire, while raw cotton was exported to Britain; India's share of world output fell sharply (Maddison estimates in section 2).
- [ ] I3 Raw materials and cash crops: cotton, jute, indigo, tea (Assam plantations), opium (exported to China); farmers forced or pushed to grow cash crops instead of food crops.
- [ ] I4 Land revenue and the peasantry: heavy land taxes collected through zamindars (landlords) under the Permanent Settlement (1793); rural debt and poverty; recurrent famines under British rule (named in section 2) while grain was still exported.
- [ ] I5 The "drain of wealth": revenue, profits and salaries sent to Britain (Dadabhai Naoroji's argument).
- [ ] I6 Railways and infrastructure: the first railway in 1853; the network was built mainly to carry raw materials to ports (Bombay, Calcutta, Madras) and to move troops; ports and colonial cities (Mumbai, Kolkata, Chennai) grew; Kolkata was the capital until 1911.
- [ ] I7 Social and political legacies: English language (now an asset for IT: link to `geo-region-continental-s1` L6); a civil service, courts and parliamentary system; very low literacy and life expectancy at independence (section 2); Partition's violence and displacement; the Kashmir dispute with Pakistan.
- [ ] I8 Resistance: Gandhi's non-violent independence movement; boycott of British cloth (swadeshi, spinning khadi); the Salt March (1930). One or two bullets.
- [ ] I9 Adjustment 1 — state-led development after 1947: five-year plans; import substitution behind high tariffs; state-owned heavy industry; the "Licence Raj"; slow growth (the "Hindu rate of growth" nickname, one bullet). Reuse `geo-region-continental-s1` M2.
- [ ] I10 Adjustment 2 — the Green Revolution made India self-sufficient in food (one bullet; reuse figures from `geo-region-continental-s1` A7).
- [ ] I11 Adjustment 3 — the 1991 crisis and liberalisation: foreign-exchange reserves almost ran out; gold was pledged; an IMF loan; reforms under Narasimha Rao and Manmohan Singh: removed most licences, cut tariffs, welcomed foreign investment, privatised some state firms, devalued the rupee. India was a founding member of the WTO (1995).
- [ ] I12 Results of opening up: fast growth; services above half of GDP; the IT and outsourcing industry (Bengaluru, TCS, Infosys); multinationals and Make in India; India among the world's five largest economies; the world's largest recipient of remittances. Reuse `geo-region-continental-s1` facts; don't add new firms.
- [ ] I13 Problems remaining: most workers in the informal sector; agriculture still employs about half of workers with low productivity; inequality between states (Kerala vs Bihar) and between rich and poor; HDI still medium (section 2); infrastructure gaps; pollution.

Brazil
- [ ] B1 Colonial timeline: Treaty of Tordesillas (1494) gave the east of South America to Portugal; Pedro Álvares Cabral reached Brazil in 1500; Portuguese colony until independence in 1822.
- [ ] B2 An economy of export cycles, each enriching Portugal and a small elite: brazilwood (the country's name); sugar plantations in the North-East (16th–17th centuries); gold in Minas Gerais (18th century); coffee in São Paulo (19th century); the Amazon rubber boom (late 19th century, Manaus).
- [ ] B3 Slavery: millions of enslaved Africans were brought to work the plantations and mines, more than to any other country; slavery was abolished only in 1888, the last in the Americas. Legacy: racial inequality; Afro-Brazilians are over-represented among the poor and in favelas.
- [ ] B4 Indigenous peoples: killed by disease, war and enslavement; pushed off their land; now a small minority (Census 2022 figure in section 2); conflict over land in the Amazon continues.
- [ ] B5 Land concentration: huge estates (latifundia, from Portuguese land grants) owned by a few families; millions of landless rural workers; the MST (Landless Workers' Movement).
- [ ] B6 Regional inequality: the colonial sugar economy left the North-East poor after sugar declined; wealth and industry shifted to the South-East (São Paulo, Rio de Janeiro, Minas Gerais); migration from the North-East to the South-East cities and favelas.
- [ ] B7 Dependence on primary exports: the colonial pattern of exporting raw materials continued after independence (coffee dominated exports into the 20th century), leaving Brazil exposed to world price changes.
- [ ] B8 Adjustment 1 — import substitution industrialisation (1930s–1970s): Getúlio Vargas; state firms (Petrobras, 1953); Juscelino Kubitschek's "fifty years in five" and the new capital Brasília (1960) to open the interior; car industry; the "Brazilian miracle" of very fast growth (1968–73) under military rule.
- [ ] B9 Adjustment 2 — debt and crisis: heavy foreign borrowing in the 1970s; the 1980s debt crisis ("lost decade"); hyperinflation; IMF programmes; the Plano Real (1994) ended hyperinflation.
- [ ] B10 Adjustment 3 — opening up: privatisation (e.g. Vale, the iron-ore miner, 1997); Mercosur trade bloc (1991); a commodity export boom to China (soya, iron ore, oil, beef, coffee); China as Brazil's largest trading partner; BRICS membership; Embraer aircraft as a high-tech export.
- [ ] B11 Reducing poverty and its limits: Bolsa Família (2003) cash transfers to poor families conditional on school attendance and vaccinations; Brazil left the UN World Hunger Map in 2014. Inequality remains among the highest in the world; HDI high (section 2).
- [ ] B12 Costs of adjustment: Amazon deforestation driven by cattle ranching and soya; dependence on commodity prices (recession 2015–16 when prices fell); favelas; the North-East still poorest. The WTO cotton case against US subsidies (link to `-s4`, one bullet).

Sudan
- [ ] U1 Colonial timeline: Egyptian (Ottoman) rule from 1821; the Mahdist state (1885–98); British reconquest at the Battle of Omdurman (1898); the Anglo-Egyptian Condominium (1899–1956), in practice British rule; independence 1 January 1956.
- [ ] U2 The Gezira Scheme: a huge irrigation scheme between the Blue and White Niles, watered by the Sennar Dam (1925), built to grow long-staple cotton for the mills of Lancashire; tenant farmers growing cotton under strict control; cotton became Sudan's main export, so the economy depended on one crop and its world price.
- [ ] U3 Uneven development: investment was concentrated in the Nile valley around Khartoum and Gezira (the north and centre); the south, Darfur and other peripheries were neglected.
- [ ] U4 The "Southern Policy" (from the 1920s): Britain ran the Arab and Muslim north and the African, largely Christian and animist south as separate regions, restricted movement between them, used English and Christian missions in the south and Arabic in the north; then joined them in one state at independence. This laid the basis for civil war.
- [ ] U5 Artificial borders: Sudan's colonial boundaries enclosed many ethnic, language and religious groups; at independence power went to a northern elite.
- [ ] U6 The legacy — civil wars: the first civil war (1955–72; Addis Ababa Agreement 1972); the second (1983–2005; Comprehensive Peace Agreement 2005), about 2 million deaths; South Sudan's independence (2011); the Darfur conflict from 2003; the war that began in April 2023 (section 2).
- [ ] U7 Adjustment 1 — after independence: continued dependence on cotton from Gezira; attempts at state-led development; heavy borrowing and debt in the 1970s–80s; IMF programmes; famine in the 1980s (drought plus war).
- [ ] U8 Adjustment 2 — oil: exports began in 1999 through a pipeline to Port Sudan on the Red Sea, mainly with Chinese, Malaysian and Indian state oil firms; oil became the main export and GDP grew fast in the 2000s; but most oil lay in the south, so South Sudan's independence in 2011 removed about three-quarters of production and caused an economic crisis (inflation, protests).
- [ ] U9 Adjustment 3 — isolation and debt: US sanctions and the "state sponsor of terrorism" listing (1993–2020) cut Sudan off from Western banks and investment; one of the world's largest debt burdens; entered the HIPC debt-relief process after 2019 (one bullet; detail of HIPC in `-s4`).
- [ ] U10 Other primary exports: gum arabic (Sudan is the world's largest producer), gold, livestock, sesame.
- [ ] U11 Political turmoil and present situation: President Omar al-Bashir (1989–2019) overthrown after mass protests in April 2019; military coup October 2021; war between the army (SAF) and the Rapid Support Forces (RSF) since 15 April 2023; the world's largest displacement crisis; famine confirmed in 2024. Development has gone into reverse. Low HDI.

## 3. Traps (known errors)

- **Each case study stands alone.** Write India, Brazil and Sudan as separate blocks; don't mix examples between them in one answer, and make the practice questions use one country only.
- **Sudan vs South Sudan:** before 2011 "Sudan" included the south. Say "Sudan (before 2011, including today's South Sudan)" where needed. South Sudan's capital is Juba; Sudan's is Khartoum.
- **Brazil was Portuguese, not Spanish.** Its language is Portuguese.
- **The Gezira cotton was for Lancashire mills**, not for Sudan's own industry. Don't call the Sennar Dam the Aswan Dam (Aswan is in Egypt).
- Brazil's slavery figures and the Bengal famine toll are estimates: give them as "about" with the range in section 2. Don't give a death toll for the 2023 Sudan war (estimates vary widely).
- Don't say India's railways were built "for Indians"; they were built mainly for exports and troops, though Indians came to use them heavily. Positive legacies are debated: present both sides.
- **India's facts must match `geo-region-continental-s1`.** Reuse its figures; don't add new Indian firms, dates or figures.
- Nigeria appears in a past-paper model answer; it is a valid choice for students but this package has no checked Nigeria facts. Do not use Nigeria.
- Past-paper model answers in the question bank include invented or doubtful figures; use only section 2.
- **Neighbouring subsections and chapters:**
  - India's physical geography, agriculture, industry, services, population and culture as a region are `geo-region-continental-s1`. Here only colonialism and economic adjustment.
  - Measuring development (GNP, HDI) is `-s1`; general factors and change over time (South Korea, China, Botswana) are `-s2`; aid, debt relief (HIPC), fair trade and justice are `-s4`.
  - Multinationals and the international division of labour are `geo-economic-globalisation`.
  - Colonialism's effect on racial and cultural groups is the Culture and Identity option; dependency theory and models of development are the Global Interdependence option.

## 4. What exams ask (topics only)

- Higher 30 marks: examine the impact of colonialism on a developing economy you have studied. Expected: named country, dates, the colonial economy (raw materials, cash crops, destroyed industries), infrastructure for export, borders and political legacy, social legacy, long-term effects on the economy today.
- Ordinary 15 + 15 marks: name a developing economy that was colonised; describe and explain the effects of colonialism on it.
- Higher 30 marks (expected under the syllabus wording): how a developing economy has adjusted to the global economy (liberalisation, exports, foreign investment, debt and IMF, trade blocs, results and problems).
