## 1. Must cover (one bullet or block for every item; tick each off)

Sustainable development as a model
- [ ] S1 Definition (Brundtland, 1987): development that meets the needs of the present without compromising the ability of future generations to meet their own needs.
- [ ] S2 The three pillars (environmental, economic, social), all to be balanced; the contrast with growth-only models (Rostow, mass consumption; link to s1).
- [ ] S3 How the idea developed: "The Limits to Growth" (1972); Brundtland (1987); the Rio Earth Summit and Agenda 21 (1992); the MDGs (2000–2015) and SDGs (2015–2030, 17 goals); the Paris Agreement (2015). One block, reuse facts.
- [ ] S4 Why it should be a priority (the 2024 question): finite resources and overuse; climate change; biodiversity loss; inequality between and within generations; the poorest suffer first; growth that destroys resources cannot last.
- [ ] S5 Problems with the model: vague and open to "greenwashing"; conflicts between pillars (jobs vs environment); poor countries argue rich ones grew without limits; costs of change; progress on the SDGs is off track. Evaluation in 3–4 bullets.
- [ ] S6 Alternative measures for a sustainable future: Bhutan's Gross National Happiness; ecological footprint (one bullet each).

The sustainable use of resources
- [ ] R1 Principles: use renewable resources no faster than they regenerate (sustainable yield); use non-renewables efficiently and switch to alternatives; reduce, reuse, recycle; protect biodiversity; involve local people.
- [ ] R2 Ireland — fisheries: past overfishing (EU waters), the Common Fisheries Policy (1983, 2013 reform: maximum sustainable yield and the discard ban), the Sea-Fisheries Protection Authority, Killybegs. The Grand Banks cod collapse (1992) as the warning. Reuse `geo-economic-environment-s4`.
- [ ] R3 Ireland — peatlands: Bord na Móna's shift from peat to renewables ("Brown to Green"); peat stations closed 2020; harvesting ended 2021; rewetting cutaway bogs; Lough Boora; peatland as a carbon store.
- [ ] R4 Ireland — energy: wind about a third of electricity; 80% renewable electricity target by 2030; offshore wind; Climate Act 2021 targets; interconnectors.
- [ ] R5 Ireland — farming and land: the Burren Programme (paying farmers for results), agri-environment schemes (REPS, GLAS, ACRES), Origin Green; the tension between dairy expansion (after milk quotas ended in 2015) and water quality and emissions.
- [ ] R6 Ireland — waste and consumption: the plastic bag levy (2002) and the Deposit Return Scheme (2024) as examples of changing behaviour.
- [ ] R7 Ireland — forestry: low forest cover, Coillte, conifer plantations; the need for more native broadleaf woodland (one bullet; managed Scandinavian forests are in s2).
- [ ] R8 Costa Rica (international case study, full block): deforestation in the 1970s–80s for cattle ranching; payments for environmental services (1997) funded by a fuel tax; forest cover rose from about a fifth to over half; national parks and protected areas; ecotourism as a major earner; almost all electricity from renewables (hydro, geothermal, wind); abolished its army (1948) and spent on education and health; Earthshot Prize 2021. Limits: still relies on fossil fuels for transport; tourism pressure.
- [ ] R9 Unsustainable use as a warning: the Aral Sea (cotton irrigation), Amazon deforestation (reuse s2 facts; one bullet each).

The goal of fair trade and its potential impact on development
- [ ] F1 The problem fair trade addresses: low and unstable commodity prices; small farmers' tiny share of the final price; middlemen; poor working conditions and child labour; declining terms of trade (link to s1 dependency).
- [ ] F2 What fair trade is: a trading partnership based on dialogue, transparency and respect that seeks greater equity in international trade; history (Max Havelaar, 1988; Fairtrade International; Garstang the first Fairtrade town).
- [ ] F3 How it works, one bullet each: the Fairtrade Minimum Price (a floor that covers the cost of sustainable production); the Fairtrade Premium (an extra sum paid to the co-operative, spent on projects the members choose democratically); long-term contracts and pre-finance (credit before harvest); democratic producer co-operatives; labour standards (no forced or child labour, freedom to join unions, safe conditions); environmental standards (restricted pesticides, protecting water and forests, no GM crops); the Fairtrade Mark as a guarantee to consumers.
- [ ] F4 Products and scale: coffee, cocoa, tea, bananas, sugar, cotton, flowers; close to 2 million farmers and workers in Fairtrade-certified organisations.
- [ ] F5 Case study — Kuapa Kokoo, Ghana (full block): a cocoa farmers' co-operative (1993) that co-owns Divine Chocolate (1998); premium spent on wells, schools, clinics and mobile banking; women's participation; farmers have a share in the profits of the brand, not just the beans.
- [ ] F6 Case study — Oromia Coffee Farmers Co-operative Union, Ethiopia: founded 1999 by Tadesse Meskela; sells members' coffee directly to buyers, cutting out middlemen; premium spent on schools, clinics and water; featured in the film "Black Gold" (2006).
- [ ] F7 Potential impact on development (the core of the 80-mark question), each as a point: higher and more stable income; security to invest and plan; community projects (education, health, clean water) through the premium; empowerment through co-operatives and democratic decisions; women's participation; environmental sustainability (organic farming, less deforestation); less child labour; producers learn processing and marketing skills; raises consumer awareness in rich countries and pressures big firms.
- [ ] F8 Fair trade in Ireland: Fairtrade Ireland; Fairtrade towns (Clonakilty the first), schools and universities; Fairtrade Fortnight; supermarket ranges; Irish NGOs promoting fair trade.
- [ ] F9 Limits and criticisms: a tiny share of world trade; higher prices put off some consumers; certification costs exclude the poorest farmers; benefits go mainly to co-operative members, not hired labourers (a 2014 SOAS study); market prices sometimes exceed the minimum; doesn't change the trade rules (subsidies, tariff escalation); big brands leaving for their own schemes (Cadbury's switch to Cocoa Life, s2); most value is still added in rich countries unless producers process (Divine as the exception).
- [ ] F10 Evaluation: fair trade is a model of sustainable, just trade that improves lives for those involved and shows what is possible, but its impact on development depends on scale and on wider changes in trade rules.

Justice issues, particularly in relation to minority groups
- [ ] J1 Why justice is part of sustainability: development is not sustainable if it excludes or harms groups; minorities often lack political power, so their land and rights are taken for "development" (dams, mines, plantations, wind farms).
- [ ] J2 International rights framework: UDHR (1948); ILO Convention 169 (1989); the UN Declaration on the Rights of Indigenous Peoples (2007), including free, prior and informed consent.
- [ ] J3 Indigenous peoples of the Amazon (developing-world case): loss of land to ranching, soya, logging, mining and dams; the Yanomami health emergency (2023); Belo Monte and the Kayapó; Chico Mendes and extractive reserves; indigenous territories are among the best-protected forests (link to sustainable resource use).
- [ ] J4 Irish Travellers (national case, full block): an ethnic minority recognised in 2017; numbers (Census 2022); health inequality (life expectancy and suicide figures, 2010 study); unemployment; accommodation shortages and unspent local authority Traveller accommodation budgets (no figures); discrimination; responses (equality law, ethnic status, National Traveller and Roma Inclusion Strategy).
- [ ] J5 The Roma in Europe: Europe's largest ethnic minority; poverty, segregated housing and schools, discrimination; the D.H. v Czech Republic case (2007); EU frameworks (2011; 2020–2030).
- [ ] J6 The Sámi and the Fosen wind farm (a justice vs sustainability conflict): Norway's Supreme Court ruled in 2021 that wind turbines at Fosen violated Sámi reindeer herders' rights; protests in 2023; agreements with the herders followed in 2023–24. Shows that "green" development must also be just.
- [ ] J7 Other minorities, one bullet each pointing to their main note: the Rohingya and Uyghurs (s2); Aboriginal Australians (`geo-option-culture-identity-s1`).

The idea of self-reliance: development as self-help
- [ ] H1 Meaning: communities and countries meet their own needs with their own resources, skills and decisions, rather than depending on aid, loans, foreign companies or imports; links to bottom-up development and appropriate technology.
- [ ] H2 National self-reliance — Tanzania under Nyerere (full block): Arusha Declaration (1967); Ujamaa ("familyhood") villages and collective farming; education for self-reliance; achievements (Swahili as a uniting national language, rising literacy and school enrolment, national unity and peace); failures (forced villagisation 1973–76, falling food and export output, economic crisis); Nyerere stepped down 1985 and Tanzania turned to IMF reforms (1986). Evaluation: social gains, economic failure.
- [ ] H3 Gandhi's swadeshi (buy Indian) and khadi (hand-spun cloth) as self-reliance against colonial dependence (reuse).
- [ ] H4 Intermediate (appropriate) technology: Schumacher and Practical Action (reuse s1): cheap, local, repairable technology.
- [ ] H5 Community self-help in Africa: the Green Belt Movement, Kenya (Wangari Maathai, 1977; Nobel Peace Prize 2004): women planting trees for fuel, soil and income; zaï pits and stone lines in Burkina Faso and farmer-managed natural regeneration in Niger (reuse); food sovereignty (La Via Campesina, 1993).
- [ ] H6 Self-help through finance and co-operation: Grameen and SEWA, co-operatives and credit unions (point to s3; one bullet).
- [ ] H7 Irish self-help (national setting): Muintir na Tíre (1937); co-operative creameries and credit unions (s3); LEADER and community development; NGOs that help people help themselves (Self Help Africa, Bóthar).
- [ ] H8 Evaluation: self-reliance builds dignity, skills and local ownership and avoids debt and dependency; but complete self-reliance cuts countries off from trade, investment and technology (Tanzania), and communities still need fair trade rules, infrastructure and some outside support.

## 3. Traps (known errors)

- **Fair trade guarantees a minimum price**, not always a higher price than the market; when the market is higher, producers get the market price plus the premium. Don't give Fairtrade minimum prices or premium rates (none checked).
- "Fair trade" (the movement) vs "Fairtrade" (the certification mark and organisation): keep the spelling consistent with the meaning.
- Kuapa Kokoo is in **Ghana**; the Oromia union is in **Ethiopia**. Don't swap. Don't give membership numbers for either (none checked).
- Costa Rica's forest recovery is from **about a fifth** to **over half**; don't give exact percentages or say it is "carbon neutral" (it has not reached that).
- Nyerere's Tanzania is a **mixed** example: credit its social gains, but don't call Ujamaa an economic success. Villagisation was often forced.
- Travellers are an **ethnic group**, not a race (same trap as `geo-option-culture-identity-s1`). Use only the AITHS figures given; don't give figures on Traveller accommodation spending or education.
- The Fosen turbines were **not** removed after the 2021 ruling; that was the cause of the protests.
- Past-paper model answers in the question bank contain invented figures (e.g. Fairtrade price levels, farmer income increases, Bangladesh "floating gardens" claims). Use none.
- **Neighbouring subsections and chapters:**
  - The elective version of sustainable economic development (EPA, EIA, fish stocks, mining, peatlands, the Aral Sea) is `geo-economic-environment-s4`; fair trade in brief is `geo-economic-development-s4`. Reuse their facts identically; the essay depth belongs here.
  - Managed Scandinavian forests, the Amazon, desertification and global warming as interdependence issues are s2; refer to them in one bullet only. Cadbury/Mondelēz and cocoa supply chains are s2.
  - Co-operatives as land and participation examples, Grameen, SEWA and NGOs are s3; mention only as self-help.
  - Models of development (Rostow, dependency, bottom-up) are s1.
  - Racial and ethnic conflict, the Gaeltacht and minority languages belong to `geo-option-culture-identity`; Travellers' recognition is also there. Here Travellers are a justice and development case.
