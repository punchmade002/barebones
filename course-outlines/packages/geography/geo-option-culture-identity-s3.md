# Package: geo-option-culture-identity-s3 — Religion as a cultural indicator

## 1. Must cover (one bullet or block for every item; tick each off)

Religion as a cultural indicator
- [ ] I1 Why religion is a cultural indicator: it shapes beliefs, values and moral codes; marks group identity (often more strongly than nationality); shows in the landscape (churches, mosques, temples, synagogues, holy wells, wayside shrines, cemeteries); shapes diet (halal, kosher, Hindu vegetarianism), dress (headscarf, turban, kippah), calendar and festivals (Christmas, Easter, Eid, Diwali, Yom Kippur), names, education and law. Census questions on religion make it easy to map.
- [ ] I2 Religion and other cultural indicators: linked with language (Arabic and the Qur'an, Latin and the Catholic Church in the past, Hebrew), with nationality (Catholicism and Irish or Polish identity; Orthodoxy and Serbian identity), and with politics.
- [ ] I3 Secularisation: religious practice and church influence declining in much of Europe (Ireland, France), while religion stays strong elsewhere and migration brings new religions into Europe.

Distribution of the major world religions
- [ ] D1 World totals and shares (Pew 2020): Christianity, Islam, religiously unaffiliated, Hinduism, Buddhism, Judaism; Sikhism as a smaller example.
- [ ] D2 Hearths and diffusion: Judaism, Christianity and Islam from the Middle East (Jerusalem, Mecca); Hinduism and Buddhism from the Indian subcontinent. Spread by: empire (Christianity in the Roman Empire; Islam with the Arab conquests from the 7th century), missionaries (including Irish missionaries), trade (Islam to Indonesia and West Africa), colonialism (Christianity to the Americas, Africa, the Philippines), and migration (Islam, Hinduism and Sikhism into Europe).
- [ ] D3 Christianity's distribution and divisions: Roman Catholic (southern Europe, Ireland, Poland, Latin America, the Philippines), Protestant (northern Europe, the UK, the USA), Orthodox (eastern Europe, Russia, Greece, Serbia); the Great Schism (1054) and the Reformation (16th century) created these divisions. Growth in sub-Saharan Africa.
- [ ] D4 Islam's distribution: the Middle East, North Africa, Central Asia, Pakistan, Bangladesh, Indonesia (the largest Muslim population), parts of West and East Africa; Sunni (the large majority) and Shia (majorities in Iran and Iraq); growth in Europe through migration.
- [ ] D5 Hinduism (India, Nepal; Indian diaspora), Buddhism (Thailand, Myanmar, Sri Lanka, Tibet, Japan, Vietnam), Judaism (Israel and the USA), Sikhism (Punjab; diaspora in the UK and Canada).
- [ ] D6 A sketch map of world religions is expected in an 80-mark answer.
- [ ] D7 Religion in Ireland (Census 2022): Catholic share falling, "no religion" rising; growth of Orthodox Christians and Muslims through immigration. Northern Ireland's Census 2021 religious balance.

Relationship between church and state
- [ ] C1 Types of relationship: theocracy (religious leaders rule; Iran since 1979; the Vatican); established/state church (Church of England); strict separation (France's laïcité; the USA's First Amendment); states with a dominant church influence (Ireland in the 20th century, Poland).
- [ ] C2 The Irish Constitution (national setting): the 1937 Constitution's religious content: the preamble (Most Holy Trinity), Article 44 (the "special position" of the Catholic Church and the other named churches), the ban on divorce, and later the Eighth Amendment (1983). Freedom of conscience and no endowment of any religion.
- [ ] C3 Catholic Church influence on Irish society and the state in the 20th century: control of most schools and many hospitals; social policy (censorship, contraception, the Mother and Child Scheme controversy); papal visit 1979.
- [ ] C4 Change: constitutional referendums removing religious influence (1972, 1995, 2015, 2018 abortion, 2018 blasphemy); the abuse reports (Ryan and Murphy, 2009) and the decline of Church authority; the debate on school patronage (Educate Together; the State's aim to divest some Catholic schools). Census 2022 shows the trend.
- [ ] C5 France's laïcité: the 1905 law; the 2004 law on religious symbols in state schools; the 2010 face-covering ban.
- [ ] C6 One example where religion and state are fused: Iran (Islamic Republic since 1979, Supreme Leader, Sharia-based law) or Saudi Arabia. One example of a secular constitution under religious-nationalist pressure: India.

Religious conflict
- [ ] K1 Why religion causes or feeds conflict: competing claims to holy sites and territory; religion overlapping with national identity and political power; discrimination against religious minorities; extremist interpretations.
- [ ] K2 Northern Ireland (national setting; full case study): the Plantation of Ulster; partition (1920–21) creating a Protestant/unionist majority state; discrimination in jobs, housing and voting; the civil rights movement; the Troubles (dates, deaths, key events); segregation (peace walls, separate schools, integrated schools); marching season; the Good Friday Agreement; Census 2021. Stress that the conflict is about national identity and power as much as theology.
- [ ] K3 The Indian subcontinent: partition along religious lines (1947); Kashmir; Hindu–Muslim tension in India (Ayodhya); Sikh separatism and Operation Blue Star (1984).
- [ ] K4 Israel and Palestine: Jerusalem's holy sites for Jews, Christians and Muslims; 1948 and the displacement of Palestinians; 1967 occupation; settlements; the Oslo Accords; the 2023 Hamas attack and the war in Gaza. Keep it factual and balanced.
- [ ] K5 Shorter examples (one or two bullets each): Lebanon (confessional system; civil war 1975–90); Bosnia (Orthodox Serbs, Catholic Croats, Muslim Bosniaks; detail in s6); Nigeria (Muslim north, Christian south; Boko Haram); Myanmar's Rohingya; Sunni–Shia conflict in Iraq and Syria.

Islam was the fastest-growing major religion 2010–2020. |

Buddhism: founded by Siddhartha Gautama (the Buddha) in north-east India around the 5th century BC; Bodh Gaya is where he is said to have gained enlightenment. Sikhism: founded by Guru Nanak in Punjab in the 15th century; the Golden Temple (Harmandir Sahib) at Amritsar is its holiest site. | D4 | Indonesia has the world's largest Muslim population. Sunnis are about 85–90% of Muslims; Shias are the majority in Iran and Iraq. Mecca and Medina (Saudi Arabia) are Islam's holiest cities; the Hajj pilgrimage to Mecca is a duty for every Muslim able to make it. |
| D5 | About four in five Indians are Hindu. Varanasi on the Ganges is a major Hindu pilgrimage city. Most of the world's Jews live in Israel and the USA. |

Vatican City: independent state ruled by the Pope, created by the Lateran Treaty with Italy, 1929. Church of England: the established church; the monarch is its Supreme Governor and 26 bishops sit in the House of Lords. USA: the First Amendment (1791) bars an established religion. Saudi Arabia: law based on Sharia; public practice of other religions is not permitted. India: its constitution describes it as a secular republic; Hindu nationalism has grown in politics since the 1990s. |
| C2 | Bunreacht na hÉireann approved by referendum on 1 July 1937. Preamble: "In the Name of the Most Holy Trinity". Article 44 originally recognised "the special position of the Holy Catholic Apostolic and Roman Church as the guardian of the Faith professed by the great majority of the citizens" and also recognised the Church of Ireland, the Presbyterian Church, the Methodist Church, the Religious Society of Friends (Quakers) and the Jewish congregations. Article 44 guarantees freedom of conscience and practice and says the State shall not endow any religion. Article 41 originally banned divorce. The Eighth Amendment (1983) gave the unborn a right to life equal to the mother's. |

Referendum of 25 May 2018 repealed the Eighth Amendment (about 66% Yes). Referendum of October 2018 removed the offence of blasphemy from the Constitution (about 65% Yes). Ryan Report (May 2009) on abuse in residential institutions run by religious orders; Murphy Report (November 2009) on the Dublin archdiocese's handling of abuse allegations. | K2 | Plantation of Ulster began in 1609, settling English and Scottish Protestants on confiscated land. Battle of the Boyne, 1690 (William of Orange defeated James II); commemorated by Orange Order parades on 12 July (the Orange Order was founded in 1795). Government of Ireland Act 1920; Northern Ireland established 1921 with six counties and a Protestant/unionist majority. Discrimination against Catholics in housing, jobs and local voting (gerrymandering, e.g. in Derry). Northern Ireland civil rights movement from 1968. The Troubles: from the late 1960s to 1998; over 3,500 people were killed. British troops deployed in 1969. Internment without trial introduced in 1971. Bloody Sunday, Derry, 30 January 1972: British paratroopers shot dead 13 civil-rights marchers (a 14th died later). 1981 hunger strikes: ten republican prisoners, including Bobby Sands, died. Good Friday (Belfast) Agreement, 10 April 1998: power-sharing Assembly and Executive, North–South bodies, the consent principle. Omagh bomb, 15 August 1998: 29 people killed by the Real IRA. Peace walls (peace lines) separating Catholic and Protestant areas of Belfast, the first built in 1969, still stand. Lagan College, Belfast (1981), was the first planned integrated (mixed Catholic and Protestant) secondary school; integrated schools still teach fewer than one in ten pupils. |
| K3 | Partition of India, August 1947: Hindu-majority India and Muslim-majority Pakistan (including East Pakistan, now Bangladesh); around 15 million people displaced and hundreds of thousands killed. Kashmir: a Muslim-majority region claimed by both India and Pakistan, divided by a Line of Control; wars between India and Pakistan over it. Ayodhya: Hindu nationalists demolished the 16th-century Babri Mosque on 6 December 1992, leading to riots in which about 2,000 people died; a Hindu temple to Ram was consecrated on the site in January 2024. Operation Blue Star, June 1984: the Indian army stormed the Golden Temple, Amritsar, to remove armed Sikh separatists; Prime Minister Indira Gandhi was assassinated by two Sikh bodyguards on 31 October 1984, followed by anti-Sikh riots in Delhi. |
| K4 | Jerusalem contains the Western Wall (Judaism), the Al-Aqsa Mosque and Dome of the Rock (Islam) and the Church of the Holy Sepulchre (Christianity). The State of Israel was declared on 14 May 1948; about 700,000 Palestinians fled or were expelled in the 1948 war (the Nakba, "catastrophe"). Six-Day War, 1967: Israel occupied the West Bank, East Jerusalem and Gaza. Israeli settlements in the occupied West Bank are illegal under international law in the view of the UN and most states. Oslo Accords, 1993: mutual recognition between Israel and the Palestine Liberation Organization and limited Palestinian self-rule. 7 October 2023: Hamas-led attack from Gaza killed about 1,200 people in Israel and took about 250 hostages; Israel's military campaign in Gaza killed tens of thousands of Palestinians and destroyed much of Gaza; a ceasefire began in October 2025. Ireland recognised the State of Palestine on 28 May 2024 (with Spain and Norway). |

Nigeria: a mainly Muslim north and mainly Christian south; Boko Haram's insurgency from 2009; it kidnapped 276 schoolgirls from Chibok in April 2014. Rohingya: see s1 (made stateless by a 1982 law; over 700,000 fled to Bangladesh from August 2017). ## 3. Traps

- Northern Ireland: Catholics became the largest group in Census 2021 but are not a majority (about 46%, counting religion brought up in). Use only these rounded figures. Don't say the Troubles were purely religious; national identity (Irish/British), civil rights and political power were central.
- Don't give the Troubles' death toll as anything but "over 3,500". Don't give a figure for Gaza's death toll or for Kashmir's wars: describe them without numbers beyond those in section 2.
- Article 44's "special position" clause was removed in 1972; don't say it is still in the Constitution or that it made Catholicism the state religion (it did not establish a state church).
- Divorce: the 1995 referendum passed; don't confuse it with the 1986 referendum, which failed (not a checked fact: don't use 1986 at all).
- Laïcité is French; the US model is separation under the First Amendment. The UK has an established church in England only (the Church in Wales was disestablished in 1920; that fact belongs to s6).
- Geneva was the centre of Calvinist Protestantism; a past-paper model answer wrongly calls French-speaking Switzerland Catholic. Swiss religion belongs to s6.
- Don't overstate the Pew figures: they are for 2020. "Unaffiliated" is not a religion.
- Overlaps: partition of Ireland as a boundary change belongs to s5 and `geo-region-european-s4`; the Good Friday Agreement's institutions and Brexit to `geo-region-european-s3`. Here focus on religion's role. Religious issues arising from migration into Ireland belong to `geo-population-migration-s5`; one bullet only (D7). Religion as a feature of a cultural region (the Islamic world) is in `geo-region-concept-s3`.
- Keep a neutral, factual tone on all conflicts; attribute contested numbers to their source or avoid them.

## 4. What exams ask (topics only)

- 80 marks (set repeatedly): the importance of religion as a cultural indicator. Expected aspects: religion and identity/daily life; distribution and spread of world religions (with sketch map); church–state relations (Irish Constitution and its changes, France); religious conflict (Northern Ireland, plus one global example).
- Religious conflicts are reused in essays on conflict between political structures and cultural groups (s5).

## 5. Practice questions to write

1. 80 marks, Higher: "Examine the importance of religion as a cultural indicator." Model answer: four aspects: religion in identity and everyday life (I1); world distribution and diffusion with Pew figures (D1–D5) and a sketch-map description; church and state in Ireland (C2–C4 with referendum dates) contrasted with France; religious conflict in Northern Ireland (K2). About 25–30 SRPs.
2. 80 marks, Higher: "Religion has been a cause of conflict in many parts of the world. Discuss." Model answer: Northern Ireland in depth, then the Indian subcontinent and Israel–Palestine; causes (K1) drawn out in each. No racial conflicts.
3. 20 marks: "Describe how the relationship between church and state in Ireland has changed since 1937." Model answer: 1937 preamble and Article 44, divorce ban, Church control of schools, then 1972, 1995, 2015, 2018 referendums, the 2009 reports and Census 2022.
