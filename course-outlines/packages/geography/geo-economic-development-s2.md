# Package: geo-economic-development-s2 — Spatial variations in development

- **loId:** `geo-economic-development-s2` · **chapterId:** `geo-economic-development` · **syllabusRef:** 4.2
- **Syllabus:** Statement (4.2): "Levels of economic development show major spatial variations
  and can change over time. Levels of economic development evolve through the complex
  interaction of factors including physical, social, cultural, and political." Statement (4.1):
  "Economic activities are unevenly distributed over the earth." Settings: "Ireland" and
  "Appropriate relevant European and global examples."
- **Prepared by:** a higher model, 2026-10-20. The writer works only from this package and
  `course-outlines/NOTES_STANDARD.md`.
- **Scale and structure:** teach the pattern at three scales (global, European, Irish), then the
  four groups of factors the syllabus names (physical, social, cultural, political), each with
  named examples, then change over time with four short country case studies (two rises, two
  falls). Each factor group and each case study needs enough facts for a 30-mark answer when
  combined. Don't re-teach material owned by the case-study chapters; reuse their facts and
  point to them.

## 1. Must cover (one bullet or block for every item; tick each off)

The global pattern
- [ ] W1 Development is uneven at every scale: between continents, between countries, between regions within a country, and within cities.
- [ ] W2 The North–South divide: the Brandt Line (section 2) separating the rich "North" (North America, Europe, Russia, Japan, Australia and New Zealand) from the poorer "South" (Latin America, Africa, most of Asia). It is a simplification: some "South" countries are now rich (Singapore, South Korea, the Gulf states) and the terms "Third World/First World" are outdated (one bullet; the critique of these labels belongs to the Global Interdependence option).
- [ ] W3 The pattern by HDI: very high in Europe, North America, Australia, Japan and the Gulf; high and medium across most of Latin America, East and South-East Asia and North Africa; low concentrated in sub-Saharan Africa plus Afghanistan, Yemen and Haiti. Reuse the rankings in `geo-economic-development-s1` section 2.
- [ ] W4 Global inequality is extreme: the richest tenth of people own about three-quarters of global wealth (section 2). One bullet; the justice angle is `-s4`.

The European and Irish pattern
- [ ] E1 Within the EU: Luxembourg and Ireland have the highest GDP per head and Bulgaria the lowest; a richer north-west core (the "Blue Banana") and poorer periphery in the east and south. Reuse `geo-region-concept-s4` for the core–periphery terms.
- [ ] E2 Within member states: Italy's North–South divide (the Mezzogiorno: reuse `geo-region-european-s2` figures, one or two bullets); EU "less developed regions" (GDP per head under 75% of the EU average) receive most cohesion funding.
- [ ] E3 Within Ireland: the Dublin region has the highest disposable income per person and most of the high-value jobs; the Border and Midland regions are consistently among the lowest; the West and North-West are peripheral (reuse `geo-region-ireland-s1`/`-s2`). Reasons: Dublin's capital and port functions, multinational and financial-services investment, universities, airport; the periphery's remoteness, weaker infrastructure, dependence on farming and small firms, out-migration.
- [ ] E4 Irish regional policy: the BMW region's Objective 1 status (2000–2006); three regional assemblies since 2015; Project Ireland 2040 (the National Planning Framework and the National Development Plan, 2018) aiming to balance growth between the Eastern and Midland region and the rest of the country and to grow Cork, Limerick, Galway and Waterford; investment in transport (Metrolink, motorways) and broadband (National Broadband Plan). Exams ask how transport investment can reduce regional income differences: better access attracts firms to regional towns, cuts costs and commuting times, widens job markets.
- [ ] E5 Within cities and countries: pockets of deprivation even in rich places; within India, Kerala vs Bihar (reuse `geo-region-continental-s1`); within Brazil, the poor North-East vs the rich South-East (reuse `-s3`). One bullet each.

Physical factors
- [ ] P1 Climate: drought and unreliable rainfall in the Sahel limit farming and cause famine; extreme heat and tropical storms; temperate climates suit productive farming. Avoid climatic determinism: climate influences but does not decide development (Singapore is tropical and rich).
- [ ] P2 Disease linked to climate: malaria and other tropical diseases reduce life expectancy and the productivity of workers and children's schooling (malaria figure in `-s1`).
- [ ] P3 Relief and soils: mountains, deserts and poor soils limit farming and make transport costly; fertile lowlands and river plains support dense populations and farming surpluses.
- [ ] P4 Location and access: landlocked countries depend on neighbours' roads and ports, raising trade costs (named examples); coastal countries with good harbours trade more easily. Peripheral location in Ireland and the EU.
- [ ] P5 Natural resources: minerals, oil and fertile land can fund development (Botswana's diamonds, Norway's oil) but can also bring the "resource curse" of corruption, conflict and dependence on one export (Nigeria's oil, the DR Congo's minerals).
- [ ] P6 Natural hazards: earthquakes, floods, cyclones and droughts destroy infrastructure and set poor countries back for years (Haiti 2010; reuse `geo-volcanoes-earthquakes-s2` facts). Rich countries recover faster.

Social factors
- [ ] S1 Population growth: rapid growth (high fertility) means spending on schools and health is spread thinly and more young dependants; a falling birth rate can give a "demographic dividend" of working-age people (Niger example).
- [ ] S2 Education and literacy: an educated workforce raises productivity and attracts investment; low literacy, especially among women, holds development back (Kerala vs Bihar).
- [ ] S3 Health: disease, malnutrition and HIV/AIDS reduce the workforce and life expectancy; health spending raises productivity.
- [ ] S4 Gender equality: where girls are educated and women work outside the home, families are smaller, children healthier and household incomes higher.
- [ ] S5 Migration: brain drain (doctors, nurses and graduates leaving poor countries) vs remittances sent home (India is the largest recipient; reuse `geo-region-continental-s1`).
- [ ] S6 Infrastructure and services: water, sanitation, electricity, roads and internet as both a cause and a result of development.

Cultural factors
- [ ] C1 Attitudes to women's roles and girls' education; early marriage reduces schooling and raises fertility.
- [ ] C2 Social structures and inequality: caste in India, land concentration in Latin America, ethnic divisions; these limit opportunity for some groups.
- [ ] C3 Language and ethnic diversity: many languages within artificial colonial borders can make national unity and education harder; a world language (English) can help (India's IT sector).
- [ ] C4 Colonial cultural legacy: European languages, education aimed at a small elite, a culture of exporting raw materials. One bullet pointing to `-s3`.
- [ ] C5 Attitudes to enterprise, saving and education (e.g. the high value placed on education in South Korea); traditional farming practices and land tenure can slow change. Keep this to one bullet and avoid stereotypes.

Political factors
- [ ] L1 Political stability and good governance attract investment and allow long-term planning (Botswana); war destroys infrastructure, displaces people and scares off investors (Syria, South Sudan, Sudan; Sudan detail in `-s3`).
- [ ] L2 Corruption diverts public money and aid; rankings in section 2.
- [ ] L3 Colonial history: borders, export economies and weak institutions (one bullet; detail in `-s3`).
- [ ] L4 Government economic policy: education, infrastructure, openness to trade and investment, industrial policy (South Korea, China, Ireland) vs mismanagement (Zimbabwe, Venezuela).
- [ ] L5 Debt and global trade rules: debt repayments take money from schools and hospitals; rich countries' farm subsidies and tariffs disadvantage poor exporters. One bullet; detail in `-s4`.
- [ ] L6 Membership of trading blocs and aid programmes: EU membership and funds helped Ireland, Spain and Portugal converge with richer states. One bullet.

Change over time
- [ ] T1 Development is not fixed: countries can rise (Ireland, South Korea, China, Botswana) or fall (Zimbabwe, Venezuela, Syria). A falling HDI is usually caused by war, economic collapse or disease (exams ask for one reason).
- [ ] T2 Rise — South Korea: case study facts in section 2 (from war-torn poverty to a high-income industrial economy: export-led industrialisation, education, chaebol firms, government planning).
- [ ] T3 Rise — China: case study facts in section 2 (reforms from 1978, Special Economic Zones, WTO entry, poverty reduction, world's second-largest economy; inequality between the coast and the interior).
- [ ] T4 Rise — Botswana: case study facts in section 2 (one of the poorest countries at independence; diamonds plus stable, relatively honest government; setback from HIV/AIDS).
- [ ] T5 Rise — Ireland: from stagnation and emigration in the 1950s to one of the richest EU states (free trade, foreign investment, free secondary education, EEC membership, the Celtic Tiger); the 2008 crash and recovery. Four or five bullets only; the full Irish case study is `geo-economic-activities`.
- [ ] T6 Fall — Zimbabwe and Venezuela: economic mismanagement and collapse (facts in section 2). Syria: civil war from 2011 reversed decades of progress. One block of short bullets.
- [ ] T7 Theory, one bullet each, name only: cumulative causation (core regions keep growing as they attract capital and skilled migrants) and the possibility of "spread" effects to the periphery. Full core–periphery theory is in `geo-economic-globalisation`; models of development (Rostow) are in the Global Interdependence option.

## 3. Traps (known errors)

- **Avoid determinism.** Physical factors influence development but do not fix it: say so once (Singapore, Botswana). Don't write that hot climates make people less hard-working or similar stereotypes.
- **The Brandt Line is from 1980**; don't present it as a current UN classification. Australia and New Zealand are in the "North" despite their location.
- **Peripheral is not less developed:** the West of Ireland is peripheral within a very rich country (same rule as `geo-region-concept-s4`).
- **Don't give euro figures** for Irish regional incomes, the NDP, or Metrolink costs. Past papers include NDP and NPF extracts; never copy their figures (e.g. NDP totals, growth shares).
- **Kerala vs Bihar, the Mezzogiorno and the Greater Dublin Area** are studied in full elsewhere. Use the figures given here only and keep each to one or two bullets.
- Don't call China "developed" or a high-income country; it is upper-middle income. Don't say South Korea was helped by oil (it has none).
- Past-paper model answers in the question bank contain invented figures (e.g. Rust Belt employment numbers, life-expectancy gaps, doctors per 1,000). Use none.
- **Neighbouring subsections and chapters:**
  - Definitions and pros and cons of GNP and HDI, and short reasons tied to single indicators (life expectancy, schooling, unemployment) are `-s1`. Don't repeat them; here explain the broad factors.
  - Colonialism and the developing-economy case studies (India, Brazil, Sudan) are `-s3`; here colonialism gets one bullet (L3/C4).
  - Aid, debt, fair trade and justice are `-s4`.
  - Core, peripheral, less-developed and industrial-decline regions as region types are `geo-region-concept-s4`; how core and peripheral regions developed in the global economy and the international division of labour are `geo-economic-globalisation`; Ireland's economy (MNCs, services, tourism, industrial decline) is `geo-economic-activities`; EU regional and social funds are `geo-economic-eu`.
  - Models of development (Rostow, dependency theory) and the critique of "Third World" language are the Global Interdependence option. Name only, as in T7 and W2.

## 4. What exams ask (topics only)

- Explaining why levels of development vary between countries or regions, using physical, social, cultural and political factors with examples.
- Two reasons why some countries have a higher HDI or GDP than others (short answers).
- Naming a country whose HDI fell over a period and giving a reason (war, economic collapse, disease).
- Regional disparity in Ireland from CSO disposable-income data: why the Dublin region has the highest income; how transport investment could reduce regional differences; which regions government plans target.
- Data questions on the National Planning Framework / National Development Plan: the aim of balanced regional growth and what transport investment achieves (reduced congestion, shorter journeys, access to jobs, attracting investment, lower emissions).
- How levels of development change over time, with an example.

## 5. Practice questions to write

1. 30 marks, Higher: "Levels of economic development show major spatial variations. Examine how physical and political factors influence levels of development, with reference to examples you have studied." Model answer: physical (climate and the Sahel, malaria, landlocked location, resources: Botswana vs DR Congo, hazards: Haiti) and political (stability vs war: Botswana vs Syria/South Sudan, corruption, policy: South Korea vs Zimbabwe). About 7–8 SRPs each.
2. 30 marks, Higher: "Levels of economic development can change over time. Discuss this statement with reference to one country you have studied." Model answer: South Korea **only**, about 15 SRPs from section 2 (starting point, policies, industries, education, crisis and recovery, present status).
3. 8 marks, short: "Explain one reason why the Dublin region has the highest disposable income in the State, and one way that investment in transport infrastructure could reduce regional differences in income." Model answer: one reason (concentration of high-paid multinational and financial-services jobs) in two or three sentences; one transport effect (motorway or rail links making regional towns accessible to investors and commuters).
