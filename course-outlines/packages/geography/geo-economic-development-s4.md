# Package: geo-economic-development-s4 — Global issues and a justice perspective

- **loId:** `geo-economic-development-s4` · **chapterId:** `geo-economic-development` · **syllabusRef:** 4.2
- **Syllabus:** Students should study "the global issues and a justice perspective relating to
  these patterns" (the uneven patterns of development in 4.1–4.2). Statement: "Levels of
  economic development show major spatial variations and can change over time." No settings
  are named; Ireland's aid programme, Irish NGOs and fair trade are natural Irish examples.
- **Prepared by:** a higher model, 2026-10-20. The writer works only from this package and
  `course-outlines/NOTES_STANDARD.md`.
- **What "justice perspective" means here:** asking who gains and who loses from the uneven
  pattern of development, whether that is fair, and what could make it fairer. Write each issue
  as a block: the issue → why it is unjust → named example(s) → responses and their limits. Each
  issue block (trade, debt, aid, labour, climate) must carry enough for a 30-mark answer when
  two are combined. SimpleStudy has no topic for this; the syllabus requires it.

## 1. Must cover (one bullet or block for every item; tick each off)

The idea
- [ ] J1 Global issues: problems that cross borders and affect the whole world (poverty, inequality, trade rules, debt, climate change, migration). A justice perspective asks whether the distribution of wealth and power is fair, who decides, and who benefits; it treats development as a matter of rights, not charity.
- [ ] J2 The scale of global poverty and inequality: extreme-poverty numbers and trend; the share of wealth held by the richest (section 2). Most extremely poor people live in sub-Saharan Africa.
- [ ] J3 Human rights: the Universal Declaration of Human Rights (1948) includes rights to food, health, education and decent work; poverty denies these rights.
- [ ] J4 Global goals: the Millennium Development Goals (2000–2015) and the Sustainable Development Goals (2015–2030); what they target (poverty, hunger, health, education, gender equality, clean water, climate action, reduced inequality); progress and shortfalls.

Issue 1: unfair trade
- [ ] T1 Dependence on primary commodities: many developing countries export a few raw materials (coffee, cocoa, cotton, minerals) whose prices are low and unstable, and import manufactured goods; the terms of trade tend to move against them.
- [ ] T2 The value chain: most of the final price of a product (coffee, chocolate, clothes) goes to processors, brands and retailers in rich countries; the grower receives a small share.
- [ ] T3 Tariff escalation: rich countries charge higher tariffs on processed goods than on raw materials, discouraging poor countries from processing their own crops.
- [ ] T4 Rich-country farm subsidies (US cotton, the EU's Common Agricultural Policy in the past) let surpluses be sold cheaply abroad ("dumping"), undercutting poor farmers. Example: West African cotton growers and the WTO cotton case (section 2).
- [ ] T5 Responses: the WTO Doha Round (stalled); the EU's "Everything But Arms" scheme; fair trade (T6–T8).
- [ ] T6 Fair trade: how it works: a guaranteed minimum price covering the cost of sustainable production, plus a premium paid to producer co-operatives for community projects (schools, clinics, clean water); long-term contracts; decent working conditions, no child labour; environmental standards; democratic co-operatives. The Fairtrade Mark on products.
- [ ] T7 Fair trade examples: coffee, tea, cocoa, bananas, sugar, cotton; Irish links (section 2): Fairtrade Ireland, Fairtrade towns, Fairtrade Fortnight.
- [ ] T8 Limits of fair trade: a tiny share of world trade; higher prices for consumers; certification costs for small producers; it does not change the trade rules themselves.

Issue 2: debt
- [ ] D1 How the debt crisis arose: 1970s oil-price rises; banks lent oil-exporters' deposits ("petrodollars") to developing countries; interest rates soared around 1980 and commodity prices fell; Mexico's default in 1982 began the debt crisis; many African and Latin American countries could not repay.
- [ ] D2 Effects: more money spent on debt repayments than on health and education; new loans to repay old ones; the 1980s "lost decade" in Latin America and Africa.
- [ ] D3 Structural adjustment: IMF and World Bank loans with conditions (cut public spending, introduce school and health fees, privatise, remove food subsidies, devalue the currency, open to imports); criticised for hurting the poorest. Reuse `-s3` K9 wording.
- [ ] D4 Responses: the Jubilee 2000 campaign; the Heavily Indebted Poor Countries (HIPC) initiative (1996) and the Multilateral Debt Relief Initiative (2005) cancelled much debt for the poorest countries, most in Africa, on condition that savings go to poverty reduction; results (more children in school, e.g. free primary education after debt relief). Debt is rising again in the 2020s.
- [ ] D5 Justice argument: some debts were "odious" (borrowed by dictators who stole or wasted the money), yet the people repay them. One bullet.

Issue 3: aid
- [ ] A1 Types of aid, one bullet each: emergency (humanitarian) aid after disasters and in famines; long-term development aid; bilateral (government to government); multilateral (through the UN, the World Bank, the EU); NGO (voluntary) aid; tied aid (must be spent on goods or services from the donor country).
- [ ] A2 The UN target: rich countries should give 0.7% of their gross national income in official development aid; only a few reach it (section 2); Ireland does not yet.
- [ ] A3 How aid helps: saves lives in emergencies; funds schools, clinics, wells and vaccination; builds roads and infrastructure; trains teachers, nurses and farmers; supports women's groups and small businesses.
- [ ] A4 How aid can hinder: tied aid benefits donor firms and may supply unsuitable technology; aid can be stolen by corrupt elites; food aid can undercut local farmers; dependency instead of self-reliance; loans add to debt; donors' political or strategic interests decide who gets aid rather than need; large prestige projects (dams) can displace people.
- [ ] A5 Better aid: untied aid; aid targeted at the poorest; local participation and ownership; support for self-help and co-operatives; transparency.
- [ ] A6 Ireland's aid: Irish Aid, the government programme, run by the Department of Foreign Affairs; partner countries mainly in sub-Saharan Africa; untied; the policy "A Better World" (2019) focuses on the furthest behind, gender equality, climate and governance. One block of 5–6 bullets.
- [ ] A7 Irish NGOs: Concern Worldwide, Trócaire, GOAL (founding facts in section 2); their emergency and long-term work and their campaigning on justice issues (e.g. Trócaire's campaigns on human rights and climate).
- [ ] A8 Ireland's own history as a reason for solidarity: the Great Famine (1845–49) and emigration. One bullet.

Issue 4: exploitation of workers and resources
- [ ] W1 Multinationals locate labour-intensive work (clothing, electronics assembly, mining) in low-wage countries; benefits (jobs, wages higher than farm work, skills) vs exploitation (very low wages, long hours, unsafe factories, banned trade unions, child labour).
- [ ] W2 Case study: the Rana Plaza collapse, Bangladesh, 2013 (facts in section 2): cause, deaths, brands involved (including Primark, an Irish-founded retailer), the Bangladesh Accord on building safety, compensation; what it shows about global supply chains.
- [ ] W3 Child labour: global figure (section 2), mostly in agriculture; cocoa and mining examples; cobalt mining in the DR Congo (section 2).
- [ ] W4 Responses: ethical consumer campaigns; codes of conduct; laws making large companies check their supply chains for human-rights and environmental abuses (EU directive, section 2); trade unions.
- [ ] W5 Land and resources: foreign companies and governments buying or leasing large areas of farmland in poor countries ("land grabbing"); indigenous peoples losing land to mining, ranching and logging (Amazon example from `-s3`). One or two bullets, no new named cases.

Issue 5: climate justice
- [ ] C1 The poorest countries have contributed least to greenhouse gas emissions but suffer most from climate change (drought in the Sahel, cyclones and floods in Bangladesh, sea-level rise threatening small island states).
- [ ] C2 Responses: climate finance from rich countries; the Loss and Damage Fund (section 2); the Mary Robinson Foundation – Climate Justice. One or two bullets; the science of global warming is `geo-economic-environment`.

Evaluation
- [ ] V1 Progress: extreme poverty has fallen greatly since 1990 (China and India especially); child mortality has fallen; more children attend school.
- [ ] V2 Remaining injustice: sub-Saharan Africa lags; inequality within and between countries is high; conflict (Sudan, see `-s3`) and climate change are reversing gains; the COVID-19 pandemic pushed millions back into extreme poverty (2020).
- [ ] V3 What individuals and Ireland can do: buy fair trade, campaign, support NGOs, press governments on aid, trade and debt policy. One bullet.

## 3. Traps (known errors)

- **Extreme-poverty lines change.** Give $2.15 a day (2017 prices) with the 2022 figure exactly as in section 2. Don't quote "$1 a day" or "$1.90" as current, and don't mix lines.
- **The MDGs had 8 goals; the SDGs have 17.** Don't swap them or give 2025 as the SDG deadline.
- **Fair trade guarantees a minimum price**, not always a higher price than the market; when market prices are higher, producers get the market price plus the premium.
- Don't say all aid is harmful or all aid is good: present both sides with reasons (exams set "help and hinder").
- **Tied aid** = must be spent on the donor's goods and services. **Conditional aid/loans** = attached policy conditions (structural adjustment). Keep them distinct.
- Don't give euro totals or percentages for Irish Aid, Irish NGOs or Fairtrade sales; none are checked.
- Rana Plaza: say "about 1,130 deaths". Don't call it a fire (that was the Tazreen factory fire, 2012; don't name it).
- Past-paper model answers in the question bank include invented examples (e.g. specific aid-debt percentages for Zambia and Mozambique, US farm-equipment tied-aid stories, Bangladesh aid claims). Use none.
- **Neighbouring subsections and chapters:**
  - The patterns these issues relate to (global, EU and Irish variation) are `-s2`; colonial roots are `-s3`. Refer to them; don't repeat them.
  - **The Global Interdependence option** covers the aid debate, debt and the cycle of poverty, NGOs, land ownership, gender roles, fair trade, self-reliance and sustainable development in essay depth for 80-mark questions. This subsection gives the elective-level treatment (about the depth of a 30-mark answer per issue). Don't add models of development, images of development, refugees or the Scandinavian managed-forest example.
  - Multinationals' structure and the international division of labour are `geo-economic-globalisation`; EU trade, the CAP and the Common Fisheries Policy in Ireland are `geo-economic-eu`; global warming, acid rain and sustainable economic development are `geo-economic-environment`.

## 4. What exams ask (topics only)

- 30 marks (expected under the syllabus wording): examine global issues relating to uneven development from a justice perspective, e.g. trade, debt, aid or worker exploitation, with examples.
- How aid programmes can help and hinder development (set as an 80-mark Option essay; the elective expects a shorter version: types of aid, benefits, problems, Irish Aid and NGOs).
- Fair trade and justice issues as part of sustainable development (set in the Option; students of this elective should be able to explain fair trade's aims, how it works and its limits).
- Short questions: explain tied aid; name an Irish NGO; one advantage of fair trade for producers.

## 5. Practice questions to write

1. 30 marks, Higher: "Discuss two global justice issues relating to uneven levels of economic development." Model answer: **unfair trade** (commodity dependence, value chain, subsidies and the cotton case, fair trade as a response and its limits) and **debt** (origins, effects, structural adjustment, HIPC/MDRI). About 7–8 SRPs each.
2. 30 marks, Higher: "Examine how aid can both help and hinder development, with reference to examples you have studied." Model answer: types of aid; three or four ways it helps; four or five ways it hinders; Irish Aid (untied, partner countries) and one Irish NGO as examples; what makes aid effective. About 15 SRPs.
3. 30 marks, Ordinary: "Explain how fair trade helps producers in developing countries." Model answer: minimum price, premium for community projects, co-operatives, working conditions, environmental standards, Fairtrade Mark and Irish examples; one limit. About 10 SRPs.
