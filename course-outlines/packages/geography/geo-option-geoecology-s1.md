## 1. Must cover (one bullet or block for every item; tick each off)

What soil is and where it comes from
- [ ] F1 Soil: the thin layer of loose material on the earth's surface, made of mineral matter, organic matter, water and air, in which plants grow. Pedogenesis = soil formation.
- [ ] F2 Parent material: the rock or deposit a soil forms from. Residual soils form from rock weathered in situ (in place) beneath them. Transported soils form on redeposited weathered material: glacial till (boulder clay), fluvio-glacial sands and gravels, alluvium (river deposits), loess (wind-blown silt), wind-blown sand, volcanic ash. Weathering supplies the regolith that becomes soil (name the link; weathering processes are taught in `geo-rocks-weathering-s3`).
- [ ] F3 Irish soils are young: most formed on glacial deposits laid down during the last glaciation, so they have developed only since the ice melted, and they often differ from the solid rock beneath them.
- [ ] F4 Soil profile, introduced only: a vertical section from the surface to bedrock, divided into horizons: O (organic litter and humus), A (topsoil, mineral matter mixed with humus), B (subsoil, receives material washed down), C (weathered parent material), R (bedrock). A soil pit or road cutting shows it. Full profiles of named soils are in s2.

Composition: the four components (each its own block; exams ask how each influences soil development)
- [ ] C1 Typical proportions by volume in a good loam: about 45% mineral matter, 5% organic matter, 25% water, 25% air. Water and air share the pore spaces, so when one rises the other falls. Draw as a pie chart.
- [ ] C2 Mineral matter: particles of weathered rock, from stones down to clay; the largest component; forms the soil's skeleton; decides texture; releases nutrients (e.g. calcium, potassium, magnesium) as it weathers. Parent rock controls the result: limestone gives base-rich, less acidic soils; granite gives sandy, acidic soils with quartz grains and clay from weathered feldspar; sandstone gives sandy, acidic, free-draining soils. Clay particles are the chemically active part of the mineral matter.
- [ ] C3 Organic matter: living organisms (bacteria, fungi, earthworms, insects, roots) plus dead plant and animal remains. Litter is broken down by decomposers into humus, a dark, sticky, jelly-like substance (humification is the process; explained in s2). Humus darkens the soil, binds particles into crumbs, holds water, holds nutrients and releases them slowly, and is the main store of nitrogen. Earthworms mix and aerate the soil and drag litter down; their casts improve structure. Nitrogen-fixing bacteria (e.g. in the root nodules of clover and other legumes) add nitrogen.
- [ ] C4 Mull and mor humus: mull is mild, crumbly, well decomposed and well mixed by earthworms, slightly acidic to neutral, formed under deciduous woodland and grassland (brown earths); mor is acidic, fibrous and poorly decomposed, with few earthworms, formed under coniferous forest and heath (podzols). Moder is in between: one bullet only.
- [ ] C5 Water: enters as precipitation and is held in pores. Dissolves nutrients so roots can absorb them (the soil solution). Three forms: gravitational water drains down through large pores after rain and carries nutrients away (the cause of leaching); capillary water is held in small pores by surface tension, is available to plants and can move up as well as down; hygroscopic water is a thin film bound tightly to particles that plants cannot use. Field capacity = the water a soil holds after gravitational water has drained; wilting point = when only water plants cannot extract is left.
- [ ] C6 Water movement and its effects: where precipitation exceeds evapotranspiration, water moves down and leaches the soil (Ireland); where evaporation exceeds precipitation, water rises by capillary action and leaves salts and lime near the surface (deserts, grasslands). Too much water fills all pores and causes waterlogging (gleying, in s2). Name these links only; the processes are taught in s2.
- [ ] C7 Air: fills the pores not occupied by water. Supplies oxygen for root respiration and for aerobic organisms that decompose litter; nitrogen for nitrogen-fixing bacteria. Soil air is richer in carbon dioxide than the atmosphere, which forms weak carbonic acid in soil water and aids chemical weathering. Poorly aerated (anaerobic) soils have slow decomposition, so organic matter builds up as peat, and iron is reduced, giving grey colours.
- [ ] C8 The four components interact: e.g. humus improves crumb structure, which opens pores for both air and water; clay holds water but reduces air; organisms need air and water to work. One short block; exams reward showing interaction.

Characteristics (each: what it is, how it is described or measured, how it affects the soil and its use)
- [ ] K1 Texture: the proportions of sand, silt and clay (particle sizes in section 2). Field test by feel: sand is gritty, silt smooth and silky, clay sticky and can be rolled into a ribbon when moist. Shown on a soil texture triangle (a triangular graph; method in `geo-graph-skills-s1`).
- [ ] K2 Texture effects: sandy soils are light, free-draining, well aerated, warm quickly in spring and are easy to plough, but hold little water and few nutrients and are easily leached. Clay soils are heavy, hold water and nutrients, but drain slowly, become waterlogged and cold, crack and harden when dry, and are hard to work. Loam, a balanced mix, combines good drainage with good water and nutrient retention and is the best for farming. Silty soils are fertile but form a surface crust and erode easily.
- [ ] K3 Structure: the way particles are grouped into clumps called peds. Types: crumb (small rounded peds, typical of humus-rich topsoil; the best for farming), granular, platy (flat horizontal plates; hinders drainage and roots), blocky (angular cubes, common in clay subsoils), prismatic or columnar (vertical columns, in some clay subsoils). Structureless soils are single-grain (loose sand) or massive (solid clay).
- [ ] K4 Structure effects: it controls the size and number of pores, so it decides drainage, aeration and how easily roots penetrate. Humus, clay, earthworms, roots and freeze-thaw build peds; compaction by heavy machinery or trampling by livestock on wet ground (called poaching in Ireland) destroys them.
- [ ] K5 Colour: a clue to composition and conditions. Black or dark brown: rich in humus (chernozems, peat). Brown: humus plus iron compounds (brown earths). Red, orange or yellow: iron oxides in a well-aerated soil (tropical latosols, terra rossa). Ash-grey or bleached: heavily leached (the E horizon of a podzol). Grey or blue-grey with orange (rusty) mottles: waterlogged, with reduced iron (gley). White: lime or salt crusts (desert soils). Colour also affects soil temperature: dark soils absorb more heat.
- [ ] K6 Water content and water-retention properties: depend on texture (clay retains, sand drains), structure, and organic content (humus holds several times its own weight of water). Porosity (how much pore space) differs from permeability (how easily water passes through): clay has high porosity but low permeability because its pores are tiny.
- [ ] K7 Organic content: the amount of humus. High in chernozems and peat; moderate in brown earths; low in desert soils (little vegetation) and, surprisingly, in tropical latosols (litter decays so fast that little humus builds up). Affects colour, structure, water retention and fertility.
- [ ] K8 pH (acidity or alkalinity): scale 0–14, 7 neutral. Soils become acidic where heavy rain leaches out bases and where acid litter (conifer needles, heather) builds up; alkaline on limestone and chalk and in dry climates. Most crops and earthworms prefer slightly acidic to neutral soils. Farmers spread lime (ground limestone) to raise pH.
- [ ] K9 Fertility and nutrients: clay particles and humus form the clay–humus complex, tiny negatively charged particles (colloids) that hold positively charged nutrient ions (e.g. calcium, magnesium, potassium) so they are not washed away, and exchange them with roots. Base-rich soils are fertile; base-poor, leached soils are not. NPK (nitrogen, phosphorus, potassium) are the main nutrients farmers add.
- [ ] K10 How characteristics influence soil development (asked repeatedly as a full 80-mark essay): write the notes so a student can build it from K1–K9 without new facts. Texture and structure control drainage, so they decide whether leaching, gleying or capillary rise dominates; pH controls which organisms live in the soil, so it decides mull or mor humus and how fast humification happens; organic content controls structure and fertility, so it decides how well the soil supports the vegetation that feeds it; water retention and aeration together decide whether decomposition is aerobic (fertile brown soils) or anaerobic (peat and gley).

## 3. Traps (known errors)

- Give the 45/5/25/25 split as "typical of a good loam", never as true of every soil.
- Clay particles are the **smallest**, sand the largest. Clay soils have **more** total pore space than sand but drain **more slowly** (tiny pores): don't say clay has little pore space.
- Humus is not the same as litter: litter is undecomposed; humus is the end product of decomposition.
- Tropical rainforest soils are **low** in humus and infertile despite the huge biomass. Don't call them rich soils.
- Do not explain the soil processes (leaching, podzolisation, laterisation, calcification, gleying, humification) in depth here; name them as consequences only. They belong to `geo-option-geoecology-s2`. The global pattern of soils and the named soil profiles are also s2.
- Weathering processes themselves (freeze-thaw, carbonation, hydrolysis) are taught in `geo-rocks-weathering-s3`. Name them as the source of mineral matter only.
- Human effects on soil (compaction, overgrazing, liming as conservation, fertilisers) belong to `geo-option-geoecology-s3`; here mention compaction and liming only as factors affecting structure and pH.
- Don't give humus, nutrient or water-holding percentages for named soils: none are checked except those in section 2.
