# Package: geo-urban-s7 — Developing-world cities

- **loId:** `geo-urban-s7` · **chapterId:** `geo-urban` · **syllabusRef:** 5.6
- **Syllabus:** Students should study "• the expansion and problems in developing world
  cities". International setting: "Case studies from developing world cities."
- **Scope:** Exams ask (almost every year at Ordinary, often at Higher): name a rapidly growing
  developing-world city; reasons for its growth; its problems; one solution. Write the general
  theory once, then three full case studies, each its own set of blocks so a student can choose
  one: **Kolkata** (main), **Mumbai** (facts consistent with `geo-region-continental-s1`) and
  **São Paulo**. Curitiba appears only as a solution. Each case study needs enough for a
  30-mark Higher answer and a 40-mark Ordinary answer.
- **Prepared by:** a higher model, 2026-10-21. The writer works only from this package and
  `course-outlines/NOTES_STANDARD.md`.

## 1. Must cover (one bullet or block for every item; tick each off)

General: expansion
- [ ] G1 Scale and pace: most of the world's urban growth is now in developing countries in Asia, Africa and Latin America; growth of megacities (over 10 million); UN urban shares (section 2).
- [ ] G2 Reasons for rapid growth: natural increase (young urban population, high birth rates, falling death rates) and rural-to-urban migration. Push factors: rural poverty, landlessness, small farms, mechanisation, drought and crop failure, lack of services, conflict, natural disasters. Pull factors: jobs (formal and informal), higher wages, schools, hospitals, perceived opportunities, family already in the city. Primate cities attracting investment and government spending. (The migration theory itself and donor-region impacts are in `geo-population-migration`; here one block only, applied to the city.)

General: problems (each a bullet with cause and effect)
- [ ] G3 Housing: shanty towns/slums/informal settlements (bustees, favelas, chawls, jhopadpattis) on marginal land (river banks, steep slopes, beside railways, rubbish dumps, floodplains); insecure tenure; overcrowding; homelessness and pavement dwellers.
- [ ] G4 Water, sanitation and health: no piped water or sewers; open drains; water-borne disease (cholera, typhoid, dysentery); shared toilets.
- [ ] G5 Employment: the informal sector (street vendors, rickshaw pullers, waste pickers, home workers): no security, low pay, child labour; underemployment.
- [ ] G6 Transport and congestion: road networks unable to cope; overcrowded trains and buses; road deaths.
- [ ] G7 Pollution: air pollution from traffic, industry and cooking fuels; untreated sewage and industrial waste in rivers; uncollected rubbish.
- [ ] G8 Flooding and hazards: monsoon or tropical downpours on low-lying, paved, badly drained land; landslides on hillside slums; fires.
- [ ] G9 Social problems: crime, drugs, street children, inequality between rich and poor districts side by side, weak local government.

General: solutions
- [ ] G10 Slum clearance (demolition and eviction): usually fails, moving people further out or into new slums.
- [ ] G11 Site-and-service schemes and self-help (aided self-help): authorities provide plots with water, drains and power; residents build their own homes.
- [ ] G12 Slum upgrading in place: paving lanes, water taps, toilets, drains, electricity, secure tenure, so people improve their homes.
- [ ] G13 Planned satellite towns and new towns to divert growth (Navi Mumbai; Kolkata's Salt Lake and New Town).
- [ ] G14 Transport solutions: metros, suburban rail, bus rapid transit (Curitiba).
- [ ] G15 Reducing migration: rural development, land reform, jobs in smaller cities. NGOs and community groups.

Case study 1: Kolkata (India)
- [ ] K1 Site and history: on the Hooghly near the Bay of Bengal, low-lying delta land; founded by the British East India Company; capital of British India until 1911; port and jute.
- [ ] K2 Reasons for growth: colonial port and administrative capital; jute and engineering industries attracting migrants from Bihar, Odisha and rural Bengal; the 1943 Bengal famine; refugees after Partition (1947) and the 1971 Bangladesh war; natural increase; regional primacy in eastern India.
- [ ] K3 Problems: bustees (slums) and pavement dwellers; overcrowding; poor water and sewerage; monsoon flooding of low-lying streets; air pollution; traffic congestion; economic decline after Partition cut the jute hinterland, so jobs did not keep pace; informal work (including hand-pulled rickshaws).
- [ ] K4 Responses: Calcutta Metropolitan Development Authority (1970) and bustee improvement (taps, toilets, paved lanes, drains); India's first metro (1984); planned satellite towns Salt Lake (Bidhannagar) and New Town (Rajarhat) with IT parks; East Kolkata Wetlands treating the city's sewage naturally; NGOs (Missionaries of Charity). Evaluation: upgrading improved conditions for many but slums remain; new towns serve the middle classes more than the poor.

Case study 2: Mumbai (India)
- [ ] M1 Site and growth: seven islands joined by reclamation, deep natural harbour; British port; cotton mills; India's financial and film capital; migrants from all over India (Maharashtra, Uttar Pradesh, Bihar). Use only `geo-region-continental-s1` facts, which this package repeats.
- [ ] M2 Problems: Dharavi and other slums; very high land and housing costs on a narrow peninsula; overcrowded suburban trains; congestion; 2005 flood; water and sanitation; decline of the cotton mills after 1982.
- [ ] M3 Responses: Navi Mumbai planned twin city (CIDCO, 1970s); Slum Rehabilitation Authority (1995) schemes giving free flats to slum dwellers in return for land; Dharavi Redevelopment Project (awarded to the Adani Group, 2022); metro lines (first 2014); Bandra–Worli Sea Link (2009); Mumbai Trans Harbour Link (2024). Evaluation: rehousing flats criticised for poor design and for breaking up Dharavi's home-based industries.

Case study 3: São Paulo (Brazil)
- [ ] S1 Site and growth: on a plateau inland from the port of Santos; founded by Jesuits in 1554; coffee wealth in the 19th century and the Santos railway; European and Japanese immigration; Brazil's industrial heartland from the 1950s (car industry in the ABC region); migrants from the drought-prone North-East (nordestinos); largest city in South America.
- [ ] S2 Problems: favelas (Paraisópolis beside wealthy Morumbi; Heliópolis); extreme inequality; traffic congestion; air pollution; flooding of the Tietê river valley; water shortage (2014–2015 drought); crime.
- [ ] S3 Responses: car-use restriction by number plate (rodízio, 1997); metro (from 1974); Rodoanel ring road; favela upgrading and the Projeto Cingapura flats (1990s); Brazil's Minha Casa, Minha Vida housing programme (2009). Evaluation: upgrading and transport helped, but favelas and congestion persist.

Solution example: Curitiba (Brazil)
- [ ] C1 Bus rapid transit (1974) under architect-mayor Jaime Lerner: dedicated bus lanes and tube stations; reduced car use; copied worldwide (e.g. Bogotá's TransMilenio). Recycling-for-food exchange. One block.

## 3. Traps

- **Lagos, Nairobi, Rio de Janeiro, Dhaka, Mexico City: no checked facts.** Don't name them as case studies or add figures for them; past-paper model answers use them with unreliable numbers.
- **Kolkata was the capital of British India until 1911**; Mumbai is the financial, not political, capital (same as `geo-region-continental-s1`). Use the current names, giving Calcutta/Bombay once.
- **No 2021 Indian census exists.** Use Census 2011 figures only for Indian cities. Don't give Dharavi's population or Kolkata's slum percentage.
- São Paulo is **not** Brazil's capital (Brasília is). It is inland, not on the coast; Santos is its port.
- City vs metropolitan figures: São Paulo city about 11.5 million, metro region about 21 million (Census 2022). Don't mix them.
- Bhopal, Singur and India-wide industry belong to `geo-region-continental-s1`; Brazil as a developing economy belongs to `geo-economic-development`. Rural-to-urban migration's impacts on **donor** regions belong to `geo-population-migration`.
- Developed-world problems are s5; global future issues (megacity projections beyond the UN figures, climate risk to coastal megacities) are s8.
- Don't copy past-paper urban-share tables or graph values.

## 4. What exams ask (topics only)

- Ordinary 40 marks (most years): name a rapidly growing developing-world city; explain reasons for its growth; describe the problems caused; explain one solution. Also: three factors influencing its growth; three problems explained.
- Higher 30 marks: discuss the problems arising from the growth of cities in the developing world; rural-to-urban migration in the developing world (shared with `geo-population-migration`).
- Short questions: two potential problems of rapid urban growth in sub-Saharan Africa or another region (general G3–G9 answer).

## 5. Practice questions to write

1. 30 marks, Higher: "Discuss the problems that can arise from the growth of cities in the developing world." Model answer: G3–G9 illustrated with **Kolkata** facts, about 15 SRPs.
2. 40 marks, Ordinary: "(i) Name one developing-world city that is growing rapidly. (ii) Explain the reasons for its growth. (iii) Describe the problems caused. (iv) Explain one solution." Model answer on **São Paulo**; the solution is the rodízio or favela upgrading, explained in 4–5 points.
3. 30 marks, Higher: "Examine the measures taken to solve the problems of one developing-world city." Model answer on **Mumbai only** with evaluation of each measure.
