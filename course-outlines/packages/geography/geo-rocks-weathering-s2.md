## 1. Must cover (one bullet or block for every item; tick each off)

The interaction in general (one short block, before the four options)
- [ ] H1 Humans interact with the rock cycle by extracting rock, minerals and energy from the crust. This speeds up the removal of rock far beyond natural weathering and erosion, exposes fresh rock to weathering, and creates new deposits (spoil heaps, tailings).
- [ ] H2 The interaction is an exogenic process done by people: it is the human equivalent of erosion and deposition.

Option A: Mining
- [ ] M1 What is mined and why: metal ores (zinc, lead, copper, gold), coal, salt. Ore bodies form where minerals are concentrated by igneous activity or by hot fluids moving through rock.
- [ ] M2 Methods: exploration (geological surveys, test drilling); open-cast (open-pit) mining where the ore is near the surface (overburden removed); underground (shaft) mining where it is deep (shafts, tunnels; room and pillar).
- [ ] M3 Processing: crushing and grinding ore, separating the mineral; waste rock and fine waste (tailings) stored in tailings ponds.
- [ ] M4 Irish case study: Tara Mines, Navan, Co. Meath (facts from section 2).
- [ ] M5 International case study: an open-pit mine (from section 2) and the Aberfan disaster as an example of mining waste causing a mass movement.
- [ ] M6 Impacts: landscape scars and subsidence; spoil heaps and tailings; acid mine drainage and heavy-metal pollution of rivers (Avoca); dust, noise and heavy traffic; versus jobs, exports and local spending.
- [ ] M7 Management: environmental licensing and monitoring, lining and capping of tailings ponds, rehabilitation after closure (Lisheen).

Option B: Extraction of building materials (quarrying)
- [ ] B1 Materials: limestone (cement, aggregate, lime for agriculture), sand and gravel (from glacial and fluvio-glacial deposits, for concrete), granite and other dimension stone (cut blocks for building, kerbs, headstones), slate (roofing), marble (decoration).
- [ ] B2 Process: remove the overburden (soil and glacial till), cut benches into the rock face, drill and blast, crush and screen (grade by size); dimension stone is cut, not blasted.
- [ ] B3 Cement: limestone is crushed, mixed with shale and heated in a kiln to make clinker, then ground with gypsum. Heating limestone releases carbon dioxide (calcium carbonate → lime + carbon dioxide), returning to the atmosphere carbon that had been locked in rock for hundreds of millions of years.
- [ ] B4 Irish case study: limestone quarrying and cement at Platin, Co. Meath (and the other cement works in section 2); sand and gravel near Blessington, Co. Wicklow; granite at Ballyknockan, Co. Wicklow; Connemara marble; Valentia slate.
- [ ] B5 Impacts: permanent scars, loss of farmland and habitat, dust, noise and blasting vibration, heavy lorries on rural roads, lowering of the local water table; versus jobs and essential materials for housing and roads.
- [ ] B6 Management: planning permission and conditions (working hours, dust and noise limits, screening with earth banks and trees), and rehabilitation of worked-out quarries as lakes, wildlife habitats, landfill or amenities.

Option C: Oil and gas exploitation
- [ ] O1 Formation: remains of tiny marine organisms buried in sea-floor sediment; heat and pressure over millions of years turn them into oil and gas (a sedimentary process); they migrate upward through porous, permeable reservoir rock (e.g. sandstone) until trapped beneath impermeable cap rock (e.g. shale) in a trap (anticline, fault or salt dome).
- [ ] O2 Exploitation: seismic surveys, exploratory wells, production platforms or subsea wells, pipelines to an onshore terminal.
- [ ] O3 Irish case study 1: Kinsale Head gas field (facts from section 2).
- [ ] O4 Irish case study 2: Corrib gas field and the Bellanaboy terminal controversy (facts from section 2).
- [ ] O5 International case study: the Deepwater Horizon spill (facts from section 2); the North Sea as a major oil and gas province.
- [ ] O6 Fracking: hydraulic fracturing of shale to release gas; concerns over groundwater pollution and induced earthquakes; banned in Ireland (section 2).
- [ ] O7 Impacts: energy supply and security, revenue, jobs; versus oil spills, damage to marine life and fishing, conflict with local communities, and burning fossil fuels returning stored carbon to the atmosphere (climate change).

Option D: Geothermal energy production
- [ ] G1 Source: heat from the earth's interior (radioactive decay and residual heat). Temperature rises with depth (the geothermal gradient). Heat is greatest near plate boundaries and hotspots, where magma is close to the surface.
- [ ] G2 High-temperature (high-enthalpy) systems: groundwater heated by hot rock near magma; production wells bring steam and hot water to the surface; steam drives turbines to generate electricity; hot water heats buildings (district heating); cooled water is pumped back down a reinjection well.
- [ ] G3 Low-temperature systems: ground-source heat pumps use the steady warmth of shallow ground to heat homes; this is the type suited to Ireland, which is far from a plate boundary.
- [ ] G4 Enhanced geothermal systems: water is pumped into hot dry rock to fracture it and pick up heat.
- [ ] G5 International case study: Iceland (facts from section 2), with why Iceland has geothermal heat (Mid-Atlantic Ridge plus a hotspot).
- [ ] G6 Other international examples: Larderello, Italy; Wairakei, New Zealand.
- [ ] G7 Ireland: low-temperature potential only; warm springs show heated groundwater exists; heat pumps are the main use.
- [ ] G8 Advantages: renewable, low carbon emissions, reliable (not weather-dependent), cheap once built. Disadvantages: limited to suitable locations; high drilling cost; release of hydrogen sulphide gas (rotten-egg smell); minerals deposited in pipes; induced earthquakes (Basel).

## 3. Traps (known errors)

- A marking-scheme answer cites a basalt quarry "at Ballyclare" and limestone quarries "throughout the Burren". Neither is checked; do not use them. The Burren is a protected landscape, not a quarrying district.
- Do not quote cement's share of global CO2, aggregate tonnages or temperatures from past marking schemes: none are checked.
- Kilkenny "black marble" is limestone. Use Connemara marble for marble.
- Do not name an Irish geothermal power station or deep geothermal project: there is none to name.
- Aberfan is a mining-waste disaster in Wales, not a coal-mine collapse. Keep its figures exactly as given.
- The formation of rock types is in `geo-rocks-weathering-s1`; give only the one-line context each option needs (e.g. limestone formed in a warm shallow sea).
- Mass movement processes are taught in `geo-rocks-weathering-s5`; refer to Aberfan as a flow slide of saturated waste in one or two bullets.
- Keep each option self-contained: a student who studies only geothermal must find everything they need in that block.
