# Package: geo-economic-activities-s2 — The mobility of modern economic activities and factors in their location

## 1. Must cover (one bullet or block for every item; tick each off)

Key terms
- [ ] K1 Location factor; footloose industry (link s1 F1, define again only as a key term); branch plant (a factory or office owned by a company headquartered elsewhere, usually carrying out routine production); headquarters (HQ) and research and development (R&D) functions; greenfield site vs brownfield site; industrial estate / business park; cluster (agglomeration); linkages (backward: suppliers; forward: customers); infrastructure.
- [ ] K2 Bulk-reducing industries (raw material loses weight in processing, so the plant locates near the raw material, e.g. alumina refining, dairy processing, fish processing, mining) vs bulk-gaining industries (product gains weight or bulk, so the plant locates near the market, e.g. brewing, soft drinks bottling, bakeries). This is the core idea of least-cost location (Alfred Weber, 1909): firms locate where total transport costs are lowest. One block.

Factors in the location of economic activities (each: what it means, why it matters, an Irish example from section 2)
- [ ] L1 Raw materials: weight-losing raw materials pull processing to their source or to a port where they arrive.
- [ ] L2 Energy/power: historically industry clustered on coalfields; national electricity grids freed most industry, but energy-hungry activities (alumina refining, data centres) still need large, reliable, affordable power.
- [ ] L3 Labour: quantity, skills and education, cost, productivity, flexibility. High-tech and services seek graduates; standardised assembly seeks low wages.
- [ ] L4 Markets: size and wealth of the market; access to the EU single market; bulk-gaining and perishable goods near the market; services locate near customers.
- [ ] L5 Transport and communications: ports (deep water for bulk carriers), airports (for light, high-value goods and business travel), motorways, rail, broadband and fibre. Include the advantages of each freight mode (road, rail, sea, air) and one environmental challenge of air transport (CO2 emissions, noise, land take).
- [ ] L6 Capital: money for investment from banks, shareholders, government grants and venture capital.
- [ ] L7 Government and EU policy: tax rates, grants, serviced sites and advance factories (IDA Ireland), regional policy steering firms to less-developed areas, planning permission, EU state-aid rules and funds, trade agreements.
- [ ] L8 Site: flat, well-drained, large enough for expansion, cheap land, water supply and effluent/waste disposal, distance from homes for hazardous industry.
- [ ] L9 Services and infrastructure: water, electricity, gas, broadband, waste treatment, housing, schools.
- [ ] L10 Agglomeration and clusters: firms in the same industry gather to share skilled workers, suppliers, research and universities; this is why Cork Harbour (pharma) and Galway (medtech) grew.
- [ ] L11 Personal and historical factors; industrial inertia (an industry stays put after the original reason has gone, e.g. Guinness at St James's Gate).
- [ ] L12 Quality of life and environment: attractive places to live help firms recruit skilled staff.
- [ ] L13 Local opposition and environmental regulation can stop or delay a project (one example: Corrib gas terminal).
- [ ] L14 How the relative importance of factors has changed: raw materials and power (19th century) → markets and labour (20th century) → footloose activities chasing skills, tax, connectivity and quality of life (today).

Irish location case studies (each a full block with section 2 facts)
- [ ] C1 Aughinish Alumina, Co. Limerick: a raw-material and port-oriented heavy industry (bulk-reducing; deep-water estuary; imported bauxite; flat island site away from towns; energy; government support). Problems: bauxite residue (red mud) storage; ownership by a Russian company.
- [ ] C2 Intel, Leixlip, Co. Kildare: footloose high-technology manufacturing (labour and graduates; IDA support and tax; access to EU market; M4 motorway and Dublin Airport; large greenfield site; electricity and water supply).
- [ ] C3 Cork Harbour pharmaceutical cluster (Ringaskiddy): deep water, large flat sites zoned for industry, water supply and waste facilities, skilled labour from UCC and Munster Technological University, IDA, tax, clustering.
- [ ] C4 Galway medical-technology cluster: DEC's closure (1993) left skilled engineers; Boston Scientific (1994) and Medtronic followed; University of Galway research; cluster of suppliers. Detail of Galway industry is in `geo-region-ireland-s2`; keep this to two or three bullets.
- [ ] C5 Food processing near raw materials: dairy plants in Munster (Kerry Group, Listowel/Tralee); fish processing at Killybegs. Brewing near the market: Guinness, Dublin.
- [ ] C6 Data centres as a modern location problem: why they choose Ireland and Dublin (cool climate, fibre links to the USA and Europe, existing clusters, tax), and why the grid now limits them.

Mobility of modern economic activities
- [ ] M1 Why modern activities are mobile: lower transport costs (containerisation, air freight), instant communications (internet, fibre, video calls), standardised production that low-skilled workers can do, the removal of trade barriers (EU single market, World Trade Organization), and multinationals able to compare countries and move capital quickly.
- [ ] M2 Consequences for host countries: rapid job creation when investment arrives, but jobs can leave just as quickly; branch plants are vulnerable because decisions are made at a distant HQ; competition between countries for investment (tax, grants).

Corporate strategies and branch plants
- [ ] B1 Corporate strategies that open branch plants: expansion into new markets; getting inside a trade bloc (tariff-free access to the EU); lower costs; access to skilled labour; following competitors into a cluster; tax planning.
- [ ] B2 Strategies that close branch plants: rationalisation (cutting surplus capacity after a fall in demand or a merger); relocation to lower-cost countries; outsourcing to contract manufacturers; automation; mergers and takeovers; a product reaching the end of its life cycle.
- [ ] B3 Upgrading as an alternative to closure: plants survive by moving up the value chain (more R&D, advanced products, services). Intel's continuing investment at Leixlip (Fab 34) is the example.
- [ ] B4 One illustrative bullet each, as location changes: Dell moved manufacturing from Limerick to Łódź, Poland in 2009 (lower costs inside the enlarged EU); DEC closed in Galway in 1993 and its skilled workforce helped attract the medtech cluster (C4). The full Dell case study is in `geo-economic-globalisation-s2`; don't write it out here.

Product life cycle and changes in location
- [ ] P1 The location pattern of the product life cycle, in one block of four bullets: introduction near HQ and R&D in a core region with highly skilled labour; growth: branch plants in other developed countries near large markets (Ireland gained many); maturity: a standardised product is mass-produced in low-wage locations; decline: closure or concentration in the cheapest plant. Name the stages only; the sales-against-time graph and the MNC case are in `geo-economic-globalisation-s2`.
- [ ] P2 Apple Cork as a location shift (section 2): opened 1980 to make computers; as Apple's manufacturing moved to contract manufacturers in Asia, the Cork site shifted mainly to operations, support and services.
- [ ] P3 What a host country does in response: attract firms early in new life cycles (pharma, medtech, software), and invest in education and research (Science Foundation Ireland, universities) so jobs are harder to move.

The future of economic activities
- [ ] U1 Information technology: computing and the internet have made many services footloose and made Ireland a European base for software and internet firms (links to s1 F2).
- [ ] U2 Teleservices: services delivered at a distance by phone or internet (customer support, technical support, remote data processing). Location effect: they can locate anywhere with broadband, including peripheral areas, but low-skilled work moves easily to cheaper countries or is automated.
- [ ] U3 E-commerce: buying and selling goods and services over the internet (exams ask for the definition; keep it as a key term). Location effects only: warehouses and delivery depots near motorways and airports; pressure on town-centre shops; bank branch closures. Effects on an MNC's operations are in `geo-economic-globalisation-s2`.
- [ ] U4 Remote and hybrid working after COVID-19: the National Broadband Plan, Connected Hubs, and the chance to spread jobs to rural Ireland; regional broadband differences (exams use regional broadband data).
- [ ] U5 Artificial intelligence and automation as future drivers of location and job change: one or two bullets, no figures.

Locating an activity on an Ordnance Survey map or aerial photograph (skills)
- [ ] S1 Method for "explain two reasons why X might locate here and one reason why not": each reason = name the factor, give map/photo evidence (with grid reference or accepted notation, or the photo location), explain why it matters. Three to five SRPs per reason.
- [ ] S2 Factor checklists for each activity the exams set: (a) factory or multinational: flat land, edge-of-town site, national primary road or motorway, railway, port or airport, town labour force, services (schools, hospital, housing), water, existing industrial estate; (b) fish processing plant: pier/harbour/fishing port, coastal location, roads to markets, water, labour, low-lying land; (c) hotel: coast, beach, scenery, lakes or mountains, antiquities, golf course, nearby town services, road access, tourist information, water sports. Reasons not to locate: steep or high land, floodplain, protected or scenic area (planning conflict), poor roads, remoteness from labour, distance from services, impact on residents.
- [ ] S3 Worked example spec: the writer invents a fictional map extract (e.g. "the invented town of Ballyglas beside an invented N-road and river") with clearly labelled illustrative grid references. Never use a real past-paper map, place or grid reference.

## 3. Traps

- Bauxite is the raw material; alumina is the product of Aughinish; aluminium is made from alumina elsewhere, in smelters. Don't say Aughinish makes aluminium.
- Brewing and soft drinks are **bulk-gaining** (market-oriented); alumina refining, dairy processing and fish processing are **bulk-reducing** (raw-material-oriented). Don't swap them.
- Dell moved manufacturing to **Łódź, Poland**, in 2009, not to China, and not in 2008. Dell did not leave Ireland entirely. Its full case study (direct-sales model, reasons, EGF, effects on Limerick) is in `geo-economic-globalisation-s2`: one bullet here only.
- The product life cycle graph and stage theory, IT's impact on an MNC's operations, teleservices and e-commerce as MNC topics, and Intel's Apollo joint venture belong to `geo-economic-globalisation-s2`. Keep this subsection's versions short and about location.
- DEC closed its Galway plant in **1993**; Boston Scientific came in **1994**.
- Don't call Intel or Google "footloose" because they could move easily tomorrow: Intel's plant is a huge fixed investment. Footloose means free to choose a location, not likely to leave.
- The studied MNC's full structure, sourcing, processing and markets, and how globalisation changed world trade, belong to `geo-economic-globalisation`. If the writer profiles Intel, keep to location factors and corporate strategy; don't give a sourcing/markets map.
- Industrial decline as an Irish case study (Cork 1980s closures, Waterford Crystal, sugar) is in s1. Here, only Dell and DEC are developed, as corporate-strategy and life-cycle examples.
- Tourism's development factors and Irish tourism regions are in s1. Here, tourism appears only as hotel siting on maps and photos.
- Factors for secondary activity in a named Irish region (GDA, the West) as a regional-geography answer belong to `geo-region-ireland`. Here, the factors are taught in general with Irish firms as examples.
- Renewable energy siting questions (wind farms on maps) belong to `geo-economic-environment` and mapwork. Leave them out.
- Map and photo skills: never use real past-paper maps (Arklow, Macroom, Templemore, Sligo, Foxford or others), grid references or features. Invent a clearly labelled fictional example.
- Don't give a time-zone or distance figure for Intel beyond "close to Dublin Airport".
- Don't state Aughinish's alumina destinations, workforce, or any EU sanctions outcome.

Model answer on a clearly labelled, invented map description: harbour/pier, road links to markets, labour from the town (two reasons, about 4 SRPs each with invented grid references labelled illustrative); one disadvantage (e.g. narrow coastal roads, scenic area conflict).
