# Package: geo-option-geoecology-s5 — Human alteration of biomes

- **loId:** `geo-option-geoecology-s5` · **chapterId:** `geo-option-geoecology` · **syllabusRef:** 7.4
- **Syllabus:** Statement: "Biomes have been altered by human activities." Students should
  study the impact of "early settlement and clearing of forests", "the felling of tropical rain
  forests", "intensive agricultural practices" and "industrial development". International
  settings: "Appropriate European and global examples."
- **Level:** Higher Level only; 80-mark essays. Exams ask for all three of the last three
  activities in one answer, or for a general discussion, so each of the **four activities** is a
  self-contained set of blocks with its own case studies. SimpleStudy has no topic for this
  subsection, so this package sets the full scope.
- **Prepared by:** a higher model, 2026-10-20. The writer works only from this package and
  `course-outlines/NOTES_STANDARD.md`.

## 1. Must cover (one bullet or block for every item; tick each off)

The idea
- [ ] I1 Natural (climax) vegetation is what would grow if left undisturbed. Humans alter biomes by removing, replacing or degrading vegetation, changing soils and water, introducing or removing species, and polluting. Very little of the earth's land remains untouched. Effects reach every part of the system: climate, soil, plants, animals, people.

Early settlement and clearing of forests
- [ ] E1 Ireland's post-glacial forest: after the ice melted, forest of birch, hazel, pine, oak, elm and ash spread over almost all of Ireland (temperate deciduous forest biome).
- [ ] E2 Mesolithic hunter-gatherers had little impact. Neolithic farmers (from about 6,000 years ago) cleared forest with stone axes and fire to grow cereals and graze animals: the start of human alteration. The elm decline in pollen records dates from this time.
- [ ] E3 Céide Fields case study: field walls built by Neolithic farmers, later buried by blanket bog. Shows clearance followed by soil leaching and peat growth: forest clearance, a wetter climate and leaching turned forest soils into bog.
- [ ] E4 Later clearance: Bronze and Iron Age, early Christian and medieval farming; then the great clearance of the 16th–17th centuries, when woods were felled for shipbuilding, barrel staves, charcoal for iron smelting, and to remove cover for Irish resistance; by 1900 Ireland was among the least wooded countries in Europe.
- [ ] E5 Results for the Irish biome: native deciduous forest replaced by farmland, hedgerows, bog and grassland; loss of woodland species (e.g. the wolf, hunted to extinction); soils leached and podzolised under heath; blanket bog spread on uplands.
- [ ] E6 Modern response: state afforestation, mostly with fast-growing non-native conifers (Sitka spruce), which forms a new, species-poor plantation habitat and acidifies soils and water; native woodland schemes and protection of remnants (Killarney).
- [ ] E7 European example: the Mediterranean, deforested since ancient times for shipbuilding, fuel, farming and goat grazing; evergreen oak and pine forest degraded to maquis (dense scrub) and garrigue (low, open scrub on thin soils), with soil erosion; the cedar forests of Lebanon reduced to small remnants.
- [ ] E8 Global example of early settlement: Iceland (from s3: birch woodland cleared and grazed after settlement; one bullet, no new facts).

The felling of tropical rainforests (Amazon case study in full; South-East Asia as second example)
- [ ] F1 Extent and rate: dated deforestation figures for the Brazilian Amazon and their trend (section 2).
- [ ] F2 Causes, each its own bullet with a named example: cattle ranching (the largest single cause); soya farming; logging of hardwoods (selective logging opens roads and the canopy); road building and planned settlement (the Trans-Amazonian Highway; BR-364 in Rondônia with its "fishbone" pattern of clearings); mining (Carajás iron ore; illegal gold mining, garimpo); hydroelectric dams (Belo Monte); government policy, debt and global demand for beef, soya, timber and minerals.
- [ ] F3 Impacts on the biome: loss of habitat and species (many endemic species); fragmentation and "edge effects"; the nutrient cycle is broken, so cleared latosols lose fertility within a few years and pasture is abandoned; soil erosion and laterite crust; rivers silt up; less evapotranspiration so less rainfall downwind, and longer dry seasons; more fires; release of stored carbon to the atmosphere; scientists warn that beyond a threshold parts of the forest could turn to degraded savanna (a "tipping point").
- [ ] F4 Impacts on indigenous peoples: loss of land, disease, violence and conflict with ranchers and miners (Yanomami and gold miners; Chico Mendes).
- [ ] F5 Responses and evaluation: protected areas and indigenous territories; extractive reserves; the Forest Code; satellite monitoring by INPE; the soy moratorium; the Amazon Fund; changes in national policy and enforcement and their effect on the rate (fall to 2012, rise to 2021, fall again from 2023). Evaluate: success depends on political will and enforcement.
- [ ] F6 South-East Asia: palm oil and logging in Indonesia and Malaysia (Borneo and Sumatra); peat swamp forest drained and burned, causing haze; the orangutan as the flagship threatened species.
- [ ] F7 European link: Europe's demand for beef, soya (animal feed), timber and palm oil drives felling; the EU Deforestation Regulation (one bullet).

Intensive agricultural practices
- [ ] A1 What it is: high inputs of capital, machinery, fertiliser, pesticides and water to get maximum output per hectare; monoculture; larger fields; drained wetlands.
- [ ] A2 Irish example: intensification of dairying and tillage: hedgerow removal and field enlargement, drainage of wetlands and callows, reseeding of species-rich meadows with ryegrass, silage replacing hay (earlier cutting destroys nests of ground-nesting birds such as the corncrake), heavy use of fertiliser and slurry polluting rivers and lakes (eutrophication), loss of wild bees and farmland birds; agriculture's share of greenhouse gas emissions. Response: the Nitrates Directive, agri-environment schemes, the All-Ireland Pollinator Plan.
- [ ] A3 European example: the Common Agricultural Policy's price supports encouraged intensification from the 1960s–80s (hedgerow loss in eastern England, wetland drainage); later reforms paid for environmental farming.
- [ ] A4 Temperate grassland: the North American prairie almost entirely ploughed for wheat and maize; the Dust Bowl (reuse from s3; one bullet).
- [ ] A5 Irrigation case study: the Aral Sea, Central Asia (cotton irrigation; one of the world's worst environmental disasters).
- [ ] A6 Rainforest overlap: cattle and soya in the Amazon are intensive agriculture too; point back to F2 without repeating.

Industrial development
- [ ] D1 Acid rain: sulphur dioxide and nitrogen oxides from coal- and oil-burning power stations, industry and vehicles form acids carried downwind; effects on coniferous forests (needle loss, dieback, nutrient leaching from soils) and on lakes (fish killed, aluminium released); Scandinavia and the "Black Triangle" of Central Europe. Responses: international agreements, flue-gas desulphurisation, lime spreading on lakes, the switch away from coal; acid rain has declined greatly in Europe.
- [ ] D2 Irish example: industrial peat extraction by Bord na Móna altered the raised-bog biome of the Midlands (milled peat for power stations and horticulture); the end of peat-fired electricity; rehabilitation and rewetting of cutaway bogs; Clara Bog as a protected intact raised bog. Moneypoint power station and acid emissions.
- [ ] D3 Mining and oil: the Athabasca oil sands, Alberta, Canada (boreal forest and peatland removed by open-pit mining; tailings ponds); oil pollution in the Niger Delta, Nigeria (mangroves and farmland).
- [ ] D4 Nuclear accident: Chernobyl, 1986 (the "Red Forest"; the exclusion zone, now a refuge for wildlife in the absence of people).
- [ ] D5 Industry and global warming (one bullet only): greenhouse gases from industry shift climate belts and stress biomes (coral bleaching, thawing tundra permafrost, more drought and fire). Global warming as an issue is taught in Global Interdependence; don't develop it here.
- [ ] D6 Urban and industrial sprawl replaces habitats and seals soils (one bullet).

Evaluation (one short block)
- [ ] V1 Not all alteration is negative: protected areas, rewilding, rewetting bogs, results-based farming (the Burren, from s3) and pollution controls can restore biomes; the balance between economic gain and biome loss.

## 2. Checked facts and examples (the only named places, dates and figures you may use)

Do not name any place, date or figure that is not on this list.

### Early settlement and forest clearance

| Item | Example(s) |
|---|---|
| E1 | After the last glaciation (ended about 10,000 years ago), Ireland was colonised by birch and hazel, then pine, oak, elm and ash; by about 6,000 years ago forest covered almost all of the island. |
| E2 | Farming (the Neolithic) reached Ireland about 6,000 years ago (about 4000 BC). A sharp fall in elm pollen around this time (the elm decline) is seen across Ireland and Britain; clearance by farmers and elm disease are both thought to have contributed. |
| E3 | Céide Fields, north Co. Mayo: a Neolithic landscape of stone-walled fields, about 5,500 years old, preserved under blanket bog; among the oldest known field systems in the world. Discovered after a local teacher, Patrick Caulfield, found stone walls under the bog while cutting turf in the 1930s; his son Séamas Caulfield, an archaeologist, later studied the site. |
| E4 | Clearance in the 16th and 17th centuries, during the Tudor conquest and plantations, for timber exports, shipbuilding, barrel staves, and charcoal for ironworks. From `geo-rocks-weathering-s7`: about 1% of Ireland was forested around 1900; forest cover is now about 11%, mostly conifer plantations. |
| E5 | The last wolf in Ireland is generally said to have been killed in the late 18th century. Use "the late 1700s" only. |
| E6 | Sitka spruce (native to western North America) is the main plantation tree in Ireland. Native woodland remnants: the oakwoods of Killarney National Park, Co. Kerry, and the Reenadinna yew wood on the Muckross peninsula there, a rare pure yew wood. |
| E7 | The Mediterranean: maquis and garrigue scrub replaced much of the original evergreen oak and pine forest; soil erosion on cleared slopes (reuse `geo-region-european-s2`). The cedars of Lebanon, prized by the ancient Egyptians and Phoenicians for shipbuilding and temples, survive only in small groves, e.g. the protected Cedars of God grove, a UNESCO World Heritage site. |

### Felling of tropical rainforests

| Item | Example(s) |
|---|---|
| F1 | Annual deforestation in the Brazilian Amazon, measured by Brazil's space agency INPE (the PRODES satellite programme): a peak of about 27,800 km² in 2004; a fall to about 4,600 km² in 2012; a rise to about 13,000 km² in 2021; a fall again from 2023 under renewed enforcement. Close to a fifth of the original Amazon forest has been cleared. Round these figures; don't add other years. |
| F2 cattle and soya | Cattle ranching accounts for most cleared land in the Brazilian Amazon (the largest single cause). Soya, much of it exported as animal feed (including to Europe and China), spread north from Mato Grosso. |
| F2 roads | The Trans-Amazonian Highway (BR-230), begun in the early 1970s by Brazil's military government to open the Amazon to settlers from the poor, drought-prone north-east. BR-364 into Rondônia, paved in the 1980s with World Bank support (the Polonoroeste programme), led to a "fishbone" pattern of clearings along side roads seen on satellite images. |
| F2 mining | Carajás, Pará state: one of the world's largest iron-ore mines (operated by Vale), opened in the 1980s, with a railway to the coast and charcoal-fuelled pig-iron smelters that used forest wood. Illegal gold mining (garimpo) on Yanomami land pollutes rivers with mercury. |
| F2 dams | Belo Monte dam, Xingu River, Pará: one of the world's largest hydroelectric schemes by capacity, completed in 2019; it flooded forest and land, diverted the river past the Volta Grande (Big Bend) and displaced thousands of people; strongly opposed by the Kayapó and other indigenous groups. |
| F4 | Chico Mendes, a rubber tapper and union leader who campaigned to protect the forest, was murdered by a rancher's family at Xapuri, Acre, on 22 December 1988; extractive reserves, where local people harvest rubber and nuts sustainably, were set up after his death (e.g. the Chico Mendes Extractive Reserve, Acre). In 2023 the Brazilian government declared a public health emergency in Yanomami territory after illegal gold mining caused malnutrition and disease. |
| F5 | Brazil's Forest Code requires landowners in the Amazon to keep 80% of their land as forest (the "legal reserve"); it was revised in 2012. The Amazon Soy Moratorium (2006): major traders agreed not to buy soya grown on newly deforested Amazon land. The Amazon Fund (2008), with Norway the largest donor, pays for forest protection. INPE's satellite systems (PRODES annual; DETER near-real-time alerts) guide enforcement by IBAMA, the federal environment agency. |
| F3 | The rainforest stores huge amounts of carbon; burning and decay release it as carbon dioxide. Scientists, notably Carlos Nobre and Thomas Lovejoy, have warned that loss of about 20–25% of the forest could push parts of the Amazon past a tipping point towards degraded savanna. |
| F6 | Indonesia and Malaysia together produce about 85% of the world's palm oil. Lowland rainforest and peat swamp forest in Borneo and Sumatra cleared for oil palm plantations and timber; draining and burning of peat causes regional haze (notably in 1997 and 2015) affecting Singapore and Malaysia. The Bornean orangutan was listed as Critically Endangered by the IUCN in 2016. |
| F7 | The EU Deforestation Regulation (adopted 2023) will require firms selling products such as beef, soya, palm oil, cocoa, coffee, rubber and timber in the EU to prove they were not grown on land deforested after 2020; its application has been delayed. Give no start date. |

### Intensive agricultural practices

| Item | Example(s) |
|---|---|
| A2 | EU milk quotas ended on 1 April 2015, after which Irish dairy herds expanded. The Environmental Protection Agency (EPA) reports that agriculture is the largest source of Ireland's greenhouse gas emissions, over a third of the total, and that nitrogen from farming has raised nitrate levels in rivers and estuaries, especially in the south and south-east. The corncrake, once widespread in Irish hayfields, has declined to a small population mainly in north-west Donegal, west Mayo and the Shannon Callows, partly because of earlier, mechanised silage cutting. The All-Ireland Pollinator Plan began in 2015. Nitrates Directive (1991) and agri-environment schemes (REPS 1994, GLAS 2015, ACRES 2023) from s3. |
| A3 | The CAP began in 1962; guaranteed prices encouraged farmers to remove hedgerows, enlarge fields and drain wetlands, notably in the arable east of England (East Anglia). Food surpluses ("butter mountains", "wine lakes") followed in the 1980s; later reforms (1992 onwards) began paying for environmental farming. |
| A4 | The tallgrass prairie of North America has been almost entirely converted to farmland; only a few per cent survives. Dust Bowl facts as in s3. |
| A5 | The Aral Sea, on the border of Kazakhstan and Uzbekistan: in 1960 the world's fourth-largest lake. From the 1960s Soviet planners diverted its feeder rivers, the Amu Darya and Syr Darya, to irrigate cotton (and rice) in the desert. The sea shrank to a small fraction of its former size and split into separate parts; salinity rose and the fishing industry collapsed; former fishing ports such as Moynaq (Muynak), Uzbekistan, were left far from the shore, with ships stranded on the dry seabed; dust storms carry salt and pesticide residues from the exposed bed onto farmland and people, with health effects. Salinisation and waterlogging damaged irrigated soils. The Kok-Aral Dam (2005), built by Kazakhstan with World Bank funding, has raised the level of the small North Aral Sea, and fishing has partly recovered there. |

### Industrial development

| Item | Example(s) |
|---|---|
| D1 | Acid rain from British and Central European industry acidified thousands of lakes in southern Norway and Sweden from the 1960s–80s; fish populations died out in many. The "Black Triangle", where the borders of Germany, Poland and the Czech Republic meet, had lignite-burning power stations; large areas of spruce forest in the Ore Mountains (Erzgebirge) and neighbouring ranges died in the 1970s–80s (Waldsterben, "forest death"). Responses: the Convention on Long-Range Transboundary Air Pollution (1979); EU limits on power-station emissions; flue-gas desulphurisation; liming of lakes in Sweden and Norway. Sulphur dioxide emissions in Europe have since fallen by well over half and many forests and lakes have partly recovered. |
| D2 Bord na Móna | Bord na Móna, a state company set up in 1946, harvested milled peat on a large scale from Midland raised bogs for power stations and briquettes. The peat-burning power stations at Shannonbridge (West Offaly) and Lanesborough (Lough Ree) closed in December 2020, and Bord na Móna ended peat harvesting in 2021 ("Brown to Green"); its cutaway bogs are being rehabilitated and rewetted. Lough Boora Discovery Park, Co. Offaly: cutaway bog converted to wetlands, woodland and amenity land. Clara Bog, Co. Offaly: one of the largest relatively intact raised bogs in Ireland, a nature reserve and Special Area of Conservation. Peatlands cover about a fifth of Ireland (from s2). |
| D2 Moneypoint | Moneypoint, Co. Clare, on the Shannon Estuary: Ireland's only coal-fired power station, opened in the 1980s; flue-gas desulphurisation was later fitted to cut sulphur emissions; ESB ended coal-burning there in 2025. |
| D3 oil sands | The Athabasca oil sands, around Fort McMurray, northern Alberta, Canada: surface (open-pit) mining strips away boreal forest and muskeg (peatland) to reach bitumen-soaked sand; large tailings ponds of polluted water; high greenhouse gas emissions per barrel. Companies must reclaim mined land, but little has been certified as reclaimed. |
| D3 Niger Delta | The Niger Delta, Nigeria: oil production since the late 1950s (Shell and others); repeated spills from pipelines and theft have polluted creeks, mangrove forest and farmland. A UNEP study of Ogoniland (2011) found pollution so widespread that a clean-up could take 25 to 30 years. Ken Saro-Wiwa, an Ogoni writer and campaigner against the pollution, was executed by Nigeria's military government in 1995. |
| D4 | Chernobyl nuclear power station, Ukraine (then the USSR), exploded on 26 April 1986. Radiation killed a nearby pine forest, whose needles turned reddish-brown: the "Red Forest". A 30 km exclusion zone was evacuated; with people gone, wildlife such as wolves, elk, boar and the reintroduced Przewalski's horse has become common there. |
| D5 | No checked figures. Describe effects without numbers. |

## 3. Traps (known errors)

- Don't say the Amazon produces "20% of the world's oxygen": it is a widely repeated error (the forest consumes most of the oxygen it produces). Call it a major carbon store and source of rainfall.
- Don't give daily or per-minute deforestation rates ("a football pitch every minute") or species-loss-per-day figures: none are checked, and model answers in the past-paper file contain invented ones.
- Use only the rounded PRODES figures for the dates given. Don't attribute the 2019–2022 rise or the 2023 fall to named politicians unless describing policy in general ("weaker enforcement", "renewed enforcement").
- Cattle ranching, not soya or logging, is the largest single cause of Amazon clearance.
- Céide Fields are in **north Co. Mayo**, not Co. Sligo. Don't give their area.
- The elm decline has more than one cause; don't say farmers alone caused it.
- Moneypoint is in Co. Clare. Lanesborough is Co. Longford, Shannonbridge is Co. Offaly; just name the stations as Midland peat stations if unsure.
- The Aral Sea shrank because of **irrigation**, not climate change.
- Acid rain in Scandinavia came largely from other countries' emissions: it is a transboundary problem.
- Soil-focused material (overgrazing, desertification, the Sahel, the Burren, conservation methods) belongs to `geo-option-geoecology-s3`; reuse only as one-line references.
- The biome descriptions (rainforest layers, adaptations, latosols) are in s4 and s2; refer to them without re-teaching.
- Deforestation, desertification and global warming as **global environmental issues** belong to `geo-option-global-interdependence`. Here the focus is how each activity alters a **biome**. Keep global warming to one bullet.
- Hydroelectric dams on rivers and their river impacts belong to `geo-rivers-s4`; Belo Monte here is a forest and people example.
- Acid rain's chemistry and its effects on buildings and health are taught in `geo-economic-environment-s2`; Bord na Móna's energy history and the Aral Sea as resource-use cases are in `geo-economic-environment-s4` (and `geo-economic-environment-s1` for energy). Facts here match those packages; give no Aral Sea area or percentage figures. Here keep the focus on forests, lakes, bogs and wildlife as biomes.
- Peat slides at wind farms (Derrybrien, Meenbog) belong to `geo-rocks-weathering-s7`.

## 4. What exams ask (topics only)

- How each of the felling of tropical rainforests, intensive agricultural practices and industrial development impacts on biomes (asked as one 80-mark question in several years, including the most recent): three aspects, each with named examples and specific impacts on vegetation, animals, soils and climate.
- "Biomes are altered by human activity. Discuss." (asked in several years): any four aspects from the four syllabus activities, with case studies; early settlement and forest clearance is a strong fourth aspect.

## 5. Practice questions to write

1. 80 marks: "Examine how each of the following alters biomes: the felling of tropical rainforests; intensive agricultural practices; industrial development." Model answer: three aspects of roughly equal weight: the Amazon (causes, impacts, responses); intensive farming in Ireland and the Aral Sea; acid rain in Scandinavia and the Black Triangle, plus Bord na Móna. About 22 points.
2. 80 marks: "Biomes have been altered by human activities. Discuss." Model answer: four aspects: early settlement and forest clearance in Ireland (Céide Fields, plantations-era clearance, conifer afforestation); rainforest felling; intensive agriculture; industrial development. One case study per aspect, not all of them.
3. 80 marks: "Examine the impact of early settlement and forest clearance on one biome you have studied." Model answer: the temperate deciduous forest of Ireland only, from post-glacial forest through Neolithic clearance, Céide Fields, 16th–17th-century clearance, to afforestation and native woodland remnants.
