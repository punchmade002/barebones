## 1. Must cover (one bullet or block for every item; tick each off)

Mobility of modern economic activities
- [ ] B1 Meaning: modern manufacturing and services can move between regions and countries far more easily than older heavy industry. They are footloose: not tied to a raw material, a coalfield or a port.
- [ ] B2 Why they are mobile: light, high-value products (chips, medicines, software) cheap to move by air; containerisation and cheaper transport; information and communications technology lets headquarters control branches anywhere; free movement of capital and lower trade barriers (WTO, EU single market); governments compete with grants and low taxes; MNCs can switch production between their own plants.
- [ ] B3 What drives moves: lower labour and land costs; tax and grants; being inside a trade bloc to avoid tariffs; being near growing markets; skills; political and economic stability. Moves go both ways: into Ireland from the 1970s–90s, and out of Ireland when costs rose.
- [ ] B4 Mobility examples beyond the case study, one line each (section 2): Japanese car makers locating inside the EU; call centres and IT services moving to India; Apple shifting iPhone assembly from China to India; Intel's new fab in Ireland and its cancelled fab in Germany.
- [ ] B5 Effects of mobility on regions: the receiving region gains jobs, skills and exports; the losing region suffers unemployment, loss of spin-off jobs and emigration. Branch-plant economies are vulnerable because decisions are taken abroad.

Corporate strategies and branch plants
- [ ] C1 Branch plant: a factory or office owned and controlled by a company headquartered elsewhere. Openings and closures are decided by headquarters to suit the whole company, not the host region.
- [ ] C2 Strategies that open branch plants: expansion into new markets; locating inside a trade bloc; following tax incentives and grants; acquiring other companies (mergers and takeovers); joint ventures to share the cost of huge investments; government subsidy schemes (e.g. the US and EU Chips Acts).
- [ ] C3 Strategies that close branch plants: cost-cutting and rationalisation (concentrating production in fewer, larger plants); relocation to cheaper countries; outsourcing to contract manufacturers; falling demand for a mature product; mergers that leave duplicate plants; reshoring (bringing production back home) for security of supply.
- [ ] C4 Case study: Dell in Limerick (company, arrival, direct-sales model, role in Ireland, the 2009 closure of manufacturing, the move to Łódź, Poland, the reasons, the effects on Limerick, the EU Globalisation Adjustment Fund, what Dell still does in Ireland). Write it as one full block, about 12–15 SRPs.
- [ ] C5 Intel's corporate strategy (one block): investing billions in Leixlip; selling 49% of Fab 34 to Apollo to raise cash; cancelling the Magdeburg fab and cutting jobs in 2024–25 when profits fell; government support (US CHIPS Act; the US government stake in 2025).

Product life cycle (PLC) and location
- [ ] P1 The stages: introduction (development) → growth → maturity → decline. Draw the curve of sales against time.
- [ ] P2 How location changes through the cycle: introduction and growth take place in the core, near headquarters, R&D, skilled workers and wealthy early customers; at maturity the product is standardised, competition cuts prices, so production moves to cheaper locations (first semi-peripheral countries such as Ireland in the past, later Eastern Europe and Asia); in decline the plant closes or is upgraded to a new product.
- [ ] P3 PLC and Dell: the personal computer became a mature, standardised product with falling prices, so Dell moved assembly from Ireland to cheaper Poland.
- [ ] P4 PLC and Intel: each chip generation has a short life (a few years). New processes are developed in Oregon, then copied exactly into fabs such as Leixlip ("Copy Exactly!"); older fabs are upgraded or closed. Ireland survives by winning each new generation (Fab 34). Intel missed the start of the smartphone and AI chip cycles, which helped rivals.
- [ ] P5 Exams ask "the impact of the product life cycle on the operations of one MNC": answer with the stages plus the MNC's real location changes, not the theory alone.

Information technology and the future of economic activities
- [ ] T1 Impact of IT developments on the MNC's operations (exams pair this with the PLC): computer-aided design and automation in the fab; global communication lets one headquarters run plants on several continents; real-time stock control and just-in-time supply; online sales (Dell's direct model online); demand changes from new technology (PCs → smartphones → cloud and AI), which create and destroy markets.
- [ ] T2 Teleservices: services delivered at a distance using telecommunications (call centres, customer support, technical help desks, shared services such as payroll and accounts). Why Ireland attracted them: English-speaking and multilingual workforce, time zone between the USA and Europe, good broadband, IDA support. Examples in section 2. Threat: routine teleservices move to cheaper India and the Philippines; automation and AI chatbots.
- [ ] T3 Information technology sector in Ireland: European headquarters of major internet companies in Dublin (the "Silicon Docks"); Ireland as one of the world's largest exporters of computer services.
- [ ] T4 Data centres (cloud computing): large buildings of servers; attracted by Ireland's cool climate (less cooling), connectivity and the companies already here; issue: they use a large share of Ireland's electricity (figure in section 2), so new grid connections in the Dublin area were restricted.
- [ ] T5 E-commerce: buying and selling online. Effects: online-only companies, decline of some high-street shops, growth of warehouses and delivery services, more cross-border parcels from Asia; Dell was an early large online seller. Accelerated by COVID-19 lockdowns in 2020.
- [ ] T6 Future trends (one block): remote and hybrid working after COVID-19; artificial intelligence; automation reducing routine jobs; reshoring and "friend-shoring" of strategic industries such as chips; risks for Ireland from global minimum tax and US tariffs.

## 3. Traps

- Dell **closed manufacturing** in Limerick in 2009; it did not leave Ireland. Don't say Dell has no Irish presence.
- The Dell move was to **Łódź, Poland** (not Warsaw, not Czechia). About 1,900 jobs: don't give a different number.
- Dell (1984, Texas) is not Intel (1968, California). Dell assembled computers; Intel makes the chips inside them.
- Don't present the product life cycle as theory only; every PLC point needs a location change in a named MNC.
- "Copy Exactly!" moves technology from Oregon **to** Ireland; it doesn't mean Ireland designs the chips.
- Don't use EirGrid or data-centre figures other than those in section 2 (the 5% for 2015 and 21% for 2023).
- The company profile, sourcing, basic processing units and markets of Intel and Apple belong to `geo-economic-globalisation-s1`. Don't repeat them; refer to "the multinational company" note by title.
- The general factors of industrial location and Ireland as a developed economy (footloose industry regions, financial services, IFSC) belong to `geo-economic-activities`. Here, mobility is explained through MNC examples.
- The global spread of basic processing units, core and periphery, and the impact of globalisation on world trade belong to `geo-economic-globalisation-s4`; the triad and trade terms to `-s3`.
- The EU Globalisation Adjustment Fund is the only EU fund to use here. The Regional Development and Social Funds belong to `geo-economic-eu`.
- Don't copy past-paper model answers' car-industry or iPhone component chains: they contain invented details.
