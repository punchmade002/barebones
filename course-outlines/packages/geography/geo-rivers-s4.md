# Package: geo-rivers-s4 — Human interaction: river processes

- **loId:** `geo-rivers-s4` · **chapterId:** `geo-rivers` · **syllabusRef:** 1.7
- **Syllabus:** "river processes and the impact of hydro-electric dams, canalisation and flood
  control measures." Statement: "Human activities can impact on the operation of surface
  processes." Students study one of three human interactions (mass movement, river or coastal
  processes); this is the river option. Cover **all three** measures in full, with Irish and
  international examples.
- **Prepared by:** a higher model, 2026-10-19. The writer works only from this package and
  `course-outlines/NOTES_STANDARD.md`.

## 1. Must cover (one bullet or block for every item; tick each off)

Focus throughout: what each measure does to the river's **processes** (velocity, erosion,
transport, deposition, flooding), not only its economic or social effects. Exams ask students
to "examine the impact on river processes".

Why people interfere
- [ ] W1 Reasons: electricity, water supply, irrigation, navigation, draining farmland, protecting towns from flooding. One bullet.

Hydro-electric dams
- [ ] H1 How a dam works: the dam stores water in a reservoir; water falls through turbines; the drop in height (head) gives the power. Two bullets at most.
- [ ] H2 Impact upstream: velocity falls to near zero in the reservoir, so the river deposits its load there; the reservoir slowly fills with sediment (sedimentation), reducing its capacity; a delta can build where the river enters the reservoir.
- [ ] H3 Impact downstream: the water released is clear of sediment, so it has spare energy and erodes the bed and banks below the dam (clear-water erosion); the floodplain and delta receive no new silt, so deltas and coasts erode.
- [ ] H4 Impact on flow: discharge is regulated, so natural floods and low flows are evened out; less flooding means less deposition on the floodplain. Where water is diverted out of the old channel, the bypassed channel carries much less water.
- [ ] H5 Impact on fish: dams block migrating salmon and eels; fish passes and trapping-and-transporting fish around dams are responses.
- [ ] H6 Irish case study: the Shannon Scheme at Ardnacrusha (full detail from section 2).
- [ ] H7 Other Irish dams, one bullet each: Poulaphouca (Liffey), Inniscarra (Lee), Cathaleen's Fall (Erne).
- [ ] H8 International case study: the Aswan High Dam, River Nile (full detail for a 30-mark answer).
- [ ] H9 Second international example: the Three Gorges Dam, Yangtze. Short block.

Canalisation
- [ ] C1 What it is: straightening, deepening, widening and sometimes lining a river channel with concrete; cutting through meanders; dredging; building locks and weirs for navigation. Purposes: navigation, faster drainage of land, flood control.
- [ ] C2 Impact on processes: a straighter, shorter course has a steeper gradient and less friction, so velocity rises; water and floods are passed downstream faster, so flood peaks downstream are higher and arrive sooner; faster flow erodes the bed and banks; where the channel widens or the flow slows again, deposition increases and dredging is needed; meanders, oxbow lakes, pools and riffles are lost; the river is cut off from its floodplain.
- [ ] C3 Irish example: arterial drainage by the Office of Public Works (OPW) under the Arterial Drainage Act 1945, e.g. the Moy and Boyne schemes: rivers deepened and straightened to drain farmland; effects on fish spawning beds, bank erosion and flooding downstream.
- [ ] C4 International examples: the Mississippi (cut-offs made by the US Army Corps of Engineers shortened the river); the Rhine (straightened in the 19th century by Johann Gottfried Tulla, which sped flood waves downstream and caused the bed to erode downward).
- [ ] C5 Navigation channels in Ireland: locks and weirs on the Shannon Navigation control water levels and slow the flow. One bullet; no dates.

Flood control measures
- [ ] F1 Causes of river flooding that measures respond to: prolonged or intense rain, saturated ground, urban surfaces and drains speeding runoff, deforestation and drainage of bogs and farmland upstream, building on floodplains. One or two bullets.
- [ ] F2 Hard engineering, each with its effect on processes: embankments and levees (flow confined to the channel, so deposition raises the bed and the banks must be raised again; floodplain gets no silt; failure gives sudden, severe floods); flood walls and demountable (removable) barriers in towns; dams and reservoirs storing floodwater; dredging and deepening (increased capacity, but faster flow and downstream flooding); relief (bypass) channels.
- [ ] F3 Soft engineering: floodplain zoning (planning controls on building), afforestation upstream (interception slows runoff), restoring wetlands and bogs, washlands that are allowed to flood, giving the river more room, flood forecasting and warning. Effect on processes: natural flooding and floodplain deposition continue.
- [ ] F4 Irish case study: the Cork flood of November 2009 (River Lee, releases from Inniscarra dam); the Clonmel flood relief scheme (River Suir, demountable barriers); Shannon flooding in winter 2015–16 after Storm Desmond; the OPW's CFRAM programme mapping flood risk.
- [ ] F5 International case study: the Mississippi levee system: the 1927 flood, the 1993 flood, Hurricane Katrina and New Orleans in 2005, and delta wetland loss because levees stop silt reaching it.
- [ ] F6 International soft-engineering example: the Netherlands' Room for the River programme.
- [ ] F7 Evaluation: benefits (protection of homes, electricity, navigation, water supply) against costs (problems moved downstream, sediment starvation, habitat loss, high cost, false sense of security); trend towards working with natural processes. Paired points.

Inniscarra and Carrigadrohid dams, River Lee, Co. Cork. Cathaleen's Fall power station, River Erne, at Ballyshannon, Co. Donegal. Give no dates for these. |

## 3. Traps

- The Shannon Scheme is **one** power station (Ardnacrusha) fed by a weir (Parteen) and a canal. It is not "a series of dams from Parteen to Limerick". Don't call Parteen Weir a dam producing power.
- Don't say the Cork 2009 flood was caused only by the dam: the rain was exceptional; the controversy was over the timing and volume of releases.
- Don't use claims of an earlier Cork flood leading to dam construction, or the Thames Barrier (a tidal surge barrier, better in coastal work). They are not checked here.
- The Grand Canal and Royal Canal are artificial canals, not canalisation of a river. Don't use them as canalisation examples.
- Canalisation is not the same as building a canal: it modifies an existing river channel.
- Arterial drainage was mainly to drain farmland, not to protect towns.
- Focus on processes: a 30-mark answer must explain changes in velocity, erosion, transport, deposition and flood behaviour, not list costs and benefits only.
- Coastal defences belong to `geo-sea-s2`; overgrazing and deforestation as mass movement interaction belong to `geo-rocks-weathering-s7`. Leave them out.
- Landform formation (meanders, levees, deltas, floodplains) is in `geo-rivers-s1`. Use the landform names without re-explaining their formation.
- Don't give megawatt outputs, dam heights, reservoir volumes, costs or death tolls except those in section 2. None are checked.

## 4. What exams ask (topics only)

- 30 marks, Higher: "Human activity impacts on surface processes" with reference to the impact of flood control measures on river processes. Expected: named measures (levees, dams, dredging, canalisation, soft measures), how each alters velocity, erosion, deposition and flooding, with Irish and international examples and evaluation.
- 30 marks, Ordinary: how humans interact with river processes (dams, canalisation, flood control, with examples).
- Possible 30 marks: the impact of hydro-electric dams, or of canalisation, on river processes.
