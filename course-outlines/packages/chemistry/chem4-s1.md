## Part: Development of the modern periodic table (formerly `chem4-s1`)

- **loId:** `chem4-s1` · **chapterId:** `chem4` · **syllabusRef:** 1.3.1
- **Syllabus:** 1.3.1 "describe the development of the modern periodic table". Learn about:
  "the significance of the contribution of Mendeleev, with key refinements, to the development
  of the modern periodic table". Normal type: Ordinary and Higher level.
- **Prepared by:** a higher model, 2026-10-30. Order: before Mendeleev (brief), Mendeleev (main
  block), key refinements (Moseley, noble gases, modern table). The arrangement into groups,
  periods and blocks is "Groups, periods and blocks".

### 1. Must cover

Before Mendeleev (one block)
- [ ] B1 (assumed) An element is a substance that cannot be split into simpler substances by chemical means; by the mid-1800s over 60 elements were known, and chemists looked for patterns among them.
- [ ] B2 Döbereiner (1829): triads, groups of three elements with similar properties in which the middle element's atomic weight is about the average of the other two, e.g. lithium, sodium, potassium; calcium, strontium, barium; chlorine, bromine, iodine.
- [ ] B3 Newlands (1864–1865): the law of octaves: arranged by atomic weight, every eighth element had similar properties; it worked for the lighter elements but broke down for heavier ones, and he left no gaps.

Mendeleev's periodic table (one block)
- [ ] M1 Dmitri Mendeleev (1869) arranged the elements in order of increasing atomic weight (relative atomic mass), putting elements with similar properties in the same vertical column (group).
- [ ] M2 Periodic law (Mendeleev's version): the properties of the elements vary periodically with their atomic weights.
- [ ] M3 He left gaps for elements not yet discovered and predicted their properties, e.g. eka-aluminium (gallium, discovered 1875) and eka-silicon (germanium, discovered 1886); their discovery with the predicted properties convinced chemists the table was correct.
- [ ] M4 He reversed the order of some pairs where atomic weight order put an element in the wrong group, e.g. tellurium placed before iodine so that iodine sits with the other halogens.
- [ ] M5 Significance: it organised all known elements, showed relationships between them, made predictions that were confirmed, and was the basis of the modern table.

Key refinements (one block)
- [ ] R1 The noble gases (helium, neon, argon, krypton, xenon), discovered in the 1890s, were added as a new group (now group 18).
- [ ] R2 Henry Moseley (1913) showed by experiment that each element has a whole-number atomic number, equal to the number of protons in its nucleus.
- [ ] R3 The modern periodic table is arranged in order of increasing atomic number, which removes the anomalies in Mendeleev's table (tellurium, atomic number 52, now naturally comes before iodine, 53).
- [ ] R4 Modern periodic law: the properties of the elements are a periodic function of their atomic numbers.
- [ ] R5 The repeating pattern is explained by electron arrangement: elements in a group have the same outer-electron arrangement.
- [ ] R6 Differences between Mendeleev's table and the modern table: order by atomic weight vs atomic number; gaps vs no gaps (all elements known to 118); noble gases absent vs present; fewer elements vs 118; no d-block arrangement vs separate blocks.

### 3. Traps

- Mendeleev ordered by atomic weight, not atomic number (atomic number wasn't known in 1869).
- Moseley's X-ray method isn't required; say "by experiment".
- Don't call the d-block "transition metals" (transition metals are not on the specification).

### 4. What exams ask (topics only)

- Describe Döbereiner's triads with an example; Newlands' octaves.
- Describe how Mendeleev arranged the elements and why his table was accepted.
- Give differences between Mendeleev's table and the modern table; state the contribution of Moseley.

2. 6 marks: "State two differences between Mendeleev's periodic table and the modern periodic table and explain the contribution of Moseley." Model answer: two of R6; R2, R3.

## Part: Groups, periods and blocks (formerly `chem4-s2`)

- **loId:** `chem4-s2` · **chapterId:** `chem4` · **syllabusRef:** 1.3.3
- **Syllabus:** 1.3.3 "examine and explain the arrangement of elements in groups, periods and
  **blocks** in the periodic table of elements". "Blocks" is bold: Higher Level only.
- **Prepared by:** a higher model, 2026-10-30. Groups and periods for all students; blocks in a
  Higher Level block. The properties of specific groups are "Families and trends in the periodic table" in the next
  chapter; trends are "Families and trends in the periodic table".

### 1. Must cover

Groups (one block)
- [ ] G1 A group is a vertical column of elements; there are 18 groups, numbered 1 to 18.
- [ ] G2 Elements in the same group have the same number of electrons in their outer energy level, so they have similar chemical properties.
- [ ] G3 For groups 1 and 2, the group number equals the number of outer electrons; for groups 13 to 18, the number of outer electrons is the group number minus 10 (e.g. group 17 has 7 outer electrons), except helium, which has 2.
- [ ] G4 Group names: group 1 alkali metals; group 2 alkaline earth metals; group 17 halogens; group 18 noble gases.

Periods (one block)
- [ ] P1 A period is a horizontal row of elements; there are 7 periods.
- [ ] P2 The period number equals the number of occupied main energy levels, e.g. sodium (2,8,1) is in period 3.
- [ ] P3 Across a period, one proton and one electron are added at each step, and the elements change from metals on the left to non-metals on the right.
- [ ] P4 Period 1 has 2 elements (hydrogen and helium); periods 2 and 3 have 8 each; period 4 has 18.

Metals, non-metals and metalloids (one block)
- [ ] M1 About three-quarters of the elements are metals, on the left and in the centre; non-metals are on the right (with hydrogen).
- [ ] M2 A staircase line from boron to astatine separates metals from non-metals; elements next to it, such as silicon and germanium, are metalloids (semi-metals) with some properties of each.
- [ ] M3 (assumed) Metals: shiny, good conductors of heat and electricity, malleable, ductile, form positive ions. Non-metals: dull, poor conductors (except graphite), brittle as solids, often form negative ions or share electrons.

Blocks (Higher Level only; one block)
- [ ] K1 [HL] A block is the set of elements whose highest-energy electron enters the same type of sublevel.
- [ ] K2 [HL] s-block: groups 1 and 2 (and helium); outer electrons in an s sublevel.
- [ ] K3 [HL] p-block: groups 13 to 18 (except helium); highest-energy electron in a p sublevel.
- [ ] K4 [HL] d-block: groups 3 to 12, in the middle of periods 4 to 7; filling a d sublevel.
- [ ] K5 [HL] f-block: the lanthanides and actinides, usually shown below the main table; filling an f sublevel.
- [ ] K6 [HL] The shape of the table follows from sublevel capacities: 2 columns for s, 6 for p, 10 for d, 14 for f.
- [ ] K7 [HL] Diagram: an outline of the periodic table with the s, p, d and f blocks shaded and labelled, and group and period numbers marked.

### 2. Checked facts and examples

| Item | Fact |
|---|---|
| G1, P1 | IUPAC numbering: 18 groups, 7 periods. |
| M2 | Metalloids include boron, silicon, germanium, arsenic, antimony, tellurium. |

### 3. Traps

- The d-block is not called "transition metals" here (not on the specification).
- Group properties (state, conductivity, reactivity) belong in "Families and trends in the periodic table"; keep this to the arrangement.
- Keep "blocks" in the Higher Level block.

### 4. What exams ask (topics only)

- Define group and period; relate group and period to electron arrangement.
- State the block an element belongs to (Higher Level).

### 5. Practice questions to write

1. 6 marks: "Explain the terms group and period, and state the group and period of an element with the electron arrangement 2,8,6." Model answer: G1, G2, P1, P2; group 16, period 3 (sulfur).
2. 6 marks (Higher Level): "Name the four blocks of the periodic table and explain why the d-block is ten elements wide." Model answer: K2–K6.

## Part: Using the table as a predictive guide (formerly `chem4-s3`)

- **loId:** `chem4-s3` · **chapterId:** `chem4` · **syllabusRef:** 1.3 LA
- **Syllabus:** 1.3 learn about: "how to use the periodic table, and trends in the periodic
  table, as a guide to thinking about useful and predictive relationships". Normal type.
- **Prepared by:** a higher model, 2026-10-30. This is the "how to use it" subsection: reading
  information from the table and making predictions. The trends themselves (radius, ionisation
  energy, electronegativity, reactivity) are taught in "Families and trends in the periodic table" and
  "Families and trends in the periodic table"; name the direction of a trend only in a worked
  prediction.

### 1. Must cover

Reading the table (one block)
- [ ] R1 Each box gives the symbol, atomic number and relative atomic mass of an element.
- [ ] R2 From the position of an element you can tell: its number of protons and electrons, its outer-electron arrangement (group), its number of occupied energy levels (period), and whether it is a metal, non-metal or metalloid.

Predicting with groups (one block)
- [ ] G1 Elements in the same group react in similar ways, so the reactions of one predict those of another, e.g. if sodium reacts with water to give sodium hydroxide and hydrogen, potassium will too.
- [ ] G2 The usual charge on an ion can be predicted from the group: groups 1, 2 and 13 form 1+, 2+ and 3+ ions; groups 15, 16 and 17 form 3−, 2− and 1− ions.
- [ ] G3 Formulae of compounds can be predicted from these charges (valency), e.g. magnesium (group 2) and chlorine (group 17) form MgCl₂; aluminium and oxygen form Al₂O₃.
- [ ] G4 Noble gases (group 18) have full outer levels and are very unreactive.

Predicting with trends (one block)
- [ ] T1 Properties change gradually and predictably down a group and across a period, so an unknown property can be estimated from its neighbours, e.g. predicting that astatine is a dark solid less reactive than iodine, from the trend in the halogens.
- [ ] T2 Mendeleev used this to predict the properties of undiscovered elements.
- [ ] T3 The underlying explanation of every trend is electron arrangement: the number of outer electrons, the number of energy levels and the nuclear charge.

### 2. Checked facts and examples

Only the examples above (sodium and potassium with water; MgCl₂; Al₂O₃; astatine). Astatine (At, atomic number 85) is predicted to be a dark solid.

### 3. Traps

- Don't teach the trends in full here.
- Transition metal ion charges are not required; predictions of ion charge are for groups 1, 2, 13, 15, 16, 17 only.

### 4. What exams ask (topics only)

- Predict the formula of a compound of two elements from their groups.
- Predict a property of an element from its position.

### 5. Practice questions to write

1. 6 marks: "Use the periodic table to predict the formula of the compound formed between (i) calcium and fluorine, (ii) aluminium and sulfur, and explain how you made each prediction." Model answer: G2, G3; CaF₂, Al₂S₃.
2. 6 marks: "Rubidium is in group 1 below potassium. Predict two properties of rubidium and explain your predictions." Model answer: G1, T1, T3 (reacts vigorously with water forming RbOH and hydrogen, more reactive than potassium; forms Rb⁺).
