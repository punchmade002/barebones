## Part: Rate of reaction (formerly `chem17-s1`)

- **loId:** `chem17-s1` · **chapterId:** `chem17` · **syllabusRef:** 3.2.1
- **Syllabus:** 3.2.1 "define and explain rate of reaction". Learn about: "a definition of rate
  of reaction and factors that may affect the rate of chemical reactions that can be explained
  through the collision theory". Normal type.
- **Prepared by:** a higher model, 2026-10-30. The definition, what rate means, how it is
  followed in the laboratory, and why a reaction slows down as it goes. The factors and their
  explanation are in "Collision theory"; average, initial and instantaneous rates, their
  calculation from graphs and the three set experiments are in "Investigation: factors affecting
  rate". Name the factors here in one bullet and point there; don't explain them.

### 1. Must cover

What rate of reaction means (one block)
- [ ] D1 Rate of reaction is the change in concentration of a reactant or product per unit time.
- [ ] D2 Units: mol L⁻¹ s⁻¹ (or mol L⁻¹ min⁻¹) when concentration is measured; when another quantity is followed, the rate is given in that quantity per unit time, e.g. cm³ of gas per minute (cm³ min⁻¹) or grams of mass lost per second (g s⁻¹).
- [ ] D3 Rate can be followed by the disappearance of a reactant or the appearance of a product; the two are linked by the mole ratio in the balanced equation (e.g. in 2H₂O₂ → 2H₂O + O₂, H₂O₂ is used up twice as fast, in moles, as O₂ is formed).
- [ ] D4 A fast reaction has a large change in a short time; a slow reaction has a small change over a long time. Rate is inversely related to the time taken: rate ∝ 1/time for the same amount of change.
- [ ] D5 The factors that can change the rate are concentration, surface area (particle size), temperature, a catalyst and, for reactions of gases, pressure; the nature of the reactants also matters. These are explained in "Collision theory".

Following a reaction in the laboratory (one block)
- [ ] L1 Measure the volume of gas produced at regular time intervals, collected in a gas syringe or over water in an inverted graduated cylinder, e.g. oxygen from hydrogen peroxide.
- [ ] L2 Measure the loss in mass as a gas escapes, with the flask on an electronic balance (a cotton-wool plug lets the gas out but stops acid spray), e.g. carbon dioxide from calcium carbonate and hydrochloric acid.
- [ ] L3 Measure the time for a fixed amount of reaction to happen, e.g. the time for a precipitate of sulfur to hide a cross under the flask (sodium thiosulfate and hydrochloric acid); 1/time is then used as the rate.
- [ ] L4 Follow a colour change, by eye or with a light sensor, when a reactant or product is coloured.
- [ ] L5 The method chosen must measure a property that changes as the reaction proceeds and can be measured quickly without disturbing the reaction.

How rate changes during a reaction (one block)
- [ ] C1 The rate is greatest at the start, when the concentration of reactants is highest.
- [ ] C2 As the reaction proceeds, reactants are used up, their concentration falls, and the rate decreases.
- [ ] C3 The reaction stops when one reactant (the limiting reactant) is used up; the rate is then zero.
- [ ] C4 On a graph of product (e.g. volume of gas) against time, the curve is steepest at the start, becomes less steep, and levels off (a horizontal line) when the reaction is complete. The steepness (gradient) of the curve at any point is the rate at that time.
- [ ] C5 On a graph of reactant concentration against time, the curve falls steeply at first and levels off; its gradient is negative, but the rate is quoted as a positive number.
- [ ] C6 The final amount of product depends only on the amount of limiting reactant, not on how fast the reaction went.
- [ ] C7 Diagram: a graph of volume of gas (y-axis) against time (x-axis): a curve rising steeply from the origin, flattening, and becoming horizontal at the final volume; label "reaction fastest", "slowing down" and "reaction complete".

Why rate matters (one block)
- [ ] W1 Industry wants fast reactions, to make products quickly and economically.
- [ ] W2 Slowing reactions is also useful: food is kept in a fridge because the reactions that spoil it are slower at low temperature.
- [ ] W3 Health and medicines: the rates of reactions in the body are controlled by enzymes (see "Catalysis").

### 3. Traps

- Rate is a change per unit time, not "the time a reaction takes" and not "how much product is formed".
- Rate is not constant during a reaction; it falls as reactants are used up.
- Don't teach the factors or the calculations of average, initial and instantaneous rate here.
- Collect gas in a graduated cylinder (or gas syringe), not a gas jar, when its volume is measured.

### 4. What exams ask (topics only)

- Define rate of reaction.
- Does the rate increase or decrease with time; why does the reaction slow down?
- How the progress of a reaction can be followed (gas volume, mass loss, time for a cross to disappear).

### 5. Practice questions to write

1. 6 marks: "Define rate of reaction. Describe one way of measuring the rate of the reaction between calcium carbonate and hydrochloric acid." Model answer: D1; L2 (or the gas-volume method, L1, with the CO₂ collected in a gas syringe).
2. 6 marks: "A graph of the volume of oxygen produced against time when hydrogen peroxide decomposes is steepest at the start and becomes horizontal after 12 minutes. Explain both features of the graph." Model answer: C1, C2, C3, C4.
3. 3 marks: "Explain why 1/time can be used as a measure of rate in the reaction between sodium thiosulfate and hydrochloric acid." Model answer: D4, L3 (the same amount of sulfur forms in each run, so a shorter time means a faster rate).

## Part: Collision theory (formerly `chem17-s2`)

- **loId:** `chem17-s2` · **chapterId:** `chem17` · **syllabusRef:** 3.2.2
- **Syllabus:** 3.2.2 "describe collision theory, and give examples of slow and fast
  reactions". Learn about: "factors that may affect the rate of chemical reactions that can be
  explained through the collision theory: concentration, surface area, temperature, the
  presence of a catalyst (for gaseous reactions, pressure is also considered)"; "**limitations:
  concentration may not always affect the rate, catalysts can become saturated/poisoned and

- **Prepared by:** a higher model, 2026-10-30. The theory, activation energy, slow and fast
  examples, then one block explaining each factor; the limitations are a separate HL block.
  Catalysts are explained here only as "lowers the activation energy"; types of catalysis,
  enzymes and energy profiles are in "Catalysis", and how catalysts work in
  "Catalysis". The experiments that test each factor are in "Investigation: factors
  affecting rate".

### 1. Must cover

The collision theory (one block)
- [ ] T1 For a reaction to happen, the reacting particles (atoms, molecules or ions) must collide.
- [ ] T2 Most collisions do not lead to reaction: the particles just bounce apart.
- [ ] T3 A collision leads to reaction only if the particles collide with at least a minimum amount of energy, the activation energy, so that bonds can begin to break.
- [ ] T4 Activation energy (Ea) is the minimum energy that colliding particles must have for a reaction to occur.
- [ ] T5 An effective collision is one that results in the formation of products.
- [ ] T6 So the rate of a reaction depends on the number of effective collisions per second: anything that increases the number of collisions per second, or the fraction of collisions with energy ≥ Ea, increases the rate.

Slow and fast reactions (one block)
- [ ] S1 Fast reactions: mixing silver nitrate and sodium chloride solutions gives a white precipitate of silver chloride instantly (Ag⁺(aq) + Cl⁻(aq) → AgCl(s)); neutralisation of an acid by an alkali; the explosion of a hydrogen–oxygen mixture when ignited; burning magnesium.
- [ ] S2 Slow reactions: rusting of iron (days to years); fermentation of glucose by yeast (days); a mixture of hydrogen and oxygen at room temperature without a spark or catalyst (no detectable reaction).
- [ ] S3 Reactions between oppositely charged ions in solution are usually very fast: the ions are already free, attract each other, and no bonds need to be broken before they combine, so the activation energy is very low.
- [ ] S4 Reactions between covalent molecules are usually slower, because covalent bonds must be broken before new bonds can form, so the activation energy is higher.
- [ ] S5 A reaction with a high activation energy is slow at room temperature (few collisions have enough energy); a reaction with a low activation energy is fast.

Concentration (one block)
- [ ] N1 Increasing the concentration of a reactant in solution increases the number of particles per unit volume.
- [ ] N2 The particles collide more often, so there are more collisions, and more effective collisions, per second, and the rate increases.
- [ ] N3 In many reactions the rate is directly proportional to the concentration of a reactant: a graph of rate against concentration is a straight line through the origin (shown by the thiosulfate experiment in "Investigation: factors affecting rate").

Surface area (particle size) (one block)
- [ ] A1 Only particles at the surface of a solid can collide with the other reactant.
- [ ] A2 Breaking a solid into smaller pieces (or a powder) increases the surface area exposed for the same mass, so there are more collisions per second and the rate increases, e.g. powdered calcium carbonate reacts faster with hydrochloric acid than marble chips of the same mass.
- [ ] A3 Dust explosions: a very fine combustible powder spread in air has an enormous surface area and can burn so fast that it explodes; they are a risk in flour mills, grain silos and coal mines.
- [ ] A4 Conditions for a dust explosion: a combustible dust, finely divided and dry, dispersed in air (oxygen), in an enclosed space, with a source of ignition (spark or flame).

Temperature (one block)
- [ ] H1 Raising the temperature increases the rate of almost all reactions; for many reactions near room temperature the rate roughly doubles for each 10 °C rise.
- [ ] H2 Reason 1: at a higher temperature particles have more kinetic energy and move faster, so they collide more often (more collisions per second).
- [ ] H3 Reason 2: a much greater fraction of the collisions have energy equal to or greater than the activation energy, so a greater fraction of collisions are effective.
- [ ] H4 Reason 2 is the more important: a 10 °C rise increases the number of collisions per second only slightly, but greatly increases the number of particles with energy ≥ Ea.
- [ ] H5 Raising the temperature does not change the activation energy; it changes how many particles have enough energy to overcome it.
- [ ] H6 This can be shown on a graph of the number of particles against their kinetic energy (a Maxwell–Boltzmann distribution): a skewed hump starting at the origin with a long tail to the right; the area under the curve to the right of Ea is the number of particles with enough energy to react. At a higher temperature the curve is flatter, its peak moves to the right, and the area to the right of Ea is much larger.
- [ ] H7 Diagram: two energy-distribution curves on the same axes (number of particles against kinetic energy) at T₁ and a higher T₂, with Ea marked on the energy axis and the area beyond Ea shaded for each.
- [ ] H8 Everyday example: food lasts longer in a fridge or freezer because the reactions that spoil it are slower at low temperature.

Catalyst (one block)
- [ ] K1 A catalyst is a substance that alters the rate of a chemical reaction but is itself chemically unchanged at the end of the reaction.
- [ ] K2 A catalyst provides an alternative pathway for the reaction with a lower activation energy, so a greater fraction of collisions have energy ≥ Ea and are effective, and the rate increases. (Types, enzymes and energy profiles: "Catalysis".)

Pressure, for reactions of gases (one block)
- [ ] P1 Increasing the pressure of a gas squeezes the same number of molecules into a smaller volume, which is the same as increasing its concentration.
- [ ] P2 The molecules collide more often, so the rate increases.
- [ ] P3 Pressure affects only reactions in which one or more reactants are gases; it has almost no effect on reactions in solution or between solids.

Limitations of the simple theory (Higher Level only; one block)
- [ ] X1 [HL] Collisions must be correctly oriented: even with enough energy, a collision is effective only if the particles meet with the reacting parts of the molecules facing each other; this is why only a small fraction of energetic collisions lead to reaction, especially for large molecules.
- [ ] X2 [HL] Concentration may not always affect the rate: when a reaction takes place on a surface (a solid reactant or a solid catalyst) and every site on the surface is already occupied, adding more of the other reactant cannot increase the number of collisions that react, so the rate stays the same.
- [ ] X3 [HL] Example of X2: an enzyme-catalysed reaction speeds up as the substrate concentration rises, but levels off when all the enzyme's active sites are occupied; further substrate has no effect.
- [ ] X4 [HL] A catalyst can become saturated: it has a limited number of active sites; when they are all in use, increasing the reactant concentration no longer increases the rate.
- [ ] X5 [HL] A catalyst can be poisoned: a substance (a catalyst poison) binds strongly to the active sites and blocks them, so the catalyst stops working.
- [ ] X6 [HL] Example of X5: the catalysts in a car's catalytic converter are poisoned by lead compounds (one reason leaded petrol was phased out where converters are used), and by sulfur and phosphorus compounds.

### 3. Traps

- Temperature: the main reason is the greater fraction of collisions with energy ≥ Ea, not just "more collisions". Give both reasons and say which matters more.
- A higher temperature does not lower the activation energy; only a catalyst does (by a different pathway).
- Surface area is about particle size of the same mass of solid; "more marble" is a change in amount, not surface area.
- Pressure only matters for gases.
- Don't call the activated complex an intermediate (see "Catalysis").
- Don't name a "negative catalyst" example; none is checked.

### 4. What exams ask (topics only)

- Explain activation energy and effective collision.
- Two reasons why rate increases with temperature; which is more significant, and why.
- Why ionic reactions in solution are very fast (silver nitrate and sodium chloride).
- Why powdered solids react faster; conditions for a dust explosion.
- Why not every collision leads to reaction (energy and orientation).
- How a catalyst can be poisoned (catalytic converter).
