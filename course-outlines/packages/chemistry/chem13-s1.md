## Part: Solving acid–base volumetric problems (formerly `chem13-s1`)

- **loId:** `chem13-s1` · **chapterId:** `chem13` · **syllabusRef:** 4.1.7
- **Syllabus:** 4.1.7 "solve volumetric problems". Learn about: "problem solving using varied
  units (see quantifying matter section); volumetric calculations in familiar and **unfamiliar
  contexts** for acid-base and redox titrations". Bold = Higher Level only. This chapter covers
  acid–base titrations; redox problems are in the redox volumetric chapters.
- **Prepared by:** a higher model, 2026-10-30. The general method and the tools (formula, mole
  ratios, dilution, unit conversion); worked problems are in "Worked acid–base volumetric
  problems". The unit definitions are in "Solutions and concentration"; use them without redefining.

### 1. Must cover

The method (one block, one step per bullet)
- [ ] M1 Step 1: Write the balanced equation and note the mole ratio of the two reactants.
- [ ] M2 Step 2: Calculate the moles of the substance whose concentration and volume are both known: n = c × V, with V in litres (cm³ ÷ 1000).
- [ ] M3 Step 3: Use the mole ratio to find the moles of the other substance.
- [ ] M4 Step 4: Divide by the volume of that solution (in litres) to get its concentration in mol L⁻¹.
- [ ] M5 Step 5: Convert to the units asked for (g L⁻¹ by × M; %w/v by ÷ 10 after g L⁻¹), and allow for any dilution.

The titration formula (one block)
- [ ] F1 For the reaction aA + bB → products: (cA × VA) ÷ a = (cB × VB) ÷ b, where a and b are the coefficients in the balanced equation; volumes can be in cm³ on both sides because the units cancel.
- [ ] F2 Example: for Na₂CO₃ + 2HCl, (c(Na₂CO₃) × V(Na₂CO₃)) ÷ 1 = (c(HCl) × V(HCl)) ÷ 2.
- [ ] F3 The formula gives the same answer as the step-by-step method; show the working either way.

Dilution factors (one block)
- [ ] D1 If a sample was diluted before titration (e.g. 25.0 cm³ made up to 250 cm³), the titration gives the diluted concentration; multiply by the dilution factor (250 ÷ 25.0 = 10) to get the original.
- [ ] D2 If a solid was dissolved and made up to a volume, the titration of a portion gives the moles in that portion; scale up by (total volume ÷ portion volume) to get the moles in the whole sample (e.g. × 10 for 25.0 cm³ out of 250 cm³).

Good practice (one block)
- [ ] G1 Use the mean of the concordant titres, not the rough titre.
- [ ] G2 Give answers to three significant figures with units, matching the precision of titration data.
- [ ] G3 Check the answer is sensible (e.g. a dilute laboratory solution is usually 0.01–2 M; a % purity can't exceed 100%).

Unfamiliar contexts (Higher Level only; one block)
- [ ] U1 [HL] An unfamiliar problem may involve an unusual substance, a back titration, finding a formula or percentage purity, or a substance used in everyday life; the same method applies.
- [ ] U2 [HL] Read the question for the balanced equation (or write it), list the known quantities with units, and plan the route through moles before calculating.
- [ ] U3 [HL] Back titration: an excess of a known amount of reagent is added to the sample (e.g. acid to an insoluble antacid); the excess is then titrated; moles that reacted with the sample = moles added − moles in excess. Used when the sample is insoluble or reacts slowly.

### 2. Checked facts and examples

No named facts. Examples use standard formulae and equations.

### 3. Traps

- Convert cm³ to L when using n = c × V (or keep cm³ on both sides of the titration formula).
- Don't forget the mole ratio (e.g. 1 : 2 for Na₂CO₃ : HCl; 1 : 2 for H₂SO₄ : NaOH).
- Don't forget the dilution factor.

### 4. What exams ask (topics only)

- Calculate a concentration from titration data; convert to g L⁻¹ and %w/v; allow for dilution.
- Find the formula of a hydrated salt, the percentage purity of a sample, or the amount of a base in a tablet (Higher Level: unfamiliar contexts).

### 5. Practice questions to write

1. 9 marks: "25.0 cm³ of sulfuric acid needed 20.0 cm³ of 0.150 M sodium hydroxide for neutralisation. Write the balanced equation and calculate the concentration of the sulfuric acid in (i) mol L⁻¹, (ii) g L⁻¹." Model answer: H₂SO₄ + 2NaOH → Na₂SO₄ + 2H₂O; moles NaOH = 3.00 × 10⁻³; moles H₂SO₄ = 1.50 × 10⁻³; 0.0600 M; × 98 = 5.88 g L⁻¹.
2. 6 marks: "Explain what is meant by a back titration and when it is used." Model answer: U3.

### 1. Must cover

Problem 1: standardising hydrochloric acid (one block)
- [ ] P1 Data: 25.0 cm³ of 0.0500 M sodium carbonate needed a mean titre of 24.2 cm³ of hydrochloric acid, using methyl orange.
- [ ] P2 Equation: Na₂CO₃ + 2HCl → 2NaCl + H₂O + CO₂.
- [ ] P3 Moles Na₂CO₃ = 0.0500 × 0.0250 = 1.25 × 10⁻³ mol.
- [ ] P4 Moles HCl = 2 × 1.25 × 10⁻³ = 2.50 × 10⁻³ mol.
- [ ] P5 [HCl] = 2.50 × 10⁻³ ÷ 0.0242 = 0.103 mol L⁻¹.

Problem 2: ammonia in a household cleaner (one block)
- [ ] Q1 Data: 25.0 cm³ of a cleaner was diluted to 250 cm³; 25.0 cm³ of the diluted solution needed 21.5 cm³ of 0.100 M hydrochloric acid, using methyl orange.
- [ ] Q2 Equation: NH₃ + HCl → NH₄Cl (1 : 1).
- [ ] Q3 Moles HCl = 0.100 × 0.0215 = 2.15 × 10⁻³ mol = moles of NH₃ in 25.0 cm³ of diluted cleaner.
- [ ] Q4 Diluted concentration = 2.15 × 10⁻³ ÷ 0.0250 = 0.0860 mol L⁻¹; original = 0.0860 × 10 = 0.860 mol L⁻¹.
- [ ] Q5 In g L⁻¹: 0.860 × 17 = 14.6 g L⁻¹ of ammonia.

Problem 3: water of crystallisation in washing soda (Higher Level only; one block)
- [ ] R1 [HL] Data: 3.58 g of washing soda crystals, Na₂CO₃·xH₂O, were dissolved and made up to 250 cm³; 25.0 cm³ portions needed a mean titre of 25.0 cm³ of 0.100 M hydrochloric acid.
- [ ] R2 [HL] Moles HCl = 0.100 × 0.0250 = 2.50 × 10⁻³; moles Na₂CO₃ in 25.0 cm³ = 1.25 × 10⁻³; in 250 cm³ = 1.25 × 10⁻².
- [ ] R3 [HL] Mass of Na₂CO₃ = 1.25 × 10⁻² × 106 = 1.325 g; mass of water = 3.58 − 1.325 = 2.255 g.
- [ ] R4 [HL] Moles of water = 2.255 ÷ 18 = 0.125 mol; ratio water : Na₂CO₃ = 0.125 ÷ 0.0125 = 10, so x = 10 (Na₂CO₃·10H₂O).

Problem 4: a back titration of an antacid tablet (Higher Level only; one block)
- [ ] S1 [HL] Data: an antacid tablet containing calcium carbonate was added to 50.0 cm³ of 1.00 M hydrochloric acid (an excess); when it had reacted, the remaining acid needed 30.0 cm³ of 0.500 M sodium hydroxide.
- [ ] S2 [HL] Moles HCl added = 1.00 × 0.0500 = 0.0500 mol.
- [ ] S3 [HL] Moles HCl in excess = moles NaOH = 0.500 × 0.0300 = 0.0150 mol.
- [ ] S4 [HL] Moles HCl that reacted with the tablet = 0.0500 − 0.0150 = 0.0350 mol.
- [ ] S5 [HL] CaCO₃ + 2HCl → CaCl₂ + H₂O + CO₂, so moles CaCO₃ = 0.0350 ÷ 2 = 0.0175 mol; mass = 0.0175 × 100 = 1.75 g of calcium carbonate per tablet.
- [ ] S6 [HL] A back titration is needed because calcium carbonate is insoluble and reacts too slowly to titrate directly.

### 5. Practice questions to write

1. 12 marks: "2.65 g of anhydrous sodium carbonate was dissolved and made up to 500 cm³. 25.0 cm³ of this solution needed 22.0 cm³ of hydrochloric acid. Calculate (i) the concentration of the sodium carbonate solution, (ii) the concentration of the hydrochloric acid." Model answer: 2.65 ÷ 106 = 0.0250 mol in 0.500 L = 0.0500 M; moles in 25.0 cm³ = 1.25 × 10⁻³; HCl = 2.50 × 10⁻³ ÷ 0.0220 = 0.114 M.
2. 12 marks: "An antacid tablet containing magnesium hydroxide was dissolved in 40.0 cm³ of 0.500 M hydrochloric acid (an excess). The excess acid needed 12.0 cm³ of 0.500 M sodium hydroxide. Calculate the mass of magnesium hydroxide in the tablet (Mg(OH)₂ = 58)." Model answer: HCl added 0.0200 mol; excess 6.00 × 10⁻³ mol; reacted 0.0140 mol; Mg(OH)₂ + 2HCl → MgCl₂ + 2H₂O; 7.00 × 10⁻³ mol × 58 = 0.406 g.
