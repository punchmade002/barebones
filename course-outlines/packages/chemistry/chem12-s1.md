## Part: Primary standards and standard solutions (formerly `chem12-s1`)

- **loId:** `chem12-s1` · **chapterId:** `chem12` · **syllabusRef:** 4.1.1
- **Syllabus:** 4.1.1 "recognise the importance of primary standards and standard solutions".
  Learn about: "primary standards, and standard solutions, for acid-base and redox volumetric
  analysis". Normal type.
- **Prepared by:** a higher model, 2026-10-30. Why they matter in volumetric analysis and the
  idea of standardising. The properties of a primary standard and how to make up a standard
  solution are taught in "Solutions and concentration" in the mole chapter: recap in one bullet
  each and refer there; don't repeat the method.

### 1. Must cover

Volumetric analysis (one block)
- [ ] V1 Volumetric analysis (titration) finds the concentration of a solution (the analyte) by measuring the volume that reacts exactly with a known volume of another solution of known concentration.
- [ ] V2 The calculation depends on knowing one concentration exactly, so at least one solution must be a standard solution.
- [ ] V3 (assumed) Key terms: titre (the volume added from the burette); end point (when the indicator changes colour); equivalence point (when the reactants have reacted in exactly the ratio of the balanced equation); a good indicator's end point coincides with the equivalence point.

Primary standards (one block)
- [ ] P1 Recap: a primary standard is a very pure, stable substance of known formula that can be weighed accurately and dissolved to give a standard solution directly (properties and method in "Solutions and concentration").
- [ ] P2 Acid–base primary standard: anhydrous sodium carbonate, Na₂CO₃.
- [ ] P3 Redox primary standard: ammonium iron(II) sulfate, (NH₄)₂Fe(SO₄)₂·6H₂O.
- [ ] P4 Importance: every result in volumetric analysis is only as accurate as the standard; an impure or unstable standard gives a wrong concentration and so a wrong answer for the analyte.

Standardising solutions (one block)
- [ ] S1 Many reagents can't be made up directly as standard solutions: sodium hydroxide absorbs water and carbon dioxide from the air; hydrochloric acid is volatile; potassium manganate(VII) solution slowly decomposes; sodium thiosulfate crystals have uncertain water content.
- [ ] S2 Standardisation is finding the exact concentration of such a solution by titrating it against a primary standard (or a solution already standardised against one).
- [ ] S3 Example: hydrochloric acid is standardised by titration against a standard solution of sodium carbonate: Na₂CO₃ + 2HCl → 2NaCl + H₂O + CO₂.
- [ ] S4 Example: sodium hydroxide is then standardised against the standardised hydrochloric acid; potassium manganate(VII) is standardised against ammonium iron(II) sulfate.
- [ ] S5 A solution standardised in this way is sometimes called a secondary standard.

  titrations (iodine–thiosulfate, iron(II)) are in the redox volumetric chapter; vinegar is
  "Titration curves, indicators and vinegar".
- **Prepared by:** a higher model, 2026-10-30. The full titration technique once (it is reused
  by every titration), then the two acid–base titrations with equations, indicators and sample

### 1. Must cover

Apparatus (one block)
- [ ] A1 Pipette (with pipette filler): delivers an exact volume (e.g. 25.0 cm³) of one solution into a conical flask.
- [ ] A2 Burette: delivers measured, variable volumes of the other solution; read to 0.05 cm³.
- [ ] A3 Conical flask (can be swirled without splashing), white tile (shows the colour change clearly), funnel (to fill the burette), wash bottle of deionised water, indicator.
- [ ] A4 Diagram: a burette clamped above a conical flask on a white tile, with the pipette and filler shown separately.

Preparing the pipette and burette (one block, one step per bullet)
- [ ] P1 Rinse the pipette with deionised water, then with a little of the solution it is to measure, so that any water left in it doesn't dilute the solution.
- [ ] P2 Using a pipette filler, draw the solution up above the mark, then let it down until the bottom of the meniscus sits on the mark at eye level.
- [ ] P3 Let the solution run into the conical flask, touching the tip against the inside of the flask at the end; don't blow out the last drop (the pipette is calibrated to allow for it).
- [ ] P4 Rinse the burette with deionised water, then with the solution it is to contain; fill it using a funnel, then remove the funnel (drips from it would change the reading).
- [ ] P5 Run a little solution out to fill the part below the tap and remove any air bubble; read the burette at eye level from the bottom of the meniscus.
- [ ] P6 Rinsing the conical flask with deionised water is fine (it doesn't change the number of moles in the pipetted volume); it must not be rinsed with either solution.

Carrying out the titration (one block, one step per bullet)
- [ ] T1 Add two or three drops of the indicator to the conical flask.
- [ ] T2 Add solution from the burette while swirling the flask; do a rough titration first to find the approximate end point.
- [ ] T3 In the accurate titrations, add the solution quickly to within about 1 cm³ of the rough titre, then drop by drop, swirling, until one drop gives a permanent colour change.
- [ ] T4 Wash the inside walls of the flask with deionised water near the end point, so all the reagent reacts.
- [ ] T5 Repeat until two accurate titres agree to within 0.1 cm³; average them (don't include the rough titre).

Strong acid–strong base: hydrochloric acid and sodium hydroxide (one block)
- [ ] S1 Equation: HCl(aq) + NaOH(aq) → NaCl(aq) + H₂O(l); mole ratio 1 : 1.
- [ ] S2 Indicator: phenolphthalein (colourless to pink when NaOH is added from the burette) or methyl orange; either works because the pH changes sharply over a wide range at the equivalence point.
- [ ] S3 Sample data: 25.0 cm³ of NaOH needs a mean titre of 22.5 cm³ of 0.100 M HCl. Moles HCl = 0.100 × 0.0225 = 2.25 × 10⁻³; moles NaOH = 2.25 × 10⁻³; concentration of NaOH = 2.25 × 10⁻³ ÷ 0.0250 = 0.0900 M.

Strong acid–weak base: hydrochloric acid and sodium carbonate (one block)
- [ ] W1 Used to standardise hydrochloric acid against the primary standard sodium carbonate: Na₂CO₃(aq) + 2HCl(aq) → 2NaCl(aq) + H₂O(l) + CO₂(g); mole ratio 1 : 2.
- [ ] W2 Indicator: methyl orange (yellow in the sodium carbonate, turning orange-red at the end point as acid is added); phenolphthalein is unsuitable because it changes colour in alkaline solution, before the end point.
- [ ] W3 Sample data: 25.0 cm³ of 0.0500 M Na₂CO₃ needs 23.8 cm³ of HCl. Moles Na₂CO₃ = 0.0500 × 0.0250 = 1.25 × 10⁻³; moles HCl = 2 × 1.25 × 10⁻³ = 2.50 × 10⁻³; [HCl] = 2.50 × 10⁻³ ÷ 0.0238 = 0.105 M.
- [ ] W4 Ammonia (a weak base) is titrated with hydrochloric acid in the same way, using methyl orange: NH₃(aq) + HCl(aq) → NH₄Cl(aq).

Safety (one block)
- [ ] H1 Wear goggles; sodium hydroxide is corrosive (especially to eyes); hydrochloric acid is corrosive or irritant; use a pipette filler, never the mouth; fill the burette below eye level, with the burette removed from the stand or using a step.

### 3. Traps

- The pipette and burette are rinsed with their own solutions; the conical flask is not.
- Don't include the rough titre in the mean.
- The mole ratio must come from the balanced equation (1 : 2 for Na₂CO₃ : HCl).
- pH curves are Higher Level, in "Titration curves, indicators and vinegar".

### 4. What exams ask (topics only)

- Describe the correct procedure for rinsing and filling the pipette and burette; why each rinse is needed.
- How is the end point detected; why a white tile; why wash down the sides; why swirl.
- Name a suitable indicator and give the colour change.
- Calculate the concentration from titration data.
