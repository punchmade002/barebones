## Part: Arrhenius and Brønsted–Lowry theories (formerly `chem11-s3`)

- **loId:** `chem11-s3` · **chapterId:** `chem11` · **syllabusRef:** 3.4.3
- **Syllabus:** 3.4.3 "compare two theories of acid-base systems and justify why Brønsted-Lowry
  theory is a more extensive model for explaining behaviour". Learn about: "two theories of
  acid-base systems: Brønsted-Lowry; Arrhenius". Normal type.
- **Prepared by:** a higher model, 2026-10-30. Definitions, examples, comparison and
  justification. Conjugate pairs are Higher Level, in "Applying Brønsted–Lowry theory";
  mention only that every acid–base reaction involves a proton transfer.

### 1. Must cover

The Arrhenius theory (one block)
- [ ] A1 Svante Arrhenius (1887): an acid is a substance that dissociates in water to produce hydrogen ions (H⁺): HCl(aq) → H⁺(aq) + Cl⁻(aq).
- [ ] A2 A base is a substance that dissociates in water to produce hydroxide ions (OH⁻): NaOH(aq) → Na⁺(aq) + OH⁻(aq).
- [ ] A3 Neutralisation is the combination of hydrogen and hydroxide ions to form water: H⁺ + OH⁻ → H₂O.

Limitations of the Arrhenius theory (one block)
- [ ] L1 It applies only to aqueous solutions.
- [ ] L2 It can't explain substances that act as bases but contain no hydroxide, such as ammonia (NH₃) and sodium carbonate.
- [ ] L3 It can't explain acid–base reactions that happen without water, e.g. ammonia gas and hydrogen chloride gas reacting to form ammonium chloride: NH₃(g) + HCl(g) → NH₄Cl(s).
- [ ] L4 It ignores the role of water: a bare H⁺ ion (a proton) doesn't exist in water; it bonds to a water molecule to form the hydronium ion, H₃O⁺.

The Brønsted–Lowry theory (one block)
- [ ] B1 Johannes Brønsted and Thomas Lowry (1923), independently: an acid is a proton (H⁺) donor; a base is a proton acceptor.
- [ ] B2 An acid–base reaction is the transfer of a proton from an acid to a base.
- [ ] B3 Example: HCl(g) + H₂O(l) → H₃O⁺(aq) + Cl⁻(aq): HCl donates a proton (acid) and water accepts it (base).
- [ ] B4 Example: NH₃(aq) + H₂O(l) ⇌ NH₄⁺(aq) + OH⁻(aq): ammonia accepts a proton (base) and water donates it (acid).
- [ ] B5 Water can act as an acid or a base depending on what it reacts with (it is amphoteric).

Why Brønsted–Lowry is more extensive (one block, comparison)
- [ ] J1 It includes every Arrhenius acid and base, and more: the hydroxide ion is itself a proton acceptor.
- [ ] J2 It explains bases with no OH⁻, such as ammonia and the carbonate ion (CO₃²⁻ + H⁺ → HCO₃⁻).
- [ ] J3 It applies to reactions without water, such as NH₃ + HCl in the gas phase.
- [ ] J4 It shows the role of water as a base (forming H₃O⁺) or as an acid.
- [ ] J5 Comparison as paired points: H⁺ producer vs proton donor; OH⁻ producer vs proton acceptor; aqueous only vs any solvent or none; doesn't explain ammonia as a base vs does.

### 2. Checked facts and examples

| Item | Fact |
|---|---|
| A1 | Svante Arrhenius, theory of electrolytic dissociation, 1887. |
| B1 | Johannes Brønsted and Thomas Lowry, 1923. |

### 3. Traps

- A proton and a hydrogen ion are the same thing (H⁺).
- Conjugate acid–base pairs are Higher Level; keep to proton donor and acceptor here.
- Lewis theory is not on the specification.

### 4. What exams ask (topics only)

- Define an acid and a base according to each theory.
- Explain why ammonia is a base according to Brønsted–Lowry but not Arrhenius.
- Justify why Brønsted–Lowry theory is more extensive.

### 5. Practice questions to write

1. 9 marks: "Define an acid and a base according to (i) the Arrhenius theory, (ii) the Brønsted–Lowry theory." Model answer: A1, A2, B1.
2. 9 marks: "Justify why the Brønsted–Lowry theory is a more extensive model of acids and bases than the Arrhenius theory, using the reaction of ammonia with hydrogen chloride gas as one example." Model answer: J1–J4, L3.

## Part: Applying Brønsted–Lowry theory (formerly `chem11-s4`)

- **loId:** `chem11-s4` · **chapterId:** `chem11` · **syllabusRef:** 3.4.4
- **Syllabus:** 3.4.4 "**apply Brønsted-Lowry theory to identify, in chemical equations:
  conjugate acid-base pairs; species acting as acids and bases**". All bold: the whole
  subsection is Higher Level only. Mark every block heading " (Higher Level only)".
- **Prepared by:** a higher model, 2026-10-30. Definitions, the method of identifying the
  species, worked examples, strength relationships, amphoteric species.

### 1. Must cover

Conjugate pairs (one block)
- [ ] C1 [HL] When an acid donates a proton, what is left can accept a proton back: it is the conjugate base of that acid.
- [ ] C2 [HL] When a base accepts a proton, it forms the conjugate acid of that base.
- [ ] C3 [HL] A conjugate acid–base pair is two species that differ by one proton (H⁺), e.g. HCl and Cl⁻; H₃O⁺ and H₂O; NH₄⁺ and NH₃.
- [ ] C4 [HL] Every Brønsted–Lowry reaction has two conjugate pairs: acid₁ + base₂ ⇌ acid₂ + base₁.

Identifying the species (one block, skills)
- [ ] M1 [HL] Step 1: For each reactant, see whether it loses or gains a proton in going to the products.
- [ ] M2 [HL] Step 2: The reactant that loses a proton is the acid; the one that gains a proton is the base.
- [ ] M3 [HL] Step 3: On the product side, the species formed from the base is the conjugate acid, and the species formed from the acid is the conjugate base; in a reversible reaction they act as an acid and a base in the reverse reaction.

Worked examples (one block; one bullet per equation)
- [ ] W1 [HL] HCl + H₂O → H₃O⁺ + Cl⁻: acids HCl and H₃O⁺; bases H₂O and Cl⁻; pairs HCl/Cl⁻ and H₃O⁺/H₂O.
- [ ] W2 [HL] NH₃ + H₂O ⇌ NH₄⁺ + OH⁻: acids H₂O and NH₄⁺; bases NH₃ and OH⁻; pairs NH₄⁺/NH₃ and H₂O/OH⁻.
- [ ] W3 [HL] CH₃COOH + H₂O ⇌ CH₃COO⁻ + H₃O⁺: pairs CH₃COOH/CH₃COO⁻ and H₃O⁺/H₂O.
- [ ] W4 [HL] HCO₃⁻ + H₂O ⇌ CO₃²⁻ + H₃O⁺ (HCO₃⁻ as an acid) and HCO₃⁻ + H₃O⁺ ⇌ H₂CO₃ + H₂O (HCO₃⁻ as a base).
- [ ] W5 [HL] NH₃ + HCl → NH₄⁺ + Cl⁻ (no water involved): pairs NH₄⁺/NH₃ and HCl/Cl⁻.

Strength of conjugates and amphoteric species (one block)
- [ ] S1 [HL] The stronger an acid, the weaker its conjugate base: HCl is a strong acid, so Cl⁻ is a very weak base that barely accepts protons.
- [ ] S2 [HL] A weak acid has a relatively strong conjugate base: ethanoic acid is weak, and the ethanoate ion (CH₃COO⁻) readily accepts a proton.
- [ ] S3 [HL] An amphoteric (amphiprotic) species can act as either an acid or a base: water (W1, W2) and the hydrogencarbonate ion (W4).

### 2. Checked facts and examples

All equations above are correct.

### 3. Traps

- Conjugate pairs differ by exactly one H⁺: H₂O and OH⁻ are a pair; H₃O⁺ and OH⁻ are not.
- The conjugate base of an acid is on the product side, not the other reactant.
- Ka and pH calculations are in the pH chapter.

### 4. What exams ask (topics only)

- Identify the acids, bases and conjugate pairs in a given equation.
- Explain the term conjugate pair; why water is amphoteric.

### 5. Practice questions to write

1. 9 marks: "In the reaction NH₃ + H₂O ⇌ NH₄⁺ + OH⁻, identify the two species acting as acids and the two conjugate acid–base pairs, and explain why water is described as amphoteric." Model answer: W2, S3.
2. 6 marks: "Explain why the chloride ion is a very weak base while the ethanoate ion is a stronger base." Model answer: S1, S2.

## Part: Strong vs weak, concentrated vs dilute (formerly `chem11-s5`)

- **loId:** `chem11-s5` · **chapterId:** `chem11` · **syllabusRef:** 3.4.7
- **Syllabus:** 3.4.7 "distinguish between: weak and strong acids (and bases); concentrated and
  dilute acids (and bases)". Normal type.
- **Prepared by:** a higher model, 2026-10-30. The two distinctions, examples, experimental
  evidence, and the trap of confusing them. Ka, Kb and pH calculations are in the pH chapter
  (Higher Level).

### 1. Must cover

Strong and weak acids (one block)
- [ ] A1 A strong acid is completely dissociated (ionised) into ions in water: HCl(aq) → H⁺(aq) + Cl⁻(aq) (single arrow).
- [ ] A2 Strong acids: hydrochloric (HCl), nitric (HNO₃), sulfuric (H₂SO₄).
- [ ] A3 A weak acid is only slightly dissociated in water; most molecules stay undissociated and an equilibrium is set up: CH₃COOH(aq) ⇌ CH₃COO⁻(aq) + H⁺(aq).
- [ ] A4 Weak acids: ethanoic acid (vinegar), citric acid, carbonic acid (CO₂ in water), most organic acids.

Strong and weak bases (one block)
- [ ] B1 A strong base is completely dissociated in water, e.g. sodium hydroxide and potassium hydroxide: NaOH(aq) → Na⁺(aq) + OH⁻(aq).
- [ ] B2 A weak base reacts only slightly with water to produce hydroxide ions, e.g. ammonia: NH₃(aq) + H₂O(l) ⇌ NH₄⁺(aq) + OH⁻(aq).

Concentrated and dilute (one block)
- [ ] C1 Concentration is the amount of acid (or base) dissolved per litre of solution (mol L⁻¹).
- [ ] C2 A concentrated acid has a large amount of acid per litre (e.g. 10 M HCl); a dilute acid has a small amount (e.g. 0.1 M HCl).
- [ ] C3 Strength and concentration are independent: a dilute strong acid (0.01 M HCl) is still fully dissociated; a concentrated weak acid (glacial ethanoic acid) is still only slightly dissociated.

Evidence: comparing acids of the same concentration (one block, investigation)
- [ ] E1 pH: 0.1 M hydrochloric acid has a pH of 1.0; 0.1 M ethanoic acid has a pH of about 2.9, because it produces far fewer H⁺ ions.
- [ ] E2 Rate of reaction: magnesium ribbon fizzes much faster in 0.1 M HCl than in 0.1 M ethanoic acid (higher concentration of H⁺ ions).
- [ ] E3 Conductivity: 0.1 M HCl conducts electricity much better than 0.1 M ethanoic acid (more ions).
- [ ] E4 The same volumes of the two acids at the same concentration still neutralise the same amount of sodium hydroxide and give the same total volume of hydrogen with excess magnesium, because the weak acid's equilibrium shifts to release more H⁺ as H⁺ is used up.
- [ ] E5 Safety: concentrated acids and alkalis are corrosive: goggles, gloves; dilute by adding acid to water.

### 2. Checked facts and examples

| Item | Fact |
|---|---|
| E1 | pH of 0.1 M HCl = 1.0; pH of 0.1 M ethanoic acid ≈ 2.9 (Ka 1.8 × 10⁻⁵). |

### 3. Traps

- "Strong" doesn't mean "concentrated"; "weak" doesn't mean "dilute".
- E4 is a common exam trap: same concentration, same volume means the same amount of base is needed.
- Don't do Ka or pH calculations here (Higher Level, in the pH chapter).

### 4. What exams ask (topics only)

- Distinguish strong and weak acids, concentrated and dilute acids, with examples.
- Describe evidence that ethanoic acid is weaker than hydrochloric acid.

### 5. Practice questions to write

1. 9 marks: "Distinguish between a strong acid and a weak acid, and between a concentrated acid and a dilute acid, giving an example of each." Model answer: A1, A3, C2.
2. 9 marks: "Describe two experiments that would show that 0.1 M ethanoic acid is a weaker acid than 0.1 M hydrochloric acid, and explain why equal volumes of the two acids need the same volume of sodium hydroxide to neutralise them." Model answer: two of E1–E3; E4.
