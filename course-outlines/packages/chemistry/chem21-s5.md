### 1. Must cover

Combustion and heat of combustion (one block)
- [ ] D1 Combustion is the reaction of a substance with oxygen, giving out heat (and usually light); it is always exothermic.
- [ ] D2 Complete combustion (in plenty of oxygen): all the carbon becomes carbon dioxide and all the hydrogen becomes water; for hydrocarbons and alcohols the only products are CO₂ and H₂O.
- [ ] D3 Incomplete combustion (limited oxygen) also gives carbon monoxide (a toxic gas) and soot (carbon), and releases less energy, e.g. 2CH₄(g) + 3O₂(g) → 2CO(g) + 4H₂O(l).
- [ ] D4 Standard heat of combustion, ΔHc°: the enthalpy change when one mole of a substance is completely burned in excess oxygen under standard conditions (25 °C, 101.3 kPa); always negative.
- [ ] D5 Because it is per mole of the substance burned, the equation for a heat of combustion has 1 mol of fuel, so fractions of O₂ may be needed, e.g. C₂H₆(g) + 3½O₂(g) → 2CO₂(g) + 3H₂O(l), ΔHc = −1560 kJ mol⁻¹ (equivalently 2C₂H₆ + 7O₂ → 4CO₂ + 6H₂O, ΔH = −3120 kJ).

Balancing combustion equations (one block, one step per bullet)
- [ ] B1 Step 1: Write the formula of the fuel and the products CO₂ and H₂O.
- [ ] B2 Step 2: Balance carbon: number of CO₂ = number of C atoms in the fuel.
- [ ] B3 Step 3: Balance hydrogen: number of H₂O = half the number of H atoms in the fuel.
- [ ] B4 Step 4: Balance oxygen last: count the O atoms in the products, subtract any O already in the fuel (one for an alcohol), and divide by two for the number of O₂; double everything if you want whole numbers.
- [ ] B5 Check: count each type of atom on both sides; add state symbols (fuel, O₂(g), CO₂(g), H₂O(l) under standard conditions).

General equations (one block)
- [ ] G1 Alkanes: CₙH₂ₙ₊₂ + ((3n + 1)/2) O₂ → nCO₂ + (n + 1)H₂O.
- [ ] G2 Alkenes (one C=C): CₙH₂ₙ + (3n/2) O₂ → nCO₂ + nH₂O.
- [ ] G3 Alkynes (one C≡C): CₙH₂ₙ₋₂ + ((3n − 1)/2) O₂ → nCO₂ + (n − 1)H₂O.
- [ ] G4 Primary alcohols: CₙH₂ₙ₊₁OH + (3n/2) O₂ → nCO₂ + (n + 1)H₂O.

Worked equations (one block)
- [ ] E1 Methane: CH₄(g) + 2O₂(g) → CO₂(g) + 2H₂O(l).
- [ ] E2 Propane: C₃H₈(g) + 5O₂(g) → 3CO₂(g) + 4H₂O(l).
- [ ] E3 Butane: C₄H₁₀(g) + 6½O₂(g) → 4CO₂(g) + 5H₂O(l), or 2C₄H₁₀(g) + 13O₂(g) → 8CO₂(g) + 10H₂O(l).
- [ ] E4 Ethene: C₂H₄(g) + 3O₂(g) → 2CO₂(g) + 2H₂O(l); propene: C₃H₆(g) + 4½O₂(g) → 3CO₂(g) + 3H₂O(l).
- [ ] E5 Ethyne: C₂H₂(g) + 2½O₂(g) → 2CO₂(g) + H₂O(l), or 2C₂H₂(g) + 5O₂(g) → 4CO₂(g) + 2H₂O(l).
- [ ] E6 Methanol: CH₃OH(l) + 1½O₂(g) → CO₂(g) + 2H₂O(l), or 2CH₃OH(l) + 3O₂(g) → 2CO₂(g) + 4H₂O(l).
- [ ] E7 Ethanol: C₂H₅OH(l) + 3O₂(g) → 2CO₂(g) + 3H₂O(l).
- [ ] E8 Propan-1-ol: C₃H₇OH(l) + 4½O₂(g) → 3CO₂(g) + 4H₂O(l); butan-1-ol: C₄H₉OH(l) + 6O₂(g) → 4CO₂(g) + 5H₂O(l).
- [ ] E9 Pentan-1-ol: C₅H₁₁OH(l) + 7½O₂(g) → 5CO₂(g) + 6H₂O(l); hexan-1-ol: C₆H₁₃OH(l) + 9O₂(g) → 6CO₂(g) + 7H₂O(l).

Standard heats of combustion (one block)
- [ ] V1 Alkanes (kJ mol⁻¹): methane −890; ethane −1560; propane −2220; butane −2877.
- [ ] V2 Primary alcohols (kJ mol⁻¹): methanol −726; ethanol −1367; propan-1-ol −2021; butan-1-ol −2676; pentan-1-ol −3331; hexan-1-ol −3984.
- [ ] V3 Two-carbon compounds (kJ mol⁻¹): ethane −1560; ethene −1411; ethyne −1300; ethanol −1367.
- [ ] V4 Three-carbon compounds (kJ mol⁻¹): propane −2220; propene −2058.

Trend 1: chain length (Higher Level only; one block)
- [ ] T1 [HL] In a homologous series, ΔHc becomes more negative as the number of carbon atoms increases, by an almost constant amount for each extra CH₂ (alkanes about 650–670 kJ mol⁻¹, e.g. 1560 − 890 = 670, 2877 − 2220 = 657; primary alcohols about 640–655, e.g. 1367 − 726 = 641, 2021 − 1367 = 654).
- [ ] T2 [HL] Explanation: each extra CH₂ group adds one C–C and two C–H bonds to break and needs 1½ more O₂ molecules, but forms one more CO₂ (two C=O bonds) and one more H₂O (two O–H bonds); more energy is released by the new bonds formed than is needed to break the extra bonds.
- [ ] T3 [HL] Because exactly the same extra bonds are broken and formed for every CH₂ added, the increase is nearly constant, so a graph of ΔHc against number of carbon atoms is a straight line.
- [ ] T4 [HL] Bond enthalpy estimate per CH₂: broken 348 + 2(412) + 1½(498) = 1919 kJ; formed 2(799) + 2(463) = 2524 kJ; difference −605 kJ (an estimate for gaseous water; the measured increase is larger because the water formed is liquid).
- [ ] T5 [HL] The straight-line trend can be used to predict values: e.g. hexan-1-ol ≈ −3331 − 655 = −3986 kJ mol⁻¹, close to the data value of −3984 kJ mol⁻¹.

Trend 2: alcohols compared with alkanes (Higher Level only; one block)
- [ ] U1 [HL] A primary alcohol has a less negative ΔHc than the alkane with the same number of carbon atoms (e.g. ethanol −1367 against ethane −1560 kJ mol⁻¹; methanol −726 against methane −890).
- [ ] U2 [HL] Explanation: the alcohol molecule already contains an oxygen atom bonded to carbon and hydrogen (C–O–H): it is already partly oxidised, so it needs less oxygen (ethanol 3 O₂, ethane 3½ O₂) and less energy is released on complete oxidation, although the same numbers of CO₂ and H₂O are formed.

Trend 3: alkenes and alkynes compared with alkanes (Higher Level only; one block)
- [ ] W1 [HL] For the same number of carbon atoms, ΔHc becomes less negative in the order alkane > alkene > alkyne (ethane −1560; ethene −1411; ethyne −1300 kJ mol⁻¹).
- [ ] W2 [HL] Explanation: the alkene has two fewer hydrogen atoms than the alkane, and the alkyne four fewer, so fewer water molecules (fewer O–H bonds) are formed and less energy is released.

### 3. Traps

- Balance oxygen last, and remember the O atom in an alcohol.
- A heat of combustion refers to one mole of fuel; if the equation is doubled, so is ΔH.
- Primary alcohols up to C6 only; hydrocarbons with at most one double or triple bond only.
- Alcohols produce the same numbers of CO₂ and H₂O as the corresponding alkane; the difference is that they need less O₂ and are already partly oxidised. Don't say "fewer C=O bonds form".
- Don't explain the alkene trend only by "the C=C bond needs more energy to break"; the main reason is fewer O–H bonds formed.
- Fuels, octane numbers, kilogram calorific values and the bomb calorimeter are not on the specification.

### 4. What exams ask (topics only)

- Define heat of combustion; write the balanced equation for the complete combustion of a named hydrocarbon (methane, propane, butane) or alcohol.
- Is a combustion reaction exothermic or endothermic, and why?
- Explain the trend in heats of combustion in a homologous series; compare alcohols with alkanes, alkenes with alkanes (Higher Level).

## Part: Investigation: heat of combustion of alcohols (formerly `chem21-s8`)

- **loId:** `chem21-s8` · **chapterId:** `chem21` · **syllabusRef:** 3.1.8
- **Syllabus:** 3.1.8 "investigate, using primary data, the energy change of combustion and
  compare experimental values to standard values, accounting for differences" (EI:
  Experimental Investigation). Learn about: "the heat of combustion of a series of alcohols,
  which can be estimated experimentally using spirit burners". Normal type.
- **Prepared by:** a higher model, 2026-10-30. Full investigation of methanol, ethanol,
  propan-1-ol and butan-1-ol with spirit burners and a copper can: aim, variables, method,
  reasons, sample results and calculation, comparison with standard values, errors and safety.
  Equations and the trends are in "Combustion"; Q = mcΔT is in "Calculating ΔH and energy
  profiles". Specific heat capacity of water 4.18 kJ kg⁻¹ K⁻¹.

### 1. Must cover

Aim and chemistry (one block)
- [ ] A1 Aim: to estimate the heats of combustion of a series of primary alcohols (methanol, ethanol, propan-1-ol, butan-1-ol) using spirit burners, and to compare them with standard values.
- [ ] A2 Heat of combustion: the enthalpy change when one mole of a substance is completely burned in excess oxygen.
- [ ] A3 Principle: the heat released by burning a measured mass of alcohol is absorbed by a known mass of water; Q = m(water) × c × ΔT; ΔHc = −Q ÷ moles of alcohol burned.
- [ ] A4 Hypothesis: the heat of combustion becomes more negative by a roughly constant amount for each extra CH₂ in the alcohol.

Variables (one block)
- [ ] V1 Independent variable: the alcohol (number of carbon atoms).
- [ ] V2 Dependent variable: the heat released per mole (calculated from the mass of alcohol burned and the temperature rise of the water).
- [ ] V3 Controlled variables: the same volume (mass) of water; the same copper can; the same distance from wick to can; the same temperature rise (e.g. 20 °C); the same wick length; draught shield in the same position; same starting temperature of water.

Apparatus and method (one block, one step per bullet)
- [ ] M1 Measure 100 cm³ (100 g) of water with a graduated cylinder into a copper can (calorimeter) and clamp it a fixed distance (about 2–3 cm) above the wick of the spirit burner; surround with a draught shield.
- [ ] M2 Weigh the spirit burner containing the alcohol, with its cap on, on a balance reading to 0.01 g.
- [ ] M3 Record the initial temperature of the water.
- [ ] M4 Light the burner and stir the water gently with the thermometer or a stirrer.
- [ ] M5 When the temperature has risen by about 20 °C, put out the flame by replacing the cap, and record the highest temperature reached.
- [ ] M6 Reweigh the burner with its cap at once.
- [ ] M7 Repeat with each alcohol (fresh water each time) and repeat each to check reproducibility.
- [ ] M8 Diagram: a copper can of water held by a clamp above a spirit burner, a thermometer and stirrer in the water, a draught shield around the burner and can, the burner on a heat-proof mat, and a balance for weighing the burner.

Why each choice (one block)
- [ ] W1 Copper is a good conductor of heat, so heat passes quickly from the flame into the water.
- [ ] W2 The draught shield reduces heat lost by air currents and keeps the flame steady.
- [ ] W3 Capping the burner as soon as the flame is out, and weighing immediately, stops the volatile alcohol evaporating from the wick (which would be counted as burned).
- [ ] W4 Stirring spreads the heat evenly so the thermometer reading represents all the water.
- [ ] W5 The same temperature rise and the same set-up for each alcohol make the heat losses similar, so the comparison between alcohols is fair even if the absolute values are low.
- [ ] W6 A temperature rise of about 20 °C is large enough to measure accurately but not so large that heat losses (which increase as the water gets hotter) become very large.

Sample results and calculation (one block, one step per bullet; sample data)
- [ ] C1 For each alcohol: 100 g (0.100 kg) of water was heated through 20.0 °C, so Q = 0.100 × 4.18 × 20.0 = 8.36 kJ.
- [ ] C2 Ethanol (M = 46 g mol⁻¹): mass burned = 0.63 g; moles = 0.63 ÷ 46 = 0.0137 mol.
- [ ] C3 Heat per mole = 8.36 ÷ 0.0137 = 610 kJ mol⁻¹; ΔHc(experimental) = −610 kJ mol⁻¹ (to 3 s.f., from 8.36 ÷ (0.63/46)).
- [ ] C4 Results table (sample data): methanol (M 32) 0.82 g, −326 kJ mol⁻¹; ethanol (M 46) 0.63 g, −610 kJ mol⁻¹; propan-1-ol (M 60) 0.55 g, −912 kJ mol⁻¹; butan-1-ol (M 74) 0.51 g, −1210 kJ mol⁻¹.
- [ ] C5 Standard values: −726, −1367, −2021, −2676 kJ mol⁻¹; each experimental value is about 45% of the standard value (percentage error about 55%).
- [ ] C6 Percentage error = (standard − experimental) ÷ standard × 100, e.g. ethanol (1367 − 610) ÷ 1367 × 100 = 55%.
- [ ] C7 A graph of experimental ΔHc against number of carbon atoms is a straight line, like the standard values but less steep: the trend is confirmed even though the values are too low.

Accounting for the differences (one block)
- [ ] D1 Heat loss: much of the heat from the flame goes to the surrounding air rather than into the water; this is the main reason the values are much less negative than standard values.
- [ ] D2 Heat absorbed by the copper can, thermometer and clamp is not included in Q (it could be added as m(copper) × c(copper) × ΔT).
- [ ] D3 Incomplete combustion: with limited air at the wick, some carbon forms soot (seen on the base of the can) and carbon monoxide, releasing less energy than complete combustion.
- [ ] D4 Evaporation of alcohol from the wick: alcohol lost without burning is counted as burned, making the heat per mole too small.
- [ ] D5 Non-standard conditions: standard values assume products at 25 °C with water as a liquid; in the experiment the water leaves as vapour, so its heat of condensation is not released into the can.
- [ ] D6 Improvements: insulate the can and add a lid; reduce the distance between flame and can; use a draught shield on all sides; ensure a good air supply for complete combustion; cap and weigh the burner immediately; include the heat absorbed by the can. (Accurate values are measured in a sealed calorimeter in oxygen, which is not required.)

Safety (one block)
- [ ] S1 Alcohols are highly flammable: keep bottles stoppered and away from flames; never refill a burner while it is lit or hot; refill in a fume cupboard or away from flames.
- [ ] S2 Methanol is toxic (by swallowing, skin contact and inhaling vapour); avoid skin contact and work in a well-ventilated room.
- [ ] S3 Wear safety glasses; tie back long hair; stand the burner on a heat-proof mat; let the copper can cool before handling.

### 3. Traps

- Use the mass of water in Q = mcΔT, not the mass of alcohol.
- Moles of alcohol = mass burned ÷ M (mass burned = initial − final mass of burner).
- Experimental values are less negative than standard values; a value more negative than the standard value means an error in the data or calculation.
- Give ΔHc a negative sign.
- The bomb calorimeter is not on the specification; mention only that accurate values are measured in a sealed calorimeter.
- Sources of error must explain the direction of the difference (why the value is too low), not just list problems.

### 4. What exams ask (topics only)

- Define heat of combustion; describe a laboratory method of measuring the heat of combustion of a liquid such as methanol; the main sources of error.
- Calculate ΔHc from mass burned, mass of water and temperature rise; compare with the standard value.
