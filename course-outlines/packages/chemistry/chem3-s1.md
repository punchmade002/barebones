## Part: The current orbital model (formerly `chem3-s1`)

- **loId:** `chem3-s1` · **chapterId:** `chem3` · **syllabusRef:** 1.2.2
- **Syllabus:** 1.2.2 "describe the atom using the current orbital model of atomic theory,
  including subatomic particles". Normal type: Ordinary and Higher level.
- **Prepared by:** a higher model, 2026-10-30. Describe the whole atom in the orbital model at a
  level all students need: nucleus, main energy levels, orbitals. Sublevels (s, p, d), orbital
  shapes and the filling rules are Higher Level and belong to "Electronic structure"; mention
  only that energy levels divide into sublevels, in one sentence. The history of models is in
  "Atomic structure: the nuclear model".

### 1. Must cover

The atom in the orbital model (one block)
- [ ] A1 An atom has a tiny, dense, positively charged nucleus containing protons and neutrons (see "Atomic structure: the nuclear model"), surrounded by electrons.
- [ ] A2 The electrons are arranged in main energy levels (n = 1, 2, 3, 4…), increasing in energy and in average distance from the nucleus as n increases.
- [ ] A3 Within an energy level, electrons occupy orbitals.
- [ ] A4 An orbital is a region of space around the nucleus in which there is a high probability of finding an electron.
- [ ] A5 Each orbital can hold a maximum of two electrons.
- [ ] A6 The maximum number of electrons in main energy level n is 2n²: 2 in n = 1, 8 in n = 2, 18 in n = 3, 32 in n = 4.
- [ ] A7 Main energy levels are divided into sublevels made of orbitals (taught in "Electronic structure").

How it differs from the Bohr model (one block)
- [ ] D1 The Bohr model gives electrons fixed circular paths at fixed distances; the orbital model does not give an exact path or position, only where an electron is likely to be.
- [ ] D2 The orbital model keeps the idea of fixed energy levels from the Bohr model.
- [ ] D3 Electrons behave both as particles and as waves, so their exact position and motion can't both be known; the orbital describes probability.
- [ ] D4 Diagram: the nucleus at the centre with fuzzy regions (electron clouds) around it, labelled nucleus (protons, neutrons) and orbitals (electron density), compared with a Bohr diagram of circular shells.

### 2. Checked facts and examples

| Item | Fact |
|---|---|
| A6 | Maximum electrons per main energy level = 2n². |

### 3. Traps

- Don't describe s and p orbital shapes or the 1s 2s 2p order here: Higher Level, in "Electronic structure".
- Don't call an orbital a path or orbit.
- Heisenberg's name is not required; describe the idea without it.

### 4. What exams ask (topics only)

- Define an atomic orbital.
- Describe the structure of an atom.

### 5. Practice questions to write

1. 6 marks: "Describe the structure of an atom according to the current orbital model, referring to the nucleus, energy levels and orbitals." Model answer: A1–A5.
2. 6 marks: "Give two differences between the Bohr model and the orbital model of the atom." Model answer: D1, D3.

## Part: Energy levels and the hydrogen emission spectrum (formerly `chem3-s2`)

- **loId:** `chem3-s2` · **chapterId:** `chem3` · **syllabusRef:** 1.2.3
- **Syllabus:** 1.2.3 "**describe and explain the origin of lines on the atomic emission
  spectrum of hydrogen**". Learn about: "**electron transition, photon energy and frequency,
  ground state, excited states, Em − En = hf**". All bold: the whole subsection is Higher Level
  only. Mark every block heading " (Higher Level only)".
- **Prepared by:** a higher model, 2026-10-30. The basic flame-colour explanation that Ordinary
  level needs is in "Identifying elements"; this subsection gives the full Higher Level
  treatment.

### 1. Must cover

Energy levels and states (one block)
- [ ] E1 [HL] The electron in a hydrogen atom can exist only in certain allowed energy levels, n = 1, 2, 3…; its energy is quantised (it can have only certain fixed values).
- [ ] E2 [HL] Ground state: the lowest available energy level for an electron (n = 1 for hydrogen).
- [ ] E3 [HL] Excited state: any higher energy level, reached when the atom absorbs energy (e.g. from heat or an electric discharge).
- [ ] E4 [HL] Excited states are unstable; the electron soon falls back to a lower level.

Electron transitions and photons (one block)
- [ ] T1 [HL] An electron transition is a jump of an electron between energy levels.
- [ ] T2 [HL] When an electron falls from a higher level (energy Em) to a lower level (energy En), the energy difference is emitted as a photon (a packet of light energy).
- [ ] T3 [HL] The photon's energy is related to its frequency by Em − En = hf, where h is Planck's constant (6.626 × 10⁻³⁴ J s) and f is the frequency in hertz (s⁻¹).
- [ ] T4 [HL] Because the energy levels are fixed, each transition has a definite energy difference, so it emits light of one definite frequency, seen as one line (one colour) in the spectrum.
- [ ] T5 [HL] Larger energy gaps give higher-frequency (shorter-wavelength) light.

The hydrogen emission spectrum (one block)
- [ ] S1 [HL] An emission spectrum is obtained by passing an electric discharge through hydrogen gas at low pressure and viewing the light through a spectroscope (or diffraction grating).
- [ ] S2 [HL] It is a line spectrum: a series of separate bright coloured lines on a dark background, not a continuous spectrum like that of white light.
- [ ] S3 [HL] Each line corresponds to one transition from a higher to a lower energy level.
- [ ] S4 [HL] The lines in the visible region (the Balmer series) come from electrons falling to n = 2 from n = 3, 4, 5, 6 (red, blue-green, blue and violet lines).
- [ ] S5 [HL] Transitions down to n = 1 (the Lyman series) give ultraviolet lines; transitions to n = 3 (the Paschen series) give infrared lines.
- [ ] S6 [HL] The lines get closer together at higher energy, because the energy levels themselves get closer together further from the nucleus.
- [ ] S7 [HL] The spectrum is evidence for fixed energy levels: if an electron could have any energy, the spectrum would be continuous.
- [ ] S8 [HL] Diagram: an energy-level diagram with levels n = 1 to 6 (getting closer together going up) and downward arrows to n = 2 labelled with the visible lines, plus arrows to n = 1 (ultraviolet) and n = 3 (infrared); and a sketch of the line spectrum with four visible lines.

### 2. Checked facts and examples

| Item | Fact |
|---|---|
| T3 | Planck's constant h = 6.626 × 10⁻³⁴ J s. |
| S4 | Balmer series: transitions to n = 2; visible lines 656 nm (red, from n = 3), 486 nm (blue-green, n = 4), 434 nm (blue-violet, n = 5), 410 nm (violet, n = 6). |
| S5 | Lyman series → n = 1 (ultraviolet); Paschen series → n = 3 (infrared). |

### 3. Traps

- Light is emitted when electrons fall to lower levels, not when they jump up (they absorb energy then).
- The electron falls to a lower level, not necessarily to the ground state.
- Don't introduce atomic absorption spectroscopy (not on the specification).
- Flame tests and identifying elements from their spectra are in "Identifying elements".

### 4. What exams ask (topics only)

- Distinguish ground state and excited state.
- Explain how the lines in the hydrogen emission spectrum arise.
- Explain why the spectrum is a line spectrum and how it is evidence for energy levels.
- Which series is in the visible region; which level do the electrons fall to?

### 5. Practice questions to write

1. 12 marks: "Explain how the lines in the emission spectrum of hydrogen arise and why each line has a definite frequency. Refer to the equation Em − En = hf." Model answer: E1–E4, T2–T4, S3.
2. 6 marks: "Distinguish between the ground state and an excited state of an electron, and state the final energy level of the transitions that produce the visible lines in the hydrogen spectrum." Model answer: E2, E3, S4.

## Part: Identifying elements (formerly `chem3-s3`)

- **loId:** `chem3-s3` · **chapterId:** `chem3` · **syllabusRef:** 1.2.4
- **Syllabus:** 1.2.4 "identify an element using appropriate primary or secondary data".
  Learn about: "identifying elements: from flame tests (primary data, limited to salts of: Na,
  K, Cu, Li, Ba and Sr); from their line emission spectra (primary or secondary data, limited to
  salts of: Na, Sr, Cu)". Normal type: Ordinary and Higher level.
- **Prepared by:** a higher model, 2026-10-30. Write the flame test as a full investigation
  (method, safety, results, explanation) and a block on line spectra. Give the basic
  explanation (electrons excited, emit light falling back) without Em − En = hf, which is
  Higher Level in "Energy levels and the hydrogen emission spectrum".

### 1. Must cover

Why elements give colours (one block)
- [ ] W1 Heating an atom in a flame gives energy to its electrons, which move up to higher energy levels.
- [ ] W2 The electrons fall back to lower levels and give out the energy as light.
- [ ] W3 Each element has its own set of energy levels, so it gives out its own colours of light: a flame colour or line spectrum identifies the element.

Flame test method (one block, investigation)
- [ ] F1 Aim: to identify the metal in a salt by the colour it gives to a flame.
- [ ] F2 Equipment: Bunsen burner, platinum or nichrome wire loop (or wooden splints soaked overnight in deionised water), watch glasses, concentrated hydrochloric acid, samples of the chlorides of the six metals, deionised water.
- [ ] F3 Step 1: Clean the wire by dipping it in concentrated hydrochloric acid and holding it in the hottest part of a non-luminous (blue) Bunsen flame until it gives no colour.
- [ ] F4 Step 2: Dip the wire in the acid again, then into the salt so a little sticks to it (chlorides are used because they are volatile).
- [ ] F5 Step 3: Hold it at the edge of the non-luminous flame and note the colour.
- [ ] F6 Step 4: Clean the wire again before testing the next salt; use a different splint for each salt if splints are used.
- [ ] F7 Viewing potassium through cobalt-blue glass filters out yellow light from any sodium impurity.
- [ ] F8 Safety: wear goggles; concentrated hydrochloric acid is corrosive (gloves, small volume); barium salts are toxic (wash hands; don't ingest); keep the hot wire away from skin.
- [ ] F9 Diagram: a Bunsen burner with a non-luminous flame and a wire loop holding the salt at the edge of the flame.

Flame colours (one block, one bullet per metal)
- [ ] C1 Lithium: crimson red.
- [ ] C2 Sodium: bright yellow-orange (persistent).
- [ ] C3 Potassium: lilac (pale purple).
- [ ] C4 Copper: blue-green.
- [ ] C5 Barium: pale (apple) green.
- [ ] C6 Strontium: red (scarlet).
- [ ] C7 Lithium and strontium are both red and are hard to tell apart by eye; their line spectra distinguish them.

Line emission spectra (one block)
- [ ] L1 A spectroscope splits the light from a flame or discharge into its separate colours.
- [ ] L2 An element gives a line emission spectrum: a pattern of bright lines at specific wavelengths on a dark background, unique to that element like a fingerprint.
- [ ] L3 To identify an element, compare the lines from an unknown (primary data from a spectroscope, or secondary data such as a published spectrum) with reference spectra of known elements; a match of all lines identifies it.
- [ ] L4 Sodium: two very close bright yellow lines at about 589 nm (the "sodium D lines"), which give the yellow flame.
- [ ] L5 Strontium: several lines, strongest in the red; copper: lines mainly in the green, giving the blue-green flame.
- [ ] L6 Line spectra are used to identify elements in samples and in stars; fireworks use these metal salts for their colours (e.g. strontium for red, barium for green, sodium for yellow, copper for blue-green).

### 2. Checked facts and examples

| Item | Fact |
|---|---|
| C1–C6 | Li crimson; Na yellow-orange; K lilac; Cu blue-green; Ba pale/apple green; Sr red (scarlet). |
| L4 | Sodium D lines at 589.0 and 589.6 nm. |
| L6 | Firework colours: Sr red, Ba green, Na yellow, Cu blue-green. |

No other wavelengths.

### 3. Traps

- Only the six metals named in the specification; don't add calcium or others.
- Use a non-luminous flame (air hole open); a yellow luminous flame hides colours.
- Don't introduce Em − En = hf, ground and excited states (Higher Level, in "Energy levels and the hydrogen emission spectrum").
- Atomic absorption spectroscopy is not on the specification.

### 4. What exams ask (topics only)

- Describe how to carry out a flame test; how to clean the wire.
- State the flame colours of named metals.
- Explain why elements give characteristic colours; why cobalt-blue glass is used.

### 5. Practice questions to write

1. 12 marks: "Describe how you would carry out a flame test to identify the metal in an unknown salt, and state the colour you would expect if the salt contained (i) lithium, (ii) potassium, (iii) barium." Model answer: F3–F6, C1, C3, C5.
2. 6 marks: "Explain why different metals give different colours in a flame and how a line emission spectrum can be used to distinguish lithium from strontium." Model answer: W1–W3, L2, L3, C7.

## Part: Electronic structure (formerly `chem3-s4`)

- **loId:** `chem3-s4` · **chapterId:** `chem3` · **syllabusRef:** 1.2.5
- **Syllabus:** 1.2.5 "describe the electronic structure of elements and associated ions,
  identifying stable electronic configurations". Learn about: "arrangements of electrons (in
  main energy levels, **in sublevels and in orbitals**) for the first 20 (**36**) elements and
  their ions (excluding transition metal ions), **including s, p, d sublevels, and shapes of s,
  p orbitals**". Bold = Higher Level only.
- **Prepared by:** a higher model, 2026-10-30. Two parts: an Ordinary-level part (main energy
  levels for elements 1–20 and their ions, stable configurations), then Higher Level blocks
  (sublevels, filling rules, s p d notation to element 36, orbital diagrams, shapes, exceptions,
  ions).

### 1. Must cover

Arrangements in main energy levels (one block; Ordinary and Higher)
- [ ] M1 Electrons fill the lowest available energy levels first; for elements 1–20 the levels hold up to 2, 8, 8 (then the fourth level starts at potassium).
- [ ] M2 Examples: H 1; He 2; C 2,4; O 2,6; Na 2,8,1; Mg 2,8,2; Cl 2,8,7; Ar 2,8,8; K 2,8,8,1; Ca 2,8,8,2.
- [ ] M3 Diagram: a Bohr-style shell diagram for sodium (nucleus labelled 11p, 12n; shells with 2, 8, 1 electrons).
- [ ] M4 The outer-shell (valence) electrons decide chemical properties; elements in the same group have the same number of outer electrons.

Ions and stable configurations (one block; Ordinary and Higher)
- [ ] I1 A stable electronic configuration is a full outer energy level, as in the noble gases (He 2; Ne 2,8; Ar 2,8,8); usually eight outer electrons (an octet), or two for the first level.
- [ ] I2 Metals in groups 1, 2 and 13 lose electrons to form positive ions with a noble-gas configuration: Na⁺ 2,8; Mg²⁺ 2,8; Al³⁺ 2,8; K⁺ 2,8,8; Ca²⁺ 2,8,8.
- [ ] I3 Non-metals in groups 15, 16 and 17 gain electrons to form negative ions with a noble-gas configuration: N³⁻ 2,8; O²⁻ 2,8; F⁻ 2,8; S²⁻ 2,8,8; Cl⁻ 2,8,8.
- [ ] I4 Ions with the same electron arrangement as each other (e.g. Na⁺, Mg²⁺, F⁻, O²⁻ and Ne all 2,8) are isoelectronic.

Sublevels and orbitals (Higher Level only; one block)
- [ ] S1 [HL] A sublevel is a subdivision of a main energy level, made of orbitals of equal energy: s, p, d (and f).
- [ ] S2 [HL] An s sublevel has 1 orbital (holds 2 electrons); p has 3 orbitals (6 electrons); d has 5 orbitals (10 electrons).
- [ ] S3 [HL] Level 1 has only 1s; level 2 has 2s and 2p; level 3 has 3s, 3p and 3d; level 4 has 4s, 4p, 4d and 4f.
- [ ] S4 [HL] Order of filling (increasing energy): 1s, 2s, 2p, 3s, 3p, 4s, 3d, 4p. The 4s sublevel is lower in energy than 3d, so it fills first.

Filling rules (Higher Level only; one block)
- [ ] R1 [HL] Aufbau principle: electrons occupy the lowest-energy orbitals available first.
- [ ] R2 [HL] Pauli exclusion principle: an orbital holds at most two electrons, and they must have opposite spins (shown as ↑↓).
- [ ] R3 [HL] Hund's rule: when filling orbitals of equal energy (e.g. the three 2p orbitals), electrons occupy them singly with parallel spins before any pairing occurs.
- [ ] R4 [HL] Orbital (box) diagrams: one box per orbital, arrows for electrons; e.g. nitrogen 1s ↑↓, 2s ↑↓, 2p ↑ ↑ ↑ (three unpaired electrons); oxygen 2p ↑↓ ↑ ↑.

Writing configurations to element 36 (Higher Level only; one block)
- [ ] W1 [HL] Notation: e.g. sodium 1s² 2s² 2p⁶ 3s¹; chlorine 1s² 2s² 2p⁶ 3s² 3p⁵; calcium 1s² 2s² 2p⁶ 3s² 3p⁶ 4s².
- [ ] W2 [HL] Beyond calcium the 3d sublevel fills: e.g. iron 1s² 2s² 2p⁶ 3s² 3p⁶ 3d⁶ 4s² (written in level order, though 4s fills before 3d); bromine 1s² 2s² 2p⁶ 3s² 3p⁶ 3d¹⁰ 4s² 4p⁵; krypton ends 3d¹⁰ 4s² 4p⁶.
- [ ] W3 [HL] Noble-gas shorthand: e.g. potassium [Ar] 4s¹; iron [Ar] 3d⁶ 4s².
- [ ] W4 [HL] Two exceptions: chromium is 1s² 2s² 2p⁶ 3s² 3p⁶ 3d⁵ 4s¹ and copper is 1s² 2s² 2p⁶ 3s² 3p⁶ 3d¹⁰ 4s¹, because a half-full or full d sublevel is especially stable.
- [ ] W5 [HL] Ions (excluding transition metal ions): remove or add electrons from the outermost sublevel, e.g. Na⁺ 1s² 2s² 2p⁶; Cl⁻ 1s² 2s² 2p⁶ 3s² 3p⁶; Br⁻ ends 4s² 4p⁶; Ca²⁺ 1s² 2s² 2p⁶ 3s² 3p⁶.
- [ ] W6 [HL] Stable configurations at this level: full sublevels (s², p⁶) and the noble-gas configuration ns² np⁶.

Shapes of s and p orbitals (Higher Level only; one block)
- [ ] H1 [HL] An s orbital is spherical, centred on the nucleus; the 2s orbital is larger than the 1s.
- [ ] H2 [HL] A p orbital is dumbbell-shaped (two lobes either side of the nucleus).
- [ ] H3 [HL] The three p orbitals in a sublevel, px, py and pz, are at right angles to each other along the x, y and z axes, and have equal energy.
- [ ] H4 [HL] Diagram: a sphere labelled s; three dumbbells on x, y and z axes labelled px, py and pz.

### 2. Checked facts and examples

All configurations above are standard; the chromium and copper exceptions are correct as given. Element 36 is krypton.

### 3. Traps

- Transition metal ions (e.g. Fe²⁺, Cu²⁺) are excluded by the specification: don't write their configurations.
- Ordinary level uses main energy levels only, for elements 1–20; keep s, p, d notation in the Higher Level blocks.
- 4s fills before 3d; don't write the potassium configuration as 2,8,9.
- Don't introduce f-orbital shapes or d-orbital shapes.

### 4. What exams ask (topics only)

- Write the s, p, d configuration of an element (often a first-row d-block element such as vanadium, chromium or iron) or of an ion.
- How many unpaired electrons / how many p orbitals are occupied in a given atom.
- State Hund's rule, the Pauli principle; draw the shapes of s and p orbitals.
- Name an ion isoelectronic with a given atom.

### 5. Practice questions to write

1. 6 marks: "Give the arrangement of electrons in main energy levels for (i) a magnesium atom, (ii) a magnesium ion, (iii) a sulfide ion, and state what is stable about the ions' arrangements." Model answer: 2,8,2; Mg²⁺ 2,8; S²⁻ 2,8,8; I1.
2. 9 marks (Higher Level): "Write the s, p, d electron configuration of (i) a chromium atom, (ii) a bromide ion, and state Hund's rule." Model answer: W4, W5, R3.
3. 6 marks (Higher Level): "Draw the shapes of an s orbital and a 2px orbital, and state how many unpaired electrons there are in an atom of nitrogen in its ground state." Model answer: H1, H2, R4 (three).
