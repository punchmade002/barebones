## Part: Using the kinetic theory (formerly `chem9-s3`)

- **loId:** `chem9-s3` · **chapterId:** `chem9` · **syllabusRef:** 1.1.2
- **Syllabus:** 1.1.2 "analyse the kinetic theory of matter to: explain the nature and
  behaviour of matter at the particulate level; model how matter changes state". Learn about:
  "changes of state: solid ↔ liquid; solid ↔ gas; liquid ↔ gas (analysis is qualitative **and
  should include assumptions and limitations of the model**)". Bold = Higher Level only.
- **Prepared by:** a higher model, 2026-10-30. Particle explanations of properties of the
  three states; each change of state; a heating curve; then an [HL] block of assumptions and
  limitations. The ideal gas model is in "Gas laws and the ideal gas equation".

### 1. Must cover

Explaining properties of the states (one block)
- [ ] P1 Solids have a fixed shape and volume and can't be compressed: the particles are held tightly in fixed positions by strong forces and only vibrate.
- [ ] P2 Liquids have a fixed volume but take the shape of their container and flow: the particles are close together but can slide past one another.
- [ ] P3 Gases fill their container and are easily compressed: the particles are far apart, with weak forces between them, and move rapidly and randomly.
- [ ] P4 Gas pressure is caused by gas particles colliding with the walls of the container.

Changes of state (one block; one bullet per change, with the particle explanation)
- [ ] C1 Melting (solid → liquid): heating makes the particles vibrate more until they have enough energy to overcome some of the forces holding them in place and begin to move past one another; at the melting point.
- [ ] C2 Freezing (liquid → solid): cooling removes energy, the particles slow down and the forces pull them back into fixed positions.
- [ ] C3 Evaporation (liquid → gas, below the boiling point): the fastest-moving particles at the surface escape; the remaining liquid cools.
- [ ] C4 Boiling (liquid → gas at the boiling point): particles throughout the liquid have enough energy to overcome the forces between them; bubbles of vapour form within the liquid.
- [ ] C5 Condensation (gas → liquid): cooling slows the particles so the attractive forces pull them close together.
- [ ] C6 Sublimation (solid → gas directly, without melting): e.g. iodine and solid carbon dioxide ("dry ice"); the reverse (gas → solid) is deposition, e.g. iodine vapour forming crystals on a cold surface.
- [ ] C7 Diagram: a triangle of the three states with arrows labelled melting, freezing, evaporation/boiling, condensation, sublimation and deposition.

Heating curve (one block)
- [ ] H1 When a pure solid is heated at a steady rate, its temperature rises until it reaches the melting point, then stays constant until it has all melted.
- [ ] H2 During a change of state the energy supplied is used to overcome the forces between particles, not to increase their kinetic energy, so the temperature doesn't rise.
- [ ] H3 The temperature then rises again until the boiling point, where it stays constant until all the liquid has boiled.
- [ ] H4 The boiling plateau is longer than the melting plateau because far more energy is needed to separate the particles completely than to loosen them.
- [ ] H5 Diagram: a heating curve for water (temperature against time) from ice below 0 °C to steam above 100 °C, with the two flat sections labelled melting at 0 °C and boiling at 100 °C.

Assumptions and limitations of the model (Higher Level only; one block)
- [ ] A1 [HL] Assumptions: particles are tiny spheres in constant random motion; the higher the temperature, the greater their average kinetic energy; collisions are perfectly elastic (no loss of kinetic energy); in a gas the particles' own volume is negligible and there are no forces between them except during collisions.
- [ ] A2 [HL] Limitation: real particles are not hard spheres and they do attract one another; the forces between particles (intermolecular forces) are what make liquids and solids exist and decide melting and boiling points, which the simple gas model ignores.
- [ ] A3 [HL] Limitation: the model treats all particles as identical and doesn't explain why different substances have different melting and boiling points; that needs the types of bonding and intermolecular forces (see "Polarity, shape and physical properties").
- [ ] A4 [HL] Limitation: it doesn't describe the structure inside particles or what happens to bonds; it is qualitative and gives no values.
- [ ] A5 [HL] Limitation: in real gases at high pressure or low temperature the particles' volume and attractions matter, so gases deviate from the model (see "Gas laws and the ideal gas equation").

### 3. Traps

- Boiling and evaporation are different: evaporation is from the surface at any temperature.
- During a change of state the temperature is constant even though heat is being supplied.
- Keep assumptions and limitations in the Higher Level block.

### 4. What exams ask (topics only)

- Explain changes of state in terms of particles.
- Interpret a heating curve; explain the flat sections.
- Give the assumptions of the kinetic theory (Higher Level).

2. 6 marks (Higher Level): "State two assumptions of the kinetic theory and one limitation of the model." Model answer: two of A1; one of A2–A5.

## Part: Separation techniques (formerly `chem9-s4`)

- **loId:** `chem9-s4` · **chapterId:** `chem9` · **syllabusRef:** 1.1.3
- **Syllabus:** 1.1.3 "justify the use of different separation techniques for isolating one or
  more components from a mixture". Learn about: "how separation techniques can be applied to
  homogeneous and heterogeneous mixtures within the appropriate experimental activities".
  Normal type. The specification names no particular techniques.
- **Prepared by:** a higher model, 2026-10-30. One block per standard technique: the property
  it uses, the method in steps, an example, and a diagram cue; then a block on choosing and
  justifying a technique. The recrystallisation and melting-point test of benzoic acid and the
  separating-funnel step for an ester belong to the organic chapters; mention once as examples.

### 1. Must cover

Choosing a technique (one block)
- [ ] J1 Choose the technique from the physical property in which the components differ: particle size and solubility (filtration), solubility and volatility (evaporation, crystallisation), boiling point (distillation), solubility in a solvent and attraction to paper (chromatography), immiscibility and density (separating funnel).
- [ ] J2 Heterogeneous mixtures (an insoluble solid in a liquid, two immiscible liquids) are separated by filtration, decanting or a separating funnel.
- [ ] J3 Homogeneous mixtures (solutions, miscible liquids) need evaporation, crystallisation, distillation or chromatography.

Filtration (one block)
- [ ] F1 Separates an insoluble solid from a liquid, e.g. sand from salt solution.
- [ ] F2 Steps: fold filter paper into a cone in a funnel; pour the mixture through; the solid (residue) stays on the paper and the liquid (filtrate) passes through.
- [ ] F3 Diagram: filter funnel with filter paper over a conical flask, labelled residue and filtrate.

Evaporation and crystallisation (one block)
- [ ] E1 Evaporation recovers a dissolved solid by heating the solution until all the solvent has gone, e.g. salt from salt water; used when the solid doesn't decompose on heating.
- [ ] E2 Crystallisation: heat the solution to evaporate some solvent until it is saturated (a drop on a cold glass rod forms crystals), then leave it to cool slowly; crystals form and are filtered off, washed with a little cold solvent and dried, e.g. copper(II) sulfate crystals.
- [ ] E3 Crystallisation gives purer, larger crystals than evaporating to dryness, because soluble impurities stay in the solution.

Distillation (one block)
- [ ] D1 Simple distillation separates a solvent from a solution, or two liquids with very different boiling points, e.g. pure water from salt water.
- [ ] D2 Steps: heat the mixture in a flask with anti-bumping granules; the liquid with the lower boiling point boils, its vapour passes into a water-cooled condenser (water in at the bottom, out at the top) and condenses; the distillate is collected; a thermometer at the side arm reads the boiling point of what is distilling.
- [ ] D3 Fractional distillation separates miscible liquids with close boiling points, using a fractionating column, e.g. ethanol (bp 78 °C) from water (bp 100 °C); crude oil is separated into fractions this way.
- [ ] D4 Safety: flammable liquids are heated with a heating mantle or water bath, not a naked flame; never heat a closed system.
- [ ] D5 Diagram: a distillation set-up: round-bottomed flask with granules, thermometer bulb at the side arm, Liebig condenser with water flow, receiving flask.

Paper chromatography (one block)
- [ ] C1 Separates and identifies small amounts of soluble substances, e.g. the dyes in an ink or food colouring, or a mixture of indicators.
- [ ] C2 Steps: draw a pencil line near the bottom of chromatography paper; put small spots of the mixture and of known substances on it; stand the paper in a little solvent (below the line) in a covered container; let the solvent rise; remove and mark the solvent front.
- [ ] C3 Principle: components that are more soluble in the solvent (mobile phase) and less attracted to the paper (stationary phase) travel further.
- [ ] C4 Retention factor Rf = distance moved by the spot ÷ distance moved by the solvent; matching spots and Rf values identify components.
- [ ] C5 Diagram: a chromatogram with the pencil start line, spots, solvent front and separated spots.

Separating funnel (one block)
- [ ] S1 Separates two immiscible liquids of different density, e.g. oil and water, or an organic layer from an aqueous layer (used to purify an ester).
- [ ] S2 Steps: pour the mixture into the funnel, let the layers settle, remove the stopper and run off the lower (denser) layer through the tap, then collect the upper layer separately.
- [ ] S3 Diagram: a separating funnel with two layers, stopper and tap.

### 3. Traps

- Use pencil, not ink, for the chromatography start line (ink would run).
- The solvent level must be below the spots.
- Instrumental chromatography (GC, HPLC, TLC as analytical instruments) is not on the specification; keep to paper chromatography.
- Steam distillation is not required.

### 4. What exams ask (topics only)

- Choose and justify a technique for a given mixture.
- Describe how to separate a mixture of indicators or dyes by chromatography.
- Describe distillation; why water enters the condenser at the bottom.

### 5. Practice questions to write

2. 9 marks: "Describe how paper chromatography can be used to separate the dyes in a food colouring and identify one of them, and state the principle on which it works." Model answer: C2–C4.
