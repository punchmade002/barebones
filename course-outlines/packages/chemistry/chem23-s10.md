## 1. Must cover

Allotropy (one block)
- [ ] A1 Allotropes are different physical forms of the same element in the same state, with the atoms bonded together in different ways.
- [ ] A2 Carbon's allotropes are diamond, graphite, graphene and the fullerenes.
- [ ] A3 Because they are all pure carbon, they have the same chemical properties: each burns in excess oxygen to give only carbon dioxide, C(s) + O₂(g) → CO₂(g); this is evidence that diamond and graphite are the same element.
- [ ] A4 Their physical properties differ because their structures differ.
- [ ] A5 Diamond, graphite and graphene are giant covalent (macromolecular) structures, in which every atom is joined to its neighbours by covalent bonds throughout; fullerenes are separate molecules (simple molecular).

Diamond (one block)
- [ ] D1 Structure: each carbon atom is covalently bonded to four other carbon atoms arranged tetrahedrally (109.5°), forming a rigid three-dimensional giant covalent network.
- [ ] D2 It is the hardest natural substance, because a great many strong covalent bonds in all directions must be broken to scratch or deform it.
- [ ] D3 Very high melting point (above 3500 °C), because strong covalent bonds throughout the structure must be broken.
- [ ] D4 Does not conduct electricity: all four outer electrons of each carbon are used in bonds, so there are no free electrons or ions.
- [ ] D5 Conducts heat very well (better than copper), because the rigid, strongly bonded lattice passes on vibrations efficiently.
- [ ] D6 Transparent, colourless and sparkling (it bends and reflects light strongly); insoluble in water and all solvents; density 3.51 g cm⁻³ (the atoms are closely packed).
- [ ] D7 Diagram: a section of the diamond lattice showing one carbon bonded tetrahedrally to four others.

Graphite (one block)
- [ ] G1 Structure: each carbon atom is covalently bonded to three others at 120° in flat layers of hexagonal rings; the layers are stacked on top of one another.
- [ ] G2 Each carbon uses three of its four outer electrons in bonds; the fourth is delocalised, free to move along the layer.
- [ ] G3 The layers are held together only by weak London dispersion forces, and are relatively far apart (about 0.335 nm, compared with 0.142 nm between carbons within a layer).
- [ ] G4 Soft and slippery, and marks paper: the layers slide over each other easily because only weak forces hold them together.
- [ ] G5 Conducts electricity (along the layers), because the delocalised electrons can move and carry charge.
- [ ] G6 Very high melting point, because the strong covalent bonds within the layers must be broken.
- [ ] G7 Grey-black, shiny, opaque solid; insoluble in water; less dense than diamond (about 2.2 g cm⁻³) because of the space between the layers.
- [ ] G8 Diagram: two or three stacked layers of hexagons, labelled strong covalent bonds within layers, weak forces between layers and delocalised electrons.

Graphene (one block)
- [ ] N1 Structure: a single layer of graphite, one carbon atom thick: a flat (two-dimensional) sheet of hexagonal rings, each carbon bonded to three others, with one delocalised electron per carbon.
- [ ] N2 It was first isolated in 2004 by Andre Geim and Konstantin Novoselov at the University of Manchester, who peeled layers off graphite with sticky tape; they won the Nobel Prize in Physics in 2010.
- [ ] N3 Extremely strong for its thickness (one of the strongest materials known), because of the strong covalent bonds across the sheet, yet very light and flexible.
- [ ] N4 Conducts electricity extremely well, because the delocalised electrons move freely across the sheet; it is also an excellent conductor of heat.
- [ ] N5 Almost transparent (a single sheet absorbs only about 2% of the light passing through it) and impermeable to gases.
- [ ] N6 Diagram: a flat honeycomb sheet of hexagons one atom thick.

Fullerenes (one block)
- [ ] F1 Fullerenes are molecules of carbon atoms joined into closed hollow cages (or tubes) made of hexagonal and pentagonal rings, each carbon bonded to three others.
- [ ] F2 Buckminsterfullerene, C₆₀, has 60 carbon atoms arranged in 20 hexagons and 12 pentagons, the shape of a football ("buckyball"); it was discovered in 1985 by Harry Kroto, Robert Curl and Richard Smalley (Nobel Prize in Chemistry, 1996) and named after the architect Buckminster Fuller, whose domes it resembles.
- [ ] F3 C₆₀ is a simple molecular substance: covalent bonds hold the atoms within each ball, but only weak London dispersion forces act between the balls.
- [ ] F4 So, unlike the giant structures, C₆₀ is soft, has a relatively low sublimation temperature, and dissolves in non-polar solvents (e.g. methylbenzene, giving a purple solution), though not in water.
- [ ] F5 C₆₀ is a poor conductor of electricity: the delocalised electrons can move within a molecule but not from one molecule to the next.
- [ ] F6 Carbon nanotubes are cylindrical fullerenes, like a sheet of graphene rolled into a tube about a nanometre across; they are very strong and conduct electricity along the tube.
- [ ] F7 Diagram: a C₆₀ ball showing hexagons and pentagons; a short section of a nanotube.

Comparing the allotropes (one block)
- [ ] C1 Number of bonds per carbon: diamond 4; graphite, graphene and fullerenes 3 (with one delocalised electron).
- [ ] C2 Structure type: diamond 3D giant covalent; graphite giant covalent layers; graphene a single giant covalent layer (2D); fullerenes discrete molecules (nanotubes are very long tubes).
- [ ] C3 Hardness: diamond hardest; graphite soft and slippery; graphene strong and flexible; C₆₀ soft.
- [ ] C4 Electrical conductivity: diamond none; graphite good (along layers); graphene excellent; C₆₀ poor; nanotubes good.
- [ ] C5 Melting point: diamond, graphite and graphene very high; C₆₀ much lower (weak forces between molecules).
- [ ] C6 Solubility: diamond, graphite and graphene insoluble in all solvents; C₆₀ soluble in non-polar organic solvents.
- [ ] C7 General rule: properties depend on how many bonds each carbon forms (4 → no free electrons; 3 → delocalised electrons) and on whether the structure is a continuous giant network or separate molecules.

## 3. Traps

- Graphite's free electron is delocalised within a layer, not "between the layers".
- Graphite is soft because of weak forces between layers, not weak covalent bonds.
- Fullerenes are molecules; C₆₀ is not a giant structure, so its melting point is not very high.
- Don't call allotropes "isotopes" or "isomers".
- X-ray crystallography and crystal types (ionic, metallic, molecular crystals) as a topic are not on the specification; describe each allotrope's structure only.
- Amorphous carbon (soot, charcoal) is not one of the four named allotropes; don't add it.

## 4. What exams ask (topics only)

- Explain, in terms of bonding, the hardness of diamond, the softness and lubricating action of graphite, and the electrical conductivity of graphite.
- Name the football-shaped C₆₀ molecule; what type of bond joins its atoms.
- Compare the structures and properties of the allotropes.
