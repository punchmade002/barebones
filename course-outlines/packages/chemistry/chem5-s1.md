## 1. Must cover

Why atoms bond (one block)
- [ ] W1 A chemical bond is a force of attraction that holds atoms or ions together in an element or compound.
- [ ] W2 Atoms bond to reach a more stable arrangement of electrons, usually a full outer energy level like the nearest noble gas (the octet rule), and bonding lowers their energy.
- [ ] W3 Only the outer (valence) electrons take part in bonding.

Ionic bonding (one block)
- [ ] I1 An ionic bond is the electrostatic attraction between oppositely charged ions.
- [ ] I2 It forms when electrons are transferred from one atom (usually a metal, which forms a positive ion) to another (usually a non-metal, which forms a negative ion).
- [ ] I3 Example: sodium (2,8,1) gives one electron to chlorine (2,8,7): Na → Na⁺ + e⁻ and Cl + e⁻ → Cl⁻; both ions have noble-gas arrangements.
- [ ] I4 Ionic compounds form a giant lattice of ions, not molecules; in sodium chloride each Na⁺ ion is surrounded by six Cl⁻ ions and each Cl⁻ by six Na⁺; the formula NaCl gives the ratio of ions.
- [ ] I5 Diagram: a section of the sodium chloride lattice showing alternating Na⁺ and Cl⁻ ions in a cube.

Covalent bonding (one block)
- [ ] C1 A covalent bond is a shared pair of electrons between two atoms, usually two non-metals; both nuclei are attracted to the shared pair.
- [ ] C2 Single bond: one shared pair (H–H, Cl–Cl); double bond: two shared pairs (O=O, C=O in CO₂); triple bond: three shared pairs (N≡N).
- [ ] C3 Covalently bonded atoms form molecules (e.g. H₂O, CH₄, NH₃) or, in a few substances, giant covalent structures (e.g. diamond).

Pure and polar covalent bonds (one block)
- [ ] P1 Pure (non-polar) covalent bond: the electrons are shared equally, because both atoms attract them equally, e.g. H–H, Cl–Cl, and C–H is nearly non-polar.
- [ ] P2 Polar covalent bond: the electrons are shared unequally, because one atom attracts them more strongly, e.g. H–Cl, O–H.
- [ ] P3 The atom that attracts the electrons more gets a partial negative charge (δ−) and the other a partial positive charge (δ+), e.g. Hδ+–Clδ−.

The bonding continuum (one block)
- [ ] B1 Bonds are not simply "ionic" or "covalent"; they lie on a continuum from pure covalent (equal sharing), through polar covalent (unequal sharing), to ionic (complete transfer).
- [ ] B2 Examples along the continuum: Cl–Cl (pure covalent) → H–Cl (polar covalent) → Na⁺Cl⁻ (ionic).
- [ ] B3 Most bonds have some ionic and some covalent character; the difference in electronegativity between the atoms decides where a bond lies (see "Electronegativity and bond type").
- [ ] B4 Diagram: a horizontal line labelled pure covalent – polar covalent – ionic, with the electron pair drawn shared equally, shifted towards one atom, and fully transferred.

Comparing the types (one block)
- [ ] K1 Ionic: transfer of electrons; ions; metal with non-metal; giant lattice.
- [ ] K2 Covalent: sharing of electrons; molecules (mostly); non-metal with non-metal; polar or non-polar depending on the atoms.
- [ ] K3 (assumed) Metallic bonding, in metals: positive metal ions held together by a "sea" of delocalised outer electrons; it explains why metals conduct electricity. (The specification names only the ionic–covalent continuum; keep this to one bullet.)

## 2. Checked facts and examples

Only the examples above. In NaCl each ion has six nearest neighbours of opposite charge.

## 3. Traps

- An ionic bond is the attraction between ions, not the transfer itself.
- Don't teach electronegativity values or the cut-off differences here (they belong to "Electronegativity and bond type").
- Don't teach dative (coordinate) bonding (not on the specification).
- Ionic compounds have no molecules; don't write "a molecule of sodium chloride".

## 4. What exams ask (topics only)

- Define ionic bond and covalent bond; distinguish polar and non-polar covalent bonds.
- Explain how an ionic compound forms, with an example.
- Describe the bonding continuum.

## 5. Practice questions to write

2. 6 marks: "Explain what is meant by saying that chemical bonds lie on a continuum." Model answer: B1–B3.
