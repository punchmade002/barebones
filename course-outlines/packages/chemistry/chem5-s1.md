## Part: Types of chemical bonding (formerly `chem5-s1`)

- **loId:** `chem5-s1` · **chapterId:** `chem5` · **syllabusRef:** 2.1.1
- **Syllabus:** 2.1.1 "describe and compare different types of chemical bonding". Learn about:
  "the nature of chemical bonds that lies on a continuum from ionic through polar covalent to
  pure covalent bonds". Normal type: Ordinary and Higher level.
- **Prepared by:** a higher model, 2026-10-30. Describe ionic, polar covalent and pure covalent
  bonding and the continuum. How electronegativity decides where a bond lies is in
  "Bond type and intermolecular forces"; properties of ionic and covalent compounds are in
  "Bond type and intermolecular forces"; Lewis diagrams and predicting formulae are in "Modelling bonding and
  predicting formulae". Name them; don't teach them here.

### 1. Must cover

Why atoms bond (one block)
- [ ] W1 A chemical bond is a force of attraction that holds atoms or ions together in an element or compound.
- [ ] W2 Atoms bond to reach a more stable arrangement of electrons, usually a full outer energy level like the nearest noble gas (the octet rule), and bonding lowers their energy.
- [ ] W3 Only the outer (valence) electrons take part in bonding.

Ionic bonding (one block)
- [ ] I1 An ionic bond is the electrostatic attraction between oppositely charged ions.
- [ ] I2 It forms when electrons are transferred from one atom (usually a metal, which forms a positive ion) to another (usually a non-metal, which forms a negative ion).
- [ ] I3 Example: sodium (2,8,1) gives one electron to chlorine (2,8,7): Na → Na⁺ + e⁻ and Cl + e⁻ → Cl⁻; both ions have noble-gas arrangements.
- [ ] I4 Ionic compounds form a giant lattice of ions, not molecules; in sodium chloride each Na⁺ ion is surrounded by six Cl⁻ ions and each Cl⁻ by six Na⁺; the formula NaCl gives the ratio of ions.
- [ ] I5 Diagram: a section of the sodium chloride lattice showing alternating Na⁺ and Cl⁻ ions in a cube.

Covalent bonding (one block)
- [ ] C1 A covalent bond is a shared pair of electrons between two atoms, usually two non-metals; both nuclei are attracted to the shared pair.
- [ ] C2 Single bond: one shared pair (H–H, Cl–Cl); double bond: two shared pairs (O=O, C=O in CO₂); triple bond: three shared pairs (N≡N).
- [ ] C3 Covalently bonded atoms form molecules (e.g. H₂O, CH₄, NH₃) or, in a few substances, giant covalent structures (e.g. diamond).

Pure and polar covalent bonds (one block)
- [ ] P1 Pure (non-polar) covalent bond: the electrons are shared equally, because both atoms attract them equally, e.g. H–H, Cl–Cl, and C–H is nearly non-polar.
- [ ] P2 Polar covalent bond: the electrons are shared unequally, because one atom attracts them more strongly, e.g. H–Cl, O–H.
- [ ] P3 The atom that attracts the electrons more gets a partial negative charge (δ−) and the other a partial positive charge (δ+), e.g. Hδ+–Clδ−.

The bonding continuum (one block)
- [ ] B1 Bonds are not simply "ionic" or "covalent"; they lie on a continuum from pure covalent (equal sharing), through polar covalent (unequal sharing), to ionic (complete transfer).
- [ ] B2 Examples along the continuum: Cl–Cl (pure covalent) → H–Cl (polar covalent) → Na⁺Cl⁻ (ionic).
- [ ] B3 Most bonds have some ionic and some covalent character; the difference in electronegativity between the atoms decides where a bond lies (see "Bond type and intermolecular forces").
- [ ] B4 Diagram: a horizontal line labelled pure covalent – polar covalent – ionic, with the electron pair drawn shared equally, shifted towards one atom, and fully transferred.

Comparing the types (one block)
- [ ] K1 Ionic: transfer of electrons; ions; metal with non-metal; giant lattice.
- [ ] K2 Covalent: sharing of electrons; molecules (mostly); non-metal with non-metal; polar or non-polar depending on the atoms.
- [ ] K3 (assumed) Metallic bonding, in metals: positive metal ions held together by a "sea" of delocalised outer electrons; it explains why metals conduct electricity. (The specification names only the ionic–covalent continuum; keep this to one bullet.)

### 2. Checked facts and examples

Only the examples above. In NaCl each ion has six nearest neighbours of opposite charge.

### 3. Traps

- An ionic bond is the attraction between ions, not the transfer itself.
- Don't teach electronegativity values or the cut-off differences here (they belong to "Bond type and intermolecular forces").
- Don't teach dative (coordinate) bonding (not on the specification).
- Ionic compounds have no molecules; don't write "a molecule of sodium chloride".

### 4. What exams ask (topics only)

- Define ionic bond and covalent bond; distinguish polar and non-polar covalent bonds.
- Explain how an ionic compound forms, with an example.
- Describe the bonding continuum.

### 5. Practice questions to write

1. 9 marks: "Distinguish between an ionic bond, a polar covalent bond and a pure covalent bond, giving one example of each." Model answer: I1–I2, P1–P3, with NaCl, HCl, Cl₂.
2. 6 marks: "Explain what is meant by saying that chemical bonds lie on a continuum." Model answer: B1–B3.

## Part: Modelling bonding and predicting formulae (formerly `chem5-s2`)

- **loId:** `chem5-s2` · **chapterId:** `chem5` · **syllabusRef:** 2.1.3
- **Syllabus:** 2.1.3 "model different types of bonding to predict chemical formulae **and
  outline the limitations in predicting bonding between atoms**". Learn about: "using Lewis
  diagrams to represent bonds and how bonding can be described in terms of orbital overlap,
  **including the orbital overlap in sigma and pi bonds, and delocalised bonding**"; "predicting
  how atoms bond involving the use of valence and the application of the octet rule (limited to
  the first 20 elements) **(36)**". Bold = Higher Level only.
- **Prepared by:** a higher model, 2026-10-30. Ordinary part: valency, octet rule, formulae,
  Lewis diagrams, simple idea of orbital overlap. Higher Level blocks: sigma and pi bonds,
  delocalised bonding, extension to element 36, limitations. Shapes are in "VSEPR theory".

### 1. Must cover

Valency and the octet rule (one block)
- [ ] V1 Valency: the number of electrons an atom loses, gains or shares when it bonds (the combining power of an element).
- [ ] V2 The octet rule: atoms tend to lose, gain or share electrons to reach eight electrons in their outer energy level (two for hydrogen, which matches helium).
- [ ] V3 Valencies from the group: group 1 = 1, group 2 = 2, group 13 = 3, group 14 = 4, group 15 = 3, group 16 = 2, group 17 = 1.

Predicting formulae (one block, skills)
- [ ] F1 Ionic: write the charge on each ion and combine them so the total charge is zero, e.g. Ca²⁺ and Cl⁻ give CaCl₂; Al³⁺ and O²⁻ give Al₂O₃; Mg²⁺ and N³⁻ give Mg₃N₂.
- [ ] F2 (assumed) Common compound ions and their charges: hydroxide OH⁻, nitrate NO₃⁻, hydrogencarbonate HCO₃⁻, carbonate CO₃²⁻, sulfate SO₄²⁻, sulfite SO₃²⁻, phosphate PO₄³⁻, ammonium NH₄⁺; brackets are used when more than one is needed, e.g. Ca(OH)₂, (NH₄)₂SO₄.
- [ ] F3 Covalent: atoms share enough electron pairs to complete each octet, e.g. hydrogen (valency 1) and oxygen (valency 2) form H₂O; nitrogen (3) and hydrogen form NH₃; carbon (4) and hydrogen form CH₄; carbon and oxygen form CO₂ with two double bonds.

Lewis (dot-and-cross) diagrams (one block)
- [ ] L1 A Lewis diagram shows the outer electrons of each atom as dots or crosses; shared pairs sit between the atoms; lone pairs are pairs not used in bonding.
- [ ] L2 Ionic: show the ions in square brackets with their charges, e.g. [Na]⁺ [Cl]⁻ with eight electrons around chlorine; MgO as [Mg]²⁺ [O]²⁻.
- [ ] L3 Covalent examples to draw: H₂, Cl₂, HCl, H₂O (two lone pairs on O), NH₃ (one lone pair on N), CH₄, O₂ (double bond), N₂ (triple bond), CO₂ (two double bonds).
- [ ] L4 Diagram: dot-and-cross diagrams of sodium chloride, water and nitrogen.

Orbital overlap (one block)
- [ ] O1 A covalent bond forms when an orbital of one atom, holding one electron, overlaps an orbital of another atom holding one electron; the shared pair occupies the overlap region between the nuclei.

Sigma and pi bonds (Higher Level only; one block)
- [ ] S1 [HL] A sigma (σ) bond is formed by head-on overlap of orbitals along the line joining the two nuclei, e.g. s–s (H₂), s–p (HCl), p–p end-on (Cl₂).
- [ ] S2 [HL] A pi (π) bond is formed by sideways overlap of two parallel p orbitals, above and below the line joining the nuclei.
- [ ] S3 [HL] Every single bond is a σ bond; a double bond is one σ and one π bond; a triple bond is one σ and two π bonds (N₂).
- [ ] S4 [HL] π bonds are weaker than σ bonds because sideways overlap is less effective, and they prevent rotation about a double bond.
- [ ] S5 [HL] Diagram: head-on overlap of two s orbitals and of two p orbitals (σ); sideways overlap of two p orbitals showing electron density above and below the axis (π).

Delocalised bonding (Higher Level only; one block)
- [ ] D1 [HL] In delocalised bonding, electrons are not confined between two atoms but are spread over several atoms.
- [ ] D2 [HL] In benzene (C₆H₆), each carbon uses one p orbital; the six p orbitals overlap sideways all round the ring, forming a ring of delocalised π electrons above and below the plane of the ring.
- [ ] D3 [HL] As a result all six carbon–carbon bonds in benzene are identical, in between single and double bonds in length. (Benzene's structure is used again in "Shapes in terms of sigma and pi bonds" in the hydrocarbons chapter.)

Predicting bonding to element 36 (Higher Level only; one block)
- [ ] E1 [HL] The same rules apply to elements 21–36 in the main groups: e.g. potassium bromide K⁺Br⁻; calcium bromide CaBr₂; gallium (group 13) forms Ga³⁺; arsenic (group 15) and hydrogen form AsH₃; selenium (group 16) and hydrogen form H₂Se.

Limitations in predicting bonding (Higher Level only; one block)
- [ ] X1 [HL] Some molecules are stable with fewer than eight outer electrons on the central atom, e.g. BF₃ (six around boron).
- [ ] X2 [HL] Elements in period 3 and beyond can have more than eight outer electrons, e.g. PCl₅ (ten around phosphorus) and SF₆ (twelve around sulfur).
- [ ] X3 [HL] The octet rule and Lewis diagrams don't predict the shape of a molecule, how polar a bond is, or bond strength; they can't show delocalised bonding as in benzene.
- [ ] X4 [HL] Predictions from valency assume purely ionic or purely covalent bonding, but real bonds lie on a continuum.

### 2. Checked facts and examples

All formulae above are standard.

### 3. Traps

- Transition metal ions are not required; don't predict formulae with variable-valency metals.
- Dative (coordinate) bonding is not on the specification; NH₄⁺ is given only as an ion to use in formulae.
- VSEPR shapes belong to "VSEPR theory"; don't explain shapes here.

### 4. What exams ask (topics only)

- Draw dot-and-cross diagrams for given ionic and covalent compounds.
- Predict the formula of a compound.
- Distinguish sigma and pi bonds; state the number of each in a molecule (Higher Level).
- State an exception to the octet rule (Higher Level).

### 5. Practice questions to write

1. 9 marks: "Draw dot-and-cross diagrams to show the bonding in (i) magnesium oxide, (ii) ammonia, (iii) carbon dioxide." Model answer: L2, L3.
2. 9 marks (Higher Level): "Distinguish between a sigma bond and a pi bond, and state the number of each in a nitrogen molecule." Model answer: S1–S3; one σ and two π.
3. 6 marks (Higher Level): "Explain, with two examples, the limitations of the octet rule in predicting bonding." Model answer: X1, X2 (or X3).

## Part: VSEPR theory (formerly `chem5-s4`)

- **loId:** `chem5-s4` · **chapterId:** `chem5` · **syllabusRef:** 2.2.5
- **Syllabus:** 2.2.5 "**use VSEPR theory to predict and model the shapes of molecules**".
  Learn about: "**the shapes of molecules that can be explained by the VSEPR theory (of the form
  ABn for up to four pairs of electrons around a central atom, single bonds only) and
  visualised using diagrams, 3D models and digital models**". All bold: the whole subsection is
  Higher Level only. Mark every block heading " (Higher Level only)".
- **Prepared by:** a higher model, 2026-10-30. How shape decides polarity is in "Shape,
  polarity and properties"; don't teach polarity here.

### 1. Must cover

The theory (one block)
- [ ] T1 [HL] VSEPR stands for valence shell electron pair repulsion.
- [ ] T2 [HL] Electron pairs in the outer (valence) shell of the central atom repel each other and arrange themselves as far apart as possible, which fixes the shape of the molecule.
- [ ] T3 [HL] Both bonding pairs and lone pairs count.
- [ ] T4 [HL] Lone pairs repel more strongly than bonding pairs: lone pair–lone pair > lone pair–bond pair > bond pair–bond pair. So lone pairs squeeze the bond angles smaller.
- [ ] T5 [HL] The shape is described by the positions of the atoms only, not the lone pairs.

Method (one block, skills)
- [ ] M1 [HL] Step 1: Draw the Lewis (dot-and-cross) diagram of the molecule.
- [ ] M2 [HL] Step 2: Count the electron pairs around the central atom and how many are bonding pairs and lone pairs.
- [ ] M3 [HL] Step 3: Arrange the pairs as far apart as possible and name the shape from the positions of the atoms, adjusting the angle for lone pairs.

The five shapes (one block; one bullet per shape with example and angle)
- [ ] S1 [HL] Two bonding pairs, no lone pairs: linear, 180°, e.g. BeCl₂ (gas).
- [ ] S2 [HL] Three bonding pairs, no lone pairs: trigonal planar, 120°, e.g. BF₃.
- [ ] S3 [HL] Four bonding pairs, no lone pairs: tetrahedral, 109.5°, e.g. CH₄, CCl₄, SiH₄.
- [ ] S4 [HL] Three bonding pairs and one lone pair: pyramidal (trigonal pyramidal), about 107°, e.g. NH₃. (PH₃ is also pyramidal, but its measured angle is much smaller, about 93.5°.)
- [ ] S5 [HL] Two bonding pairs and two lone pairs: V-shaped (bent), about 104.5°, e.g. H₂O. (H₂S is also V-shaped, but its measured angle is much smaller, about 92°.)
- [ ] S6 [HL] NH₃ and H₂O have four electron pairs arranged roughly tetrahedrally like CH₄, but the lone pairs push the bonds closer together, so 109.5° → 107° → 104.5° as lone pairs increase from 0 to 2.

Visualising shapes (one block)
- [ ] V1 [HL] Diagrams: draw 3D shapes with wedges (bond towards you) and dashed lines (bond away from you), and show lone pairs on the central atom.
- [ ] V2 [HL] 3D models: ball-and-stick kits show bond angles; digital models (molecular-modelling software or apps) can be rotated.
- [ ] V3 [HL] Diagram: the five shapes, each with its example, bond angle and any lone pairs labelled.

### 2. Checked facts and examples

| Item | Fact |
|---|---|
| S1–S5 | 180° (BeCl₂), 120° (BF₃), 109.5° (CH₄), 107° (NH₃), 104.5° (H₂O); measured PH₃ 93.5°, H₂S 92°. |

### 3. Traps

- Single bonds only, up to four pairs (ABn): don't include double bonds (e.g. CO₂) or five or six pairs (PCl₅, SF₆).
- NH₃ is pyramidal, not trigonal planar; H₂O is V-shaped, not linear.
- Polarity and symmetry are in "Polarity, shape and physical properties".

### 4. What exams ask (topics only)

- Use VSEPR theory to predict and explain the shapes of molecules such as CH₄, NH₃, H₂O, BF₃, BeCl₂, PH₃, H₂S.
- Explain why the bond angle in water is less than in methane.

### 5. Practice questions to write

1. 12 marks: "Use VSEPR theory to predict the shape of (i) a methane molecule, (ii) an ammonia molecule, and explain why the bond angle in ammonia is smaller than in methane." Model answer: T2, T4, S3, S4, S6.
2. 6 marks: "State the shape and bond angle of (i) BF₃, (ii) H₂S." Model answer: trigonal planar, 120°; V-shaped (bent), with two bonding pairs and two lone pairs; VSEPR predicts less than 109.5°, and the measured angle is about 92°.
