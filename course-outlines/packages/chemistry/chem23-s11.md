## 1. Must cover

Common commercial products (one block)
- [ ] P1 Diamond in jewellery, because it is transparent, sparkles and is hard enough not to scratch.
- [ ] P2 Diamond (mostly synthetic industrial diamond) on the tips of drill bits, saw blades for cutting stone and glass, and grinding and polishing tools, because it is the hardest substance.
- [ ] P3 Graphite in pencil "leads" (mixed with clay), because its layers slide off and mark the paper.
- [ ] P4 Graphite as a dry lubricant (e.g. for locks and machinery that gets hot), because its layers slide over each other and it withstands high temperatures.
- [ ] P5 Carbon nanotubes and graphene added to plastics to make strong, light composite materials for sports equipment (e.g. tennis rackets and bicycle frames).

Electrochemistry (one block)
- [ ] E1 Graphite is used for electrodes because it conducts electricity, has a very high melting point and is fairly unreactive (inert), e.g. graphite (carbon) electrodes in the electrolysis of solutions in the laboratory and industry.
- [ ] E2 In a zinc–carbon dry cell, the positive electrode is a carbon (graphite) rod.
- [ ] E3 In a lithium-ion cell, the negative electrode is usually graphite: lithium ions fit between its layers when the cell is charged and leave again when it discharges (see "The lithium-ion cell").
- [ ] E4 Graphene's very large surface area and high conductivity are being developed for faster-charging batteries and supercapacitors.

Electronics (one block)
- [ ] L1 Graphene conducts electricity extremely well and is transparent and flexible, so it is being developed for touchscreens, flexible displays, wearable sensors and very fast transistors.
- [ ] L2 Carbon nanotubes conduct electricity and are being developed for transistors and conductive films and inks.
- [ ] L3 Diamond conducts heat very well but not electricity, so it is used as a heat sink to carry heat away from powerful electronic components without short-circuiting them.
- [ ] L4 Graphite and graphene conduct heat well and are used in heat-spreading sheets in phones and laptops.

Nanotechnology (one block)
- [ ] N1 Nanotechnology is the use of materials and structures on the scale of 1–100 nanometres (1 nm = 10⁻⁹ m); fullerenes, nanotubes and graphene are nanomaterials.
- [ ] N2 Fullerene cages and nanotubes are being researched for drug delivery: a drug molecule can be held inside or attached to the outside and carried to target cells (e.g. cancer cells).
- [ ] N3 Nanotubes are among the strongest materials known for their mass, used to reinforce composites.
- [ ] N4 C₆₀ molecules are tiny, roughly spherical molecules held by weak forces, and have been researched as nano-scale lubricants ("molecular ball bearings").
- [ ] N5 Graphene-based membranes are being researched for filtering and purifying water, because a graphene sheet is strong and gases and many substances can't pass through it.

Discussion: benefits, limits and risks (one block)
- [ ] D1 Benefits: lighter, stronger materials (less fuel used in vehicles), better batteries for electric vehicles and renewable energy storage (sustainability), new medical treatments (health) and faster, flexible electronics (technology).
- [ ] D2 Limits: many graphene and nanotube uses are still at the research stage, because making large, defect-free sheets cheaply is difficult.
- [ ] D3 Risks: the health effects of nanoparticles are not fully known; some long, thin nanotubes inhaled as dust may damage the lungs in a similar way to asbestos fibres, so they need careful handling and testing.
- [ ] D4 Mining natural diamonds has environmental and social costs ("conflict diamonds" have funded wars); synthetic diamonds are an alternative for many uses.
- [ ] D5 In each case, the use follows from the structure: four bonds per carbon in a 3D network → hardness and no electrical conduction (diamond); three bonds per carbon with delocalised electrons → conduction (graphite, graphene, nanotubes); weak forces between layers or molecules → softness and lubrication (graphite, C₆₀).

## 3. Traps

- Graphite in pencils is not lead (the metal); pencil "lead" is graphite mixed with clay.
- Don't claim graphene is in widespread everyday use in phones; most graphene uses are being developed.
- Don't give amorphous carbon uses (charcoal, activated carbon, carbon black) as uses of the four allotropes.
- Graphite moderators in nuclear reactors are not on the specification (radioactivity is off the course).
- Explain each use from a property; a list of uses without reasons doesn't meet "discuss".

## 4. What exams ask (topics only)

- Give a use of diamond and of graphite, and relate each to a property.
- Why graphite is used as a lubricant and for electrodes.
- Discuss applications of graphene or fullerenes in technology or medicine.
