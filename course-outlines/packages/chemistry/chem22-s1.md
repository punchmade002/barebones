### 1. Must cover

Electrochemical cells: the terms (one block)
- [ ] T1 Electrochemistry is the study of the link between chemical reactions and electricity: redox reactions can produce electricity (galvanic cells), and electricity can drive redox reactions (electrolytic cells).
- [ ] T2 A galvanic (voltaic) cell, also called a chemical cell, converts chemical energy into electrical energy using a spontaneous redox reaction; a battery is strictly two or more cells joined together (e.g. a 12 V car battery is six 2 V cells), though the word is also used for single cells.
- [ ] T3 An electrode is a solid electrical conductor (a metal or graphite) through which electrons enter or leave the cell.
- [ ] T4 Anode: the electrode at which oxidation (loss of electrons) happens. In a galvanic cell it is the negative terminal, the source of the electrons that flow out into the external circuit.
- [ ] T5 Cathode: the electrode at which reduction (gain of electrons) happens. In a galvanic cell it is the positive terminal, where electrons from the external circuit are accepted.
- [ ] T6 Memory aid: "An Ox, Red Cat" (anode oxidation, reduction cathode), or CROA: Cathode Reduction, Oxidation Anode. This is true in every type of cell; only the signs change in electrolysis.
- [ ] T7 Electrolyte: a substance that conducts electricity by the movement of ions, as a solution, a molten compound, a paste or a gel. In a cell it carries ions between the electrodes, completing the circuit inside the cell; it does not let electrons through, so they must travel through the external circuit.
- [ ] T8 Electrons flow through the external wire from anode to cathode; inside the cell the charge is carried by ions in the electrolyte.

Primary cells (one block)
- [ ] P1 A primary cell is a galvanic cell that can be used only once: its redox reaction cannot be readily reversed, so when the reactants are used up the cell is "flat" and must be discarded (and recycled).
- [ ] P2 Zinc–carbon cell (1.5 V): a zinc case is the anode (negative), oxidised: Zn(s) → Zn²⁺(aq) + 2e⁻; a central carbon (graphite) rod surrounded by manganese(IV) oxide is the cathode (positive), where MnO₂ is reduced; the electrolyte is a moist paste of ammonium chloride (with zinc chloride).
- [ ] P3 Alkaline cell (1.5 V): powdered zinc anode and manganese(IV) oxide cathode, with potassium hydroxide (an alkali) as the electrolyte; it lasts longer and gives a steadier voltage than a zinc–carbon cell.
- [ ] P4 Lithium primary cells (about 3 V): small button or coin cells used in watches, calculators and car keys, with lithium metal as the anode.
- [ ] P5 Uses of primary cells: low-power or occasional devices that must work for a long time without attention, e.g. remote controls, clocks, smoke alarms, torches.

Secondary cells (one block)
- [ ] S1 A secondary cell is a galvanic cell that can be recharged: connecting it to an external electrical supply forces its redox reaction to run in reverse, regenerating the reactants, so it can be used many times.
- [ ] S2 During discharge it works as a galvanic cell (chemical → electrical energy); during recharging it works as an electrolytic cell (electrical → chemical energy). The electrode that is the anode on discharge becomes the cathode on charge, and vice versa.
- [ ] S3 Lead–acid cell (2 V per cell; six in a 12 V car battery): lead plates (negative) and lead(IV) oxide plates (positive) in sulfuric acid; on discharge both plates become coated with lead(II) sulfate and the acid becomes more dilute; recharging (by the car's alternator) reverses this.
- [ ] S4 Nickel–metal hydride (NiMH) cell (1.2 V): rechargeable AA and AAA cells used in place of alkaline cells.
- [ ] S5 Lithium-ion cell (about 3.6–3.7 V): in phones, laptops and electric vehicles; see "The lithium-ion cell".
- [ ] S6 Recharging is not perfect: side reactions and changes in the electrode structure slowly reduce how much charge a cell can hold, so secondary cells wear out after many cycles (hundreds to thousands).

Comparing primary and secondary cells (one block; one bullet per point of comparison)
- [ ] C1 Rechargeable: primary no; secondary yes.
- [ ] C2 Reaction: primary not readily reversible; secondary reversible by an external current.
- [ ] C3 Cost: primary cheap to buy but replaced often; secondary dearer to buy (and needs a charger) but cheaper per use over its life.
- [ ] C4 Waste and resources: primary cells produce far more waste per unit of energy delivered; secondary cells reduce waste and the mining of raw materials, which is why rechargeable batteries are a more sustainable use of energy (see "Fuel cells and sustainability").
- [ ] C5 Storage: primary cells keep their charge for years on the shelf (low self-discharge), so they suit emergency and low-drain devices; many secondary cells lose charge more quickly when stored.
- [ ] C6 Uses: primary for low-drain or occasional devices; secondary for high-drain devices used daily (phones, laptops, power tools, electric vehicles, car starter batteries).
- [ ] C7 Similarities: both are galvanic cells when supplying electricity; both have an anode (oxidation), a cathode (reduction) and an electrolyte; both should be returned for recycling, not put in household waste, because they contain metals that can pollute soil and water.

### 3. Traps

- "Secondary" does not mean a cell of a different kind of chemistry: it means rechargeable.
- In a galvanic cell the anode is negative; in an electrolytic cell it is positive. Oxidation is always at the anode.
- Electrons do not travel through the electrolyte; ions do.
- Recharging a primary cell is dangerous (it can leak or burst); don't suggest it.
- Don't give the half-equations of the lead–acid or alkaline cells; they aren't required. The lithium-ion reactions are in "The lithium-ion cell".

- Give advantages and disadvantages of rechargeable cells; why secondary cells are more sustainable.

### 1. Must cover

Origins (one block)
- [ ] H1 Luigi Galvani, in Bologna in the 1780s, found that a dissected frog's leg twitched when touched by two different metals at once; he believed the electricity came from the animal ("animal electricity").
- [ ] H2 Alessandro Volta showed that the electricity came from two different metals in contact with a conducting liquid, and in 1800 built the first battery, the voltaic pile: a stack of zinc and copper (or silver) discs separated by cardboard or cloth soaked in salt water.
- [ ] H3 Galvanic cells (also called voltaic cells) are named after them.

How a redox reaction makes a current (one block)
- [ ] R1 When zinc is put directly into copper(II) sulfate solution, zinc atoms give electrons straight to Cu²⁺ ions: Zn(s) + Cu²⁺(aq) → Zn²⁺(aq) + Cu(s); copper is deposited, the blue colour fades and the energy is released as heat, with no useful current (a displacement reaction).
- [ ] R2 A galvanic cell separates the oxidation and the reduction into two half-cells, so the electrons have to travel through an external wire from one to the other: this flow of electrons is an electric current.
- [ ] R3 A half-cell is an electrode dipping into a solution of its own ions, e.g. zinc in zinc sulfate solution.
- [ ] R4 The metal higher in the electrochemical series (the one more easily oxidised) becomes the anode (negative); the lower metal is the cathode (positive).
- [ ] R5 The further apart the two metals are in the electrochemical series, the greater the voltage of the cell. Under standard conditions (1 M solutions, 25 °C): magnesium–copper 2.71 V; zinc–copper 1.10 V; iron–copper 0.78 V.

The copper–zinc (Daniell) cell (one block)
- [ ] D1 Set-up: a zinc strip in 1 M zinc sulfate solution and a copper strip in 1 M copper(II) sulfate solution, in separate beakers; the metals are joined by wires through a voltmeter (or bulb), and the solutions by a salt bridge. Voltage about 1.1 V.
- [ ] D2 Zinc electrode (anode, negative, oxidation): Zn(s) → Zn²⁺(aq) + 2e⁻. Zinc atoms lose electrons and go into solution as ions.
- [ ] D3 Copper electrode (cathode, positive, reduction): Cu²⁺(aq) + 2e⁻ → Cu(s). Copper(II) ions from the solution gain electrons and are deposited as copper.
- [ ] D4 Overall cell reaction: Zn(s) + Cu²⁺(aq) → Zn²⁺(aq) + Cu(s), the same redox reaction as the displacement in R1. Zinc is the reducing agent; Cu²⁺ is the oxidising agent.
- [ ] D5 Electrons flow through the external wire from the zinc to the copper.
- [ ] D6 Observations over time: the zinc electrode wears away (loses mass); the copper electrode gains a fresh pink-brown coating (gains mass); the blue colour of the copper(II) sulfate solution fades as Cu²⁺ is used up; the voltage slowly falls.

The salt bridge (one block)
- [ ] B1 A salt bridge connects the two solutions: a strip of filter paper soaked in potassium nitrate (or potassium chloride) solution, or a U-tube of the salt solution set in agar gel.
- [ ] B2 It completes the circuit: ions move through it, carrying charge between the half-cells, without the two solutions mixing.
- [ ] B3 It keeps each solution electrically neutral: as Zn²⁺ ions build up in the zinc half-cell, negative ions (NO₃⁻) move into it; as Cu²⁺ ions are removed from the copper half-cell, positive ions (K⁺) move into it.
- [ ] B4 Without it, charge would build up in each beaker and the current would stop at once.
- [ ] B5 Potassium nitrate is used because its ions don't react with the electrode solutions (e.g. it forms no precipitate).

Practical: creating a simple galvanic cell (one block, investigation)
- [ ] P1 Aim: to build a copper–zinc galvanic cell, measure its voltage, and compare the voltages from different pairs of metals.
- [ ] P2 Clean strips of zinc and copper with sandpaper (emery paper) to remove the oxide layer, which would otherwise reduce the voltage.
- [ ] P3 Half-fill one beaker with 1 M zinc sulfate solution and another with 1 M copper(II) sulfate solution; stand the zinc strip in the first and the copper strip in the second.
- [ ] P4 Soak a strip of filter paper in potassium nitrate solution and drape it between the two beakers, with each end in a solution.
- [ ] P5 Connect the metals to a voltmeter with leads and crocodile clips (zinc to the negative terminal) and record the voltage; replace the voltmeter with a small bulb or LED to show a current flows.
- [ ] P6 Remove the salt bridge and record the voltage again (it falls to zero), showing the bridge is needed.
- [ ] P7 Repeat with other metal pairs (e.g. magnesium–copper, iron–copper), each metal in a solution of its own ions, using a fresh salt bridge each time.
- [ ] P8 Variables: independent, the pair of metals; dependent, the voltage; controlled, the concentration and volume of the solutions, the temperature, the size and cleanliness of the strips.
- [ ] P9 A simpler cell: zinc and copper strips pushed into a lemon, or dipped into one beaker of salt solution or dilute acid, also give a voltage, because the juice or solution is an electrolyte.
- [ ] P10 Results: the measured voltages are usually a little lower than the standard values, and the larger the gap between the metals in the electrochemical series, the larger the voltage.
- [ ] P11 Safety: wear goggles; copper(II) sulfate is harmful and an irritant and is toxic to aquatic life (don't pour it down the sink; dispose of it as directed); zinc sulfate is an irritant; wash hands afterwards.
- [ ] P12 Sources of error: oxide layer left on the metals; solutions not exactly 1 M or not at 25 °C; the salt bridge drying out; the voltmeter drawing some current.
- [ ] P13 Diagram: two beakers, zinc in zinc sulfate and copper in copper(II) sulfate, joined by a potassium nitrate salt bridge; wires from each metal to a voltmeter; label anode (−) and cathode (+), and the direction of electron flow in the wire from zinc to copper.

### 3. Traps

- In a galvanic cell the anode is negative and the cathode positive (the reverse of electrolysis).
- Electrons flow through the wire, never through the salt bridge; ions flow through the salt bridge.
- The zinc is not "attracted" to anything: it is oxidised because it is more easily oxidised than copper.
- Don't calculate cell voltages from standard electrode potentials; they aren't required.

### 1. Must cover

How an electrolytic cell works (one block)
- [ ] E1 Electrolysis is the use of electricity to bring about a chemical reaction (a redox reaction) that would not happen on its own; it is carried out in an electrolytic cell.
- [ ] E2 An electrolytic cell has two electrodes dipping into an electrolyte (a molten ionic compound or a solution of ions), connected to an external source of direct current (d.c.), e.g. a battery or power pack.
- [ ] E3 The external supply pumps electrons onto one electrode, making it negative, and removes electrons from the other, making it positive.
- [ ] E4 Positive ions (cations) move to the negative electrode, the cathode, where they gain electrons (reduction); negative ions (anions) move to the positive electrode, the anode, where they lose electrons (oxidation).
- [ ] E5 The current is carried by electrons in the wires and by moving ions in the electrolyte.
- [ ] E6 Inert electrodes, platinum or carbon (graphite), conduct electricity but don't take part in the reaction; they are used so that the products come only from the electrolyte.
- [ ] E7 The word "electrolysis" (breaking up by electricity) and the terms electrode, anode, cathode and ion were introduced by Michael Faraday in 1834.

Galvanic and electrolytic cells compared (one block; one bullet per point)
- [ ] G1 Energy: galvanic converts chemical energy to electrical; electrolytic converts electrical energy to chemical.
- [ ] G2 Reaction: galvanic is spontaneous; electrolytic is non-spontaneous, driven by the external supply.
- [ ] G3 Signs: galvanic anode negative, cathode positive; electrolytic anode positive, cathode negative.
- [ ] G4 In both, oxidation happens at the anode and reduction at the cathode.
- [ ] G5 Galvanic needs two different electrodes in two half-cells (usually with a salt bridge); electrolytic can use two identical inert electrodes in one electrolyte.

Splitting water: electrolysis of acidified water (one block, practical)
- [ ] W1 Pure water conducts electricity very poorly, so a little dilute sulfuric acid is added to provide ions and let a current flow.
- [ ] W2 Apparatus: a Hofmann voltameter (or two inverted test tubes filled with the solution over the electrodes in a beaker), with platinum (or carbon) electrodes connected to a 6–12 V d.c. supply.
- [ ] W3 Observation: bubbles of gas form at both electrodes; the volume of gas at the negative electrode is twice that at the positive electrode.
- [ ] W4 Test the gas from the negative electrode (cathode) with a lighted splint: it burns with a squeaky pop, so it is hydrogen.
- [ ] W5 Test the gas from the positive electrode (anode) with a glowing splint: it relights, so it is oxygen.
- [ ] W6 The 2 : 1 volume ratio of hydrogen to oxygen matches the formula H₂O; water has been split into its elements. In practice the oxygen volume is often slightly less than half, because oxygen is more soluble in water than hydrogen.
- [ ] W7 The sulfuric acid is not used up: its concentration stays the same (only water is removed).
- [ ] W8 Water can also be split by electrolysing sodium sulfate solution with universal indicator added: the same gases form, and the solution turns blue-purple around the cathode (OH⁻ formed) and red around the anode (H⁺ formed).
- [ ] W9 Diagram: a Hofmann voltameter with platinum electrodes and a d.c. supply, labelled cathode (−) with hydrogen and anode (+) with oxygen, the hydrogen column twice the height of the oxygen column.

Electrolysis of copper(II) chloride solution (one block, practical)
- [ ] C1 Set-up: copper(II) chloride solution in a U-tube or beaker with two carbon (graphite) electrodes connected to a d.c. supply; carry out in a fume cupboard or for a short time only.
- [ ] C2 At the negative electrode (cathode): a pink-brown coating of copper metal forms.
- [ ] C3 At the positive electrode (anode): bubbles of a pale green gas with a sharp smell; it turns damp blue litmus paper red and then bleaches it, so it is chlorine.
- [ ] C4 The blue-green colour of the solution fades as copper(II) ions are removed.
- [ ] C5 Safety: chlorine is toxic; use a fume cupboard, keep the current on only briefly, and don't sniff the gas directly.
- [ ] C6 Diagram: U-tube of copper(II) chloride solution with graphite electrodes and a d.c. supply, labelled copper at the cathode (−) and chlorine at the anode (+), with damp litmus paper at the anode.

Electrolysis of potassium iodide solution (one block, practical)
- [ ] K1 Set-up: potassium iodide solution with a few drops of phenolphthalein in a U-tube with two platinum (or carbon) electrodes connected to a d.c. supply.
- [ ] K2 At the positive electrode (anode): the solution turns brown (yellow-brown) as iodine forms; a drop of starch solution turns it blue-black, confirming iodine.
- [ ] K3 At the negative electrode (cathode): bubbles of hydrogen gas form, and the solution turns pink, showing it has become alkaline (hydroxide ions have formed).
- [ ] K4 Potassium is not formed at the cathode: potassium is very high in the electrochemical series, so K⁺ ions are very hard to reduce and water is reduced instead.
- [ ] K5 Safety: wear goggles; iodine stains skin and is harmful.
- [ ] K6 Diagram: U-tube of potassium iodide with phenolphthalein and platinum electrodes, labelled brown iodine at the anode (+) and pink colour with hydrogen bubbles at the cathode (−).

Reactions at the electrodes (Higher Level only; one block, one bullet per electrode)
- [ ] R1 [HL] Acidified water, cathode (reduction): 4H⁺(aq) + 4e⁻ → 2H₂(g) (or 2H⁺ + 2e⁻ → H₂).
- [ ] R2 [HL] Acidified water, anode (oxidation): 2H₂O(l) → O₂(g) + 4H⁺(aq) + 4e⁻.
- [ ] R3 [HL] Acidified water, overall: 2H₂O(l) → 2H₂(g) + O₂(g). The same 4 electrons give 2 H₂ and 1 O₂, which explains the 2 : 1 volume ratio; the H⁺ used at the cathode is replaced at the anode, so the acid is not used up.
- [ ] R4 [HL] Sodium sulfate solution, cathode: 2H₂O(l) + 2e⁻ → H₂(g) + 2OH⁻(aq), which turns universal indicator blue-purple; the anode reaction is R2, which turns it red.
- [ ] R5 [HL] Copper(II) chloride, cathode (reduction): Cu²⁺(aq) + 2e⁻ → Cu(s).
- [ ] R6 [HL] Copper(II) chloride, anode (oxidation): 2Cl⁻(aq) → Cl₂(g) + 2e⁻.
- [ ] R7 [HL] Copper(II) chloride, overall: Cu²⁺(aq) + 2Cl⁻(aq) → Cu(s) + Cl₂(g).
- [ ] R8 [HL] Potassium iodide, anode (oxidation): 2I⁻(aq) → I₂(aq) + 2e⁻ (the iodine dissolves in the iodide solution, colouring it brown).
- [ ] R9 [HL] Potassium iodide, cathode (reduction): 2H₂O(l) + 2e⁻ → H₂(g) + 2OH⁻(aq); the OH⁻ turns phenolphthalein pink.
- [ ] R10 [HL] Potassium iodide, overall: 2I⁻(aq) + 2H₂O(l) → I₂(aq) + H₂(g) + 2OH⁻(aq).
- [ ] R11 [HL] Oxidation numbers confirm each half-reaction: e.g. Cl −1 → 0 (oxidation) and Cu +2 → 0 (reduction).

### 3. Traps

- In electrolysis the cathode is negative and the anode positive; oxidation is still at the anode.
- Hydrogen forms at the negative electrode, oxygen at the positive; the hydrogen volume is double.
- In potassium iodide solution, hydrogen (not potassium) forms at the cathode.
- Use d.c., not a.c.
- Faraday's laws and electrolysis calculations (charge, current, time) are not on the specification; electrolysis of molten salts, electroplating and industrial electrolysis are not required.
