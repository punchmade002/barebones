## 1. Must cover

Structure of a hydrogen fuel cell (one block)
- [ ] S1 A hydrogen fuel cell is a galvanic cell in which the fuel, hydrogen, is oxidised and the oxidising agent, oxygen (from the air), is reduced, producing electricity and water.
- [ ] S2 Hydrogen gas is fed continuously to the anode (negative electrode); oxygen or air is fed continuously to the cathode (positive electrode).
- [ ] S3 The electrodes are porous carbon coated with a platinum catalyst: the pores let the gases reach the electrolyte, and the platinum speeds up the electrode reactions.
- [ ] S4 In the common type the electrolyte is a proton exchange membrane (PEM), a thin sheet of solid polymer between the electrodes.
- [ ] S5 The membrane lets H⁺ ions (protons) pass from the anode to the cathode; it does not conduct electrons, so they must go round the external circuit; and it keeps the hydrogen and oxygen apart.

How it works (one block, one step per bullet)
- [ ] O1 Step 1: At the anode, hydrogen molecules are oxidised to hydrogen ions, releasing electrons: H₂(g) → 2H⁺(aq) + 2e⁻.
- [ ] O2 Step 2: The electrons flow through the external circuit to the cathode, powering a motor or other device on the way.
- [ ] O3 Step 3: The H⁺ ions move through the membrane to the cathode.
- [ ] O4 Step 4: At the cathode, oxygen is reduced by combining with the H⁺ ions and the electrons to form water: O₂(g) + 4H⁺(aq) + 4e⁻ → 2H₂O(l).
- [ ] O5 Step 5: The water is removed from the cathode side (as liquid or vapour), so the reaction can continue.
- [ ] O6 Overall: 2H₂(g) + O₂(g) → 2H₂O(l) (doubling the anode equation to balance the electrons: 2H₂ → 4H⁺ + 4e⁻).

Redox in the fuel cell (one block)
- [ ] R1 Hydrogen is the reducing agent: its oxidation number rises from 0 in H₂ to +1 in H₂O (oxidation, loss of electrons, at the anode).
- [ ] R2 Oxygen is the oxidising agent: its oxidation number falls from 0 in O₂ to −2 in H₂O (reduction, gain of electrons, at the cathode).
- [ ] R3 The reaction is strongly exothermic (ΔH = −286 kJ per mole of liquid water formed); in the fuel cell much of this energy is released as electrical energy rather than heat.

Practical points (one block)
- [ ] P1 A single hydrogen fuel cell gives a maximum of about 1.2 V and, in use, about 0.6–0.8 V, so many cells are connected in series in a stack; a car's stack contains several hundred cells.
- [ ] P2 Fuel cells also produce some heat, which can be used, e.g. for heating in buildings or buses.
- [ ] P3 The hydrogen must be pure: impurities such as carbon monoxide poison the platinum catalyst.

The alkaline fuel cell (one block)
- [ ] A1 In an alkaline fuel cell the electrolyte is potassium hydroxide solution, and OH⁻ ions carry the charge through it.
- [ ] A2 Anode (oxidation): 2H₂(g) + 4OH⁻(aq) → 4H₂O(l) + 4e⁻.
- [ ] A3 Cathode (reduction): O₂(g) + 2H₂O(l) + 4e⁻ → 4OH⁻(aq).
- [ ] A4 Overall: 2H₂(g) + O₂(g) → 2H₂O(l), the same as in the PEM cell.
- [ ] A5 Alkaline fuel cells powered the Apollo spacecraft and produced drinking water for the astronauts.

Uses, advantages and limitations (one block)
- [ ] U1 Uses: hydrogen buses, cars and trucks; spacecraft; backup and off-grid power supplies.
- [ ] U2 Advantages: the only product is water, so there is no CO₂, CO, nitrogen oxides or soot at the point of use; more efficient than burning hydrogen; quiet; refuelled in minutes; runs as long as fuel is supplied.
- [ ] U3 Limitations: hydrogen is highly flammable and forms explosive mixtures with air; it has a very low density, so it must be stored compressed at high pressure (or liquefied at very low temperature) in strong, heavy tanks; there are few hydrogen refuelling stations; platinum is scarce and expensive.
- [ ] U4 Most hydrogen today is made from natural gas, which releases carbon dioxide; a fuel cell is only low-carbon overall when the hydrogen is made by electrolysis using renewable electricity (see "Electrochemistry and sustainability").
- [ ] U5 Diagram: a PEM fuel cell: hydrogen in at the anode (−) and oxygen or air in at the cathode (+), the membrane between them with H⁺ moving across, the external circuit with a load and electrons flowing from anode to cathode, and water leaving from the cathode side; write the half-equations beside each electrode.

## 3. Traps

- Hydrogen is oxidised at the anode (negative); oxygen is reduced at the cathode (positive). Don't swap them.
- The membrane carries H⁺ ions, not electrons.
- A fuel cell is a galvanic cell, not an electrolytic cell, and it involves no combustion.
- Don't write OH⁻ in the PEM equations or H⁺ in the alkaline-cell equations.
- Hydrogen production methods other than electrolysis (e.g. steam reforming) are named only; their equations aren't required.

## 4. What exams ask (topics only)

- Define a fuel cell; name the fuel and the oxidising agent in a hydrogen fuel cell.
- Describe how a hydrogen fuel cell works; write the half-equations at each electrode and the overall equation.
- State the functions of the proton exchange membrane and of the platinum.
- Give advantages and disadvantages of hydrogen fuel cells for vehicles.
