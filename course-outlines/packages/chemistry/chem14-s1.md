## 1. Must cover

Early definitions: oxygen and hydrogen (one block)
- [ ] E1 Oxidation (early definition): the addition of oxygen to a substance, e.g. C + O₂ → CO₂; 2Mg + O₂ → 2MgO.
- [ ] E2 Reduction (early definition): the removal of oxygen from a substance, or the addition of hydrogen, e.g. CuO + H₂ → Cu + H₂O (black copper(II) oxide turns to pink-brown copper when hydrogen is passed over it while heated).
- [ ] E3 The name "reduction" comes from extracting metals from their ores: the ore is reduced to a smaller mass of metal as oxygen is removed.
- [ ] E4 The oxygen definitions cover only some reactions; many redox reactions involve no oxygen or hydrogen at all (e.g. sodium and chlorine), so a broader definition is needed.

Oxidation and reduction as electron transfer (one block)
- [ ] T1 Oxidation is the loss of electrons; reduction is the gain of electrons. Memory aid: OIL RIG (Oxidation Is Loss, Reduction Is Gain).
- [ ] T2 Example: 2Na(s) + Cl₂(g) → 2NaCl(s). Each sodium atom loses one electron (Na → Na⁺ + e⁻: oxidation); each chlorine atom gains one electron (Cl₂ + 2e⁻ → 2Cl⁻: reduction).
- [ ] T3 A half-equation shows one process with its electrons, e.g. Zn(s) → Zn²⁺(aq) + 2e⁻ (oxidation); Cu²⁺(aq) + 2e⁻ → Cu(s) (reduction). Electrons appear on the right in an oxidation and on the left in a reduction.
- [ ] T4 A redox reaction is one in which oxidation and reduction take place together. They must happen together: electrons lost by one substance can't disappear, so another substance must gain them, and the number lost equals the number gained.
- [ ] T5 Example: Zn(s) + Cu²⁺(aq) → Zn²⁺(aq) + Cu(s), from zinc in copper(II) sulfate solution: each zinc atom transfers two electrons to a copper(II) ion; the zinc becomes coated with brown copper and the blue colour of the solution fades.

Oxidising and reducing agents (one block)
- [ ] A1 An oxidising agent brings about oxidation in another substance by accepting its electrons; the oxidising agent is itself reduced.
- [ ] A2 A reducing agent brings about reduction in another substance by giving it electrons; the reducing agent is itself oxidised.
- [ ] A3 To identify them: find which species loses electrons (it is oxidised and is the reducing agent) and which gains electrons (it is reduced and is the oxidising agent).
- [ ] A4 Example: in Zn + CuSO₄ → ZnSO₄ + Cu, zinc is the reducing agent (oxidised, Zn → Zn²⁺) and the copper(II) ion (copper(II) sulfate) is the oxidising agent (reduced, Cu²⁺ → Cu); the sulfate ion takes no part (a spectator ion).
- [ ] A5 Example: in 2K + Cl₂ → 2KCl, potassium is oxidised (it is the reducing agent) and chlorine is reduced (it is the oxidising agent).
- [ ] A6 Example: bromine water oxidises sulfite ions to sulfate ions, and the orange-yellow colour of the bromine disappears: SO₃²⁻(aq) + Br₂(aq) + H₂O(l) → SO₄²⁻(aq) + 2Br⁻(aq) + 2H⁺(aq); bromine is the oxidising agent and sulfite the reducing agent. (Halogen displacement reactions, another example, are in "Families and trends in the periodic table".)

Everyday oxidising and reducing agents (one block)
- [ ] U1 Many bleaches and disinfectants are oxidising agents: they oxidise coloured substances to colourless ones, or kill microorganisms by oxidising them.
- [ ] U2 Oxidising agents in use: hydrogen peroxide (H₂O₂) bleaches hair; sodium hypochlorite (NaOCl, the hypochlorite ion ClO⁻) is household bleach; chlorine (Cl₂) disinfects drinking water (see "Treating drinking water"); iodine solution (tincture of iodine) is an antiseptic for cuts.
- [ ] U3 Reducing agents in use: sulfur dioxide (SO₂, food additive E220) is a preservative, e.g. in wine and dried fruit, because it prevents oxidation of the food; vitamin C (ascorbic acid) is an antioxidant (reducing agent).

Corrosion and its prevention (one block)
- [ ] C1 Corrosion is the unwanted conversion of a metal to one of its compounds by reaction with its environment (e.g. oxygen and water); the metal is oxidised. Rusting is the corrosion of iron and steel.
- [ ] C2 Rusting needs both oxygen and water: iron is oxidised, Fe → Fe²⁺ + 2e⁻ and then to iron(III), while oxygen is reduced; rust is hydrated iron(III) oxide, Fe₂O₃·xH₂O, a brown flaky solid. Salt (e.g. sea spray or road salt) speeds up rusting.
- [ ] C3 Copper corrodes slowly in air to form a green layer of copper compounds (verdigris, or a patina), as on copper roofs and the copper skin of the Statue of Liberty.
- [ ] C4 Aluminium is a reactive metal but resists corrosion because a thin layer of aluminium oxide, Al₂O₃, forms on its surface and sticks firmly, stopping air and water reaching the metal underneath.
- [ ] C5 Barrier methods keep out oxygen and water: painting, coating with plastic, oil or grease (for moving parts), and plating with a less reactive metal (e.g. tin on steel food cans; chromium on taps). Once the barrier is scratched, the iron underneath rusts; with tin plating the exposed iron rusts even faster than bare iron, because iron is more reactive than tin.
- [ ] C6 Galvanising: coating iron or steel with zinc (e.g. buckets, wheelbarrows, fencing). Zinc is more easily oxidised than iron (higher in the electrochemical series), so even if the coating is scratched the zinc is oxidised instead of the iron.
- [ ] C7 Sacrificial protection: a block of a more reactive metal (zinc or magnesium) is attached to steel ship hulls or underground pipes; it is oxidised (corrodes) instead of the steel and is replaced from time to time.

Combustion, respiration and iron tablets (one block)
- [ ] R1 Combustion of fuels is oxidation: the fuel is oxidised and oxygen is the oxidising agent (it is reduced), e.g. CH₄(g) + 2O₂(g) → CO₂(g) + 2H₂O(l). The energy released is in "Combustion and heats of combustion".
- [ ] R2 Respiration is the oxidation of glucose in cells to release energy: C₆H₁₂O₆(aq) + 6O₂(g) → 6CO₂(g) + 6H₂O(l); glucose is oxidised and oxygen is reduced. Photosynthesis is the reverse: carbon dioxide is reduced to glucose.
- [ ] R3 Iron is needed to make haemoglobin, which carries oxygen in the blood; a lack of iron causes iron-deficiency anaemia, treated with iron tablets.
- [ ] R4 Iron tablets contain iron as iron(II), Fe²⁺ (e.g. iron(II) sulfate), because iron(II) is the form the body absorbs best. Fe²⁺ is easily oxidised to Fe³⁺ by oxygen in the air: Fe²⁺ → Fe³⁺ + e⁻.
- [ ] R5 Vitamin C (ascorbic acid), a reducing agent, helps the body absorb iron: it reduces Fe³⁺ to Fe²⁺ (Fe³⁺ + e⁻ → Fe²⁺), so iron tablets are often taken with vitamin C or orange juice.
- [ ] R6 The iron(II) in a tablet can be measured by titration with potassium manganate(VII), which oxidises Fe²⁺ to Fe³⁺ (see "Redox titrations and iron tablets").

Oxidation numbers: meaning and use (Higher Level only)
- [ ] N1 [HL] The oxidation number (oxidation state) of an atom is the charge it has, or appears to have, when the electrons in its bonds are assigned according to a set of rules; in a covalent bond the shared electrons are given to the more electronegative atom.
- [ ] N2 [HL] Oxidation numbers are not real charges in covalent substances; they are a bookkeeping tool for tracking electron transfer. They are written with the sign first (+2, −1), unlike ionic charges (2+, 1−).
- [ ] N3 [HL] Oxidation is an increase in oxidation number; reduction is a decrease in oxidation number. The size of the change is the number of electrons lost or gained per atom.
- [ ] N4 [HL] Example: 2H₂ + O₂ → 2H₂O. Hydrogen goes from 0 to +1 (oxidised; H₂ is the reducing agent); oxygen goes from 0 to −2 (reduced; O₂ is the oxidising agent).
- [ ] N5 [HL] Example: Cu + 2H₂SO₄ (concentrated) → CuSO₄ + SO₂ + 2H₂O. Copper goes from 0 to +2 (oxidised; copper is the reducing agent); sulfur goes from +6 in H₂SO₄ to +4 in SO₂ (reduced; sulfuric acid is the oxidising agent). The sulfur that ends up in CuSO₄ stays at +6: only one of the two sulfur atoms is reduced.
- [ ] N6 [HL] Oxidation numbers are assigned only for compounds of the first 36 elements (hydrogen to krypton).

Rules for assigning oxidation numbers (Higher Level only)
- [ ] O1 [HL] Rule 1: an uncombined element has oxidation number 0, e.g. Zn, Fe, Na, O₂, Cl₂, P₄.
- [ ] O2 [HL] Rule 2: a simple (monatomic) ion has an oxidation number equal to its charge, e.g. Na⁺ +1, Mg²⁺ +2, Fe²⁺ +2, Fe³⁺ +3, Cl⁻ −1, Br⁻ −1, O²⁻ −2.
- [ ] O3 [HL] Rule 3: the oxidation numbers in a neutral compound add up to 0, e.g. NaCl: (+1) + (−1) = 0; H₂O: 2(+1) + (−2) = 0.
- [ ] O4 [HL] Rule 4: the oxidation numbers in a polyatomic ion add up to the charge on the ion, e.g. NO₃⁻: N + 3(−2) = −1, so N = +5.
- [ ] O5 [HL] Rule 5: oxygen is −2 in its compounds, except in peroxides (H₂O₂, Na₂O₂), where it is −1 (2(+1) + 2(O) = 0), and in OF₂, where it is +2 (fluorine is more electronegative than oxygen, so fluorine takes the electrons and is −1).
- [ ] O6 [HL] Rule 6: hydrogen is +1 in its compounds, except in metal hydrides (e.g. NaH, CaH₂), where it is −1: hydrogen is more electronegative than the metal, so the bonding electrons are assigned to hydrogen (and NaH contains the H⁻ ion).
- [ ] O7 [HL] Rule 7: fluorine is always −1 in its compounds; the other halogens are −1 except when bonded to a more electronegative element (oxygen or fluorine), e.g. Cl is +1 in NaClO and Cl₂O, +5 in ClO₃⁻, +7 in Cl₂O₇ and ClO₄⁻.
- [ ] O8 [HL] Rule 8: in a compound of two non-metals not covered above, the more electronegative element takes its usual negative value and the other is calculated, e.g. CS₂: S = −2, so C = +4.
- [ ] O9 [HL] Group 1 metals are always +1 and group 2 metals +2 in their compounds.

Assigning oxidation numbers: worked examples (Higher Level only)
- [ ] W1 [HL] Method: write the known values (from the rules), then solve for the unknown so that the total equals 0 (compound) or the ion's charge. Example: KMnO₄: (+1) + Mn + 4(−2) = 0, so Mn = +7.
- [ ] W2 [HL] K₂Cr₂O₇: 2(+1) + 2Cr + 7(−2) = 0, so 2Cr = +12 and Cr = +6; in Cr³⁺ (e.g. chromium(III) sulfate) Cr = +3.
- [ ] W3 [HL] CuSO₄: the sulfate ion is SO₄²⁻, so Cu = +2; in SO₄²⁻, S + 4(−2) = −2, so S = +6; O = −2.
- [ ] W4 [HL] Sulfur in other species: H₂S −2; SO₂ +4; SO₃²⁻ +4; H₂SO₄ +6; S₂O₃²⁻ +2; S₄O₆²⁻ +2.5.
- [ ] W5 [HL] Nitrogen: NH₃ −3; N₂ 0; NO +2; NO₂ +4; NO₃⁻ and HNO₃ +5. Carbon: CH₄ −4; CO +2; CO₂ +4; C₂O₄²⁻ +3. Phosphorus: P₄ 0; PO₄³⁻ +5. Manganese: MnO₂ +4; Mn²⁺ +2.
- [ ] W6 [HL] An oxidation number can be a fraction: it is then the average over atoms of the same element, e.g. in S₄O₆²⁻, 4S + 6(−2) = −2, so S = +2.5.
- [ ] W7 [HL] The Roman numeral in a name gives the oxidation number of the element before it: iron(II) oxide FeO (+2), iron(III) oxide Fe₂O₃ (+3), copper(I) oxide Cu₂O (+1), copper(II) oxide CuO (+2), manganese(IV) oxide MnO₂ (+4, also called manganese dioxide), potassium manganate(VII) KMnO₄ (+7, also called potassium permanganate), potassium dichromate(VI) K₂Cr₂O₇ (+6).

## 3. Traps

- The oxidising agent is reduced; the reducing agent is oxidised. Students often swap them.
- Identify the species, not the element in words: in Zn + CuSO₄, the oxidising agent is Cu²⁺ (or copper(II) sulfate), not "copper".
- An oxidation number is written +2 (sign first); an ionic charge is written 2+.
- Oxidation numbers are required only for compounds of the first 36 elements. Iodine is element 53: I₂ (0) and I⁻ (−1) follow from rules 1 and 2, but don't assign oxidation numbers in iodine's compounds (e.g. iodates). Transition-metal chemistry and variable valency as a topic are not on the specification; Mn, Cr, Fe and Cu appear only as oxidation-number examples and in the redox titrations.
- Hydrogen in metal hydrides is −1 and oxygen in peroxides is −1 and in OF₂ is +2; these exceptions are asked often.
- Combustion energy belongs to "Combustion and heats of combustion"; electrochemical cells belong to the electrochemistry chapter; don't teach cells here.
- Metal extraction (blast furnace, thermit reaction) is not on the specification; use E3 only to explain the word "reduction".

## 4. What exams ask (topics only)

- Define oxidation and reduction in terms of electron transfer (and, HL, in terms of oxidation number).
- Identify the species oxidised and reduced, and the oxidising and reducing agents, in a given equation (e.g. Na + Cl₂, Zn + Cu²⁺, K + Cl₂); how many electrons are transferred.
- Classify a half-equation as oxidation or reduction.
- What corrosion is; how painting, coating with another metal and galvanising protect iron.
- HL: define oxidation number; assign oxidation numbers (e.g. Cl in NaClO, N in NO₃⁻, Mn in KMnO₄, Cr in a dichromate, P in PO₄³⁻); explain H = −1 in metal hydrides and O = +2 in OF₂.

## 5. Practice questions to write

1. 6 marks: "Define oxidation and reduction in terms of electron transfer. In the reaction Zn(s) + CuSO₄(aq) → ZnSO₄(aq) + Cu(s), identify the oxidising agent and the reducing agent, and state one observation." Model answer: T1; A4 (Cu²⁺ oxidising agent, Zn reducing agent); T5 observation (brown copper on zinc, or blue colour fades).
2. 9 marks: "What is meant by corrosion? Explain how (i) painting and (ii) galvanising protect iron from corrosion, and why galvanised iron is still protected when it is scratched." Model answer: C1, C2 (oxygen and water needed); C5 (barrier); C6 (zinc more easily oxidised, so zinc is oxidised instead of iron).
3. 9 marks (Higher Level): "Define oxidation number. Assign the oxidation number of (i) chlorine in NaClO, (ii) nitrogen in NO₃⁻, (iii) manganese in KMnO₄. Explain why hydrogen has an oxidation number of −1 in sodium hydride." Model answer: N1; (i) +1 (Na +1, O −2); (ii) +5; (iii) +7 (W1); O6 (H more electronegative than Na, so the bonding electrons are assigned to H).
