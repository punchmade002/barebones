# Package: chem6-s2 — Bonding and properties

- **loId:** `chem6-s2` · **chapterId:** `chem6` · **syllabusRef:** 2.1.4
- **Syllabus:** 2.1.4 "relate the properties of simple compounds to the nature of bonding
  present". Learn about: "properties of compounds to include: electrical conductivity, melting
  and boiling points, solubility in water and state of matter at room temperature". Normal type.
- **Prepared by:** a higher model, 2026-10-30. Compare ionic and covalent (molecular) compounds
  property by property, each with its explanation. Intermolecular forces are explained in the
  next subsections; here say "weak forces between molecules" and refer to "Intramolecular
  bonding and intermolecular forces".

## 1. Must cover

Ionic compounds (one block)
- [ ] I1 State: solids at room temperature, e.g. sodium chloride, magnesium oxide.
- [ ] I2 High melting and boiling points (NaCl melts at 801 °C), because a lot of energy is needed to overcome the strong electrostatic attractions between oppositely charged ions throughout the lattice.
- [ ] I3 Electrical conductivity: they don't conduct when solid, because the ions are held in fixed positions; they conduct when molten or dissolved in water, because the ions are free to move and carry charge.
- [ ] I4 Solubility: many are soluble in water, because polar water molecules attract and surround the ions; they are insoluble in non-polar solvents such as cyclohexane.

Covalent (molecular) compounds (one block)
- [ ] C1 State: many are gases or liquids at room temperature (e.g. methane, carbon dioxide, water), or solids with low melting points (e.g. iodine, sugar).
- [ ] C2 Low melting and boiling points, because only the weak forces between molecules have to be overcome, not the strong covalent bonds within them.
- [ ] C3 Electrical conductivity: they don't conduct, because there are no ions or free electrons to carry charge (some, like hydrogen chloride, form ions when they dissolve in water and then conduct).
- [ ] C4 Solubility: non-polar molecules (e.g. methane, iodine) are insoluble or only slightly soluble in water but dissolve in non-polar solvents; polar molecules that can form hydrogen bonds (e.g. ethanol, sugar, ammonia) dissolve in water. "Like dissolves like."

Comparison and testing (one block)
- [ ] K1 Summary as paired points: ionic high vs covalent low melting points; ionic conduct when molten or in solution vs covalent don't conduct; ionic usually soluble in water vs covalent usually soluble in non-polar solvents; ionic solids vs covalent often gases or liquids.
- [ ] K2 A simple test: dissolve or melt the substance and test whether it conducts with a battery, bulb (or ammeter) and two carbon electrodes; a lit bulb shows ions are present.
- [ ] K3 Diagram: a conductivity circuit (battery, bulb, two electrodes in a beaker of solution).
- [ ] K4 Giant covalent substances are the exception: diamond and graphite have very high melting points because strong covalent bonds must be broken (taught in the carbon allotropes subsection of the hydrocarbons chapter).

## 3. Traps

- When a molecular substance melts or boils, the covalent bonds don't break; only the forces between molecules are overcome.
- Solid ionic compounds don't conduct; don't say "ionic compounds conduct" without the condition.
- Don't teach the types of intermolecular force here.

## 4. What exams ask (topics only)

- Give and explain two properties of ionic compounds.
- Explain why sodium chloride has a high melting point and conducts only when molten or in solution.
- Explain why a covalent compound such as methane has a low boiling point.
