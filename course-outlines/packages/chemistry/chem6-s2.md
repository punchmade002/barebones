## Part: Intermolecular forces and physical properties (formerly `chem6-s4`)

- **loId:** `chem6-s4` · **chapterId:** `chem6` · **syllabusRef:** 2.2.2
- **Syllabus:** 2.2.2 "relate observed physical properties for a range of compounds to the type
  of intermolecular forces, **accounting for trends**". Learn about: "how the nature of
  intermolecular forces can influence physical properties (changes of state and solubility)
  and evidence for the effects of intermolecular forces can be analysed using appropriate
  secondary data (the range of compounds includes water and appropriate inorganic and organic
  compounds)". Bold = Higher Level only.
- **Prepared by:** a higher model, 2026-10-30. Relationship of forces to boiling point and
  solubility with secondary data examples; Higher Level block explaining trends in the data.

### 1. Must cover

Changes of state (one block)
- [ ] C1 To melt or boil a molecular substance, energy must be supplied to overcome the intermolecular forces; the stronger the forces, the higher the melting and boiling points.
- [ ] C2 Substances with only London dispersion forces between small molecules are gases at room temperature (e.g. methane, bp −162 °C).
- [ ] C3 Hydrogen bonding gives unusually high boiling points: water boils at 100 °C, but hydrogen sulfide, a heavier molecule with no hydrogen bonding, boils at −60 °C.

Solubility (one block)
- [ ] S1 A substance dissolves when the attractions between solvent and solute particles are similar to or stronger than those they replace: "like dissolves like".
- [ ] S2 Polar and hydrogen-bonding substances (ethanol, ammonia, sugar) dissolve in water, because they form hydrogen bonds with water molecules.
- [ ] S3 Non-polar substances (e.g. iodine, hexane, oils) don't dissolve in water but dissolve in non-polar solvents such as cyclohexane, where London forces are similar.
- [ ] S4 Hexane and water don't mix: they form two layers.

Water: special properties from hydrogen bonding (one block)
- [ ] W1 Water has a high boiling point and high melting point for such a small molecule.
- [ ] W2 Ice is less dense than liquid water: hydrogen bonds hold the molecules in an open lattice, so ice floats.
- [ ] W3 Water is a good solvent for ionic and polar substances.

Secondary data and trends (one block, with sample tables to interpret)
- [ ] D1 Noble gas boiling points: He −269 °C; Ne −246 °C; Ar −186 °C; Kr −153 °C; Xe −108 °C.
- [ ] D2 Halogens at room temperature: fluorine and chlorine are gases, bromine is a liquid and iodine is a solid.
- [ ] D3 Alkanes: methane −162 °C; ethane −89 °C; propane −42 °C; butane −1 °C; pentane 36 °C; hexane 69 °C.
- [ ] D4 Group 16 hydrides: H₂O 100 °C; H₂S −60 °C; H₂Se −41 °C; H₂Te −2 °C.
- [ ] D5 Group 15 and 17 hydrides: NH₃ −33 °C vs PH₃ −88 °C; HF 20 °C vs HCl −85 °C.
- [ ] D6 Compounds of similar mass: ethanol (bp 78 °C, hydrogen bonding) vs propane (bp −42 °C, London forces only).

Accounting for trends (Higher Level only; one block)
- [ ] T1 [HL] Down group 18, group 17 and the alkane series, boiling points rise because molecules have more electrons and larger electron clouds, so London dispersion forces get stronger.
- [ ] T2 [HL] In the group 16 hydrides, boiling point rises from H₂S to H₂Te as London forces increase with size; water is far higher than the trend predicts because of hydrogen bonding.
- [ ] T3 [HL] The same anomaly appears for NH₃ in group 15 and HF in group 17.
- [ ] T4 [HL] Molecules of similar mass have higher boiling points if they are polar (dipole–dipole) and much higher if they hydrogen bond (ethanol vs propane).
- [ ] T5 [HL] Diagram: a graph of boiling point against period for the hydrides of groups 14, 15, 16 and 17, showing the high values of H₂O, HF and NH₃ and the steady rise of CH₄ to SnH₄.

### 3. Traps

- Boiling breaks intermolecular forces, not covalent bonds.
- Branching effects on boiling points belong to the hydrocarbons chapter; don't add them.
- "Accounting for trends" is Higher Level only; Ordinary level relates each property to the force type.

- Explain why water has a much higher boiling point than hydrogen sulfide.
- Explain the trend in boiling points of the halogens, noble gases or alkanes.
- Explain why ethanol dissolves in water but hexane doesn't.

## Part: Polarity and symmetry (formerly `chem6-s5`)

- **loId:** `chem6-s5` · **chapterId:** `chem6` · **syllabusRef:** 2.2.3
- **Syllabus:** 2.2.3 "**explain qualitatively the influence of polarity, and symmetry, on
  intermolecular forces**". Learn about: "**how symmetry can give rise to non-polar compounds
  even in the case where individual polar bonds exist within the molecule**". All bold: the
  whole subsection is Higher Level only. Mark every block heading " (Higher Level only)".
- **Prepared by:** a higher model, 2026-10-30. Qualitative only: no dipole moments. The
  Ordinary-level use of shapes to predict properties, and the charged-rod demonstration, are in
  "Shape, polarity and properties".

### 1. Must cover

Polar bonds and polar molecules (one block)
- [ ] P1 [HL] A polar bond has an uneven share of electrons (δ+ and δ− ends); a polar molecule has an overall uneven distribution of charge, with a δ+ end and a δ− end.
- [ ] P2 [HL] Whether a molecule is polar depends on both its bonds and its shape: polar bonds arranged symmetrically cancel out.

Symmetry and cancelling dipoles (one block)
- [ ] S1 [HL] Carbon dioxide: each C=O bond is polar, but the molecule is linear (O=C=O), so the two bond polarities point in opposite directions and cancel; CO₂ is non-polar.
- [ ] S2 [HL] Tetrachloromethane (CCl₄): four polar C–Cl bonds arranged tetrahedrally cancel; non-polar.
- [ ] S3 [HL] Boron trifluoride (BF₃): three polar B–F bonds at 120° in a trigonal plane cancel; non-polar.
- [ ] S4 [HL] Water: two polar O–H bonds in a V-shaped (bent) molecule don't cancel; water is polar with δ− on oxygen.
- [ ] S5 [HL] Ammonia: three polar N–H bonds in a pyramidal molecule don't cancel; polar.
- [ ] S6 [HL] Trichloromethane (CHCl₃): the C–H bond differs from the three C–Cl bonds, so the tetrahedral molecule is unsymmetrical and polar.
- [ ] S7 [HL] Diagram: CO₂, CCl₄, H₂O and NH₃ with arrows (or δ+/δ− labels) on each bond showing whether the polarities cancel.

Effect on intermolecular forces (one block)
- [ ] F1 [HL] Polar molecules attract each other by permanent dipole–dipole forces (or hydrogen bonds) as well as London forces, so they have stronger intermolecular forces than non-polar molecules of similar size.
- [ ] F2 [HL] Non-polar molecules, including symmetrical ones with polar bonds, have only London dispersion forces between them, e.g. CO₂ is a gas at room temperature; CCl₄ is immiscible with water.
- [ ] F3 [HL] So a symmetrical molecule usually has a lower boiling point and lower solubility in water than an unsymmetrical one of similar size (e.g. CCl₄ is immiscible with water while CHCl₃ is slightly soluble; both dissolve in non-polar solvents).

### 2. Checked facts and examples

Only the molecules above.

### 3. Traps

- Polar bonds do not always make a polar molecule.
- No dipole moment values or units.
- Shapes are taught in "Bonding, formulae and shapes"; state them without re-deriving.

### 4. What exams ask (topics only)

- Explain why carbon dioxide is non-polar although its bonds are polar.
- Explain why water is polar but tetrachloromethane is not.

### 5. Practice questions to write

1. 9 marks: "Explain why the carbon dioxide molecule is non-polar but the water molecule is polar, although both contain polar bonds, and state the effect of this on the intermolecular forces in each." Model answer: S1, S4, F1, F2.
2. 6 marks: "Tetrachloromethane (CCl₄) is non-polar but trichloromethane (CHCl₃) is polar. Explain." Model answer: S2, S6.

## Part: Shape, polarity and properties (formerly `chem6-s6`)

- **loId:** `chem6-s6` · **chapterId:** `chem6` · **syllabusRef:** 2.2.4
- **Syllabus:** 2.2.4 "use the shapes and polarity of molecules of simple compounds to predict
  physical properties". Learn about: "how the shapes of molecules can determine overall polarity
  and also influence physical properties". Normal type: Ordinary and Higher level.
- **Prepared by:** a higher model, 2026-10-30. Ordinary-level treatment: given a shape and
  polarity, predict boiling point and solubility; include the charged-rod demonstration as an
  investigation. Write it without VSEPR (Higher Level) reasoning: state shapes as given facts.
  The full symmetry explanation is in "Polarity and symmetry".

### 1. Must cover

Shape decides overall polarity (one block)
- [ ] S1 A molecule with polar bonds is polar overall only if its shape is unsymmetrical, so that the charge is unevenly spread.
- [ ] S2 Water is V-shaped (bent): it is polar.
- [ ] S3 Ammonia is pyramidal: it is polar.
- [ ] S4 Carbon dioxide is linear and methane is tetrahedral: both are non-polar.

Predicting physical properties (one block)
- [ ] P1 Polar molecules have stronger forces between them than non-polar molecules of similar size, so they have higher boiling points.
- [ ] P2 Polar molecules tend to dissolve in polar solvents such as water; non-polar molecules dissolve in non-polar solvents such as cyclohexane.
- [ ] P3 Prediction example: water (polar, hydrogen bonds) is a liquid at room temperature and dissolves salts; methane (non-polar) is a gas and is almost insoluble in water.
- [ ] P4 Prediction example: ammonia (polar) is very soluble in water; carbon dioxide (non-polar) is only slightly soluble.

Investigation: testing liquids for polarity with a charged rod (one block)
- [ ] I1 Aim: to show whether a liquid is made of polar molecules.
- [ ] I2 Equipment: a burette clamped above a beaker, a polythene (or acetate) rod, a cloth, and liquids such as water and cyclohexane.
- [ ] I3 Step 1: Charge the rod by rubbing it with the cloth.
- [ ] I4 Step 2: Let a thin stream of the liquid run from the burette and hold the rod close to the stream without touching it.
- [ ] I5 Step 3: Repeat with the other liquid, recharging the rod each time.
- [ ] I6 Results: the stream of water is deflected (attracted) towards the rod; the stream of cyclohexane is not deflected.
- [ ] I7 Explanation: polar molecules turn so that their oppositely charged end faces the rod and are attracted to it; non-polar molecules are not.
- [ ] I8 Safety: cyclohexane is flammable and harmful: no flames, small volumes, good ventilation, collect it for disposal.
- [ ] I9 Diagram: a burette releasing a thin stream of liquid into a beaker, with a charged rod held beside the stream and the stream bending towards it.

### 2. Checked facts and examples

Only the molecules and liquids above.

### 3. Traps

- Don't use VSEPR or symmetry arguments to derive shapes in this Ordinary-level subsection; state the shape.
- The stream of water is attracted whether the rod is positive or negative (the molecules rotate).

### 4. What exams ask (topics only)

- Describe how to show that water is polar using a charged rod.
- Predict the polarity and a property of a molecule from its shape.

### 5. Practice questions to write

1. 9 marks: "Describe an experiment to show that water molecules are polar and cyclohexane molecules are not, and explain the results." Model answer: I2–I7.
2. 6 marks: "Methane and water have similar relative molecular masses. Use the shape and polarity of each molecule to explain why water is a liquid at room temperature and methane is a gas." Model answer: S2, S4, P1, P3.
