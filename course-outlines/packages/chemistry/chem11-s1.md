## Part: Categorising acids and bases (formerly `chem11-s1`)

- **loId:** `chem11-s1` · **chapterId:** `chem11` · **syllabusRef:** 3.4.1
- **Syllabus:** 3.4.1 "justify categorisation of commonly used substances as acid or base, based
  on the display of certain properties and outline common everyday examples of
  neutralisation". Learn about: "commonly used substances including, but not limited to:
  vinegar, citric juices, antacids, toothpaste; common acid-base indicators; everyday examples
  of neutralisation including, but not limited to: use of lime in agriculture; remedies for
  acid indigestion". Normal type.
- **Prepared by:** a higher model, 2026-10-30. Properties of acids and bases, indicators, the
  four named substances, and neutralisation examples. Reaction equations in general are in
  "Acid–base reactions"; theories are in "Acid–base theories and strength"; the pH scale
  is in the pH chapter.

### 1. Must cover

Properties of acids (one block)
- [ ] A1 Acids taste sour (never taste laboratory chemicals).
- [ ] A2 They turn blue litmus red and have a pH below 7.
- [ ] A3 They react with reactive metals such as magnesium and zinc to give hydrogen, and with carbonates to give carbon dioxide (fizzing).
- [ ] A4 They neutralise bases.
- [ ] A5 Examples: hydrochloric acid (HCl), sulfuric acid (H₂SO₄), nitric acid (HNO₃), ethanoic acid (CH₃COOH, in vinegar), citric acid (in citrus fruits).

Properties of bases (one block)
- [ ] B1 Bases taste bitter and feel soapy or slippery (they react with oils on the skin).
- [ ] B2 They turn red litmus blue and have a pH above 7.
- [ ] B3 They neutralise acids.
- [ ] B4 An alkali is a base that dissolves in water, e.g. sodium hydroxide (NaOH), potassium hydroxide (KOH), aqueous ammonia (NH₃); many bases, such as copper(II) oxide and magnesium hydroxide, are insoluble.
- [ ] B5 Concentrated alkalis are corrosive (e.g. NaOH in oven and drain cleaners).

Acid–base indicators (one block)
- [ ] I1 An indicator is a substance that changes colour depending on whether it is in an acidic or a basic solution.
- [ ] I2 Litmus: red in acid, blue in base.
- [ ] I3 Phenolphthalein: colourless in acid, pink in base.
- [ ] I4 Methyl orange: red in acid, yellow in base.
- [ ] I5 Universal indicator is a mixture of indicators that gives a range of colours across the pH scale (red in strong acid, through orange, yellow and green at pH 7, to blue and purple in strong base).
- [ ] I6 Natural indicators, such as red cabbage extract, also change colour.

Commonly used substances (one block; one bullet per substance, with the property that justifies the category)
- [ ] S1 Vinegar: an acid; it contains ethanoic acid, tastes sour, turns blue litmus red and fizzes with baking soda.
- [ ] S2 Citric juices (lemon, lime, orange): acids; they contain citric acid, taste sour and have a pH of about 2–4.
- [ ] S3 Antacids: bases; they contain magnesium hydroxide, aluminium hydroxide, calcium carbonate or sodium hydrogencarbonate, and neutralise excess stomach acid.
- [ ] S4 Toothpaste: mildly basic; it neutralises acids produced by bacteria in plaque that would attack tooth enamel.
- [ ] S5 Other examples: baking soda (sodium hydrogencarbonate, a weak base); oven cleaner (sodium hydroxide, a strong alkali); lemonade and cola (acids: they contain citric, carbonic or phosphoric acid).

Neutralisation in everyday life (one block)
- [ ] N1 Neutralisation is the reaction of an acid with a base to form a salt and water; the acid and base cancel out each other's properties.
- [ ] N2 Lime in agriculture: crops grow poorly in acidic soil, so farmers spread lime (calcium oxide or calcium hydroxide) or ground limestone (calcium carbonate) to neutralise the acid and raise the soil pH; e.g. CaCO₃ + 2H⁺ → Ca²⁺ + H₂O + CO₂.
- [ ] N3 Remedies for acid indigestion: the stomach contains hydrochloric acid; excess acid causes indigestion and heartburn; antacid tablets neutralise it, e.g. Mg(OH)₂ + 2HCl → MgCl₂ + 2H₂O; NaHCO₃ + HCl → NaCl + H₂O + CO₂.
- [ ] N4 Other examples: toothpaste neutralising plaque acid; treating a bee sting (acidic) with baking soda; adding lime to acidic lakes.

### 2. Checked facts and examples

| Item | Fact |
|---|---|
| I3, I4 | Phenolphthalein colourless/pink (changes pH 8.3–10); methyl orange red/yellow (changes pH 3.1–4.4). |
| S2 | Lemon juice pH about 2–3; orange juice about 3–4. |
| S3 | Common antacid bases: Mg(OH)₂, Al(OH)₃, CaCO₃, NaHCO₃. |

### 3. Traps

- Not all bases are alkalis: an alkali is a soluble base.
- Wasp stings are often said to be alkaline and bee stings acidic, but the evidence is weak; use only the bee sting example, as an example of a claimed remedy, or leave stings out.
- The pH scale and its calculation are taught in the pH chapter; give pH values only as descriptions.

### 4. What exams ask (topics only)

- Give properties of acids and bases; name an indicator and its colours.
- Give everyday examples of neutralisation (antacids, lime in agriculture) with equations.

### 5. Practice questions to write

1. 9 marks: "Using two properties of each, explain why vinegar is classed as an acid and antacid tablets are classed as bases." Model answer: S1, A2, A3; S3, B2, B3.
2. 9 marks: "Describe two everyday examples of neutralisation, and write a balanced equation for one of them." Model answer: N2, N3 with an equation.

## Part: Acid–base reactions (formerly `chem11-s2`)

- **loId:** `chem11-s2` · **chapterId:** `chem11` · **syllabusRef:** 3.4.2
- **Syllabus:** 3.4.2 "predict the products of, and write balanced equations for, acid-base
  reactions". Learn about: "various types of reactions involving acids and bases: acid-base
  neutralisation; acid-metal; acid-carbonate". Normal type.
- **Prepared by:** a higher model, 2026-10-30. Naming salts; one block per reaction type with a
  general word equation, worked equations and observations; gas tests.

### 1. Must cover

Salts (one block)
- [ ] S1 A salt is a compound formed when the hydrogen of an acid is replaced by a metal (or ammonium) ion.
- [ ] S2 Naming: hydrochloric acid gives chlorides; sulfuric acid gives sulfates; nitric acid gives nitrates; ethanoic acid gives ethanoates; carbonic acid gives carbonates.
- [ ] S3 Sulfuric acid is diprotic (two replaceable hydrogens), so it reacts with twice as much sodium hydroxide as hydrochloric acid does.

Acid + base (neutralisation) (one block)
- [ ] N1 General: acid + base → salt + water.
- [ ] N2 HCl(aq) + NaOH(aq) → NaCl(aq) + H₂O(l).
- [ ] N3 H₂SO₄(aq) + 2KOH(aq) → K₂SO₄(aq) + 2H₂O(l).
- [ ] N4 With an insoluble base (a metal oxide): 2HCl(aq) + CuO(s) → CuCl₂(aq) + H₂O(l); the black solid dissolves to give a blue solution.
- [ ] N5 With ammonia: NH₃(aq) + HCl(aq) → NH₄Cl(aq) (an ammonium salt; no water is formed).
- [ ] N6 Ionic equation for any strong acid and strong alkali: H⁺(aq) + OH⁻(aq) → H₂O(l); neutralisation is exothermic.

Acid + metal (one block)
- [ ] M1 General: acid + metal → salt + hydrogen (for metals above hydrogen in reactivity, such as magnesium, zinc and iron).
- [ ] M2 Mg(s) + 2HCl(aq) → MgCl₂(aq) + H₂(g): fizzing, the magnesium disappears and the flask gets warm.
- [ ] M3 Zn(s) + H₂SO₄(aq) → ZnSO₄(aq) + H₂(g).
- [ ] M4 Copper doesn't react with dilute hydrochloric or sulfuric acid (it is below hydrogen in the electrochemical series).
- [ ] M5 Test for hydrogen: a lighted splint held at the mouth of the tube gives a squeaky pop.

Acid + carbonate or hydrogencarbonate (one block)
- [ ] C1 General: acid + carbonate → salt + water + carbon dioxide.
- [ ] C2 CaCO₃(s) + 2HCl(aq) → CaCl₂(aq) + H₂O(l) + CO₂(g).
- [ ] C3 Na₂CO₃(aq) + H₂SO₄(aq) → Na₂SO₄(aq) + H₂O(l) + CO₂(g).
- [ ] C4 Hydrogencarbonates react the same way: NaHCO₃(s) + HCl(aq) → NaCl(aq) + H₂O(l) + CO₂(g).
- [ ] C5 Test for carbon dioxide: it turns limewater milky: Ca(OH)₂(aq) + CO₂(g) → CaCO₃(s) + H₂O(l).

Predicting products (one block, skills)
- [ ] P1 Identify the reaction type, name the salt from the metal (or ammonium) and the acid, add the other products (water; hydrogen; or water and carbon dioxide), then balance.
- [ ] P2 Example: magnesium oxide and nitric acid: MgO(s) + 2HNO₃(aq) → Mg(NO₃)₂(aq) + H₂O(l).
- [ ] P3 Example: potassium carbonate and ethanoic acid: K₂CO₃ + 2CH₃COOH → 2CH₃COOK + H₂O + CO₂.

### 2. Checked facts and examples

All equations above are balanced.

### 3. Traps

- Metal + acid gives hydrogen, not water.
- Acids react with carbonates even if the carbonate is insoluble (e.g. CaCO₃).
- Don't confuse the limewater test (CO₂) with the splint test (H₂).

### 4. What exams ask (topics only)

- Complete and balance equations for the reactions of acids with bases, metals and carbonates.
- Name the salt formed; describe a test for the gas produced.

### 5. Practice questions to write

1. 9 marks: "Write balanced equations for the reactions of hydrochloric acid with (i) zinc, (ii) sodium carbonate, (iii) calcium hydroxide." Model answer: Zn + 2HCl → ZnCl₂ + H₂; Na₂CO₃ + 2HCl → 2NaCl + H₂O + CO₂; Ca(OH)₂ + 2HCl → CaCl₂ + 2H₂O.
2. 6 marks: "Name the gas produced when magnesium reacts with dilute sulfuric acid, write the balanced equation, and describe a test for the gas." Model answer: hydrogen; Mg + H₂SO₄ → MgSO₄ + H₂; M5.
