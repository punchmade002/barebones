# Package: chem18-s5 — Investigation: disturbing an equilibrium

- **loId:** `chem18-s5` · **chapterId:** `chem18` · **syllabusRef:** 3.3.5
- **Syllabus:** 3.3.5 "investigate, using primary and/or secondary data, how changes in
  temperature and concentration can affect the state of equilibrium". Learn about: "how data can
  be gathered and analysed, through experimentation including, but not limited to iron(III)
  chloride-potassium thiocyanate and digital simulations, as evidence to show factors affecting
  dynamic equilibrium". Normal type.
- **Prepared by:** a higher model, 2026-10-30. Practical work on coloured equilibria: the
  iron(III)–thiocyanate system for concentration (the named setting), the cobalt(II) chloride
  system for concentration and temperature, and the NO₂/N₂O₄ demonstration for temperature;
  then digital simulations and secondary data. The principle and its reasoning are in
  "Le Châtelier's principle"; state the shift and refer there. Kc effects are in "The
  equilibrium constant".

## 1. Must cover

Aim and approach (one block)
- [ ] A1 Aim: to investigate how changes in concentration and temperature affect the position of an equilibrium, and to test whether the results agree with Le Châtelier's principle.
- [ ] A2 A coloured equilibrium is used, so a shift is seen as a colour change: more of the coloured product (or reactant) means a deeper colour.
- [ ] A3 Set up one equilibrium mixture, divide it into equal portions in identical test tubes, keep one untouched as a control, and change one factor in each of the others.
- [ ] A4 Compare each tube with the control against a white background, and record the colour before and after the change.

Iron(III) chloride and potassium thiocyanate: concentration (one block)
- [ ] F1 Equation: Fe³⁺(aq) + SCN⁻(aq) ⇌ FeSCN²⁺(aq). Fe³⁺ (from iron(III) chloride) is yellow, SCN⁻ (from potassium thiocyanate) is colourless, FeSCN²⁺ is blood-red.
- [ ] F2 Method: add about 1 cm³ of 0.1 M iron(III) chloride and 1 cm³ of 0.1 M potassium thiocyanate to about 100 cm³ of deionised water and stir; a pale orange-red solution forms. (Diluting it makes colour changes easy to see.)
- [ ] F3 Pour equal portions (about 10 cm³) into four test tubes; tube 1 is the control.
- [ ] F4 Tube 2: add a few drops of iron(III) chloride solution: the red deepens. [Fe³⁺] increased, so the equilibrium shifted to the right, forming more FeSCN²⁺.
- [ ] F5 Tube 3: add a few drops of potassium thiocyanate solution: the red deepens. [SCN⁻] increased; the equilibrium shifted to the right.
- [ ] F6 Tube 4: add a few drops of silver nitrate solution: a white precipitate forms and the red colour fades. Ag⁺ removes SCN⁻ as insoluble silver thiocyanate (AgSCN), lowering [SCN⁻], so the equilibrium shifted to the left.
- [ ] F7 Conclusion: adding a reactant shifts the equilibrium to the right; removing a reactant shifts it to the left, as Le Châtelier's principle predicts.
- [ ] F8 Diagram: a row of four test tubes in a rack, labelled control, + FeCl₃, + KSCN, + AgNO₃, with colours pale red, deep red, deep red, pale/colourless with white precipitate.

Cobalt(II) chloride: concentration and temperature (one block)
- [ ] B1 Equation: [Co(H₂O)₆]²⁺(aq) + 4Cl⁻(aq) ⇌ [CoCl₄]²⁻(aq) + 6H₂O(l); [Co(H₂O)₆]²⁺ is pink and [CoCl₄]²⁻ is blue; the forward reaction is endothermic.
- [ ] B2 Dissolve a little cobalt(II) chloride in water (pink); add concentrated hydrochloric acid drop by drop until the solution is violet (a mixture of pink and blue), then divide it into test tubes, keeping one as a control.
- [ ] B3 Concentration: adding more concentrated HCl (more Cl⁻) turns the solution blue: shift to the right. Adding water turns it pink: shift to the left (water is a product; diluting favours the side with more dissolved particles).
- [ ] B4 Temperature: put one tube in a beaker of hot water (about 80 °C): it turns blue, because heating favours the endothermic forward reaction. Put another in ice-water: it turns pink, because cooling favours the exothermic reverse reaction.
- [ ] B5 The changes reverse when the tubes return to room temperature, showing the reaction is reversible and still dynamic.
- [ ] B6 Since heating shifts this equilibrium to the right, Kc for it increases with temperature.
- [ ] B7 Safety: concentrated hydrochloric acid is corrosive and gives off choking fumes (goggles, gloves, fume cupboard); cobalt(II) chloride is toxic, may cause cancer and skin allergy, and is harmful to aquatic life (gloves; dispose of in the residue container, not the sink).
- [ ] B8 Diagram: test tubes of the violet mixture: one in a beaker of hot water (blue), one in a beaker of ice-water (pink), one at room temperature as a control (violet), with a thermometer in each beaker.

Nitrogen dioxide and dinitrogen tetroxide: temperature (teacher demonstration; one block)
- [ ] N1 2NO₂(g) ⇌ N₂O₄(g), forward reaction exothermic; NO₂ is brown, N₂O₄ is colourless.
- [ ] N2 Sealed glass tubes containing the same equilibrium mixture are placed in hot water and in ice-water: the hot tube becomes darker brown (shift to the left, the endothermic direction); the cold tube becomes paler (shift to the right).
- [ ] N3 Safety: NO₂ is very toxic; the tubes are sealed and the demonstration is done by the teacher.

Using digital simulations and secondary data (one block)
- [ ] D1 A digital simulation models the particles of a reversible reaction; the user can add reactant or product, or change the temperature, and watch the numbers of each particle and the forward and reverse rates change until a new equilibrium is set up.
- [ ] D2 Simulations show what can't be seen in the laboratory: both reactions continuing at equal rates at equilibrium (dynamic), and graphs of concentration against time showing the sudden change and the new constant levels.
- [ ] D3 Simulations are safe, fast and repeatable, and avoid toxic chemicals, but they are models: their results depend on the rules programmed into them, so they are checked against experimental data.
- [ ] D4 Secondary data, such as a table or graph of the percentage of ammonia at equilibrium at different temperatures and pressures, can be analysed for trends: the percentage of ammonia rises with pressure and falls with temperature, so the forward reaction reduces the moles of gas and is exothermic.
- [ ] D5 Reading a concentration–time graph: a sudden vertical jump in one line shows a substance being added; the gradual changes that follow show the shift; new horizontal lines show the new equilibrium.

Evaluating the investigation (one block)
- [ ] E1 Judging colour by eye is subjective: compare with the control tube against a white background, use the same volume in identical tubes, and have the same observer.
- [ ] E2 Add the same number of drops to each tube, and allow time for the new equilibrium to set up (the colour to stop changing) before recording.
- [ ] E3 Concentration changes change the position of equilibrium but not Kc; temperature changes both.
- [ ] E4 Adding a solution also dilutes the mixture a little, so add only a few drops (or add the same volume of water to the control).

## 3. Traps

- Don't state whether heating deepens or fades the red of the iron(III)–thiocyanate equilibrium as a fixed fact: its enthalpy change is small and sources differ. If a question tells you the colour change on heating or cooling, deduce the direction of the shift, the sign of ΔH and the change in Kc from it (e.g. "the red fades on cooling" means cooling shifts it to the left, so the forward reaction is endothermic and Kc is smaller at the lower temperature).
- Use temperature-dependent examples (cobalt chloride, NO₂/N₂O₄) to describe a temperature experiment.
- Adding water to the cobalt chloride mixture is a change in concentration, not just "dilution of colour".
- Don't name a particular simulation package; none is checked.
- Catalysts are not part of this investigation.

## 4. What exams ask (topics only)

- Describe how to set up an equilibrium with iron(III) chloride and potassium thiocyanate and show the effect of a concentration change; explain the colour changes.
- Cobalt(II) chloride equilibrium: colour change on adding concentrated HCl, on adding water, on heating and on cooling; has the reaction stopped when the colour is constant?
- Deduce from a given colour change whether the forward reaction is exothermic or endothermic, and how Kc changes.
- Interpret secondary data (percentage yield against temperature and pressure).
