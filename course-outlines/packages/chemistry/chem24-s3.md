## Part: Sources and impact of organic products (formerly `chem24-s1`)

- **loId:** `chem24-s1` · **chapterId:** `chem24` · **syllabusRef:** 4.2.1
- **Syllabus:** 4.2.1 "outline sources of organic compounds and the use and impact of products
  based on organic compounds". Learn about: "sourcing organic compounds; industrial products
  based on organic compounds, as the main constituents, such as fuels, pharmaceuticals,
  plastics, pesticides and synthetics". Normal type.
- **Prepared by:** a higher model, 2026-10-30. Where organic compounds come from, then each of the
  five named product groups with uses and impacts, and how to weigh benefits against impacts.
  The verb is "outline", so keep each point brief. Crude oil, natural gas and their hydrocarbons
  are in "Hydrocarbons: sources, uses and impact" and the impact of burning them in "The impact of
  hydrocarbons"; medicines in detail are in "Organic compounds in pharmaceuticals"; addition
  polymers in "Types of organic reaction and addition polymers". Point to these; don't repeat them.

### 1. Must cover

What organic compounds are (one block)
- [ ] O1 Organic chemistry is the chemistry of carbon compounds (apart from simple ones such as carbon oxides, carbonates and hydrogencarbonates); organic compounds contain carbon, almost always bonded to hydrogen, often with oxygen, nitrogen, halogens or sulfur.
- [ ] O2 Carbon forms so many compounds because each carbon atom forms four covalent bonds and carbon atoms bond strongly to each other, forming chains, branches and rings with single, double or triple bonds.
- [ ] O3 Organic compounds were once thought to come only from living things; in 1828 Friedrich Wöhler made urea (a compound found in urine) from an inorganic salt, ammonium cyanate, showing that organic compounds can be made in the laboratory.

Sources of organic compounds (one block)
- [ ] S1 Fossil sources: crude oil, natural gas and coal, formed from the remains of organisms over millions of years; they supply fuels and the feedstocks (starting materials) for most plastics, solvents, dyes, detergents and many medicines (see "Hydrocarbons: sources, uses and impact"). They are finite (non-renewable).
- [ ] S2 Living things (natural products): plants, animals and micro-organisms make organic compounds that can be extracted, e.g. oils and fats from plants and animals, essential oils such as clove oil (extracted by steam distillation), and medicines from plants (e.g. quinine from cinchona bark).
- [ ] S3 Fermentation: micro-organisms convert biomass into organic compounds, e.g. yeast converts glucose into ethanol and carbon dioxide: C₆H₁₂O₆(aq) → 2C₂H₅OH(aq) + 2CO₂(g), catalysed by enzymes in the yeast; antibiotics such as penicillin are also made by fermentation.
- [ ] S4 Fermentation stops at about 12–15% ethanol by volume because the ethanol kills the yeast; spirits (about 40% v/v ethanol) are made by distilling the fermented liquid.
- [ ] S5 Synthesis: chemists build more complex molecules from simpler ones (precursors) in the laboratory and in industry; most modern medicines, plastics and pesticides are made this way.
- [ ] S6 Renewable (bio-based) feedstocks: biomass such as sugar cane, maize and wood can replace fossil feedstocks, e.g. bioethanol for fuel and poly(lactic acid) (PLA) plastic made from plant starch.

Products and uses (one block; one bullet per product group named in the specification)
- [ ] P1 Fuels: hydrocarbons such as methane (natural gas), petrol, diesel and kerosene, and alcohols such as ethanol, release energy when they burn; used for transport, heating and electricity generation.
- [ ] P2 Pharmaceuticals: medicines are almost all organic compounds, e.g. aspirin and paracetamol (pain relief), penicillin (antibiotic); see "Organic compounds in pharmaceuticals".
- [ ] P3 Plastics: polymers made mainly from oil-derived monomers, e.g. poly(ethene) from ethene (bags, bottles, film), poly(chloroethene) (PVC) from chloroethene (pipes, window frames); light, cheap, durable, waterproof, insulating; see "Types of organic reaction and addition polymers".
- [ ] P4 Pesticides: compounds that kill or control pests: insecticides (insects), herbicides (weeds) and fungicides (fungi); natural examples include neem oil and pyrethrins (from chrysanthemum flowers); synthetic examples include malathion (an insecticide) and glyphosate (a herbicide).
- [ ] P5 Synthetics: man-made materials that replace or improve on natural ones, e.g. synthetic fibres (nylon and polyester, made from monomers that link into polymers, used for clothing, ropes and carpets), synthetic dyes, detergents and synthetic rubber.
- [ ] P6 Other everyday organic products: solvents (ethanol in perfumes and deodorants; propanone in nail varnish remover), flavourings and fragrances (esters), food preservatives (ethanoic acid in vinegar; sodium benzoate), soaps and detergents (see "Surfactants and soap").

Benefits and impacts (one block)
- [ ] B1 Benefits: energy for transport and heating; longer, healthier lives through medicines and sanitation; higher crop yields and less food lost to pests; cheap, light, durable materials; clothing and goods for large populations.
- [ ] B2 Fuels: burning fossil fuels releases carbon dioxide (a greenhouse gas linked to climate change) and pollutants such as carbon monoxide, nitrogen oxides and soot; see "Hydrocarbons: sources, uses and impact".
- [ ] B3 Plastics: most are not biodegradable, so they persist for decades or longer as litter, in landfill and in the oceans, where they harm wildlife and break into microplastics; burning them can release toxic gases.
- [ ] B4 Pesticides: some are toxic to non-target organisms such as bees, birds and fish; persistent pesticides such as DDT build up in food chains (bioaccumulation) and were banned for agricultural use in most countries; neonicotinoid insecticides were restricted in the EU (2018) because of harm to bees; run-off can contaminate water.
- [ ] B5 Pharmaceuticals: side effects, addiction (e.g. opiates), antibiotic resistance from overuse, and drug residues reaching waterways.
- [ ] B6 Resources: fossil feedstocks are finite, and extracting and processing them uses energy and can cause spills and pollution.

Judging a product (one block)
- [ ] J1 A product's impact is assessed across its whole life: raw material, manufacture, use and disposal (a life-cycle view).
- [ ] J2 Ways to reduce impact: recycling and reuse; renewable feedstocks; biodegradable alternatives; designing safer molecules that break down after use; using less (e.g. targeted pesticide use).
- [ ] J3 A natural source is not automatically safer or more sustainable: natural compounds can be toxic, and growing biomass uses land, water and fertiliser.
- [ ] J4 A balanced answer names a benefit and an impact, and says how the impact can be reduced.

### 3. Traps

- Fuel quality (octane numbers, cracking, reforming, oxygenates) is not on the specification; don't describe it.
- Don't say CFCs or the ozone layer; they are not on the specification.
- "Organic" in this sense means carbon compounds, not "organic farming".
- Don't present synthetic products as all harmful or natural products as all safe (J3).

### 4. What exams ask (topics only)

- Name sources of organic compounds; give examples of products made from them.
- Give a use of a named organic compound (e.g. ethanol, propanone, ethanoic acid, esters).
- Outline a benefit and an environmental or health impact of a type of organic product (plastics, pesticides, fuels).

### 5. Practice questions to write

2. 9 marks: "Products based on organic compounds include plastics and pesticides. For each, give one benefit to society and one impact on the environment, and suggest how that impact could be reduced." Model answer: plastics P3, B3, J2 (recycling, biodegradable plastics); pesticides P4, B4, J2 (targeted use, less persistent compounds).
3. 6 marks: "Write a balanced equation for the fermentation of glucose to ethanol, and explain why fermentation cannot produce a drink stronger than about 15% ethanol." Model answer: S3 equation; S4.

## Part: Organic compounds in pharmaceuticals (formerly `chem24-s6`)

- **loId:** `chem24-s6` · **chapterId:** `chem24` · **syllabusRef:** 4.2.15
- **Syllabus:** 4.2.15 "explore the use of organic compounds in pharmaceutical products" (RI:
  Research-based Investigation). Learn about: "the use of organic compounds in natural products
  for medicinal/narcotic use (examples include, but not limited to, willow bark, cinchona bark
  and opium poppy); how most products of the modern pharmaceutical industry are large complex
  organic molecules with multiple functional groups made from simpler organic precursors,
  including, but not limited to aspirin, penicillin, taxol and opiates". Normal type.
- **Prepared by:** a higher model, 2026-10-30. Key terms, the three named natural sources, the four
  named modern pharmaceuticals (each with source, functional groups, use and the chemistry of
  how it is made), why chemists synthesise and modify natural products, and a worked research
  brief. Functional-group names are in "Functional groups, naming and representation"; the yield of
  multi-step syntheses in "Theoretical, actual and percentage yield". The general method for
  researching (sources, evaluation, referencing) is in "Researching chemistry"; apply it here
  briefly.

### 1. Must cover

Key terms (one block)
- [ ] K1 A pharmaceutical (drug or medicine) is a substance used to prevent, diagnose, treat or relieve the symptoms of disease; almost all are organic compounds.
- [ ] K2 A natural product is an organic compound made by a living organism (plant, animal or micro-organism), e.g. quinine in cinchona bark, morphine in the opium poppy, penicillin from a mould.
- [ ] K3 A narcotic is a drug that dulls pain and causes drowsiness or sleep; many (e.g. morphine and heroin) are addictive and are legally controlled.
- [ ] K4 A precursor is a starting compound from which another compound is made, e.g. salicylic acid is the precursor of aspirin.
- [ ] K5 The active ingredient is the compound in a medicine that produces its effect; the rest of a tablet (fillers, binders, coatings) is inactive.
- [ ] K6 Biotechnology is the use of living organisms or biological systems to make useful products, e.g. making penicillin by fermentation.

Medicines from nature (one block; one bullet per source)
- [ ] N1 Willow bark: used to relieve pain and fever since ancient times (its use in ancient Greek medicine is traditionally credited to Hippocrates, about 400 BC); it contains salicin, which the body converts into salicylic acid, the active compound.
- [ ] N2 Cinchona bark (from trees native to the Andes of South America): contains quinine, used from the 1600s to treat malaria; quinine is also the bitter flavour in tonic water.
- [ ] N3 Opium poppy: the dried latex (sap) from the unripe seed pods is opium, which contains morphine (a powerful painkiller) and codeine (a milder painkiller and cough suppressant); morphine was one of the first active compounds to be isolated pure from a plant, by Friedrich Sertürner in the early 1800s.
- [ ] N4 Other examples: the coca plant gives cocaine (an addictive, illegal stimulant, once used as a local anaesthetic); sweet wormwood gives artemisinin, a modern anti-malarial.
- [ ] N5 Natural products often occur in tiny amounts, vary with season, weather and location, and may come from rare or slow-growing organisms, so extraction alone is often not enough.

Aspirin (one block)
- [ ] A1 Salicylic acid relieves pain but irritates the mouth and stomach; in 1897 Felix Hoffmann, a chemist at the German company Bayer, made acetylsalicylic acid from it, which was sold from 1899 as Aspirin.
- [ ] A2 Aspirin is made by converting the –OH group on the benzene ring of salicylic acid into an ester group (reacting it with ethanoic anhydride, a derivative of ethanoic acid); salicylic acid, C₇H₆O₃, is the precursor.
- [ ] A3 Aspirin, C₉H₈O₄ (M = 180), contains three functional groups: a benzene ring, a carboxylic acid group (–COOH) and an ester group (–OCOCH₃).
- [ ] A4 Uses: relieves pain, reduces fever and inflammation; low daily doses reduce blood clotting and are prescribed to lower the risk of heart attack and stroke.
- [ ] A5 Aspirin irritates the stomach less than salicylic acid but can still cause stomach irritation and bleeding; it is not given to children under 16 because of the risk of Reye's syndrome.

Penicillin (one block)
- [ ] P1 Alexander Fleming discovered in 1928 that the mould Penicillium killed bacteria; Howard Florey and Ernst Chain purified penicillin and showed it worked in patients around 1940; the three shared the 1945 Nobel Prize.
- [ ] P2 Penicillin is an antibiotic: it kills bacteria by stopping them building their cell walls; it has no effect on viruses.
- [ ] P3 Its key structural feature is a four-membered ring containing nitrogen and a C=O (the β-lactam ring), joined to a ring containing sulfur; penicillin G is C₁₆H₁₈N₂O₄S and also contains a carboxylic acid group.
- [ ] P4 It is made on a large scale by fermentation (biotechnology): the mould is grown in large tanks of nutrient broth, a process scaled up during the Second World War.
- [ ] P5 Semi-synthetic penicillins, such as amoxicillin, are made by chemically changing the side chain of the penicillin made by fermentation; they work against more types of bacteria or are better absorbed.
- [ ] P6 Overuse and misuse of antibiotics has led to antibiotic-resistant bacteria; some bacteria make enzymes that break the β-lactam ring.

Taxol (one block)
- [ ] T1 Taxol (paclitaxel) is an anti-cancer drug (used, for example, for ovarian, breast and lung cancers); it stops cancer cells dividing.
- [ ] T2 It was found in the bark of the Pacific yew tree in a US plant-screening programme in the 1960s; its structure was published in 1971.
- [ ] T3 It is a very large, complex molecule, C₄₇H₅₁NO₁₄, with many functional groups, including several ester groups, hydroxyl (–OH) groups, a ketone, an amide and three benzene rings.
- [ ] T4 Stripping the bark kills the slow-growing tree and gives very little taxol, so this was not sustainable.
- [ ] T5 It is now made semi-synthetically from a precursor extracted from the needles of the European yew (which regrow, so the tree is not killed), or by growing yew cells in fermentation tanks; complete laboratory synthesis (first achieved in 1994) needs too many steps to be economic.

Opiates (one block)
- [ ] O1 Opiates are the natural compounds of opium, morphine and codeine, and drugs made directly from them; opioids is the wider term that also includes fully synthetic drugs (e.g. fentanyl).
- [ ] O2 Morphine, C₁₇H₁₉NO₃, has several rings and functional groups, including two –OH groups, an ether link, a C=C double bond and an amine nitrogen.
- [ ] O3 Small changes to a functional group change the effect: codeine is morphine with one –OH converted to –OCH₃ (milder); heroin (diamorphine) is morphine with both –OH groups converted to ester groups (stronger and more addictive).
- [ ] O4 Opiates are essential for severe pain (e.g. after surgery and in cancer care) but cause addiction, tolerance and, in overdose, fatal slowing of breathing; they are tightly controlled.

How modern medicines are made (one block)
- [ ] M1 Most modern pharmaceuticals are large, complex organic molecules with several functional groups, each of which may be needed for the drug to bind to its target in the body.
- [ ] M2 They are built in a sequence of reactions from simpler organic precursors, many derived from crude oil or from natural sources; each step changes or adds a functional group.
- [ ] M3 Because every step loses some product, long syntheses have low overall yields, which raises cost and waste (see "Theoretical, actual and percentage yield").
- [ ] M4 Why chemists synthesise or modify natural products: a reliable supply that doesn't depend on harvests or rare organisms; pure products of consistent strength; changing the structure to improve the drug (fewer side effects, stronger effect, better absorption, e.g. aspirin from salicylic acid, amoxicillin from penicillin); and large-scale, lower-cost production.
- [ ] M5 Other common organic medicines: paracetamol (C₈H₉NO₂, pain and fever relief) and ibuprofen (pain and inflammation).
- [ ] M6 New drugs must be tested for safety and effectiveness in clinical trials and approved by a regulator before use; in Ireland the regulator is the Health Products Regulatory Authority (HPRA), and in the EU the European Medicines Agency (EMA).

Research investigation: worked brief (one block)
- [ ] R1 What the specification expects: research a use of organic compounds in pharmaceuticals, using evaluated sources, and present the chemistry involved, the benefits and the issues, with a justified conclusion.
- [ ] R2 Example question: "Should medicines that come from plants be extracted from the plant or made in the laboratory?" (taking taxol and aspirin as case studies). Other questions: how did chemists turn willow bark into aspirin; how has antibiotic resistance changed the use of penicillin; how can opiates be used safely.
- [ ] R3 Sources: textbooks; peer-reviewed journals and review articles; official bodies (HPRA, EMA, the World Health Organization, the HSE); PubChem for structures and formulae; museum and university websites for history.
- [ ] R4 Judging sources: who wrote it and their expertise; whether it is peer reviewed; how recent it is; whether it has a commercial or other bias (e.g. a drug company's advertising); whether other reliable sources agree.
- [ ] R5 Chemistry to include: the structure and functional groups of the compound; its natural source; how it is extracted or made from precursors (e.g. ester formation in aspirin; semi-synthesis of taxol); why the structural change improves it.
- [ ] R6 Issues to weigh: health benefits against side effects and addiction; sustainability of natural sources (taxol and the Pacific yew) and protecting biodiversity, where future medicines may be found; antibiotic resistance; cost and access to medicines worldwide; drug residues in the environment.
- [ ] R7 Conclusion: answer the question with evidence from both sides, e.g. semi-synthesis of taxol from renewable yew needles combines a natural precursor with laboratory chemistry and is more sustainable than either bark extraction or full synthesis.

### 3. Traps

- Don't say aspirin causes no stomach irritation; it causes less than salicylic acid.
- Willow bark contains salicin, not aspirin; aspirin is a synthetic compound.
- Antibiotics act on bacteria, not viruses.
- Opiates are natural or made directly from opium; "opioid" is the wider term.
- Don't invent step counts, yields or dates for syntheses; use only those in the table.
- Mechanisms of drug action and structural drawings of whole drug molecules are not required; name the functional groups present.

### 4. What exams ask (topics only)

- Define a natural product; give examples with their plant source, active compound and use.
- Explain why it is important that natural products can be synthesised or modified in the laboratory.
- Identify functional groups in a given drug structure (e.g. aspirin).
- Discuss a benefit and an issue linked to a pharmaceutical (addiction, resistance, sustainability).
