## Part: Physical properties of organic compounds (formerly `chem24-s4`)

- **loId:** `chem24-s4` · **chapterId:** `chem24` · **syllabusRef:** 4.2.5
- **Syllabus:** 4.2.5 "**relate the physical properties of organic molecules to molecular size,
  type of bonding present**". Learn about: "**how solubility, melting point, boiling point are
  influenced by molecular structures and properties**". All bold (checked in the PDF): the
  whole subsection is Higher Level only. Mark every block heading " (Higher Level only)".
- **Prepared by:** a higher model, 2026-10-30. The forces present in each family, comparisons at
  similar molecular mass, trends with chain length and branching, melting points, and
  solubility, using checked data. The intermolecular forces themselves (London, dipole–dipole,
  hydrogen bonding) are defined in "Bond type and intermolecular forces" and the
  general link to boiling point in "Polarity, shape and physical properties"; the
  properties of alkanes and alkenes are in "Groups of hydrocarbons and their properties". Apply them
  to the functional-group families here; don't redefine them. Electronegativities (Pauling):
  O 3.44, Cl 3.16, C 2.55, H 2.20.

### 1. Must cover

The forces in each family (Higher Level only; one block; one bullet per family)
- [ ] F1 [HL] Melting and boiling separate molecules, so they depend on the intermolecular forces, not on the covalent bonds inside the molecules; the stronger the forces, the more energy is needed and the higher the melting and boiling points.
- [ ] F2 [HL] Alkanes (and alkenes): non-polar; London dispersion forces only.
- [ ] F3 [HL] Haloalkanes: the C–Cl bond is polar (Cl 3.16, C 2.55), so there are permanent dipole–dipole forces as well as London forces, but no hydrogen bonding (no H on O, N or F).
- [ ] F4 [HL] Aldehydes and ketones: the C=O bond is strongly polar (Cδ+=Oδ−), giving dipole–dipole forces; they cannot hydrogen bond with each other because their hydrogens are bonded to carbon, not oxygen.
- [ ] F5 [HL] Esters: polar C=O and C–O bonds give dipole–dipole forces, but no O–H, so no hydrogen bonding between ester molecules.
- [ ] F6 [HL] Alcohols: the O–H bond is very polar (O 3.44, H 2.20), so alcohol molecules hydrogen bond to each other, as well as having London forces.
- [ ] F7 [HL] Carboxylic acids: the –COOH group has both O–H and C=O, so the molecules hydrogen bond strongly; in the pure liquid, pairs of molecules form dimers held by two hydrogen bonds (the O–H of each to the C=O of the other).
- [ ] F8 [HL] Diagram: two ethanoic acid molecules facing each other, with dashed hydrogen bonds from each O–H hydrogen to the C=O oxygen of the other molecule (a dimer).

Comparing compounds of similar size (Higher Level only; one block)
- [ ] S1 [HL] At similar relative molecular mass (so similar London forces), boiling point depends on the type of intermolecular force: London only < dipole–dipole < hydrogen bonding.
- [ ] S2 [HL] Data (Mr, bp): butane (58) −1 °C; methyl methanoate (60) 32 °C; propanal (58) 48 °C; propanone (58) 56 °C; propan-1-ol (60) 97 °C; ethanoic acid (60) 118 °C.
- [ ] S3 [HL] Propanal boils far higher than butane because its polar C=O gives dipole–dipole forces in addition to London forces.
- [ ] S4 [HL] Propan-1-ol boils higher than propanal because its O–H groups form hydrogen bonds between molecules, which are stronger than dipole–dipole forces.
- [ ] S5 [HL] Ethanoic acid boils higher than propan-1-ol because each acid molecule has two oxygens taking part and pairs of molecules form doubly hydrogen-bonded dimers, so more hydrogen bonding must be overcome.
- [ ] S6 [HL] Ethanoic acid (bp 118 °C) and methyl methanoate (bp 32 °C) are isomers (C₂H₄O₂) with the same mass: the acid hydrogen bonds; the ester can't, as it has no O–H.
- [ ] S7 [HL] Ethanol (bp 78 °C) against ethane (bp −89 °C): hydrogen bonding against London forces only.

Effect of molecular size (Higher Level only; one block)
- [ ] Z1 [HL] Within a homologous series, boiling point rises with chain length: each extra CH₂ adds electrons and surface area, so London forces get stronger.
- [ ] Z2 [HL] Alcohols: methanol 65 °C; ethanol 78 °C; propan-1-ol 97 °C; butan-1-ol 117 °C; pentan-1-ol 138 °C; hexan-1-ol 157 °C (a rise of about 20 °C per CH₂).
- [ ] Z3 [HL] Carboxylic acids: methanoic 101 °C; ethanoic 118 °C; propanoic 141 °C; butanoic 164 °C. Aldehydes: methanal −19 °C (a gas); ethanal 20 °C; propanal 48 °C; butanal 75 °C.
- [ ] Z4 [HL] Chloroalkanes: chloromethane −24 °C and chloroethane 12 °C are gases at room temperature; 1-chloropropane 47 °C and 1-chlorobutane 78 °C are liquids. Adding more chlorines also raises the boiling point: CH₃Cl −24 °C; CH₂Cl₂ 40 °C; CHCl₃ 61 °C; CCl₄ 77 °C (more electrons, stronger London forces).
- [ ] Z5 [HL] As the chain gets longer, the hydrocarbon part makes up more of the molecule, so the difference between families gets smaller (the functional group's effect is diluted).

Effect of shape and branching (Higher Level only; one block)
- [ ] B1 [HL] Branched isomers have lower boiling points than straight-chain isomers: they are more compact, with less surface contact between molecules, so London forces are weaker (for alkanes see "Groups of hydrocarbons and their properties").
- [ ] B2 [HL] C₄H₉OH isomers: butan-1-ol 117 °C; 2-methylpropan-1-ol 108 °C; butan-2-ol 99 °C; 2-methylpropan-2-ol 82 °C; the more compact the molecule (and the more crowded the –OH), the lower the boiling point.
- [ ] B3 [HL] Exam skill: from a table or graph of boiling point against number of carbons, describe the trend, explain it, and predict the next member by continuing the trend (e.g. continuing the primary alcohols from butan-1-ol, 117 °C, gives about 138 °C for pentan-1-ol).

Melting points (Higher Level only; one block)
- [ ] M1 [HL] Melting points depend on the intermolecular forces and also on how well the molecules pack together in the solid, so they follow trends less regularly than boiling points.
- [ ] M2 [HL] Ethanoic acid melts at 17 °C, high for its size, because of strong hydrogen bonding; on a cold day the pure acid freezes to an ice-like solid, so it is called glacial ethanoic acid.
- [ ] M3 [HL] Compact, symmetrical molecules pack well and melt higher: 2-methylpropan-2-ol melts at about 25 °C, but butan-1-ol at −89 °C; cyclohexane melts at 6.5 °C, but hexane at −95 °C.
- [ ] M4 [HL] Ionic bonding gives far higher melting points: sodium ethanoate (an ionic salt of ethanoic acid) is a solid that melts at about 324 °C.

Solubility (Higher Level only; one block)
- [ ] W1 [HL] "Like dissolves like": a compound dissolves in water if it can form hydrogen bonds with water molecules; non-polar compounds dissolve in non-polar solvents such as cyclohexane, where London forces are similar.
- [ ] W2 [HL] Lower alcohols (methanol, ethanol, propan-1-ol, propan-2-ol) and lower carboxylic acids (methanoic to butanoic acid) mix with water in all proportions (are miscible), because their –OH groups hydrogen bond with water.
- [ ] W3 [HL] Solubility in water falls as the chain gets longer: the non-polar hydrocarbon part cannot hydrogen bond and disrupts the water's hydrogen bonding, so butan-1-ol is only partly soluble and hexan-1-ol is only slightly soluble; longer chains dissolve better in non-polar solvents.
- [ ] W4 [HL] Small aldehydes and ketones (methanal, ethanal, propanone) are miscible with water: they have no O–H, but water molecules can hydrogen bond to the lone pairs on their C=O oxygen. Propanone dissolves both polar and non-polar substances, so it is a useful solvent.
- [ ] W5 [HL] Small esters are slightly soluble in water (ethyl ethanoate about 8 g per 100 g of water) for the same reason (water H-bonds to their oxygens), and dissolve well in organic solvents.
- [ ] W6 [HL] Alkanes, alkenes, benzene and haloalkanes are insoluble in water (no hydrogen bonding with water) but dissolve in non-polar solvents; haloalkanes are good solvents for grease and oil.
- [ ] W7 [HL] Benzoic acid is only slightly soluble in cold water (large non-polar benzene ring) but much more soluble in hot water, which allows it to be purified by recrystallisation (see "Investigation: synthesising benzoic acid").
- [ ] W8 [HL] Ionic salts of carboxylic acids (sodium ethanoate, sodium benzoate) are much more soluble in water than the acids, because their ions are attracted to water molecules (ion–dipole attraction).

### 3. Traps

- Boiling doesn't break covalent bonds; it overcomes forces between molecules.
- Aldehydes, ketones and esters cannot hydrogen bond to each other, but can accept hydrogen bonds from water: this is why they are more soluble than their boiling points suggest.
- Compare boiling points only between molecules of similar size when arguing about the type of force; otherwise London forces from size can dominate.
- Not all chloroalkanes are liquids: chloromethane and chloroethane are gases at room temperature.
- Butanone is not fully miscible with water; only the smallest ketone (propanone) is.

### 4. What exams ask (topics only)

- Explain why one compound has a higher boiling point than another (propanal vs butane; ethanoic acid vs propan-1-ol; ethanoic acid vs methyl methanoate; ethanol vs ethane).
- Explain why the lower alcohols and acids are soluble in water and why solubility falls with chain length.
- Plot or interpret boiling points of a homologous series against carbon number; state and explain the trend; predict a value.
- Explain the name "glacial ethanoic acid".

## Part: Acidity of carboxylic acids and alcohols (formerly `chem24-s5`)

- **loId:** `chem24-s5` · **chapterId:** `chem24` · **syllabusRef:** 4.2.12
- **Syllabus:** 4.2.12 "**explain the acidity of carboxylic acid and alcohol functional
  groups**". Learn about: "**inductive effects in carboxylic acids, resonance of the
  carboxylate ion; polarity in the alcohol structure**". All bold (checked in the PDF): the
  whole subsection is Higher Level only. Mark every block heading " (Higher Level only)".
- **Prepared by:** a higher model, 2026-10-30. Why the O–H can lose H⁺, why carboxylic acids are
  much stronger acids than alcohols (resonance of the carboxylate ion and inductive effects),
  substituent effects with Ka data, and the evidence from reactions. Weak acids, Ka and pKa are
  defined in "Acid–base theories and strength" and "pH calculations and dissociation constants"; the
  laboratory test for the acidic nature of carboxylic acids (magnesium, sodium carbonate,
  sodium hydroxide) is in "Tests, reaction schemes and preparing an ester". Use them; don't
  repeat the test method. Electronegativities (Pauling): O 3.44, Cl 3.16, C 2.55, H 2.20.

### 1. Must cover

The O–H bond and acidity (Higher Level only; one block)
- [ ] P1 [HL] An acid (Brønsted–Lowry) is a proton donor; both alcohols and carboxylic acids can donate the H⁺ of their O–H group.
- [ ] P2 [HL] The O–H bond is polar because oxygen (3.44) is much more electronegative than hydrogen (2.20): the shared electrons are pulled towards O, leaving the hydrogen δ+, so it can be removed as H⁺ by a base.
- [ ] P3 [HL] C–H bonds are almost non-polar (C 2.55, H 2.20), so hydrogens bonded to carbon are not acidic; in ethanoic acid, CH₃COOH, only the H on oxygen is lost.
- [ ] P4 [HL] How acidic the O–H is depends mainly on how stable the negative ion (the conjugate base) left behind is: the more stable the anion, the more easily H⁺ is lost and the stronger the acid.

Alcohols: very weak acids (Higher Level only; one block)
- [ ] L1 [HL] An alcohol can lose H⁺ to form an alkoxide ion: R–OH ⇌ R–O⁻ + H⁺, e.g. ethanol gives the ethoxide ion, C₂H₅O⁻.
- [ ] L2 [HL] In the alkoxide ion the negative charge is concentrated on one oxygen atom; it cannot be spread out (no resonance), so the ion is unstable and readily takes back H⁺.
- [ ] L3 [HL] The alkyl group is electron-releasing (it pushes electron density towards the oxygen), which makes the negative charge on the alkoxide ion even less stable, so alcohols are weaker acids than water.
- [ ] L4 [HL] Alcohols are therefore neutral in water: they don't change the colour of indicators, don't react with sodium carbonate, and don't react to any useful extent with sodium hydroxide.
- [ ] L5 [HL] Evidence of their acidic O–H: alcohols react with sodium metal, giving hydrogen gas and a salt, e.g. 2C₂H₅OH(l) + 2Na(s) → 2C₂H₅ONa + H₂(g) (sodium ethoxide); the reaction is gentler than sodium with water, showing ethanol is a weaker acid than water.

Carboxylic acids: resonance of the carboxylate ion (Higher Level only; one block)
- [ ] R1 [HL] A carboxylic acid loses H⁺ to form a carboxylate ion: RCOOH(aq) ⇌ RCOO⁻(aq) + H⁺(aq), e.g. CH₃COOH ⇌ CH₃COO⁻ + H⁺ (ethanoate ion).
- [ ] R2 [HL] In the carboxylate ion the negative charge is not fixed on one oxygen: it is delocalised (shared) over both oxygen atoms through the O–C–O group; the ion is a resonance hybrid of two structures, in each of which a different oxygen carries the negative charge.
- [ ] R3 [HL] Evidence: the two C–O bonds in a carboxylate ion are the same length, between that of a C–O single bond and a C=O double bond; in the acid itself the C=O bond is shorter than the C–O bond.
- [ ] R4 [HL] Spreading the charge over two oxygens stabilises the carboxylate ion, so the carboxylic acid loses H⁺ much more readily than an alcohol, whose alkoxide ion has no such stabilisation.
- [ ] R5 [HL] Diagram: the two resonance structures of the ethanoate ion, CH₃–C(=O)–O⁻ ↔ CH₃–C(–O⁻)=O, joined by a double-headed arrow, and the hybrid drawn with a dashed bond over O–C–O and a charge of −½ on each oxygen.

Carboxylic acids: inductive effects (Higher Level only; one block)
- [ ] I1 [HL] The inductive effect is the pulling (or pushing) of electron density along sigma bonds by an atom or group because of its electronegativity.
- [ ] I2 [HL] In the –COOH group, the electronegative oxygen of the C=O pulls electron density away from the O–H bond, making the O–H more polar and the hydrogen easier to lose than in an alcohol.
- [ ] I3 [HL] Electron-withdrawing groups such as chlorine attached to the carbon chain pull electron density towards themselves, spreading out the negative charge of the carboxylate ion and stabilising it, so the acid is stronger; more chlorine atoms, or chlorine nearer the –COOH, give a stronger effect.
- [ ] I4 [HL] Alkyl groups are electron-releasing; they push electron density towards the –COO⁻ group, destabilising it, so the acid is weaker: methanoic acid (H on the –COOH) is stronger than ethanoic acid (CH₃ on the –COOH), which is slightly stronger than propanoic acid.
- [ ] I5 [HL] Ka data (25 °C): methanoic acid 1.8 × 10⁻⁴; ethanoic acid 1.8 × 10⁻⁵; propanoic acid 1.3 × 10⁻⁵; chloroethanoic acid 1.4 × 10⁻³; trichloroethanoic acid about 0.2. Order of strength: CCl₃COOH > CH₂ClCOOH > HCOOH > CH₃COOH > CH₃CH₂COOH.

Comparing and the evidence (Higher Level only; one block)
- [ ] C1 [HL] Carboxylic acids are weak acids, only partly dissociated: 0.1 mol L⁻¹ ethanoic acid is about 1.3% dissociated and has a pH of about 2.9.
- [ ] C2 [HL] They are still far stronger acids than alcohols (ethanoic acid Ka 1.8 × 10⁻⁵; ethanol about 10⁻¹⁶), because of the resonance-stabilised carboxylate ion and the inductive effect of the C=O.
- [ ] C3 [HL] So carboxylic acids show typical acid properties that alcohols don't: they turn blue litmus red, fizz with sodium carbonate giving carbon dioxide, react with magnesium giving hydrogen, and are neutralised by sodium hydroxide (e.g. CH₃COOH + NaOH → CH₃COONa + H₂O); details are in "Tests, reaction schemes and preparing an ester".
- [ ] C4 [HL] Summary table: with sodium metal: alcohol reacts (H₂), acid reacts (H₂); with NaOH: alcohol no useful reaction, acid neutralised; with Na₂CO₃: alcohol no reaction, acid fizzes (CO₂); with indicator: alcohol neutral, acid acidic.
- [ ] C5 [HL] Explaining a difference in acidity: say which anion is more stable and why (resonance; electron-withdrawing or electron-releasing groups), then conclude which acid loses H⁺ more easily.

### 3. Traps

- The acidic hydrogen is the one on oxygen; CH₃COOH is monoprotic, not tetraprotic.
- Don't say the carboxylate charge is "on the carbon"; it is shared between the two oxygens.
- Electron-withdrawing groups make the acid stronger; electron-releasing (alkyl) groups make it weaker.
- Carboxylic acids are weak acids; "stronger than alcohols" does not mean "strong".
- Alcohols react with sodium but not with sodium carbonate or (usefully) with sodium hydroxide; don't use sodium carbonate to show the acidity of ethanol.
- Ka calculations belong to "pH calculations and dissociation constants"; here Ka values are only used for comparison.
