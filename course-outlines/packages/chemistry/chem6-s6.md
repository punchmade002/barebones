# Package: chem6-s6 — Shape, polarity and properties

- **loId:** `chem6-s6` · **chapterId:** `chem6` · **syllabusRef:** 2.2.4
- **Syllabus:** 2.2.4 "use the shapes and polarity of molecules of simple compounds to predict
  physical properties". Learn about: "how the shapes of molecules can determine overall polarity
  and also influence physical properties". Normal type: Ordinary and Higher level.
- **Prepared by:** a higher model, 2026-10-30. Ordinary-level treatment: given a shape and
  polarity, predict boiling point and solubility; include the charged-rod demonstration as an
  investigation. Write it without VSEPR (Higher Level) reasoning: state shapes as given facts.
  The full symmetry explanation is in "Polarity and symmetry".

## 1. Must cover

Shape decides overall polarity (one block)
- [ ] S1 A molecule with polar bonds is polar overall only if its shape is unsymmetrical, so that the charge is unevenly spread.
- [ ] S2 Water is V-shaped (bent): it is polar.
- [ ] S3 Ammonia is pyramidal: it is polar.
- [ ] S4 Carbon dioxide is linear and methane is tetrahedral: both are non-polar.

Predicting physical properties (one block)
- [ ] P1 Polar molecules have stronger forces between them than non-polar molecules of similar size, so they have higher boiling points.
- [ ] P2 Polar molecules tend to dissolve in polar solvents such as water; non-polar molecules dissolve in non-polar solvents such as cyclohexane.
- [ ] P3 Prediction example: water (polar, hydrogen bonds) is a liquid at room temperature and dissolves salts; methane (non-polar) is a gas and is almost insoluble in water.
- [ ] P4 Prediction example: ammonia (polar) is very soluble in water; carbon dioxide (non-polar) is only slightly soluble.

Investigation: testing liquids for polarity with a charged rod (one block)
- [ ] I1 Aim: to show whether a liquid is made of polar molecules.
- [ ] I2 Equipment: a burette clamped above a beaker, a polythene (or acetate) rod, a cloth, and liquids such as water and cyclohexane.
- [ ] I3 Step 1: Charge the rod by rubbing it with the cloth.
- [ ] I4 Step 2: Let a thin stream of the liquid run from the burette and hold the rod close to the stream without touching it.
- [ ] I5 Step 3: Repeat with the other liquid, recharging the rod each time.
- [ ] I6 Results: the stream of water is deflected (attracted) towards the rod; the stream of cyclohexane is not deflected.
- [ ] I7 Explanation: polar molecules turn so that their oppositely charged end faces the rod and are attracted to it; non-polar molecules are not.
- [ ] I8 Safety: cyclohexane is flammable and harmful: no flames, small volumes, good ventilation, collect it for disposal.
- [ ] I9 Diagram: a burette releasing a thin stream of liquid into a beaker, with a charged rod held beside the stream and the stream bending towards it.

## 2. Checked facts and examples

Only the molecules and liquids above.

## 3. Traps

- Don't use VSEPR or symmetry arguments to derive shapes in this Ordinary-level subsection; state the shape.
- The stream of water is attracted whether the rod is positive or negative (the molecules rotate).

## 4. What exams ask (topics only)

- Describe how to show that water is polar using a charged rod.
- Predict the polarity and a property of a molecule from its shape.

## 5. Practice questions to write

1. 9 marks: "Describe an experiment to show that water molecules are polar and cyclohexane molecules are not, and explain the results." Model answer: I2–I7.
2. 6 marks: "Methane and water have similar relative molecular masses. Use the shape and polarity of each molecule to explain why water is a liquid at room temperature and methane is a gas." Model answer: S2, S4, P1, P3.
