## Part: 3D models and formulae (formerly `chem23-s7`)

- **loId:** `chem23-s7` · **chapterId:** `chem23` · **syllabusRef:** 2.4.7
- **Syllabus:** 2.4.7 "construct and examine 3D models of hydrocarbon molecules and explain how
  bonding and isomers influence the spatial arrangement of atoms for these molecules". Learn
  about: "how structure, and some characteristic properties of hydrocarbons, can be predicted
  through bonding and spatial arrangements of atoms"; "the way 3D models also relate to the
  condensed and expanded molecular formulae of the hydrocarbons"; "structural isomers
  exemplified by alkanes and alkenes up to C6 … which can be visualised using diagrams, 3D
  models and digital models". Normal type (the cis–trans part of that last point is bold and is
  in "Structural and geometric isomerism").
- **Prepared by:** a higher model, 2026-10-30. A practical activity with model kits; the kinds of
  formula; how single, double and triple bonds fix the arrangement of atoms; and the structural
  isomers of alkanes and alkenes up to C6. Bond angles are explained in "Shapes: sigma and pi
  bonds"; boiling-point differences between isomers are in "Explaining and predicting
  properties"; naming rules are in "Groups of hydrocarbons and their properties"; formulae of other families are in
  "Functional groups, naming and representation".

### 1. Must cover

Kinds of formula (one block)
- [ ] F1 Molecular formula: the number of atoms of each element in one molecule, e.g. C₄H₁₀; it doesn't show how the atoms are joined.
- [ ] F2 Expanded (full or displayed) structural formula: shows every atom and every bond as a line, e.g. butane drawn with four C atoms in a row, each H joined by its own line.
- [ ] F3 Condensed structural formula: shows the groups on each carbon in order without drawing the bonds, e.g. butane CH₃CH₂CH₂CH₃ (or CH₃(CH₂)₂CH₃); methylpropane CH₃CH(CH₃)CH₃; but-1-ene CH₂=CHCH₂CH₃ (double and triple bonds are always shown).
- [ ] F4 A 3D model shows the real spatial arrangement of the atoms, which a flat expanded formula can't: e.g. an expanded formula draws methane's bonds at 90°, but in 3D they point to the corners of a tetrahedron.
- [ ] F5 Two flat drawings that look different can be the same molecule (e.g. a four-carbon chain drawn bent or straight is still butane); building the model shows whether they are identical.

Constructing and examining models (one block; practical activity)
- [ ] K1 Use a molecular model kit: black balls with four holes for carbon, white balls with one hole for hydrogen, short rigid links for single bonds and flexible links (springs) for double and triple bonds.
- [ ] K2 Build methane, ethane, propane and butane; note that each carbon with four single bonds is tetrahedral, so a "straight" chain is actually a zig-zag.
- [ ] K3 Rotate the two ends of ethane about the C–C bond: rotation is free about a single bond, and the different positions (conformations) are still the same molecule.
- [ ] K4 Build ethene with two flexible links: the molecule is flat and the C=C bond can't be twisted.
- [ ] K5 Build ethyne with three flexible links: the four atoms lie in a straight line.
- [ ] K6 Build all the isomers of C₄H₁₀ and C₅H₁₂; try to build "another" isomer and check by rotating whether it is really new or the same as one already made.
- [ ] K7 Record each model as an expanded and a condensed formula and give its IUPAC name.
- [ ] K8 Digital models (molecule-viewing software or apps) can be rotated on screen and show ball-and-stick and space-filling views.
- [ ] K9 Limitations of models: ball-and-stick models exaggerate the gaps between atoms and aren't to scale; space-filling models show size and shape but hide the bonds; no model shows the electrons, and real molecules are constantly vibrating and rotating.
- [ ] K10 Diagram: ball-and-stick models of methane (tetrahedral), ethene (planar) and ethyne (linear), labelled with the bond angles.

How bonding fixes the arrangement of atoms (one block)
- [ ] B1 A carbon with four single bonds is tetrahedral (109.5°) and the groups can rotate about each single bond.
- [ ] B2 A carbon in a C=C double bond is trigonal planar (120°); the double bond holds the two carbons and the four atoms attached to them in one plane and prevents rotation.
- [ ] B3 A carbon in a C≡C triple bond is linear (180°).
- [ ] B4 In benzene all twelve atoms lie in one flat hexagonal ring.
- [ ] B5 Spatial arrangement helps predict properties: branched isomers are more compact than straight chains, so they have less contact with neighbouring molecules and lower boiling points (see "Groups of hydrocarbons and their properties").

Structural isomers (one block)
- [ ] S1 Structural isomers are compounds with the same molecular formula but different structural formulae (the atoms are joined in a different order).
- [ ] S2 Isomers can differ in the carbon chain (branching), in the position of a double bond, or in having a ring instead of a double bond (e.g. cyclohexane and hex-1-ene are both C₆H₁₂).
- [ ] S3 Methane, ethane and propane have no isomers; isomerism in alkanes starts at C₄; alkenes also first have alkene isomers at C₄ (propene's only isomer is the ring compound cyclopropane).
- [ ] S4 C₄H₁₀ has two structural isomers: butane CH₃CH₂CH₂CH₃ and methylpropane CH₃CH(CH₃)CH₃.
- [ ] S5 C₅H₁₂ has three: pentane CH₃CH₂CH₂CH₂CH₃; 2-methylbutane CH₃CH(CH₃)CH₂CH₃; 2,2-dimethylpropane C(CH₃)₄.
- [ ] S6 C₆H₁₄ has five: hexane CH₃(CH₂)₄CH₃; 2-methylpentane CH₃CH(CH₃)CH₂CH₂CH₃; 3-methylpentane CH₃CH₂CH(CH₃)CH₂CH₃; 2,2-dimethylbutane CH₃C(CH₃)₂CH₂CH₃; 2,3-dimethylbutane CH₃CH(CH₃)CH(CH₃)CH₃.
- [ ] S7 Alkene isomers of C₄H₈: but-1-ene CH₂=CHCH₂CH₃; but-2-ene CH₃CH=CHCH₃; methylpropene CH₂=C(CH₃)CH₃.
- [ ] S8 Alkene isomers of C₅H₁₀: pent-1-ene CH₂=CHCH₂CH₂CH₃; pent-2-ene CH₃CH=CHCH₂CH₃; 2-methylbut-1-ene CH₂=C(CH₃)CH₂CH₃; 3-methylbut-1-ene CH₂=CHCH(CH₃)CH₃; 2-methylbut-2-ene CH₃C(CH₃)=CHCH₃.
- [ ] S9 Straight-chain alkene isomers of C₆H₁₂ differ in the position of the double bond: hex-1-ene, hex-2-ene and hex-3-ene.
- [ ] S10 Check for repeats: number the chain from the other end or rotate the model; "1-methylbutane" is really pentane and "pent-4-ene" is really pent-1-ene.

### 3. Traps

- A "straight chain" means unbranched, not a straight line of atoms: the chain zig-zags.
- Rotating a model or drawing a chain bent does not make a new isomer.
- The longest chain decides the name: a methyl group on the end carbon just lengthens the chain.
- Cis–trans (geometric) isomers are Higher Level and belong to "Structural and geometric isomerism"; here, but-2-ene counts as one structural isomer.
- Don't teach skeletal formulae or the R notation here; those are in "Functional groups, naming and representation".

### 4. What exams ask (topics only)

- What are structural isomers? Draw and name the isomers of C₄H₁₀ or C₅H₁₂.
- Draw and name two alkene isomers of C₄H₈.
- Write the condensed formula from an expanded structure, or the reverse.
- Describe the geometry around the carbon atoms in butane (tetrahedral); indicate tetrahedral carbons in a structure.

### 5. Practice questions to write

1. 9 marks: "What are structural isomers? Draw the expanded structural formula and give the IUPAC name of each of the three structural isomers of C₅H₁₂." Model answer: S1, S5 drawn in full.
2. 9 marks: "Describe how you would use a molecular model kit to show (i) that rotation is possible about the C–C bond in ethane, (ii) that the C=C bond in ethene is rigid and planar. Give one limitation of ball-and-stick models." Model answer: K1, K3, K4, K9.
3. 6 marks: "Draw and name two alkene isomers of C₄H₈ and write the condensed structural formula of each." Model answer: two of S7, F3.

## Part: Shapes: sigma and pi bonds (formerly `chem23-s8`)

- **loId:** `chem23-s8` · **chapterId:** `chem23` · **syllabusRef:** 2.4.8
- **Syllabus:** 2.4.8 "explain and compare the shapes of ethane, ethene, ethyne **and benzene
  molecules in terms of sigma and pi bonds, including delocalised pi bonding**". Learn about:
  "how structure, and some characteristic properties of hydrocarbons, can be predicted through
  bonding and spatial arrangements of atoms". Bold = Higher Level only: Ordinary level
  describes and compares the shapes of ethane, ethene and ethyne; Higher Level adds benzene and
  the explanation in terms of σ and π bonds.
- **Prepared by:** a higher model, 2026-10-30. The definitions of σ, π and delocalised bonding
  are in "Bonding, formulae and shapes" and VSEPR theory in "Bonding, formulae and shapes"; recall
  them in one line each and apply them to the four molecules. Building the models is in "3D
  models and formulae".

### 1. Must cover

Ethane (one block)
- [ ] E1 Ethane, C₂H₆ (CH₃–CH₃): each carbon forms four single bonds (three to H, one to C).
- [ ] E2 Shape: the four bonds around each carbon point to the corners of a tetrahedron, with bond angles of about 109.5°; the molecule is not flat (three-dimensional).
- [ ] E3 The two CH₃ groups can rotate freely about the C–C single bond.
- [ ] E4 Diagram: ethane drawn in 3D with wedges and dashes, angle 109.5° marked.

Ethene (one block)
- [ ] T1 Ethene, C₂H₄ (CH₂=CH₂): each carbon forms a double bond to the other carbon and single bonds to two H atoms.
- [ ] T2 Shape: each carbon is trigonal planar with bond angles of about 120°; all six atoms lie in one plane, so the molecule is planar (flat).
- [ ] T3 There is no rotation about the C=C double bond.
- [ ] T4 Diagram: flat ethene molecule with 120° angles marked.

Ethyne (one block)
- [ ] Y1 Ethyne, C₂H₂ (HC≡CH): each carbon forms a triple bond to the other carbon and a single bond to one H atom.
- [ ] Y2 Shape: linear, bond angle 180°; all four atoms lie in a straight line.
- [ ] Y3 Diagram: H–C≡C–H in a straight line with 180° marked.

Comparing the shapes (one block)
- [ ] C1 As the carbon–carbon bond changes from single to double to triple, the shape around each carbon changes from tetrahedral (109.5°) to trigonal planar (120°) to linear (180°), because the number of groups of electrons around each carbon falls from four to three to two and they spread as far apart as possible.
- [ ] C2 Carbon–carbon bond length shortens and bond strength increases from single to double to triple: C–C 0.154 nm, C=C 0.134 nm, C≡C 0.120 nm.
- [ ] C3 Ethane is three-dimensional; ethene is planar; ethyne is linear.

σ and π bonds in ethane, ethene and ethyne (Higher Level only; one block)
- [ ] S1 [HL] Recall: a σ bond is formed by head-on overlap of orbitals along the line between the nuclei; a π bond by sideways overlap of parallel p orbitals above and below that line. A single bond is σ; a double bond is σ + π; a triple bond is σ + 2π.
- [ ] S2 [HL] Only the σ bonds (and lone pairs) decide the shape: each carbon's σ bonds spread as far apart as possible (VSEPR), and the π bonds lie over the σ framework without changing the angles.
- [ ] S3 [HL] Ethane: seven σ bonds (six C–H, one C–C) and no π bonds; four σ bonds around each carbon → tetrahedral, 109.5°; rotation about the σ C–C bond doesn't change the overlap, so rotation is free.
- [ ] S4 [HL] Ethene: five σ bonds (four C–H, one C–C) and one π bond; three σ bonds around each carbon → trigonal planar, 120°; each carbon has one p orbital left at right angles to the plane, and these overlap sideways to form the π bond above and below the plane.
- [ ] S5 [HL] The π bond prevents rotation: twisting one CH₂ group would turn the p orbitals away from each other and break the π overlap; this is why the molecule is rigid and planar (and why cis–trans isomers exist; see "Structural and geometric isomerism").
- [ ] S6 [HL] Ethyne: three σ bonds (two C–H, one C–C) and two π bonds; two σ bonds around each carbon → linear, 180°; each carbon has two p orbitals left at right angles to each other, giving two π bonds at right angles, which together form a cylinder of electron density around the C–C axis.
- [ ] S7 [HL] Diagram: σ framework of ethene with the π cloud drawn above and below the plane; ethyne with its two π bonds at right angles around the C–C axis.

Benzene: delocalised π bonding (Higher Level only; one block)
- [ ] B1 [HL] Benzene, C₆H₆: each carbon forms three σ bonds (to two neighbouring carbons and one hydrogen), so each carbon is trigonal planar with 120° angles and the whole molecule is a flat regular hexagon; there are twelve σ bonds (six C–C, six C–H).
- [ ] B2 [HL] Each carbon has one remaining p orbital, holding one electron, at right angles to the ring.
- [ ] B3 [HL] The six p orbitals overlap sideways all round the ring, not in three separate pairs, forming a delocalised π system: two rings of electron density, one above and one below the plane of the ring, containing six electrons shared by all six carbons.
- [ ] B4 [HL] As a result all six C–C bonds are identical, 0.139 nm long, between a single (0.154 nm) and a double bond (0.134 nm); benzene is drawn as a hexagon with a circle inside to show the delocalised electrons.
- [ ] B5 [HL] Delocalisation makes benzene unusually stable: it doesn't decolourise bromine water, because addition would destroy the delocalised ring, so it reacts by substitution instead (keeping the ring).
- [ ] B6 [HL] Evidence against the Kekulé structure (alternating C–C and C=C): all bonds are the same length, and benzene does not undergo the addition reactions of alkenes. Kathleen Lonsdale, born in Newbridge, Co. Kildare, showed in 1929 that the benzene ring is flat and a regular hexagon.
- [ ] B7 [HL] Counting electrons (42 in C₆H₆): 12 in the six C–H bonds; 12 in the six C–C σ bonds; 6 delocalised π electrons; so 18 in carbon–carbon bonding; the remaining 12 are the inner (1s) electrons of the carbons, not involved in bonding.
- [ ] B8 [HL] Diagram: the planar ring of six C atoms with H atoms, the σ framework, the p orbitals at right angles to the ring, and the delocalised π clouds above and below the ring; and the hexagon-with-circle symbol.

Summary comparison (Higher Level only; one block)
- [ ] Z1 [HL] Table to draw — ethane: 7 σ, 0 π, tetrahedral 109.5°, free rotation; ethene: 5 σ, 1 π, planar 120°, no rotation; ethyne: 3 σ, 2 π, linear 180°; benzene: 12 σ, delocalised π (6 electrons), planar 120°, all C–C bonds equal.

| B6 | Kathleen Lonsdale (1903–1971), born in Newbridge, Co. Kildare; 1929, structure of hexamethylbenzene showed a flat, regular hexagonal benzene ring. |

### 3. Traps

- π bonds don't count when deciding the shape; count the σ bonds around each carbon.
- Ethene is planar: all six atoms in one plane, not just the two carbons.
- Benzene does not have three double bonds; the hexagon-with-circle shows delocalisation.
- Hybridisation (sp³, sp², sp) is not on the specification; explain shapes with σ/π bonds and electron-pair repulsion only.
- Resonance structures are not needed to explain benzene; use delocalised π bonding.
- The Ordinary level answer gives shapes and angles; σ/π explanations and benzene are Higher Level.

### 4. What exams ask (topics only)

- Is the geometry around the carbon atoms in ethene planar or tetrahedral? Describe the shapes of ethane, ethene and ethyne.
- Indicate the tetrahedral carbon atoms in a given structure.
- Distinguish σ and π bonds; state the number of each in a molecule (Higher Level).
- Describe the bonding in benzene; count electrons in C–H, C–C σ and delocalised π bonding (Higher Level).
- Explain why benzene doesn't readily undergo addition with bromine water.

## Part: Structural and geometric isomerism (formerly `chem23-s9`)

- **loId:** `chem23-s9` · **chapterId:** `chem23` · **syllabusRef:** 2.4.9
- **Syllabus:** 2.4.9 "**distinguish between structural and geometrical isomerism, including how
  isomerism gives rise to different properties**". Learn about: "structural isomers exemplified
  by alkanes and alkenes up to C6 **and cis-trans geometric isomers exemplified by butene**,
  which can be visualised using diagrams, 3D models and digital models". The outcome is all
  bold: the whole subsection is Higher Level only. Mark every block heading " (Higher Level
  only)".
- **Prepared by:** a higher model, 2026-10-30. Structural isomers are defined and listed in "3D
  models and formulae" and their boiling points are explained in "Explaining and predicting
  properties"; here, recall them in one line, then teach geometric (cis–trans) isomerism,
  the distinction, and how each kind of isomerism changes properties. Why there is no rotation
  about C=C (the π bond) is in "Shapes: sigma and pi bonds".

### 1. Must cover

Two kinds of isomerism (Higher Level only; one block)
- [ ] D1 [HL] Isomers are compounds with the same molecular formula but different arrangements of atoms.
- [ ] D2 [HL] Structural isomers have the same molecular formula but different structural formulae: the atoms are joined in a different order (e.g. butane and methylpropane, C₄H₁₀; but-1-ene and but-2-ene, C₄H₈).
- [ ] D3 [HL] Geometric (cis–trans) isomers have the same molecular formula and the same structural formula (the same atoms joined in the same order) but a different arrangement of the atoms in space.
- [ ] D4 [HL] To distinguish them: if converting one into the other would mean breaking bonds and joining atoms in a different order, they are structural isomers; if the order of joining is the same and only the positions in space differ, they are geometric isomers.

Cis–trans isomers of butene (Higher Level only; one block)
- [ ] G1 [HL] Geometric isomerism arises in alkenes because there is no rotation about the C=C double bond (rotation would break the π bond), so groups are fixed on one side or the other of the double bond.
- [ ] G2 [HL] Condition: each carbon of the double bond must carry two different atoms or groups.
- [ ] G3 [HL] cis-but-2-ene: the two CH₃ groups are on the same side of the double bond (and the two H atoms on the same side).
- [ ] G4 [HL] trans-but-2-ene: the two CH₃ groups are on opposite sides of the double bond.
- [ ] G5 [HL] But-1-ene (CH₂=CHCH₂CH₃) has no cis–trans isomers, because one carbon of the double bond carries two identical H atoms; methylpropene (CH₂=C(CH₃)₂) has none, because both carbons carry identical pairs.
- [ ] G6 [HL] Butene (C₄H₈) therefore has four alkene isomers: but-1-ene, cis-but-2-ene, trans-but-2-ene and methylpropene.
- [ ] G7 [HL] Alkanes have no geometric isomers, because groups rotate freely about C–C single bonds; any other alkene up to C6 with two different groups on each double-bond carbon (e.g. pent-2-ene, hex-2-ene, hex-3-ene) has cis and trans forms.
- [ ] G8 [HL] Visualising: in a 3D model of but-2-ene the double bond (two flexible links) can't be twisted, so the cis model can't be turned into the trans model without taking it apart; digital models show the same when rotated.
- [ ] G9 [HL] Diagram: cis-but-2-ene and trans-but-2-ene drawn flat with 120° angles, the CH₃ groups circled on the same and opposite sides.

How isomerism gives different properties (Higher Level only; one block)
- [ ] P1 [HL] Branched structural isomers have lower boiling points than unbranched ones (e.g. butane −1 °C, methylpropane −12 °C), because the more compact shape gives less contact and weaker London dispersion forces (see "Groups of hydrocarbons and their properties").
- [ ] P2 [HL] Structural isomers of the same family (e.g. butane and methylpropane) have very similar chemical properties, because they have the same types of bond.
- [ ] P3 [HL] Structural isomers in different families have different chemical properties: hex-1-ene and cyclohexane are both C₆H₁₂, but hex-1-ene (C=C) decolourises bromine water and cyclohexane (saturated) does not.
- [ ] P4 [HL] cis- and trans-but-2-ene have almost the same chemical properties (same C=C, same addition reactions and the same combustion: C₄H₈ + 6O₂ → 4CO₂ + 4H₂O).
- [ ] P5 [HL] Their physical properties differ: cis-but-2-ene boils at 4 °C and trans-but-2-ene at 1 °C, because the cis molecule is slightly polar (both CH₃ groups on one side) and so has weak dipole–dipole attractions as well as London forces, while the symmetrical trans molecule is non-polar (see "Polarity, shape and physical properties").
- [ ] P6 [HL] The trans isomer has the higher melting point (−106 °C, compared with −139 °C for cis), because its more symmetrical, straighter shape packs more closely in the solid.
- [ ] P7 [HL] Health link: cis–trans isomerism matters in food; unsaturated fats in natural oils are mainly cis, while trans fats (formed when vegetable oils are partly hydrogenated) raise the risk of heart disease.

### 3. Traps

- But-1-ene does not show cis–trans isomerism; it is a common exam error.
- cis and trans isomers are not structural isomers: their structural formulae are identical.
- Don't say cis and trans isomers have "different chemical properties"; their chemistry is almost the same; the differences are physical.
- The E/Z naming system is not required; use cis and trans.
- Optical isomerism is not on the specification.

- Draw the cis and trans isomers of but-2-ene; explain why but-1-ene has none.
- Explain why geometric isomers exist for alkenes but not alkanes.
- State and explain one similarity and one difference in the properties of isomers.
