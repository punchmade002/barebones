## Part: Tests for aldehydes, ketones and carboxylic acids (formerly `chem25-s1`)

- **loId:** `chem25-s1` · **chapterId:** `chem25` · **syllabusRef:** 4.2.4
- **Syllabus:** 4.2.4 "conduct tests • to distinguish between aldehydes and ketones • to show
  the acidic nature of carboxylic acids". Learn about: "Fehling's **oxidising** test";
  "Reactions of carboxylic acids with suitable metals (e.g. magnesium), suitable bases (e.g.
  NaOH and sodium carbonate)". Only the word "oxidising" is bold, so the test itself is for
  everyone and the explanation of Fehling's reagent as an oxidising agent is Higher Level only.
- **Prepared by:** a higher model, 2026-10-30. Practical tests: Fehling's test on ethanal and
  propanone, and ethanoic acid with magnesium, sodium carbonate and sodium hydroxide. Naming and
  the aldehyde and ketone functional groups are in "Functional groups, naming and representation"; why
  carboxylic acids are acidic is in "Physical properties and acidity"; the oxidation
  of alcohols and aldehydes with potassium permanganate is in "Redox and acid–base reactions of
  organic compounds". Don't repeat them. Tollens' reagent is not on the specification.

### 1. Must cover

Why aldehydes and ketones can be told apart (one block)
- [ ] K1 (assumed) An aldehyde has the –CHO group: the carbonyl carbon carries a hydrogen atom, e.g. ethanal, CH₃CHO. A ketone has the C=O group joined to two carbon atoms, e.g. propanone, CH₃COCH₃.
- [ ] K2 Aldehydes are easily oxidised to carboxylic acids, because the hydrogen on the carbonyl carbon can be replaced by –OH; they are good reducing agents.
- [ ] K3 Ketones are not oxidised by mild oxidising agents, because the carbonyl carbon has no hydrogen; oxidising a ketone would mean breaking a C–C bond.
- [ ] K4 So a mild oxidising agent that changes colour when it reacts distinguishes them: an aldehyde gives a colour change; a ketone gives none.

Fehling's test (Investigation block)
- [ ] F1 Fehling's reagent is made fresh by mixing equal volumes of Fehling's A (copper(II) sulfate solution, blue) and Fehling's B (potassium sodium tartrate and sodium hydroxide solution, colourless), giving a clear deep-blue solution containing Cu²⁺ ions.
- [ ] F2 The tartrate keeps the Cu²⁺ ions in solution in the alkaline mixture; without it, copper(II) hydroxide would precipitate.
- [ ] F3 Method: put about 2 cm³ of Fehling's reagent into each of two test tubes; add a few drops of ethanal to one and a few drops of propanone to the other.
- [ ] F4 Warm both test tubes in a beaker of hot water (water bath) for a few minutes; don't heat with a naked flame.
- [ ] F5 Result with ethanal: the blue solution gives a brick-red precipitate of copper(I) oxide, Cu₂O.
- [ ] F6 Result with propanone: no change; the solution stays blue.
- [ ] F7 Conclusion: an unknown carbonyl compound that gives a brick-red precipitate is an aldehyde; one that leaves the reagent blue is a ketone.
- [ ] F8 Safety: wear goggles (Fehling's B contains sodium hydroxide, which is corrosive); ethanal and propanone are volatile and flammable (ethanal boils at about 21 °C), so keep them away from flames and use a water bath.
- [ ] F9 Diagram: two test tubes standing in a beaker of hot water, labelled Fehling's reagent + ethanal (brick-red precipitate) and Fehling's reagent + propanone (blue).

Fehling's reagent as an oxidising agent (Higher Level only)
- [ ] O1 [HL] Fehling's reagent is a mild oxidising agent: the Cu²⁺ ions oxidise the aldehyde and are themselves reduced to copper(I).
- [ ] O2 [HL] The aldehyde is oxidised to a carboxylic acid; in the alkaline reagent it is present as its salt, e.g. ethanal → ethanoate ions.
- [ ] O3 [HL] Reduction half-equation: Cu²⁺ + e⁻ → Cu⁺ (oxidation number of copper falls from +2 to +1); the Cu⁺ forms the red precipitate Cu₂O. In full: 2Cu²⁺(aq) + 2OH⁻(aq) + 2e⁻ → Cu₂O(s) + H₂O(l).
- [ ] O4 [HL] Overall: CH₃CHO + 2Cu²⁺ + 5OH⁻ → CH₃COO⁻ + Cu₂O + 3H₂O.
- [ ] O5 [HL] Fehling's reagent is difficult to reduce (a weak oxidising agent), so the fact that ethanal reduces it shows that aldehydes are very easily oxidised; it is too weak to oxidise a ketone.

Showing the acidic nature of carboxylic acids (Investigation block)
- [ ] A1 Aim: to show that ethanoic acid behaves as an acid, by its reactions with a metal, a carbonate and a base, and its effect on indicator.
- [ ] A2 Indicator: dilute ethanoic acid turns blue litmus red and gives a pH below 7 (about 3) with universal indicator or a pH meter.
- [ ] A3 Magnesium: add a clean piece of magnesium ribbon to dilute ethanoic acid; bubbles of gas form slowly and the magnesium dissolves. The gas burns with a pop when a lighted taper is held at the mouth of the tube: it is hydrogen. 2CH₃COOH(aq) + Mg(s) → (CH₃COO)₂Mg(aq) + H₂(g).
- [ ] A4 Sodium carbonate: add a little solid sodium carbonate to ethanoic acid; there is effervescence (fizzing). The gas turns limewater milky: it is carbon dioxide. 2CH₃COOH(aq) + Na₂CO₃(s) → 2CH₃COONa(aq) + H₂O(l) + CO₂(g).
- [ ] A5 Sodium hydroxide: ethanoic acid is neutralised by sodium hydroxide, forming the salt sodium ethanoate and water: CH₃COOH(aq) + NaOH(aq) → CH₃COONa(aq) + H₂O(l). There is no visible change, so add phenolphthalein to the acid and add the sodium hydroxide until the solution just turns pink (or follow the pH), or note the temperature rise.
- [ ] A6 Control: repeat the tests with ethanol in place of ethanoic acid; ethanol gives no fizzing with magnesium or sodium carbonate and is neutral to indicator, which shows the –COOH group, not the –OH alone, is acidic.
- [ ] A7 Ethanoic acid reacts more slowly with magnesium than hydrochloric acid of the same concentration does, because it is a weak acid (only partly dissociated), so the concentration of H⁺ is lower.
- [ ] A8 Safety: goggles; concentrated (glacial) ethanoic acid is corrosive, so use the dilute acid; no flame near any flammable liquid when testing for hydrogen.
- [ ] A9 Diagram: test tube of dilute ethanoic acid with magnesium ribbon and a lighted taper at the mouth; test tube with sodium carbonate and a delivery tube bubbling the gas into limewater.

### 3. Traps

- Tollens' reagent (the silver mirror test) is not on the specification. Don't teach it.
- The precipitate is brick-red Cu₂O, copper(I) oxide, not copper metal and not copper(II) oxide.
- Fehling's reagent is the oxidising agent and is reduced; the aldehyde is the reducing agent and is oxidised. Don't reverse them.
- "No change" for the ketone means the solution stays blue; don't say "colourless".
- The reaction with magnesium shows acidic behaviour but is itself a redox reaction (Mg is oxidised, H⁺ is reduced); the reactions with carbonate and hydroxide are acid–base reactions. See "Types of organic reaction and addition polymers".
- Acidified potassium permanganate also distinguishes aldehydes from ketones, but it also oxidises primary and secondary alcohols, so Fehling's reagent is the specification's test.

### 4. What exams ask (topics only)

- Describe how to carry out Fehling's test on ethanal; the colour change; the identity of the red precipitate.
- Half-equation for the reduction of Cu²⁺ in Fehling's reagent; the organic product of oxidising ethanal; whether ethanal is easy or hard to oxidise.
- Why a ketone gives no reaction.
- The gas formed when magnesium or sodium carbonate is added to ethanoic acid, and its test; balanced equations.

- **Prepared by:** a higher model, 2026-10-30. The two-carbon scheme (ethane, ethene,

  map, the matching three-carbon conversions, a method for reading a scheme, and HL blocks on

### 1. Must cover

What a reaction scheme is (one block)
- [ ] A1 A reaction scheme is a diagram that links organic compounds by arrows, each arrow labelled with the reagent (and sometimes the reaction type) that converts one compound into the next.
- [ ] A2 It shows how compounds of different families are related, and how a target compound can be made from a simpler starting material in several steps (organic synthesis).
- [ ] A3 Reading a scheme: name each compound and its functional group; for each arrow, compare the formulae before and after and decide what has been added, removed or swapped; then name the reaction type and reagent.
- [ ] A4 Clues from the formulae: +H₂ across C=C or C=O is addition/reduction; −2H is oxidation; +O is oxidation; −H₂O or −HCl with a new C=C is elimination; one atom swapped for another is substitution; a carboxylic acid → its sodium salt is acid–base.

The two-carbon scheme (one block)
- [ ] B1 Ethane → chloroethane: chlorine in UV light (substitution).
- [ ] B2 Ethene → ethane: hydrogen with a nickel catalyst (addition; reduction).
- [ ] B3 Ethene → chloroethane: hydrogen chloride (addition); ethene → 1,2-dichloroethane: chlorine (addition); ethene → 1,2-dibromoethane: bromine (addition).
- [ ] B4 Ethene → ethanol: water (addition); ethanol → ethene: heated aluminium oxide (elimination).
- [ ] B5 Chloroethane → ethene: loss of HCl (elimination).
- [ ] B6 Ethanol → ethanal → ethanoic acid: acidified potassium permanganate (oxidation, two steps); ethanal → ethanol: hydrogen with a nickel catalyst (reduction).
- [ ] B7 Ethanoic acid + ethanol → ethyl ethanoate + water: concentrated sulfuric acid catalyst (substitution/esterification); ethyl ethanoate + sodium hydroxide → sodium ethanoate + ethanol (base hydrolysis).
- [ ] B8 Ethanoic acid → sodium ethanoate: sodium hydroxide or sodium carbonate (acid–base).
- [ ] B9 Ethene → poly(ethene) (addition polymerisation); 1,2-dichloroethane → chloroethene (elimination of HCl) → poly(chloroethene), PVC.
- [ ] B10 Diagram: the two-carbon scheme drawn as a map with ethene at the centre, arrows to ethane, chloroethane, 1,2-dichloroethane, ethanol and poly(ethene); ethanol linked to ethanal, ethanoic acid and ethyl ethanoate; each arrow labelled with reagent and type.

Three-carbon and other examples (one block)
- [ ] C1 Propan-1-ol (primary) → propanal → propanoic acid: acidified potassium permanganate (oxidation).
- [ ] C2 Propan-2-ol (secondary) → propanone: acidified potassium permanganate (oxidation); propanone → propan-2-ol: hydrogen with nickel (reduction).
- [ ] C3 Propan-2-ol → propene + water (elimination); propene → propane: hydrogen with nickel (addition).
- [ ] C4 Phenylmethanol → benzoic acid: potassium permanganate under basic conditions (oxidation); see "Investigation: synthesising benzoic acid".
- [ ] C5 Working backwards: if a scheme ends in an aldehyde, the compound before it is probably a primary alcohol; a ketone comes from a secondary alcohol; an alkene can come from an alcohol (elimination) or go to an alcohol (addition of water).
- [ ] C6 Carbon atoms are neither gained nor lost in these steps (except esterification, which joins two molecules, and hydrolysis, which splits them), so the carbon skeleton is a check on each answer.

Predicting reactions and products (Higher Level only)
- [ ] P1 [HL] To predict a product, identify the functional group and apply its typical reaction: alkene + X₂ or HX or H₂O or H₂ → addition product; primary alcohol + oxidising agent → aldehyde then carboxylic acid; carboxylic acid + alcohol → ester; ester + NaOH → salt + alcohol.
- [ ] P2 [HL] Example: ethene to ethanoic acid in two steps: add water to give ethanol (addition), then oxidise with excess acidified potassium permanganate (oxidation).
- [ ] P3 [HL] Example: ethanal to ethyl ethanoate: reduce part of the ethanal to ethanol (H₂, Ni), oxidise another part to ethanoic acid (acidified KMnO₄), then esterify the two (concentrated H₂SO₄).
- [ ] P4 [HL] Example: chloroethane to ethanol in two steps: eliminate HCl to give ethene, then add water.
- [ ] P5 [HL] Example: the ester formed when propan-1-ol is heated with methanoic acid is propyl methanoate, HCOOCH₂CH₂CH₃; the alcohol gives the "propyl" part and the acid the "methanoate" part.

Explaining behaviour: changes in geometry (Higher Level only)
- [ ] G1 [HL] A carbon atom with four single bonds is tetrahedral (bond angles about 109.5°); a carbon atom in a C=C or C=O double bond is planar (trigonal planar, about 120°).
- [ ] G2 [HL] Addition to C=C (e.g. ethene → ethane, ethene → ethanol, polymerisation): the two carbon atoms change from planar to tetrahedral.
- [ ] G3 [HL] Elimination (e.g. ethanol → ethene): the two carbon atoms change from tetrahedral to planar.
- [ ] G4 [HL] Oxidising an alcohol to an aldehyde or ketone: the carbon bearing the oxygen changes from tetrahedral to planar; reducing an aldehyde or ketone to an alcohol changes it from planar to tetrahedral.
- [ ] G5 [HL] Aldehyde → carboxylic acid and acid → ester: the carbonyl carbon stays planar, so there is no change in geometry.
- [ ] G6 [HL] Counting geometry in a molecule: ethene has no tetrahedral carbon; ethanal and ethanoic acid each have one planar carbon (the C=O carbon) and one tetrahedral carbon; ethanol and ethane have only tetrahedral carbons.

Explaining behaviour: why some reactions are not possible (Higher Level only)
- [ ] N1 [HL] Tertiary alcohols (e.g. 2-methylpropan-2-ol) are not oxidised by acidified potassium permanganate: the carbon bearing –OH has no hydrogen atom to lose, and oxidation would mean breaking a C–C bond.
- [ ] N2 [HL] Ketones are not oxidised by Fehling's reagent or potassium permanganate, for the same reason (no H on the carbonyl carbon).
- [ ] N3 [HL] Alkanes and poly(ethene) cannot undergo addition (they are saturated; no pi bond), so they do not decolourise bromine water.
- [ ] N4 [HL] Methanol cannot be dehydrated to an alkene: it has only one carbon atom, and a C=C needs two.
- [ ] N5 [HL] Benzene does not decolourise bromine water as an alkene does: its delocalised pi electrons make the ring unusually stable, so it resists addition (see "Shapes, bonding and isomerism").
- [ ] N6 [HL] A carboxylic acid such as ethanoic acid is not oxidised further by potassium permanganate; it is the end product of oxidising a primary alcohol.

### 3. Traps

- Reaction conditions are not required unless the specification names them; give the reagent (e.g. hydrogen with nickel, acidified potassium permanganate).
- Oxidising a primary alcohol gives the aldehyde first and then the acid; a secondary alcohol gives a ketone, never an acid.
- When drawing geometry, the carbon of an –OH group in an alcohol is tetrahedral; only carbons in double bonds (or benzene rings) are planar.
- Don't let a proposed product gain or lose carbon atoms in a single step except by esterification or hydrolysis.

### 4. What exams ask (topics only)

- Name the compounds in a given scheme; identify an addition, a substitution, an elimination, an oxidation and a reduction in it.
- Reagents for given conversions (H₂/Ni; acidified KMnO₄; Al₂O₃; Cl₂ in UV light).
- How the geometry around the carbon atoms changes in a given conversion; which compound has no tetrahedral carbon, or only one planar carbon.
- Intermediates in a multi-step synthesis.

## Part: Investigation: preparing an ester (formerly `chem25-s4`)

- **loId:** `chem25-s4` · **chapterId:** `chem25` · **syllabusRef:** 4.2.8
- **Syllabus:** 4.2.8 "conduct an experiment to prepare an ester". Learn about: "the
  preparation of an ester using a reflux method". Normal type.
- **Prepared by:** a higher model, 2026-10-30. Full investigation: methyl propanoate from
  propanoic acid and methanol by reflux, then separation, washing, drying and distillation;
  ethyl ethanoate as the alternative. Naming esters and their physical properties are in
  "Functional groups, naming and representation" and "Physical properties and acidity"; the
  separating funnel and distillation techniques are in "Using the kinetic theory: separation techniques"; percentage
  yield is in "Theoretical, actual and percentage yield". Don't repeat them.

### 1. Must cover

Aim and chemistry (one block)
- [ ] A1 Aim: to prepare a sample of an ester, methyl propanoate, from propanoic acid and methanol by heating under reflux.
- [ ] A2 Equation: CH₃CH₂COOH(l) + CH₃OH(l) ⇌ CH₃CH₂COOCH₃(l) + H₂O(l), propanoic acid + methanol ⇌ methyl propanoate + water; mole ratio 1 : 1 : 1.
- [ ] A3 Alternative: ethanoic acid + ethanol ⇌ ethyl ethanoate + water: CH₃COOH + C₂H₅OH ⇌ CH₃COOC₂H₅ + H₂O.
- [ ] A4 Esterification is slow and reversible: it reaches an equilibrium with only part of the acid and alcohol converted, so the product mixture also contains unreacted acid and alcohol, water and the sulfuric acid catalyst.
- [ ] A5 Evidence of how the reaction happens: when the alcohol is labelled with the isotope oxygen-18, the ¹⁸O ends up in the ester (the oxygen joining the two parts), not in the water. So the –OH of the water comes from the acid and the H from the alcohol; the bond formed is the C–O bond between the acid's carbonyl carbon and the alcohol's oxygen.
- [ ] A6 The specification needs only esters made from monocarboxylic acids and primary alcohols.

Method: reflux (Investigation block, one step per bullet)
- [ ] M1 In a fume cupboard, put measured volumes of propanoic acid and methanol (e.g. about 10 cm³ of each) into a round-bottomed flask.
- [ ] M2 Add about 1 cm³ of concentrated sulfuric acid a drop at a time, swirling and cooling the flask under the tap (mixing is exothermic).
- [ ] M3 Add a few anti-bumping granules.
- [ ] M4 Fit a Liebig condenser vertically on the flask, with cold water entering at the bottom and leaving at the top; leave the top of the condenser open.
- [ ] M5 Heat the flask in a water bath (or with a heating mantle) so that the mixture boils gently under reflux for about 20–30 minutes.

Method: isolating and purifying the ester (Investigation block)
- [ ] I1 Cool the flask, then pour the mixture into a separating funnel with some water and shake; the ester forms the upper layer (it is less dense than water and only slightly soluble in it); run off and discard the lower aqueous layer, which holds most of the acid, methanol and sulfuric acid.
- [ ] I2 Add sodium carbonate solution to the ester in the funnel and shake gently, opening the tap often (inverted) to release the carbon dioxide formed; this neutralises the remaining acid. Run off the lower aqueous layer.
- [ ] I3 Run the ester into a dry conical flask and add a few lumps of anhydrous calcium chloride to remove water (drying agent); swirl until the liquid is clear, then decant or filter it.
- [ ] I4 Distil the dry ester and collect the fraction boiling at about 80 °C (methyl propanoate boils at 79.8 °C; ethyl ethanoate at 77 °C).
- [ ] I5 Identify the product by its boiling point and its pleasant, fruity smell (waft it towards the nose; don't sniff directly).

Why each choice (one block)
- [ ] W1 Reflux allows the mixture to be boiled for a long time without losing the volatile reactants and product: vapour rises into the condenser, condenses and drips back into the flask.
- [ ] W2 Concentrated sulfuric acid is the catalyst (it provides H⁺ ions); it also absorbs water, which helps shift the equilibrium towards the ester.
- [ ] W3 A water bath or heating mantle is used, not a Bunsen burner, because methanol and the ester are flammable; it also gives gentle, even heating.
- [ ] W4 Anti-bumping granules give smooth boiling by providing sites for small bubbles to form, preventing sudden violent boiling (bumping).
- [ ] W5 The condenser is never stoppered: heating a closed system would build up pressure.
- [ ] W6 Sodium carbonate removes acid without destroying the ester; the pressure must be released because CO₂ is produced: Na₂CO₃ + 2H⁺ → 2Na⁺ + H₂O + CO₂.
- [ ] W7 Distillation separates the ester from any remaining alcohol and other impurities by boiling point and gives a purer product.

Safety, yield and errors (one block)
- [ ] S1 Safety: concentrated sulfuric acid is corrosive (goggles, gloves, add slowly); methanol is toxic and flammable; propanoic acid is corrosive and has a strong smell; work in a fume cupboard; no naked flames.
- [ ] S2 The yield is well below 100%: the reaction is an equilibrium; some ester dissolves in the aqueous layers and is lost in transfers; some evaporates. Percentage yield = actual mass ÷ theoretical mass × 100 (see "Theoretical, actual and percentage yield").
- [ ] S3 Sources of error: not drying the ester fully (cloudy product, low boiling point reading); not releasing pressure in the separating funnel (stopper blown out, product lost); heating too strongly (vapour escaping the condenser).
- [ ] S4 Diagram: reflux apparatus: round-bottomed flask containing the mixture and anti-bumping granules in a water bath, vertical Liebig condenser with water in at the bottom and out at the top, open top.
- [ ] S5 Diagram: separating funnel with the upper ester (organic) layer and lower aqueous layer, stopper and tap.

### 3. Traps

- Reflux means heating with a vertical condenser so the vapour returns; distillation (condenser sloping down to a receiver) is a separate step afterwards.
- The ester is the upper layer, not the lower.
- The oxygen joining the two parts of the ester comes from the alcohol, not the acid.
- Reaction conditions beyond those in the method are not examined; don't add extra ones.

- The ester formed from a given acid and alcohol; where an ¹⁸O label from the alcohol ends up.
