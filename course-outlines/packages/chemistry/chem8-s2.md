## Part: Solutions and concentration (formerly `chem8-s5`)

- **loId:** `chem8-s5` · **chapterId:** `chem8` · **syllabusRef:** 1.4.5
- **Syllabus:** 1.4.5 "explain and model the concept of concentration of a solution and outline
  how to prepare a range of solutions of different concentrations, including primary standard
  solutions". Learn about: "chemical solutions that includes: solutions, solutes, solvents,
  dilution and serial dilutions, concentration, unsaturated/saturated solutions, supersaturated
  solutions, standard solutions, solutions made from primary standards, taking into account
  precision, accuracy, volumes and glassware required". Normal type.
- **Prepared by:** a higher model, 2026-10-30. Terms; concentration in mol L⁻¹ with worked
  examples; making a standard solution from a primary standard as a full method; dilution and
  serial dilution. Other concentration units are in "Units of concentration"; the use of
  standard solutions in titrations is in "Standard solutions and acid–base titrations".

### 1. Must cover

Terms (one block)
- [ ] T1 Solution: a uniform (homogeneous) mixture of a solute dissolved in a solvent.
- [ ] T2 Solute: the substance that dissolves; solvent: the substance it dissolves in (water in an aqueous solution).
- [ ] T3 Concentration: the amount of solute dissolved in a given volume of solution, usually in mol L⁻¹ (molarity, M) or g L⁻¹.
- [ ] T4 Unsaturated solution: can dissolve more solute at that temperature; saturated solution: contains the maximum amount of solute that can dissolve at that temperature, with undissolved solid present.
- [ ] T5 Supersaturated solution: contains more dissolved solute than a saturated solution at that temperature; it is unstable and made by cooling a hot saturated solution carefully; adding a small seed crystal makes the excess crystallise suddenly (e.g. sodium thiosulfate or sodium ethanoate).
- [ ] T6 Standard solution: a solution whose concentration is accurately known.

Concentration calculations (one block, skills)
- [ ] C1 Concentration (mol L⁻¹) = moles of solute ÷ volume of solution (L); n = c × V.
- [ ] C2 Example: 5.30 g of anhydrous sodium carbonate (Na₂CO₃, M = 106 g mol⁻¹) = 0.0500 mol, made up to 500 cm³ (0.500 L): c = 0.0500 ÷ 0.500 = 0.100 mol L⁻¹.
- [ ] C3 Example: mass needed for 250 cm³ of 0.100 M sodium hydroxide (M = 40 g mol⁻¹): n = 0.100 × 0.250 = 0.0250 mol; m = 0.0250 × 40 = 1.00 g.
- [ ] C4 Model of concentration: the same number of solute particles in a smaller volume gives a higher concentration; diluting spreads the same particles through a larger volume.

Primary standards (one block)
- [ ] P1 A primary standard is a substance that can be weighed out accurately to make a standard solution directly.
- [ ] P2 Properties: available in a very pure state; stable in air (doesn't absorb water or carbon dioxide or react with oxygen); has a known formula; dissolves readily in water; preferably a high molar mass (to reduce weighing errors).
- [ ] P3 Examples: anhydrous sodium carbonate (acid–base); ammonium iron(II) sulfate (redox, dissolved in dilute sulfuric acid).
- [ ] P4 Not primary standards: sodium hydroxide (absorbs water and carbon dioxide from the air); hydrochloric acid (concentration changes as HCl evaporates); their solutions are standardised by titration against a primary standard.

Preparing a standard solution (one block, investigation method; one step per bullet)
- [ ] M1 Weigh accurately (to 0.01 g or better) the calculated mass of the primary standard on a clock glass or weighing boat.
- [ ] M2 Transfer it to a beaker; rinse the clock glass into the beaker with deionised water from a wash bottle.
- [ ] M3 Dissolve the solid in a small volume of deionised water, stirring with a glass rod.
- [ ] M4 Pour the solution through a funnel into a volumetric flask of the required volume (e.g. 250 cm³); rinse the beaker, rod and funnel into the flask with deionised water so that no solute is lost.
- [ ] M5 Add deionised water until near the mark, then add the last drops with a dropper until the bottom of the meniscus sits on the graduation mark, viewed at eye level.
- [ ] M6 Stopper the flask and invert it about 20 times so the solution is uniform.
- [ ] M7 Precision and accuracy: the volumetric flask is used because it holds an accurately known volume; a beaker or measuring cylinder is not accurate enough.
- [ ] M8 Diagram: a volumetric flask with its graduation mark and the meniscus at eye level, and the funnel used to transfer the solution.

Dilution and serial dilution (one block, skills)
- [ ] D1 Diluting adds solvent: the number of moles of solute stays the same, so c₁V₁ = c₂V₂.
- [ ] D2 Example: to make 250 cm³ of 0.10 M HCl from 1.0 M HCl: V₁ = (0.10 × 250) ÷ 1.0 = 25 cm³ of the stronger acid, pipetted into a 250 cm³ volumetric flask and made up to the mark.
- [ ] D3 Use a pipette (with a pipette filler) for the volume of the original solution and a volumetric flask for the final volume.
- [ ] D4 Serial dilution: a series of dilutions, each using the previous solution, usually by the same factor, e.g. 10 cm³ made up to 100 cm³ each time gives 1.0, 0.10, 0.010 and 0.0010 M; used to make a range of concentrations accurately.

### 3. Traps

- Concentration is per litre of solution, not per litre of solvent.
- In dilution, the moles stay the same; only the volume changes.
- Rinsing the burette and pipette before titration is in the volumetric analysis chapter; don't add it here.

### 4. What exams ask (topics only)

- Describe how to make up a standard solution of sodium carbonate.
- Explain why sodium hydroxide is not a primary standard; give properties of a primary standard.
- Calculate concentrations and masses; dilution calculations.

### 5. Practice questions to write

1. 12 marks: "Describe how you would prepare 250 cm³ of a 0.100 M standard solution of anhydrous sodium carbonate, including the mass needed." Model answer: n = 0.0250 mol, m = 2.65 g; M1–M6.
2. 6 marks: "State three properties of a primary standard and explain why sodium hydroxide is not used as one." Model answer: P2, P4.
3. 6 marks: "What volume of 2.0 M sulfuric acid is needed to make 500 cm³ of 0.10 M sulfuric acid?" Model answer: V₁ = (0.10 × 500) ÷ 2.0 = 25 cm³.

## Part: Units of concentration (formerly `chem8-s6`)

- **loId:** `chem8-s6` · **chapterId:** `chem8` · **syllabusRef:** 1.4.6
- **Syllabus:** 1.4.6 "convert between units of concentration". Learn about: "units for
  expressing concentration: g/L, mol/L, %w/w, %v/v, %w/v and **p.p.m.**". Bold = Higher Level
  only (p.p.m.).
- **Prepared by:** a higher model, 2026-10-30. One bullet per unit with its meaning, then
  worked conversions; p.p.m. in a Higher Level block.

### 1. Must cover

The units (one block)
- [ ] U1 mol/L (mol L⁻¹, molarity, M): moles of solute per litre of solution.
- [ ] U2 g/L (g L⁻¹): grams of solute per litre of solution.
- [ ] U3 %w/v (weight per volume): grams of solute per 100 cm³ of solution, e.g. 5% w/v glucose contains 5 g per 100 cm³.
- [ ] U4 %v/v (volume per volume): cm³ of solute per 100 cm³ of solution, used for liquids in liquids, e.g. wine labelled 12% v/v alcohol has 12 cm³ of ethanol in every 100 cm³.
- [ ] U5 %w/w (weight per weight): grams of solute per 100 g of the whole mixture, used for solids and creams, e.g. 1% w/w hydrocortisone cream has 1 g in every 100 g.

Converting (one block, skills)
- [ ] C1 mol L⁻¹ to g L⁻¹: multiply by the molar mass, e.g. 0.10 M NaOH × 40 g mol⁻¹ = 4.0 g L⁻¹.
- [ ] C2 g L⁻¹ to mol L⁻¹: divide by the molar mass, e.g. 9.8 g L⁻¹ H₂SO₄ ÷ 98 = 0.10 M.
- [ ] C3 g L⁻¹ to %w/v: divide by 10 (100 cm³ is one-tenth of a litre), e.g. 4.0 g L⁻¹ = 0.40% w/v; %w/v × 10 = g L⁻¹.
- [ ] C4 %w/v to mol L⁻¹: × 10 to get g L⁻¹, then ÷ M, e.g. 0.9% w/v saline (NaCl, M = 58.5): 9.0 g L⁻¹ ÷ 58.5 = 0.15 M.
- [ ] C5 %v/v to volume: in 750 cm³ of 12% v/v wine, ethanol = 750 × 12 ÷ 100 = 90 cm³.
- [ ] C6 %v/v to mass (using density): 90 cm³ of ethanol × 0.789 g cm⁻³ = 71 g.
- [ ] C7 %w/w to mass: 30 g of a 1% w/w cream contains 0.30 g of the active ingredient.

Parts per million (Higher Level only; one block)
- [ ] P1 [HL] p.p.m. (parts per million): the number of parts of solute in one million parts of solution or mixture, used for very dilute solutions such as pollutants and dissolved substances in water.
- [ ] P2 [HL] For dilute aqueous solutions, 1 p.p.m. = 1 mg of solute per litre of solution (1 mg L⁻¹), because 1 L of water has a mass of about 1 kg = 10⁶ mg; for solids, 1 p.p.m. = 1 mg per kg.
- [ ] P3 [HL] Example: 0.0050 g of a substance in 2.5 L of water: 5.0 mg ÷ 2.5 L = 2.0 p.p.m.
- [ ] P4 [HL] Converting: 1 p.p.m. = 0.001 g L⁻¹ = 0.0001% w/v; e.g. 1.0 mg L⁻¹ fluoride is 1.0 p.p.m.
- [ ] P5 [HL] p.p.m. to mol L⁻¹: 2.0 p.p.m. = 2.0 mg L⁻¹ = 0.0020 g L⁻¹; ÷ M.

### 3. Traps

- %w/v is per 100 cm³ of solution, not per litre.
- p.p.m. as mg L⁻¹ holds only for dilute aqueous solutions.
- Keep p.p.m. in its Higher Level block.

### 4. What exams ask (topics only)

- Convert a concentration in mol L⁻¹ to g L⁻¹ or %w/v and back.
- Calculate the volume of alcohol in a drink from its %v/v.
- Express a concentration in p.p.m. (Higher Level).

### 5. Practice questions to write

1. 9 marks: "A solution of sodium hydroxide has a concentration of 0.25 mol L⁻¹. Express its concentration in (i) g L⁻¹, (ii) %w/v." Model answer: 0.25 × 40 = 10 g L⁻¹; 1.0% w/v.
2. 6 marks: "Calculate the volume of ethanol in a 500 cm³ bottle of beer that is 4.5% v/v." Model answer: 500 × 4.5 ÷ 100 = 22.5 cm³.
3. 6 marks (Higher Level): "A 250 cm³ water sample contains 0.40 mg of nitrate ions. Express this concentration in p.p.m." Model answer: 0.40 mg ÷ 0.250 L = 1.6 mg L⁻¹ = 1.6 p.p.m.
