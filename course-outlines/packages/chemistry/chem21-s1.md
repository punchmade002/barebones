## Part: Bond enthalpy and energy change (formerly `chem21-s1`)

- **loId:** `chem21-s1` · **chapterId:** `chem21` · **syllabusRef:** 3.1.1
- **Syllabus:** 3.1.1 "define bond enthalpy and explain enthalpy changes in a reaction in terms
  of making and breaking bonds". Learn about: "the principle of the law of conservation of
  energy underpinning all thermochemical processes; how bond-making releases energy and
  bond-breaking requires energy with examples of processes involving energy transfer as heat
  including, but not limited to: combustion, neutralisation, thermal decomposition, rusting of
  iron, photosynthesis, respiration; physical change also involves enthalpy change". Normal type.
- **Prepared by:** a higher model, 2026-10-30. Conservation of energy, system and
  surroundings, enthalpy and ΔH, bond enthalpy defined, the qualitative bond explanation, and
  physical change. Exothermic/endothermic classification and the six example processes with
  their equations and ΔH values are in "Exothermic and endothermic reactions"; calculating ΔH
  from bond enthalpy values is in "ΔH from bond enthalpies" (Higher Level). Keep this note
  qualitative apart from quoting bond enthalpy values as examples of the definition.

### 1. Must cover

Conservation of energy (one block)
- [ ] L1 Law of conservation of energy: energy cannot be created or destroyed, only changed from one form to another (or transferred).
- [ ] L2 In a chemical reaction, chemical energy stored in bonds is changed into, or comes from, other forms, mainly heat (also light, sound and electrical energy).
- [ ] L3 Thermochemistry is the study of the heat changes that take place during chemical reactions (and physical changes).
- [ ] L4 The system is the reacting chemicals; the surroundings are everything else (the solvent, container, air, thermometer); energy lost by the system is gained by the surroundings, and vice versa, so the total stays the same.
- [ ] L5 This law underpins all of thermochemistry: it is why the heat a reaction gives out can be measured as the heat gained by water, and why Hess's law works (see "Hess's law").

Enthalpy and enthalpy change (one block)
- [ ] H1 Enthalpy (H) is the heat content (stored energy) of a substance; it can't be measured directly, but changes in it can.
- [ ] H2 The enthalpy change, ΔH, is the heat change in a process carried out at constant pressure: ΔH = H(products) − H(reactants); unit kJ mol⁻¹ (kJ per mole of reaction as written in the equation).
- [ ] H3 A negative ΔH means heat is given out to the surroundings (exothermic); a positive ΔH means heat is taken in (endothermic).
- [ ] H4 Heat of reaction: the heat change when the numbers of moles of reactants shown in the balanced equation react completely.

Bond enthalpy (one block)
- [ ] B1 Bond enthalpy: the energy needed to break one mole of a particular covalent bond, with the molecules and the separated atoms all in the gaseous state.
- [ ] B2 Example: H₂(g) → 2H(g), ΔH = +436 kJ mol⁻¹: the H–H bond enthalpy is 436 kJ mol⁻¹.
- [ ] B3 Bond enthalpies are always positive, because energy must always be supplied to break a bond.
- [ ] B4 The strength of the same type of bond varies slightly from one molecule to another (e.g. a C–H bond in methane and in ethanol), so tables give average bond enthalpies, averaged over many compounds.
- [ ] B5 The larger the bond enthalpy, the stronger the bond; e.g. the triple bond in nitrogen, N≡N (945 kJ mol⁻¹), is very strong, which is why nitrogen gas is so unreactive.

Making and breaking bonds (one block)
- [ ] M1 In a chemical reaction, bonds in the reactants are broken and new bonds are made in the products.
- [ ] M2 Bond breaking requires (absorbs) energy: it is endothermic.
- [ ] M3 Bond making releases energy: it is exothermic; forming a bond releases the same amount of energy as breaking it requires.
- [ ] M4 If more energy is released making the new bonds than is used breaking the old ones, the reaction is exothermic overall (ΔH negative); if more energy is needed to break bonds than is released making them, it is endothermic (ΔH positive).
- [ ] M5 Example: H₂(g) + Cl₂(g) → 2HCl(g): one H–H bond and one Cl–Cl bond are broken and two H–Cl bonds are made; the energy released forming the two strong H–Cl bonds is greater than that needed to break the reactant bonds, so the reaction is exothermic.
- [ ] M6 Example: in combustion of methane, the C–H and O=O bonds broken need less energy than is released forming the very strong C=O bonds in CO₂ and O–H bonds in H₂O, so combustion is strongly exothermic.
- [ ] M7 Every reaction needs some energy to start breaking bonds (the activation energy) even if it is exothermic overall (see "Catalysis").
- [ ] M8 The processes the specification names (combustion, neutralisation, thermal decomposition, rusting of iron, photosynthesis, respiration) all transfer energy as heat because of this balance of bond breaking and making.

Physical change also involves enthalpy change (one block)
- [ ] P1 Changes of state involve enthalpy changes because intermolecular forces are overcome or formed; covalent bonds within the molecules are not broken (see "Bond type and intermolecular forces").
- [ ] P2 Melting and boiling (and sublimation) are endothermic: energy is needed to overcome the forces between particles; e.g. H₂O(s) → H₂O(l), ΔH = +6.01 kJ mol⁻¹; H₂O(l) → H₂O(g), ΔH = +40.7 kJ mol⁻¹ (at 100 °C).
- [ ] P3 Freezing and condensing are exothermic: the same amounts of energy are released as forces form, e.g. steam condensing on skin gives a severe burn.
- [ ] P4 Dissolving can be exothermic or endothermic: e.g. dissolving sodium hydroxide or concentrated sulfuric acid in water gives out heat; dissolving ammonium nitrate takes in heat (used in instant cold packs).
- [ ] P5 Evaporation of sweat cools the body because the water takes in heat from the skin as it evaporates.

### 3. Traps

- Bond breaking is never exothermic; "breaking bonds releases energy" is the commonest error.
- Bond enthalpies refer to gaseous molecules and atoms.
- Boiling water breaks intermolecular forces (hydrogen bonds), not O–H covalent bonds.
- Don't do bond enthalpy calculations here; they are Higher Level, in "ΔH from bond enthalpies".
- "Energy is used up" is wrong; energy is transferred.

### 4. What exams ask (topics only)

- Explain in terms of bonds why a reaction is exothermic or endothermic.
- Define heat of reaction.
- Why the C≡C (or N≡N) bond enthalpy is large; a trend in bond enthalpies (see "ΔH from bond enthalpies").

## Part: Exothermic and endothermic reactions (formerly `chem21-s2`)

- **loId:** `chem21-s2` · **chapterId:** `chem21` · **syllabusRef:** 3.1.2
- **Syllabus:** 3.1.2 "explain, and model diagrammatically, processes of energy transfer using
  exothermic and endothermic reactions". The examples come from the 3.1 learn-about list:
  combustion, neutralisation, thermal decomposition, rusting of iron, photosynthesis,
  respiration. Normal type.
- **Prepared by:** a higher model, 2026-10-30. Definitions, observations, the six named
  processes with equations and ΔH, and the enthalpy level diagram. The bond explanation is in
  "Bond enthalpy and energy change"; the energy profile diagram with activation energy, standard
  conditions and calculating ΔH from measurements are in "Calculating ΔH and the heat of neutralisation".

### 1. Must cover

Definitions (one block)
- [ ] D1 An exothermic reaction gives out heat to the surroundings; ΔH is negative; the products have less enthalpy than the reactants.
- [ ] D2 An endothermic reaction takes in heat from the surroundings; ΔH is positive; the products have more enthalpy than the reactants.
- [ ] D3 Evidence: in an exothermic reaction in solution the temperature of the mixture rises (the container feels warm); in an endothermic reaction it falls (the container feels cold), unless heat is being supplied continuously, as in thermal decomposition.
- [ ] D4 A thermochemical equation is a balanced equation with state symbols and the ΔH value, e.g. CH₄(g) + 2O₂(g) → CO₂(g) + 2H₂O(l), ΔH = −890 kJ mol⁻¹; state symbols matter because changes of state also have enthalpy changes.
- [ ] D5 Reversing a reaction reverses the sign of ΔH: if A → B has ΔH = −x, then B → A has ΔH = +x.
- [ ] D6 ΔH is proportional to the amounts in the equation: doubling every coefficient doubles ΔH, e.g. 2CH₃OH(l) + 3O₂(g) → 2CO₂(g) + 4H₂O(l), ΔH = −1452 kJ (2 × −726).

Exothermic examples (one block)
- [ ] X1 Combustion: CH₄(g) + 2O₂(g) → CO₂(g) + 2H₂O(l), ΔH = −890 kJ mol⁻¹; all combustion reactions are exothermic.
- [ ] X2 Neutralisation: HCl(aq) + NaOH(aq) → NaCl(aq) + H₂O(l), ΔH ≈ −57 kJ mol⁻¹ (ionic equation H⁺(aq) + OH⁻(aq) → H₂O(l)).
- [ ] X3 Respiration: C₆H₁₂O₆(aq) + 6O₂(g) → 6CO₂(g) + 6H₂O(l), ΔH ≈ −2800 kJ mol⁻¹; it releases energy in cells, keeping warm-blooded animals warm.
- [ ] X4 Rusting of iron: iron reacts slowly with oxygen and water to form hydrated iron(III) oxide; the overall change, written simply as 4Fe(s) + 3O₂(g) → 2Fe₂O₃(s), ΔH = −1648 kJ, is exothermic; the heat is released so slowly that it isn't noticed, but hand warmers use fast oxidation of iron powder to give out heat.
- [ ] X5 Other everyday exothermic processes: reactive metals with acid or water; dissolving NaOH; condensing and freezing.

Endothermic examples (one block)
- [ ] N1 Thermal decomposition: breaking down a compound by heating needs a continuous supply of heat, e.g. CaCO₃(s) → CaO(s) + CO₂(g), ΔH = +178 kJ mol⁻¹ (making lime); sodium hydrogencarbonate also decomposes endothermically: 2NaHCO₃(s) → Na₂CO₃(s) + H₂O(g) + CO₂(g) (baking powder in baking).
- [ ] N2 Photosynthesis: 6CO₂(g) + 6H₂O(l) → C₆H₁₂O₆(aq) + 6O₂(g), ΔH ≈ +2800 kJ mol⁻¹; the energy is absorbed from sunlight.
- [ ] N3 Photosynthesis and respiration are the reverse of each other, so their ΔH values are equal in size and opposite in sign.
- [ ] N4 Other endothermic processes: melting, boiling, dissolving ammonium nitrate (cold packs), the reaction of citric acid with sodium hydrogencarbonate solution.

Modelling energy transfer: enthalpy level diagrams (one block)
- [ ] M1 An enthalpy level diagram has enthalpy (H) on the vertical axis, a horizontal line for the reactants and one for the products, and a vertical arrow between them labelled ΔH.
- [ ] M2 Exothermic: the products line is below the reactants line; the arrow points down; ΔH is negative; the energy difference is released to the surroundings as heat.
- [ ] M3 Endothermic: the products line is above the reactants line; the arrow points up; ΔH is positive; the energy difference is taken in from the surroundings.
- [ ] M4 A second way to model the transfer: draw the system (reacting chemicals) inside a box with arrows for heat flowing out to the surroundings (exothermic: surroundings get warmer) or in from them (endothermic: surroundings get cooler).
- [ ] M5 Diagram: two enthalpy level diagrams side by side: combustion of methane (CH₄ + 2O₂ above CO₂ + 2H₂O, ΔH = −890 kJ mol⁻¹, arrow down) and decomposition of calcium carbonate (CaO + CO₂ above CaCO₃, ΔH = +178 kJ mol⁻¹, arrow up).
- [ ] M6 Energy profile diagrams, which also show the activation energy along the reaction pathway, are in "Calculating ΔH and the heat of neutralisation".

### 3. Traps

- Exothermic is ΔH negative, not positive: the system loses heat.
- In an endothermic reaction the surroundings get colder; the system gains heat.
- Rust is really hydrated iron(III) oxide; the equation is simplified. Rusting also needs water.
- Don't give a ΔH value for NaHCO₃ decomposition; it depends on whether the water is liquid or gas.
- Energy profile diagrams with activation energy belong to "Calculating ΔH and the heat of neutralisation".

- What evidence shows a reaction is exothermic?
