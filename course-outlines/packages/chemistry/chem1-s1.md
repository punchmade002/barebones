## Part: How scientists work and how ideas change (formerly `chem1-s1`)

- **loId:** `chem1-s1` · **chapterId:** `chem1` · **syllabusRef:** U1.1
- **Syllabus:** U1.1 "appreciate how scientists work and how scientific ideas are modified over
  time". Learn about: "the nature and evolution of scientific knowledge; recognising bias".
  Ordinary and Higher level; no Higher Level only material.
- **Prepared by:** a higher model, 2026-10-30. Teach the vocabulary of scientific knowledge
  (observation, hypothesis, theory, law, evidence) and how and why ideas change, using the
  development of atomic theory as the running example. Models are the next subsection ("Models
  and their limits"); peer review and reproducibility are in "Science as a global enterprise and researching an issue";
  evaluating sources is in "Science as a global enterprise and researching an issue". Name them, don't teach them.

### 1. Must cover

The vocabulary of scientific knowledge (one block)
- [ ] K1 Observation: information gathered with the senses or with instruments; qualitative (descriptions, e.g. colour change) or quantitative (measurements with numbers and units).
- [ ] K2 Hypothesis: a testable, tentative explanation or prediction that can be supported or contradicted by evidence from an experiment.
- [ ] K3 Theory: a well-tested explanation of a wide range of observations, supported by a large body of evidence from many investigations, e.g. the kinetic theory of matter, collision theory.
- [ ] K4 Law: a statement, often mathematical, that describes what always happens under stated conditions, e.g. the law of conservation of mass, Boyle's law. A law describes what happens; a theory explains why.
- [ ] K5 Evidence: data from observation and experiment that is used to support or reject a hypothesis or theory. Scientific knowledge is based on evidence, not on authority or opinion.

How scientists work (one block)
- [ ] W1 The usual cycle: make observations; ask a question; research what is already known; form a hypothesis; make a prediction; test it with a fair, controlled experiment; analyse the data; draw a conclusion; accept, modify or reject the hypothesis; communicate the results so others can check them.
- [ ] W2 The process is rarely a straight line: scientists repeat, redesign and troubleshoot experiments, and unexpected results often lead to new questions (the cycle is iterative).
- [ ] W3 Scientists use creativity and imagination as well as logic, in proposing hypotheses and designing experiments and models.
- [ ] W4 One contradicting result that is reliable and reproducible can be enough to reject a hypothesis; no number of agreeing results proves it absolutely.

Why scientific ideas change (one block)
- [ ] C1 Scientific knowledge is tentative (provisional): it is the best explanation of the evidence available now and can change when new evidence appears.
- [ ] C2 Ideas change when new observations don't fit, when new technology or instruments allow new measurements, and when better explanations are proposed and tested.
- [ ] C3 Accepted theories are refined or replaced only when the new idea explains all the old evidence and the new evidence better; this is how science progresses.
- [ ] C4 Long-standing, well-tested theories are durable: they are rarely discarded completely but are modified or have their limits identified.

Example: how the model of the atom changed (one block)
- [ ] A1 Dalton (early 1800s): matter is made of tiny indivisible atoms; atoms of an element are identical. Supported by the laws of chemical combination.
- [ ] A2 Thomson (1897): discovered the electron, so atoms are not indivisible; proposed the "plum pudding" model (negative electrons in a sphere of positive charge).
- [ ] A3 Rutherford (1911): the gold-foil experiment showed that most of the atom is empty space, with a tiny, dense, positive nucleus (the nuclear model).
- [ ] A4 Bohr (1913): electrons move in fixed energy levels around the nucleus, explaining line emission spectra.
- [ ] A5 The current orbital model: electrons occupy orbitals, regions in which there is a high probability of finding an electron. Each model was replaced or refined because new evidence did not fit the earlier one. (The models themselves are taught in "Atomic structure: the nuclear model" and "The orbital model and electronic structure"; here they illustrate how ideas change.)

Bias (one block)
- [ ] B1 Bias is a tendency, deliberate or not, to collect, interpret or present information in a way that favours one outcome.
- [ ] B2 Sources of bias in science: expecting a particular result (confirmation bias), selecting only data that support a hypothesis, small or unrepresentative samples, funding or commercial interests, and personal or political beliefs.
- [ ] B3 Ways scientists reduce bias: fair tests with controls, repeats, random sampling, blind trials, publishing all results including negative ones, declaring funding and conflicts of interest, and independent checking by other scientists (see "Science as a global enterprise and researching an issue").

### 3. Traps

- A theory is not a guess: in everyday speech "theory" means a hunch; in science it is a well-supported explanation. A hypothesis is the tentative idea.
- A law is not a "proven theory"; laws describe, theories explain. Theories don't become laws.
- Don't say science "proves" things absolutely; evidence supports or contradicts.
- Don't teach the detail of each atomic model or the gold-foil observations here; that belongs to "Atomic structure: the nuclear model".
- Radioactivity is not on the specification; don't describe the alpha source beyond "positively charged particles".

### 4. What exams ask (topics only)

- Distinguish between a hypothesis, a theory and a law, with an example.
- Explain why scientific knowledge changes over time, with an example.
- Identify possible bias in a described study.

## Part: Models and their limits (formerly `chem1-s2`)

- **loId:** `chem1-s2` · **chapterId:** `chem1` · **syllabusRef:** U1 LA

  and the limitations of models". No Higher Level only material.
- **Prepared by:** a higher model, 2026-10-30. Keep this to what a model is, the kinds of model
  chemists use, why they are powerful, and their limitations, with chemical examples. The U4
  outcome (models as simplified representations with assumptions, modified with new data,
  predictive) is taught in "Representation and the Chemistry in Practice Investigation"; don't repeat its three properties
  as a list here, but you may use the words.

### 1. Must cover

What a model is (one block)
- [ ] M1 A model is a representation of an object, system, process or idea that helps us understand, explain or predict something we cannot see or handle directly, such as atoms and molecules.
- [ ] M2 Kinds of model in chemistry: physical models (ball-and-stick and space-filling molecular models), diagrams (dot-and-cross diagrams, energy-level diagrams, energy profile diagrams), mathematical models (equations such as PV = nRT), symbolic models (chemical formulae and equations) and computer or digital simulations.
- [ ] M3 Theoretical models: a theory can act as a model, e.g. the kinetic theory models matter as moving particles; collision theory models how reactions happen.

The power of models (one block)
- [ ] P1 Models make the very small, very large or invisible understandable, e.g. a molecular model shows the shape of a molecule that is far too small to see.
- [ ] P2 Models explain observations, e.g. the particle model explains why gases can be compressed and liquids cannot (much).
- [ ] P3 Models allow predictions that can be tested, e.g. the ideal gas equation predicts the volume of a gas at a new temperature and pressure; VSEPR theory predicts molecular shapes.
- [ ] P4 Models help communicate ideas between scientists and to the public.

Limitations of models (one block)
- [ ] L1 Every model is a simplification: it leaves out detail and is built on assumptions, so it is never a perfect copy of reality.
- [ ] L2 A model works only within a range of conditions, e.g. the ideal gas model fails at high pressure and low temperature, where real gases deviate.
- [ ] L3 Physical models can mislead: ball-and-stick models show atoms as hard coloured balls and bonds as rigid sticks, and show the wrong relative sizes and distances.
- [ ] L4 Different models of the same thing may each explain some observations but not others, so chemists choose the model that suits the question (e.g. the Bohr model is good enough to explain flame colours and line spectra but not the shapes of orbitals).
- [ ] L5 Models are tested against evidence and are modified or replaced when they fail to explain new observations.
