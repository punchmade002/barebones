## Part: Chemical cells and fuel cells (formerly `chem22-s4`)

- **loId:** `chem22-s4` · **chapterId:** `chem22` · **syllabusRef:** 3.5.7
- **Syllabus:** 3.5.7 "compare a chemical cell with a fuel cell". Learn about: "a chemical cell:
  using chemical reactions to convert and transfer energy to electrical energy, until the
  reactants have been used up; a fuel cell: needing a constant supply of external reactants to
  generate energy, as the products are continuously removed". Normal type.
- **Prepared by:** a higher model, 2026-10-30. The two definitions, what they share, and a
  point-by-point comparison, plus the comparison of a fuel cell with burning the fuel. Primary
  and secondary cells are in "Galvanic and electrolytic cells"; how the hydrogen fuel cell works
  (electrodes, membrane, equations) is in "The hydrogen fuel cell"; sustainability arguments
  about hydrogen are in "Electrochemistry and sustainability". Don't repeat them.

### 1. Must cover

The two definitions (one block)
- [ ] D1 A chemical cell (e.g. a zinc–carbon, alkaline, lead–acid or lithium-ion cell) uses a redox reaction between reactants sealed inside it to convert chemical energy into electrical energy, until those reactants have been used up.
- [ ] D2 When a chemical cell's reactants are used up it stops working: a primary cell is then discarded; a secondary cell must be recharged from an external electrical supply.
- [ ] D3 A fuel cell converts the chemical energy of a fuel (e.g. hydrogen) and an oxidising agent (usually oxygen from the air) into electrical energy; the reactants are not stored in the cell but are supplied continuously from outside, and the products (water, for hydrogen) are continuously removed.
- [ ] D4 A fuel cell keeps producing electricity for as long as fuel and oxidant are supplied; it is refuelled, not recharged, and it does not run down like a chemical cell.

What they have in common (one block)
- [ ] S1 Both are galvanic cells: each uses a spontaneous redox reaction to drive electrons through an external circuit.
- [ ] S2 Both have an anode where the fuel or reducing agent is oxidised (negative), a cathode where the oxidising agent is reduced (positive), and an electrolyte that carries ions between them.
- [ ] S3 In both, oxidation and reduction happen at separate electrodes, so the energy is released as electrical energy rather than as heat.

Comparison (one block; one bullet per point)
- [ ] C1 Source of reactants: chemical cell, stored inside and limited; fuel cell, supplied continuously from external tanks or the air.
- [ ] C2 Products: chemical cell, stay inside the cell; fuel cell, removed continuously (e.g. water vapour from the exhaust).
- [ ] C3 When reactants run out: chemical cell, replaced (primary) or recharged, which can take hours (secondary); fuel cell, refuelled in minutes.
- [ ] C4 Energy storage: in a chemical cell the energy is stored in the electrode materials, so more energy needs a bigger, heavier cell; in a fuel cell the energy is stored in the fuel tank, so the range depends on the tank size.
- [ ] C5 Typical uses: chemical cells in portable devices (phones, laptops, torches) and battery electric vehicles; fuel cells in hydrogen buses, cars and trucks, spacecraft and backup power supplies.
- [ ] C6 Environmental impact: a hydrogen fuel cell's only product is water; chemical cells need metals (e.g. lithium, cobalt, nickel, lead, zinc) to be mined and the cells to be recycled; but a fuel cell needs hydrogen to be produced, stored and transported, and is only as clean as the way the hydrogen was made.

Fuel cell compared with burning the fuel (one block)
- [ ] F1 In a fuel cell, hydrogen and oxygen react by the same overall reaction as when hydrogen burns (2H₂ + O₂ → 2H₂O), but there is no flame or combustion: the electrons are transferred through the external circuit.
- [ ] F2 A fuel cell converts chemical energy directly into electrical energy; an engine or power station first converts it to heat, and much of that heat is lost, so a fuel cell is more efficient than burning the fuel.
- [ ] F3 A fuel cell has no moving parts, so it is quiet; a hydrogen fuel cell produces no carbon dioxide, carbon monoxide or soot at the point of use, and, because there is no high-temperature flame, no nitrogen oxides.

| Item | Fact |
|---|---|

No numbers are needed here; efficiencies are described only as "more efficient than combustion".

### 3. Traps

- A fuel cell is not a battery that is recharged; it is refuelled.
- A fuel cell does not burn the fuel; there is no combustion.
- Both are galvanic cells: don't call the fuel cell an electrolytic cell.
- "Zero emissions" applies only at the point of use; how the hydrogen is made matters.

### 4. What exams ask (topics only)

- Define a fuel cell; distinguish it from a chemical cell.
- State advantages and disadvantages of fuel cells compared with chemical cells (or with combustion engines).

### 5. Practice questions to write

2. 6 marks: "State one similarity between a chemical cell and a fuel cell, and give two advantages of a hydrogen fuel cell over burning hydrogen in an engine." Model answer: S1 (or S2); two of F2, F3.

## Part: The hydrogen fuel cell (formerly `chem22-s5`)

- **loId:** `chem22-s5` · **chapterId:** `chem22` · **syllabusRef:** 3.5.8
- **Syllabus:** 3.5.8 "explain the operation of a simple hydrogen fuel cell". Learn about: "a
  fuel cell as a galvanic cell that converts the chemical energy of a fuel and an oxidising
  agent through redox reaction". Normal type.
- **Prepared by:** a higher model, 2026-10-30. The structure and operation of a proton exchange
  membrane (PEM) hydrogen–oxygen fuel cell with its electrode equations, the redox analysis,
  practical points (catalyst, voltage, stacks), uses and limitations, with the alkaline fuel
  cell as a second example. The comparison with chemical cells is in "Chemical cells and fuel
  cells"; where hydrogen comes from and its sustainability is in "Electrochemistry and
  sustainability". Don't repeat them.

### 1. Must cover

Structure of a hydrogen fuel cell (one block)
- [ ] S1 A hydrogen fuel cell is a galvanic cell in which the fuel, hydrogen, is oxidised and the oxidising agent, oxygen (from the air), is reduced, producing electricity and water.
- [ ] S2 Hydrogen gas is fed continuously to the anode (negative electrode); oxygen or air is fed continuously to the cathode (positive electrode).
- [ ] S3 The electrodes are porous carbon coated with a platinum catalyst: the pores let the gases reach the electrolyte, and the platinum speeds up the electrode reactions.
- [ ] S4 In the common type the electrolyte is a proton exchange membrane (PEM), a thin sheet of solid polymer between the electrodes.
- [ ] S5 The membrane lets H⁺ ions (protons) pass from the anode to the cathode; it does not conduct electrons, so they must go round the external circuit; and it keeps the hydrogen and oxygen apart.

How it works (one block, one step per bullet)
- [ ] O1 Step 1: At the anode, hydrogen molecules are oxidised to hydrogen ions, releasing electrons: H₂(g) → 2H⁺(aq) + 2e⁻.
- [ ] O2 Step 2: The electrons flow through the external circuit to the cathode, powering a motor or other device on the way.
- [ ] O3 Step 3: The H⁺ ions move through the membrane to the cathode.
- [ ] O4 Step 4: At the cathode, oxygen is reduced by combining with the H⁺ ions and the electrons to form water: O₂(g) + 4H⁺(aq) + 4e⁻ → 2H₂O(l).
- [ ] O5 Step 5: The water is removed from the cathode side (as liquid or vapour), so the reaction can continue.
- [ ] O6 Overall: 2H₂(g) + O₂(g) → 2H₂O(l) (doubling the anode equation to balance the electrons: 2H₂ → 4H⁺ + 4e⁻).

Redox in the fuel cell (one block)
- [ ] R1 Hydrogen is the reducing agent: its oxidation number rises from 0 in H₂ to +1 in H₂O (oxidation, loss of electrons, at the anode).
- [ ] R2 Oxygen is the oxidising agent: its oxidation number falls from 0 in O₂ to −2 in H₂O (reduction, gain of electrons, at the cathode).
- [ ] R3 The reaction is strongly exothermic (ΔH = −286 kJ per mole of liquid water formed); in the fuel cell much of this energy is released as electrical energy rather than heat.

Practical points (one block)
- [ ] P1 A single hydrogen fuel cell gives a maximum of about 1.2 V and, in use, about 0.6–0.8 V, so many cells are connected in series in a stack; a car's stack contains several hundred cells.
- [ ] P2 Fuel cells also produce some heat, which can be used, e.g. for heating in buildings or buses.
- [ ] P3 The hydrogen must be pure: impurities such as carbon monoxide poison the platinum catalyst.

The alkaline fuel cell (one block)
- [ ] A1 In an alkaline fuel cell the electrolyte is potassium hydroxide solution, and OH⁻ ions carry the charge through it.
- [ ] A2 Anode (oxidation): 2H₂(g) + 4OH⁻(aq) → 4H₂O(l) + 4e⁻.
- [ ] A3 Cathode (reduction): O₂(g) + 2H₂O(l) + 4e⁻ → 4OH⁻(aq).
- [ ] A4 Overall: 2H₂(g) + O₂(g) → 2H₂O(l), the same as in the PEM cell.
- [ ] A5 Alkaline fuel cells powered the Apollo spacecraft and produced drinking water for the astronauts.

Uses, advantages and limitations (one block)
- [ ] U1 Uses: hydrogen buses, cars and trucks; spacecraft; backup and off-grid power supplies.
- [ ] U2 Advantages: the only product is water, so there is no CO₂, CO, nitrogen oxides or soot at the point of use; more efficient than burning hydrogen; quiet; refuelled in minutes; runs as long as fuel is supplied.
- [ ] U3 Limitations: hydrogen is highly flammable and forms explosive mixtures with air; it has a very low density, so it must be stored compressed at high pressure (or liquefied at very low temperature) in strong, heavy tanks; there are few hydrogen refuelling stations; platinum is scarce and expensive.
- [ ] U4 Most hydrogen today is made from natural gas, which releases carbon dioxide; a fuel cell is only low-carbon overall when the hydrogen is made by electrolysis using renewable electricity.
- [ ] U5 Diagram: a PEM fuel cell: hydrogen in at the anode (−) and oxygen or air in at the cathode (+), the membrane between them with H⁺ moving across, the external circuit with a load and electrons flowing from anode to cathode, and water leaving from the cathode side; write the half-equations beside each electrode.

### 3. Traps

- Hydrogen is oxidised at the anode (negative); oxygen is reduced at the cathode (positive). Don't swap them.
- The membrane carries H⁺ ions, not electrons.
- A fuel cell is a galvanic cell, not an electrolytic cell, and it involves no combustion.
- Don't write OH⁻ in the PEM equations or H⁺ in the alkaline-cell equations.
- Hydrogen production methods other than electrolysis (e.g. steam reforming) are named only; their equations aren't required.

### 4. What exams ask (topics only)

- Define a fuel cell; name the fuel and the oxidising agent in a hydrogen fuel cell.
- Describe how a hydrogen fuel cell works; write the half-equations at each electrode and the overall equation.
- State the functions of the proton exchange membrane and of the platinum.
- Give advantages and disadvantages of hydrogen fuel cells for vehicles.

### 5. Practice questions to write

2. 6 marks: "Use oxidation numbers to identify the substance oxidised and the oxidising agent in a hydrogen fuel cell." Model answer: R1, R2.
3. 6 marks: "Give two advantages and two disadvantages of using hydrogen fuel cells to power buses." Model answer: two of U2; two of U3 (or U4).

## Part: Electrochemistry and sustainability (formerly `chem22-s6`)

- **loId:** `chem22-s6` · **chapterId:** `chem22` · **syllabusRef:** 3.5.9
- **Syllabus:** 3.5.9 "research a role of electrochemistry in an area related to sustainability
  and technology in everyday life" (RI: Research-based Investigation). Learn about: "how
  electrochemistry could provide more sustainable uses of energy, such as electrochemical cells
  and the use of rechargeable batteries; electrolysis, and its potential use for hydrogen
  production as a fuel for example, through electricity generated from sustainable sources".
  Normal type.
- **Prepared by:** a higher model, 2026-10-30. The two learn-about ideas as content, how to run
  the research investigation, then one worked research outline for each of the four options:
  rechargeable batteries and electric vehicles; green hydrogen from electrolysis; fuel cells;
  corrosion protection. Cite the chemistry from its own note and don't re-teach it: secondary
  cells in "Galvanic and electrolytic cells"; electrolysis of water in "Galvanic and electrolytic cells"; the
  fuel cell in "The hydrogen fuel cell" and "Chemical cells and fuel cells"; the lithium-ion
  cell and its life-cycle (mining, use, disposal, recycling) in "The lithium-ion cell";
  corrosion and its prevention in "Oxidation and reduction"; the electrochemical series in "The
  electrochemical series and displacement"; climate change in "The greenhouse effect".

### 1. Must cover

Electrochemistry and more sustainable energy (one block)
- [ ] E1 Electrochemical cells convert chemical energy directly into electrical energy, without first burning a fuel to make heat, so less energy is wasted than in an engine or a fossil-fuel power station.
- [ ] E2 Rechargeable (secondary) batteries can be reused hundreds or thousands of times, cutting the waste and raw materials of single-use cells.
- [ ] E3 Rechargeable batteries let electricity from renewable sources (wind, solar) be stored and used later, in devices, in electric vehicles and in large grid batteries; this matters because wind and solar power vary with the weather and time of day.
- [ ] E4 Electrolysis can split water into hydrogen and oxygen (2H₂O → 2H₂ + O₂); if the electricity comes from renewable sources, the hydrogen is made without releasing carbon dioxide ("green hydrogen").
- [ ] E5 Hydrogen stores energy as a fuel: it can be kept, transported and later used in a fuel cell to make electricity again, or used in industry; its only combustion or fuel-cell product is water.
- [ ] E6 Most hydrogen produced today is made from natural gas ("grey hydrogen"), which releases carbon dioxide; "blue hydrogen" is made the same way but with the carbon dioxide captured and stored.

How to carry out the research investigation (one block, one step per bullet)
- [ ] R1 Step 1: Choose one application and write a focused question that can be answered with evidence, e.g. "Are battery electric cars more sustainable than petrol cars in Ireland?"
- [ ] R2 Step 2: Explain the chemistry involved: the redox reactions, the type of cell (galvanic or electrolytic), and the electrode reactions where known.
- [ ] R3 Step 3: Gather evidence from a range of sources: textbooks, peer-reviewed articles, government and public agencies (e.g. the Sustainable Energy Authority of Ireland, the Environmental Protection Agency, the International Energy Agency), manufacturers and news reports.
- [ ] R4 Step 4: Judge each source: who wrote it and their expertise; whether it is peer-reviewed or official; how recent it is (this technology changes quickly); whether the author gains from the conclusion (e.g. a company selling the product); whether it gives data and references rather than opinion; and whether other sources agree.
- [ ] R5 Step 5: Consider the whole life-cycle: raw materials and mining, manufacture, the source of electricity or fuel during use, lifetime, and disposal or recycling.
- [ ] R6 Step 6: Weigh the evidence on each side (environmental, economic, social, safety) and reach a justified conclusion, stating the conditions under which it holds.
- [ ] R7 Step 7: Present the findings with references, and evaluate the investigation: gaps in the evidence, conflicting sources, and what further research would help.

Worked outline 1: rechargeable batteries and electric vehicles (one block)
- [ ] B1 Question: do rechargeable lithium-ion batteries in electric vehicles make transport more sustainable?
- [ ] B2 Chemistry: the lithium-ion cell is a secondary cell, a galvanic cell on discharge and an electrolytic cell on recharging (see "The lithium-ion cell").
- [ ] B3 Evidence for: no exhaust emissions at the point of use (no CO₂, CO, nitrogen oxides or particulates from a tailpipe), improving air quality in towns; electric motors waste less energy than petrol or diesel engines; batteries can be charged from renewable electricity; the same batteries can store surplus renewable electricity.
- [ ] B4 Evidence against: mining lithium, cobalt and nickel damages land and uses large amounts of water; much cobalt is mined in the Democratic Republic of the Congo, where child labour and unsafe working conditions have been reported; making the battery releases a lot of CO₂; the benefit depends on how the electricity is generated; recycling is not yet widespread; damaged cells can catch fire.
- [ ] B5 Justified conclusion (example): electric vehicles reduce emissions over their lifetime where electricity is increasingly renewable, as in Ireland, provided batteries are made responsibly and recycled.

Worked outline 2: green hydrogen from electrolysis (one block)
- [ ] H1 Question: can hydrogen produced by electrolysis using wind power be a sustainable fuel?
- [ ] H2 Chemistry: electrolysis of water; at the cathode 4H⁺ + 4e⁻ → 2H₂, at the anode 2H₂O → O₂ + 4H⁺ + 4e⁻ (see "Galvanic and electrolytic cells").
- [ ] H3 Evidence for: no CO₂ is released if the electricity is renewable; it can use surplus wind electricity at times when supply is greater than demand, storing it as a fuel; hydrogen can power fuel-cell vehicles and replace fossil fuels in industries that are hard to electrify; the only product when it is used is water.
- [ ] H4 Evidence against: energy is lost at every step, so converting electricity to hydrogen and back to electricity returns well under half of the original energy, much less than a battery; electrolysers need pure water and some need scarce metal catalysts (e.g. platinum and iridium); hydrogen is hard to store (high pressure or very low temperature) and highly flammable; green hydrogen currently costs more than grey hydrogen.
- [ ] H5 Justified conclusion (example): green hydrogen is most useful where batteries are unsuitable (heavy transport, industry, long-term storage), but only if powered by genuinely renewable electricity.

Worked outline 3: fuel cells (one block)
- [ ] F1 Question: should hydrogen fuel cells power public buses?
- [ ] F2 Chemistry: a galvanic cell; anode H₂ → 2H⁺ + 2e⁻; cathode O₂ + 4H⁺ + 4e⁻ → 2H₂O.
- [ ] F3 Evidence for: water is the only product at the point of use; quiet; refuelled in minutes; long range suits buses on long, fixed routes that return to one depot for refuelling.
- [ ] F4 Evidence against: few refuelling stations; expensive vehicles and platinum catalyst; high-pressure storage tanks; if the hydrogen is grey, the overall CO₂ saving is small or none.
- [ ] F5 Justified conclusion (example): fuel-cell buses are a good choice only where green hydrogen is available at a competitive price.

Worked outline 4: corrosion protection (one block)
- [ ] C1 Question: how does electrochemistry help steel structures last longer, and why is that sustainable?
- [ ] C2 Chemistry: rusting is the oxidation of iron; a metal higher in the electrochemical series, such as zinc or magnesium, is oxidised in preference to iron. Galvanising (a zinc coating) and sacrificial anodes (blocks of zinc or magnesium attached to ship hulls, pipelines and steel piers) use this (see "Oxidation and reduction" and "Balancing redox equations and the electrochemical series").
- [ ] C3 Evidence for: steel lasts much longer, so less steel has to be made to replace it, saving iron ore, energy and the CO₂ released in steelmaking; it prevents dangerous failures of bridges, pipelines and ships.
- [ ] C4 Evidence against: sacrificial anodes are used up and must be replaced; zinc released into seawater can harm marine life; galvanised coatings wear away eventually.
- [ ] C5 Justified conclusion (example): corrosion protection is a cheap and effective way of conserving metal resources, though zinc release should be monitored.
