## Part: Measurement and error (formerly `chem1-s7`)

- **loId:** `chem1-s7` · **chapterId:** `chem1` · **syllabusRef:** U2 LA
- **Syllabus:** U2 learn about: "using SI units for measurement and conversion to and from
  commonly used units, identifying potential sources of random and systematic error, and giving
  due consideration to the limits of the precision and accuracy of measurement". No Higher
  Level only material.
- **Prepared by:** a higher model, 2026-10-30. Include unit conversions used throughout the
  course (volume, mass, temperature, pressure) and significant figures (assumed knowledge).

### 1. Must cover

SI units and conversions (one block)
- [ ] U1 SI base units used in chemistry: metre (m), kilogram (kg), second (s), kelvin (K), mole (mol); derived units include m³ for volume and pascal (Pa) for pressure.
- [ ] U2 Volume: 1 dm³ = 1 L = 1000 cm³ = 1000 mL; 1 m³ = 1000 dm³ = 1 000 000 cm³.
- [ ] U3 Mass: 1 kg = 1000 g; 1 g = 1000 mg.
- [ ] U4 Temperature: T (K) = θ (°C) + 273 (more exactly 273.15); e.g. 25 °C = 298 K.
- [ ] U5 Pressure: 1 kPa = 1000 Pa; standard pressure 100 kPa = 1 × 10⁵ Pa (1 atm = 101.325 kPa).
- [ ] U6 (assumed) Convert by multiplying or dividing by the conversion factor, writing the unit at each step; e.g. 250 cm³ = 250 ÷ 1000 = 0.250 L.

Accuracy and precision (one block)
- [ ] A1 Accuracy: how close a measurement (or mean) is to the true or accepted value.
- [ ] A2 Precision: how close repeated measurements are to each other (their spread); also used for the smallest division a piece of equipment can read (its resolution).
- [ ] A3 Results can be precise but not accurate (closely grouped but all off the true value, e.g. a balance that isn't zeroed) or accurate on average but not precise.
- [ ] A4 Limits of equipment: every instrument has an uncertainty; typical school values: 25 cm³ pipette ±0.06 cm³, burette reading ±0.05 cm³, 250 cm³ volumetric flask ±0.3 cm³, a 100 cm³ measuring cylinder ±1 cm³. Volumetric glassware is far more accurate than a measuring cylinder.
- [ ] A5 Significant figures (assumed): report answers to the same number of significant figures as the least precise measurement used (usually three in titration calculations).

Errors (one block)
- [ ] E1 Random error: unpredictable variation that scatters readings above and below the true value (e.g. judging an end point, reading a meniscus, temperature fluctuations); reduced by repeating and averaging.
- [ ] E2 Systematic error: a consistent error that shifts all readings in the same direction (e.g. a balance not zeroed, a thermometer that reads 1 °C high, heat loss to the surroundings in a calorimetry experiment, parallax always from the same angle); not reduced by repeating; reduced by calibrating equipment or improving the method.
- [ ] E3 Parallax error: reading a scale from an angle; read the bottom of the meniscus at eye level.
- [ ] E4 Anomalous results (outliers) caused by mistakes are identified and repeated or excluded with a reason.
- [ ] E5 Percentage error = (experimental value − accepted value) ÷ accepted value × 100.

Present A4 values as "typical", not exact.

### 3. Traps

- "Precise" does not mean "accurate".
- Repeating reduces random error only; systematic error needs a better method or calibration.
- STP conditions are taught in "The mole and molar volume"; don't define STP here.

### 4. What exams ask (topics only)

- Convert units (cm³ to L, °C to K, g to mg).
- Distinguish random and systematic error with examples; distinguish accuracy and precision.
- Explain why a pipette is used rather than a measuring cylinder.

### 5. Practice questions to write

1. 6 marks: "Distinguish between random error and systematic error, giving one example of each from a titration." Model answer: E1, E2 applied (end-point judgement; burette not rinsed with solution, or always reading the top of the meniscus).
2. 6 marks: "Convert (i) 37 °C to kelvin, (ii) 0.150 L to cm³, (iii) 2.5 g to mg, (iv) 250 kPa to Pa." Model answer: 310 K; 150 cm³; 2500 mg; 2.5 × 10⁵ Pa.

## Part: Producing and analysing data (formerly `chem1-s8`)

- **loId:** `chem1-s8` · **chapterId:** `chem1` · **syllabusRef:** U2.4
- **Syllabus:** U2.4 "produce and select data (qualitatively quantitatively), critically analyse
  data to identify patterns and relationships, identify anomalous observations, draw and justify
  conclusions". Learn about: "how to use trends in categories, tables, graphs and data in
  general, to make predictions and deepen understanding"; "using models to understand the
  investigation; manipulating mathematical representations of data and using scientific
  notation". No Higher Level only material.
- **Prepared by:** a higher model, 2026-10-30. Include results tables, graph-drawing rules,
  reading gradients, and scientific notation (assumed knowledge).

### 1. Must cover

Qualitative and quantitative data (one block)
- [ ] Q1 Qualitative data are descriptions without numbers (colour change, effervescence, precipitate formed); quantitative data are measurements with numbers and units.
- [ ] Q2 Select the data that answer the question; record raw data first, then processed data (means, rates).

Results tables (one block)
- [ ] T1 Independent variable in the first column, dependent variable (and repeats) in the next columns, with a mean column.
- [ ] T2 Column headings give the quantity and its unit (e.g. "Time / s"); units are not written in the body of the table.
- [ ] T3 Record all readings to the same number of decimal places, matching the resolution of the instrument.

Drawing graphs (one block)
- [ ] G1 Independent variable on the x-axis, dependent variable on the y-axis; both axes labelled with quantity and unit.
- [ ] G2 Choose scales so the points fill more than half the grid, with even, sensible intervals; plot points accurately as small crosses.
- [ ] G3 Draw a line or smooth curve of best fit through the general trend; don't join point to point; don't force it through the origin unless the origin is a real data point.
- [ ] G4 Use a line graph when both variables are continuous; a bar chart for categories.

Analysing data (one block)
- [ ] A1 Describe the pattern: increases, decreases, levels off, directly proportional (straight line through the origin), inversely proportional.
- [ ] A2 Gradient of a straight line = change in y ÷ change in x; for a curve, the gradient at a point is found from a tangent drawn at that point (used for rates).
- [ ] A3 Interpolation reads a value within the range of the data; extrapolation extends the line beyond it (less reliable); both are predictions.
- [ ] A4 Use trends in tables and categories to predict, e.g. predict the boiling point of the next alkane from the trend in the series.
- [ ] A5 Mathematical representations: rearrange and use equations (e.g. PV = nRT), calculate means, percentages and rates.
- [ ] A6 (assumed) Scientific notation (standard form): a number written as A × 10ⁿ, with A between 1 and 10, e.g. 6.02 × 10²³; 0.00050 = 5.0 × 10⁻⁴.

Anomalous results and conclusions (one block)
- [ ] N1 An anomalous result does not fit the pattern of the other results; identify it on the graph or in the table, suggest a reason (e.g. a misreading), and leave it out of the mean and the line of best fit, saying so.
- [ ] N2 A conclusion states the relationship shown by the data, says whether it supports the hypothesis, and explains it using scientific theory or a model (e.g. collision theory).
- [ ] N3 Justify the conclusion with reference to the data; state its limits (range tested, amount of data, size of errors).
- [ ] N4 Correlation does not prove causation: a link between two variables may be due to a third factor.

### 3. Traps

- A mean is calculated without anomalous results.
- A line of best fit is not dot-to-dot.
- Don't redefine accuracy, precision or error types.

### 4. What exams ask (topics only)

- Plot a graph from a table of results and use it to read off a value or find a rate.
- Identify an anomalous result and explain how to treat it.
- Draw a conclusion and relate it to the hypothesis.

### 5. Practice questions to write

1. 9 marks: "A student measured the volume of gas produced every 30 s in a reaction. Describe how she should draw a graph of the results and how she could use the graph to find the rate of reaction at 60 s." Model answer: G1–G3, A2 (tangent at 60 s, gradient with units cm³ s⁻¹).
2. 6 marks: "Express in scientific notation: (i) 602 000 000 000 000 000 000 000, (ii) 0.00034, (iii) 101 325." Model answer: 6.02 × 10²³; 3.4 × 10⁻⁴; 1.01325 × 10⁵.
