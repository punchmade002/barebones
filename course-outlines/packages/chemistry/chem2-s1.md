## 1. Must cover

Early models (one block, assumed background)
- [ ] D1 (assumed) Dalton's atomic theory (early 1800s): all matter is made of tiny indivisible particles called atoms; atoms of the same element are identical; atoms of different elements differ in mass; atoms combine in simple whole-number ratios to form compounds; atoms are not created or destroyed in reactions.
- [ ] D2 (assumed) Thomson (1897) discovered the electron, using cathode rays: negatively charged particles much lighter than atoms, present in all atoms. So atoms are divisible.
- [ ] D3 (assumed) Thomson's "plum pudding" model: the atom is a sphere of positive charge with negative electrons embedded in it, so that the atom is neutral overall.

The nuclear model (one block)
- [ ] N1 The gold-foil experiment (Geiger and Marsden, directed by Rutherford): a beam of positively charged alpha particles was fired at a very thin sheet of gold foil, with a fluorescent screen around it to detect where they went.
- [ ] N2 Observations and conclusions: most particles went straight through, so most of the atom is empty space; some were deflected through large angles, so there is a concentrated region of positive charge that repels them; a very few bounced back, so this positive region (the nucleus) is very small and very dense, containing almost all the mass.
- [ ] N3 Rutherford's nuclear model (1911): a tiny, dense, positively charged nucleus at the centre, with electrons moving around it in the mostly empty space outside.
- [ ] N4 Later work found the proton (Rutherford) and the neutron (Chadwick, 1932) in the nucleus (see "Subatomic particles").
- [ ] N5 Diagram: the gold-foil set-up (source, beam, gold foil, detecting screen) and the paths of particles past the nuclei: straight through, deflected, bounced back.

The Bohr model (one block)
- [ ] B1 Bohr (1913): electrons move around the nucleus in fixed circular orbits called energy levels (shells), each with a fixed energy; electrons in a level don't lose energy.
- [ ] B2 An electron can move to a higher energy level by absorbing a definite amount of energy and emits light of a definite energy when it falls back, which explains the lines in emission spectra (see "Energy levels and the hydrogen emission spectrum").
- [ ] B3 The energy levels are numbered n = 1, 2, 3…, with n = 1 closest to the nucleus and lowest in energy; they hold up to 2, 8, 18… electrons.

The orbital model (one block)
- [ ] O1 The current model: electrons don't follow fixed paths; they occupy orbitals, regions of space around the nucleus where there is a high probability of finding an electron.
- [ ] O2 Main energy levels are divided into sublevels (s, p, d, f), each made of orbitals that each hold up to two electrons (detail in "The current orbital model").
- [ ] O3 It developed from evidence that electrons behave as waves as well as particles and that the exact position and path of an electron can't be known at the same time.

Assumptions and limitations (Higher Level only; one block)
- [ ] H1 [HL] Dalton: assumed atoms are indivisible and all atoms of an element identical; limited because subatomic particles and isotopes exist.
- [ ] H2 [HL] Thomson's plum pudding: assumed positive charge is spread through the atom; limited because it can't explain the large deflections and back-scattering in the gold-foil experiment.
- [ ] H3 [HL] Nuclear model: assumed electrons orbit like planets; limited because it doesn't say how electrons are arranged, and a circling charged electron would be expected to lose energy and spiral into the nucleus; it can't explain line spectra.
- [ ] H4 [HL] Bohr model: assumed electrons move in fixed circular orbits with fixed energies; it explains the spectrum of hydrogen (one electron) but not the spectra of atoms with more than one electron; it doesn't account for sublevels or for the wave nature of electrons, and it treats the electron's exact path as known.
- [ ] H5 [HL] Orbital model: the best fit for current evidence; its limitation is that it is mathematical and abstract and gives probabilities, not exact positions; it is hard to picture.

Evaluating the models (one block)
- [ ] E1 Each model was accepted because it explained the evidence of its time, and replaced or refined when new evidence did not fit.
- [ ] E2 Earlier models are still useful: the Bohr model is enough to explain flame colours, simple line spectra and electron arrangements in shells (2, 8, 8) used to predict bonding; the nuclear model explains why atoms are mostly empty space.

## 3. Traps

- Radioactivity is not on the specification: describe alpha particles only as "small, fast, positively charged particles"; don't explain alpha decay.
- Don't say electrons in the orbital model "orbit"; they occupy orbitals.
- Thomson's model is the plum pudding model, not the nuclear model.
- The energy-level equation Em − En = hf and the hydrogen spectrum belong to "Energy levels and the hydrogen emission spectrum".
- Keep Higher Level assumptions and limitations in their own block.

## 4. What exams ask (topics only)

- Describe the gold-foil experiment: observations and conclusions.
- Describe the Bohr model and state a limitation.
- Compare the Bohr model with the orbital model.
