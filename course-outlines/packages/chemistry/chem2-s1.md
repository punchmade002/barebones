## Part: Models of the atom (formerly `chem2-s1`)

- **loId:** `chem2-s1` · **chapterId:** `chem2` · **syllabusRef:** 1.2.1
- **Syllabus:** 1.2.1 "evaluate previous models of the atom against the current model, **stating
  assumptions and limitations in each case**". Learn about: "the nuclear, Bohr and orbital
  models of atomic structure, **considering assumptions and limitations in each case**". Bold =
  Higher Level only. The strand overview: previous models "still have use in understanding
  atomic structure" and the current model "is the best fit for the evidence available".
- **Prepared by:** a higher model, 2026-10-30. Structure: one block per model in order (Dalton
  and Thomson as background, then nuclear, Bohr, orbital), each with what it says and the
  evidence for it; then one [HL] block of assumptions and limitations of each; then an
  evaluation block. Details of the orbital model (sublevels, s and p shapes) are in "The current
  orbital model" and "The orbital model and electronic structure"; energy levels and spectra are in "Energy levels and
  the hydrogen emission spectrum". Keep each model to its main idea here.

### 1. Must cover

Early models (one block, assumed background)
- [ ] D1 (assumed) Dalton's atomic theory (early 1800s): all matter is made of tiny indivisible particles called atoms; atoms of the same element are identical; atoms of different elements differ in mass; atoms combine in simple whole-number ratios to form compounds; atoms are not created or destroyed in reactions.
- [ ] D2 (assumed) Thomson (1897) discovered the electron, using cathode rays: negatively charged particles much lighter than atoms, present in all atoms. So atoms are divisible.
- [ ] D3 (assumed) Thomson's "plum pudding" model: the atom is a sphere of positive charge with negative electrons embedded in it, so that the atom is neutral overall.

The nuclear model (one block)
- [ ] N1 The gold-foil experiment (Geiger and Marsden, directed by Rutherford): a beam of positively charged alpha particles was fired at a very thin sheet of gold foil, with a fluorescent screen around it to detect where they went.
- [ ] N2 Observations and conclusions: most particles went straight through, so most of the atom is empty space; some were deflected through large angles, so there is a concentrated region of positive charge that repels them; a very few bounced back, so this positive region (the nucleus) is very small and very dense, containing almost all the mass.
- [ ] N3 Rutherford's nuclear model (1911): a tiny, dense, positively charged nucleus at the centre, with electrons moving around it in the mostly empty space outside.
- [ ] N4 Later work found the proton (Rutherford) and the neutron (Chadwick, 1932) in the nucleus.
- [ ] N5 Diagram: the gold-foil set-up (source, beam, gold foil, detecting screen) and the paths of particles past the nuclei: straight through, deflected, bounced back.

The Bohr model (one block)
- [ ] B1 Bohr (1913): electrons move around the nucleus in fixed circular orbits called energy levels (shells), each with a fixed energy; electrons in a level don't lose energy.
- [ ] B2 An electron can move to a higher energy level by absorbing a definite amount of energy and emits light of a definite energy when it falls back, which explains the lines in emission spectra (see "The orbital model and electronic structure").
- [ ] B3 The energy levels are numbered n = 1, 2, 3…, with n = 1 closest to the nucleus and lowest in energy; they hold up to 2, 8, 18… electrons.

The orbital model (one block)
- [ ] O1 The current model: electrons don't follow fixed paths; they occupy orbitals, regions of space around the nucleus where there is a high probability of finding an electron.
- [ ] O2 Main energy levels are divided into sublevels (s, p, d, f), each made of orbitals that each hold up to two electrons (detail in "The orbital model and electronic structure").
- [ ] O3 It developed from evidence that electrons behave as waves as well as particles and that the exact position and path of an electron can't be known at the same time.

Assumptions and limitations (Higher Level only; one block)
- [ ] H1 [HL] Dalton: assumed atoms are indivisible and all atoms of an element identical; limited because subatomic particles and isotopes exist.
- [ ] H2 [HL] Thomson's plum pudding: assumed positive charge is spread through the atom; limited because it can't explain the large deflections and back-scattering in the gold-foil experiment.
- [ ] H3 [HL] Nuclear model: assumed electrons orbit like planets; limited because it doesn't say how electrons are arranged, and a circling charged electron would be expected to lose energy and spiral into the nucleus; it can't explain line spectra.
- [ ] H4 [HL] Bohr model: assumed electrons move in fixed circular orbits with fixed energies; it explains the spectrum of hydrogen (one electron) but not the spectra of atoms with more than one electron; it doesn't account for sublevels or for the wave nature of electrons, and it treats the electron's exact path as known.
- [ ] H5 [HL] Orbital model: the best fit for current evidence; its limitation is that it is mathematical and abstract and gives probabilities, not exact positions; it is hard to picture.

Evaluating the models (one block)
- [ ] E1 Each model was accepted because it explained the evidence of its time, and replaced or refined when new evidence did not fit.
- [ ] E2 Earlier models are still useful: the Bohr model is enough to explain flame colours, simple line spectra and electron arrangements in shells (2, 8, 8) used to predict bonding; the nuclear model explains why atoms are mostly empty space.

### 3. Traps

- Radioactivity is not on the specification: describe alpha particles only as "small, fast, positively charged particles"; don't explain alpha decay.
- Don't say electrons in the orbital model "orbit"; they occupy orbitals.
- Thomson's model is the plum pudding model, not the nuclear model.
- The energy-level equation Em − En = hf and the hydrogen spectrum belong to "The orbital model and electronic structure".
- Keep Higher Level assumptions and limitations in their own block.

### 4. What exams ask (topics only)

- Describe the gold-foil experiment: observations and conclusions.
- Describe the Bohr model and state a limitation.
- Compare the Bohr model with the orbital model.

### 5. Practice questions to write

2. 9 marks (Higher Level): "State one assumption and one limitation of the Bohr model of the atom, and explain how the orbital model overcomes the limitation." Model answer: H4, O1, O3.
3. 6 marks: "Explain why the Bohr model is still useful even though it has been replaced by the orbital model." Model answer: E2.

## Part: Subatomic particles (formerly `chem2-s2`)

- **loId:** `chem2-s2` · **chapterId:** `chem2` · **syllabusRef:** 1.2 LA
- **Syllabus:** 1.2 learn about: "properties of the proton, neutron and electron (mass, charge,
  location)". No Higher Level only material.
- **Prepared by:** a higher model, 2026-10-30. Include a comparison table in words, actual
  masses, the size of nucleus vs atom, and counting particles in atoms and ions (assumed).

### 1. Must cover

The three particles (one block, one bullet per particle)
- [ ] P1 Proton: relative mass 1; relative charge +1; found in the nucleus.
- [ ] P2 Neutron: relative mass 1; no charge (0); found in the nucleus.
- [ ] P3 Electron: relative mass about 1/1840 (negligible); relative charge −1; found outside the nucleus in energy levels (orbitals).
- [ ] P4 Actual masses: proton 1.673 × 10⁻²⁷ kg; neutron 1.675 × 10⁻²⁷ kg; electron 9.109 × 10⁻³¹ kg. The proton and neutron have almost the same mass.
- [ ] P5 The charge on a proton is equal in size and opposite in sign to the charge on an electron.

Structure and scale (one block)
- [ ] S1 Protons and neutrons (nucleons) make up the nucleus, which holds almost all of the atom's mass.
- [ ] S2 The nucleus is tiny compared with the atom: an atom is roughly 100 000 times wider than its nucleus, so most of the atom is empty space.
- [ ] S3 A neutral atom has equal numbers of protons and electrons.
- [ ] S4 The number of protons identifies the element.

Counting particles in atoms and ions (one block, assumed)
- [ ] C1 (assumed) Number of protons = atomic number; number of neutrons = mass number − atomic number; electrons = protons in a neutral atom.
- [ ] C2 (assumed) A positive ion has lost electrons: electrons = protons − charge, e.g. Na⁺ has 11 protons and 10 electrons.
- [ ] C3 (assumed) A negative ion has gained electrons: electrons = protons + charge, e.g. Cl⁻ (chlorine-35) has 17 protons, 18 neutrons and 18 electrons; O²⁻ has 8 protons and 10 electrons.

Discovery (one block)
- [ ] D1 Electron discovered by J. J. Thomson in 1897; proton identified by Rutherford (about 1917–1919); neutron discovered by James Chadwick in 1932. The neutron was found last because it has no charge and is hard to detect.

### 2. Checked facts and examples

| Item | Fact |
|---|---|
| P3 | Electron mass ≈ 1/1836 of a proton (use "about 1/1840"). |
| P4 | Proton 1.6726 × 10⁻²⁷ kg; neutron 1.6749 × 10⁻²⁷ kg; electron 9.109 × 10⁻³¹ kg. |
| S2 | An atom is about 10⁵ (100 000) times the diameter of its nucleus. |
| D1 | Thomson 1897; Rutherford identified the proton about 1917–1919; Chadwick discovered the neutron 1932. |

### 3. Traps

- Electrons are not "in orbits" in the current model; say "outside the nucleus, in energy levels (orbitals)".
- Don't say electrons have zero mass; their mass is very small.
- Radioactivity is not on the specification; don't explain how Chadwick's experiment worked.

### 4. What exams ask (topics only)

- Give the relative mass, charge and location of the proton, neutron and electron.
- State the number of protons, neutrons and electrons in a given atom or ion.

### 5. Practice questions to write

1. 6 marks: "Copy and complete a table giving the relative mass, relative charge and location of the proton, neutron and electron." Model answer: P1–P3.
2. 6 marks: "State the number of protons, neutrons and electrons in (i) ²⁷₁₃Al, (ii) ²⁷₁₃Al³⁺, (iii) ³²₁₆S²⁻." Model answer: 13, 14, 13; 13, 14, 10; 16, 16, 18.

## Part: Atomic number, mass number, relative atomic mass and isotopes (formerly `chem2-s3`)

- **loId:** `chem2-s3` · **chapterId:** `chem2` · **syllabusRef:** 1.2 LA
- **Syllabus:** 1.2 learn about: "atomic number, mass number, relative atomic mass and isotopes
  (including definition of the term)". No Higher Level only material.
- **Prepared by:** a higher model, 2026-10-30. Definitions, nuclear notation and isotopes. The
  calculation of relative atomic mass from isotopic abundance is taught in "Relative atomic mass
  from isotopic abundance" in the mole chapter: give only the idea and refer there.

### 1. Must cover

Atomic number and mass number (one block)
- [ ] A1 Atomic number (Z): the number of protons in the nucleus of an atom of an element. Each element has its own atomic number, and the periodic table is arranged in order of it.
- [ ] A2 Mass number (A): the total number of protons and neutrons in the nucleus of an atom.
- [ ] A3 Number of neutrons = A − Z.
- [ ] A4 Nuclear notation: mass number written at the top left and atomic number at the bottom left of the symbol, e.g. ²³₁₁Na has 11 protons, 12 neutrons and (as an atom) 11 electrons.

Isotopes (one block)
- [ ] I1 Isotopes: atoms of the same element (same atomic number, same number of protons) with different mass numbers, because they have different numbers of neutrons.
- [ ] I2 Examples: hydrogen-1 (protium, no neutrons), hydrogen-2 (deuterium, one neutron) and hydrogen-3 (tritium, two neutrons); chlorine-35 (18 neutrons) and chlorine-37 (20 neutrons); carbon-12, carbon-13 and carbon-14.
- [ ] I3 Isotopes of an element have the same chemical properties, because they have the same number of electrons and the same electron arrangement.
- [ ] I4 They have slightly different physical properties that depend on mass, such as density and rate of diffusion.
- [ ] I5 Writing isotopes: by name and mass number (chlorine-35) or in nuclear notation (³⁵₁₇Cl).

Relative atomic mass (one block)
- [ ] R1 Relative atomic mass (Ar): the average mass of the atoms of an element, taking account of the abundance of its isotopes, relative to one-twelfth of the mass of an atom of carbon-12.
- [ ] R2 Carbon-12 is the standard: an atom of carbon-12 is defined as having a mass of exactly 12 units.
- [ ] R3 Ar has no units, and is usually not a whole number because it is a weighted average of the isotopes, e.g. chlorine 35.5 (about 75% chlorine-35 and 25% chlorine-37). The calculation is in "The mole and molar volume".
- [ ] R4 The mass number of a single isotope is always a whole number; the relative atomic mass of an element is an average.

### 2. Checked facts and examples

| Item | Fact |
|---|---|
| I2 | ¹H, ²H (deuterium), ³H (tritium); ³⁵Cl (18 n), ³⁷Cl (20 n); ¹²C, ¹³C, ¹⁴C. |
| R3 | Chlorine: about 75.8% ³⁵Cl and 24.2% ³⁷Cl; Ar(Cl) = 35.45 (35.5 in tables used in school). |

### 3. Traps

- Isotopes differ in neutrons, not protons or electrons.
- Don't mention that carbon-14 is radioactive or describe radioactive decay (not on the specification).
- Don't confuse mass number (whole number, one isotope) with relative atomic mass (average for the element).

### 4. What exams ask (topics only)

- Define atomic number, mass number, isotope and relative atomic mass.
- State the number of each particle in a given isotope.

### 5. Practice questions to write

1. 6 marks: "Define (i) mass number, (ii) isotopes. Explain why isotopes of the same element have the same chemical properties." Model answer: A2, I1, I3.
2. 6 marks: "Define relative atomic mass and explain why the relative atomic mass of chlorine is 35.5 rather than a whole number." Model answer: R1, R3.
