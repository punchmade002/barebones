## 1. Must cover

Oxidation and reduction in organic compounds (Higher Level only)
- [ ] O1 [HL] An organic compound is oxidised when it gains oxygen or loses hydrogen, and reduced when it gains hydrogen or loses oxygen; the oxidation number of the carbon involved rises on oxidation and falls on reduction.
- [ ] O2 [HL] The oxidation ladder: primary alcohol → aldehyde → carboxylic acid; secondary alcohol → ketone; each arrow is an oxidation and the reverse is a reduction.
- [ ] O3 [HL] Equations may be written with [O] for oxygen from the oxidising agent: CH₃CH₂OH + [O] → CH₃CHO + H₂O; CH₃CHO + [O] → CH₃COOH.

Acidified potassium permanganate (Higher Level only)
- [ ] P1 [HL] Acidified potassium permanganate (KMnO₄ with dilute sulfuric acid) is a strong oxidising agent; it is reduced from purple MnO₄⁻ to almost colourless Mn²⁺: MnO₄⁻ + 8H⁺ + 5e⁻ → Mn²⁺ + 4H₂O.
- [ ] P2 [HL] It oxidises primary alcohols to aldehydes and then to carboxylic acids: ethanol → ethanal → ethanoic acid; propan-1-ol → propanal → propanoic acid. The purple colour fades.
- [ ] P3 [HL] Balanced example: 5C₂H₅OH + 2MnO₄⁻ + 6H⁺ → 5CH₃CHO + 2Mn²⁺ + 8H₂O (organic half-equation C₂H₅OH → CH₃CHO + 2H⁺ + 2e⁻).
- [ ] P4 [HL] To stop at the aldehyde, the aldehyde is distilled off as it forms (ethanal boils at about 21 °C, well below ethanol), with the oxidising agent not in excess; to get the acid, an excess of oxidising agent is used and the mixture is heated under reflux.
- [ ] P5 [HL] It oxidises secondary alcohols to ketones: propan-2-ol → propanone, CH₃CH(OH)CH₃ + [O] → CH₃COCH₃ + H₂O.
- [ ] P6 [HL] It does not oxidise tertiary alcohols (e.g. 2-methylpropan-2-ol) or ketones: neither has a hydrogen on the carbon bearing the oxygen, and oxidation would need a C–C bond to break. Carboxylic acids are not oxidised further.

Basic potassium permanganate (Higher Level only)
- [ ] B1 [HL] In basic (alkaline) solution, e.g. with sodium carbonate, permanganate is reduced to brown manganese(IV) oxide instead: MnO₄⁻ + 2H₂O + 3e⁻ → MnO₂ + 4OH⁻ (purple → brown precipitate).
- [ ] B2 [HL] It oxidises a primary alcohol to the carboxylic acid, which is present as its salt in the basic solution: phenylmethanol → benzoate ions → benzoic acid on acidifying (see "Investigation: synthesising benzoic acid").

Fehling's solution (Higher Level only)
- [ ] F1 [HL] Fehling's solution is a mild oxidising agent containing Cu²⁺; it oxidises aldehydes to carboxylic acids (as carboxylate salts in the alkaline reagent) and is reduced to brick-red copper(I) oxide, Cu₂O.
- [ ] F2 [HL] It does not oxidise ketones, and (being mild) it does not oxidise alcohols, so it is used to identify aldehydes (see "Tests, reaction schemes and preparing an ester").

Reduction with hydrogen and nickel (Higher Level only)
- [ ] H1 [HL] Hydrogen gas with a nickel catalyst is the reducing agent: it adds hydrogen across a C=O (or C=C) double bond.
- [ ] H2 [HL] Aldehydes are reduced to primary alcohols: CH₃CHO + H₂ → CH₃CH₂OH (ethanal → ethanol).
- [ ] H3 [HL] Ketones are reduced to secondary alcohols: CH₃COCH₃ + H₂ → CH₃CH(OH)CH₃ (propanone → propan-2-ol).
- [ ] H4 [HL] Alkenes are reduced to alkanes, CH₂=CH₂ + H₂ → CH₃CH₃; this is also an addition reaction.
- [ ] H5 [HL] Hydrogenation of vegetable oils: oils contain many C=C bonds (polyunsaturated); adding hydrogen over nickel converts some C=C to C–C, raising the melting point, so the liquid oil becomes a soft solid fat used in margarine; the degree of hydrogenation sets how hard the product is.

Acid–base reactions of carboxylic acids and alcohols (Higher Level only)
- [ ] A1 [HL] Carboxylic acids are weak acids that transfer a proton to bases: CH₃COOH + NaOH → CH₃COONa + H₂O; with carbonates they give CO₂: 2CH₃COOH + Na₂CO₃ → 2CH₃COONa + H₂O + CO₂; with hydrogencarbonates: CH₃COOH + NaHCO₃ → CH₃COONa + H₂O + CO₂.
- [ ] A2 [HL] Alcohols are far weaker acids than carboxylic acids (about as weak as water itself): they do not react with sodium carbonate or usefully with sodium hydroxide; their reaction with sodium metal, and why the two groups differ, are in "Physical properties and acidity".

Base hydrolysis of esters (Higher Level only)
- [ ] E1 [HL] Hydrolysis is the breaking of a compound by reaction with water; esters can be hydrolysed by acid (reversible, the reverse of esterification) or by base.
- [ ] E2 [HL] Base hydrolysis: heating an ester with sodium hydroxide gives the sodium salt of the carboxylic acid and the alcohol: CH₃COOC₂H₅ + NaOH → CH₃COONa + C₂H₅OH (ethyl ethanoate → sodium ethanoate + ethanol).
- [ ] E3 [HL] It is an acid–base reaction because the hydroxide ion acts as a base and the carboxylic acid formed is converted to its carboxylate ion; this is why base hydrolysis goes to completion, while acid hydrolysis reaches an equilibrium.
- [ ] E4 [HL] Acidifying the product mixture (adding H⁺) converts the carboxylate salt back into the free carboxylic acid: CH₃COO⁻ + H⁺ → CH₃COOH.

Fats and oils: tri-esters and saponification (Higher Level only)
- [ ] T1 [HL] Fats and oils are tri-esters (triglycerides) of glycerol, propane-1,2,3-triol (HOCH₂CH(OH)CH₂OH), with three long-chain carboxylic acids called fatty acids.
- [ ] T2 [HL] Fats (mostly from animals, solid at room temperature) contain mainly saturated chains; oils (mostly from plants, liquid) contain more unsaturated chains with C=C bonds.
- [ ] T3 [HL] Base hydrolysis of a fat or oil is called saponification (soap-making): fat + 3NaOH → glycerol + 3 sodium salts of fatty acids (soap).
- [ ] T4 [HL] Example: glyceryl tristearate + sodium hydroxide → sodium stearate + glycerol: (C₁₇H₃₅COO)₃C₃H₅ + 3NaOH → 3C₁₇H₃₅COONa + C₃H₅(OH)₃. Mole ratio fat : NaOH : soap : glycerol = 1 : 3 : 3 : 1.
- [ ] T5 [HL] Glycerol (the co-product) is soluble in water; it is used in cosmetics and medicines.
- [ ] T6 [HL] Diagram: structure of a triglyceride: a three-carbon glycerol backbone, each carbon joined through an ester group (–O–CO–) to a long hydrocarbon chain R; and the products, glycerol with three –OH groups and three RCOO⁻Na⁺.

## 3. Traps

- The specification names only these redox reagents: hydrogen with nickel, Fehling's solution, and potassium permanganate (acidic and basic). Past papers used sodium dichromate(VI) (orange → green); it is not named on the specification, so the notes don't need it.
- Acidified permanganate gives colourless Mn²⁺; basic permanganate gives a brown MnO₂ precipitate. Don't mix them up.
- Fehling's solution oxidises aldehydes but not alcohols or ketones; permanganate oxidises primary and secondary alcohols and aldehydes.
- Hydrogen with nickel reduces aldehydes and ketones; don't say it reduces carboxylic acids.
- Base hydrolysis gives the salt of the acid, not the free acid, until the mixture is acidified.
- Saponification is not reversible, unlike esterification.

## 4. What exams ask (topics only)

- The reagent for oxidising ethanol to ethanal or ethanoic acid; the colour change.
- How to make sure the oxidation stops at the aldehyde, or goes to the acid.
- The reagent and catalyst to reduce ethanal to ethanol, or propanone to propan-2-ol.
- Equation for the reaction of ethanol with sodium; why alcohols are weaker acids than carboxylic acids (taught in "Physical properties and acidity").
- Balance the equation for the reaction of a fat with sodium hydroxide; name the co-product.
