# Package: chem23-s1 — Hydrocarbons: sources and uses

- **Syllabus:** 2.4.1 "outline the main sources of hydrocarbons and their uses in industry and
  society". Learn about: "how organic compounds are divided into many groups, with hydrocarbons

## 1. Must cover

Organic compounds and hydrocarbons (one block)
- [ ] O1 Organic chemistry is the study of the compounds of carbon; carbon monoxide, carbon dioxide, carbonates and hydrogencarbonates are classed as inorganic even though they contain carbon.
- [ ] O2 Carbon forms so many compounds because each carbon atom forms four covalent bonds and carbon atoms bond strongly to each other, giving stable chains (straight or branched) and rings of almost any length.
- [ ] O3 Organic compounds are divided into many groups (families, or homologous series), each with its own characteristic structure and reactions (see "Groups of hydrocarbons" and "Functional groups and nomenclature").
- [ ] O4 A hydrocarbon is a compound containing carbon and hydrogen only; hydrocarbons are the simplest organic compounds.
- [ ] O5 Other organic families (alcohols, carboxylic acids and so on) can be thought of as hydrocarbons with one or more hydrogen atoms replaced by other atoms or groups, so hydrocarbons are the basis for understanding and making other organic compounds.

Sources: fossil fuels (one block)
- [ ] F1 The main sources of hydrocarbons are fossil fuels: crude oil (petroleum), natural gas and coal.
- [ ] F2 Fossil fuels formed over millions of years from the remains of plants and animals (crude oil and natural gas mainly from tiny marine organisms) buried under sediment and changed by heat and pressure in the absence of air.
- [ ] F3 Fossil fuels are finite (non-renewable): they are being used far faster than they form.
- [ ] F4 Natural gas is mainly methane (CH₄), with small amounts of ethane, propane and butane.
- [ ] F5 Crude oil is a mixture of many hydrocarbons, mostly alkanes (chain and ring), with some aromatic hydrocarbons such as benzene.
- [ ] F6 Crude oil is separated by fractional distillation into fractions, each a mixture of hydrocarbons with a similar range of boiling points (and so of similar molecular size): the smallest molecules (lowest boiling points) are collected at the top of the column and the largest at the bottom (see "Separation techniques").

Sources: living matter and synthesis (one block)
- [ ] L1 Living matter: methane is produced when bacteria break down plant and animal material in the absence of air (anaerobic decomposition), e.g. in landfill sites (refuse dumps), slurry pits, marshes and the digestive systems of cattle and sheep.
- [ ] L2 Biogas, made deliberately by anaerobic digestion of farm slurry, food waste or crops, is mainly methane and is a renewable fuel.
- [ ] L3 Plants also make hydrocarbons, e.g. natural rubber (a hydrocarbon polymer from the rubber tree) and limonene (C₁₀H₁₆, in citrus-fruit peel oil).
- [ ] L4 Synthesis: hydrocarbons can be made in industry or the laboratory from other substances, e.g. ethene is made by dehydrating ethanol (see "Investigation: preparing ethene"); ethanol made by fermenting sugar cane gives a plant-based ("bio") ethene.
- [ ] L5 Synthetic fuels can be made from carbon monoxide and hydrogen (the Fischer–Tropsch process), starting from coal, natural gas or biomass.

Uses as fuels (one block)
- [ ] U1 Most hydrocarbons are used as fuels, because they burn in oxygen releasing a lot of energy; complete combustion gives carbon dioxide and water (see "Combustion").
- [ ] U2 Methane (natural gas): heating and cooking in homes, and generating electricity in power stations.
- [ ] U3 Propane and butane: the main components of LPG (liquefied petroleum gas), sold in cylinders for patio heaters, camping stoves, gas barbecues and some vehicles such as forklift trucks; small butane canisters are used in camping stoves and lighters.
- [ ] U4 Natural gas and LPG have no smell of their own, so a small amount of a strong-smelling sulfur compound (a thiol, also called a mercaptan) is added so that leaks can be detected.
- [ ] U5 Liquid fractions of crude oil are fuels for transport: petrol (cars), kerosene (jet aircraft, home heating oil) and diesel (gas oil; lorries, buses, tractors, cars); heavy fuel oil is used in ships and furnaces.
- [ ] U6 Ethyne (acetylene) burned in pure oxygen gives a flame hotter than 3000 °C, used for cutting and welding metals (oxyacetylene torch).

Uses as raw materials (feedstocks) (one block)
- [ ] R1 A small but important share of hydrocarbons is used as raw materials (feedstocks) for the chemical industry rather than burned.
- [ ] R2 Ethene and propene are made into the plastics poly(ethene) and poly(propene) (see "Addition polymers"); ethene is also used to make ethanol and ethane-1,2-diol (antifreeze), and to ripen fruit, as it is a plant hormone.
- [ ] R3 Benzene is a feedstock for making plastics (such as polystyrene), detergents, dyes and pharmaceuticals.
- [ ] R4 Methane is the main source of hydrogen for making ammonia, which is used to make fertilisers.
- [ ] R5 Hexane and cyclohexane are used as non-polar solvents (e.g. hexane to extract vegetable oils from seeds).
- [ ] R6 The largest molecules: lubricating oils and greases for machinery; paraffin wax for candles; bitumen (from the residue) for surfacing roads and waterproofing roofs.
- [ ] R7 Uses depend on structure: small molecules are gases that burn easily (fuels); unsaturated alkenes react readily (feedstocks for plastics); large molecules are viscous or solid (lubricants, waxes, bitumen).

## 3. Traps

- Cracking, reforming, isomerisation, octane numbers and oxygenates are not on the specification: don't teach them, even though past papers ask about them.
- Don't teach the fractionating column in detail (temperature gradient, bubble caps); one item (F6) is enough.
- CO, CO₂ and carbonates contain carbon but are not organic compounds.
- Ethanol is not a hydrocarbon (it contains oxygen); nor are biogas impurities such as CO₂.
- The impacts (climate, air quality, plastic waste, economics) belong to "The impact of hydrocarbons"; mention only that the use is continued and extensive.

## 4. What exams ask (topics only)

- Define a hydrocarbon; name two (or three) main sources of hydrocarbons.
- Identify the main component of natural gas and the two main components of LPG.
- Name a source of methane not linked to the oil and gas industry (slurry pits, landfill, cattle).
- Why a smelly compound is added to natural gas and LPG.
- Give a use of a named hydrocarbon or fraction (ethene, ethyne, methane, bitumen, kerosene).
- On what property crude oil fractions are separated (boiling point, linked to molecular size).

## 5. Practice questions to write

1. 6 marks: "What is a hydrocarbon? Outline the three main sources of hydrocarbons, giving an example of each." Model answer: O4; F1 (fossil fuels); L1 or L3 (living matter: methane from slurry, natural rubber); L4 (synthesis: ethene from ethanol).
2. 9 marks: "Give one use of each of the following and explain why the hydrocarbon suits that use: (i) methane, (ii) a mixture of propane and butane, (iii) ethene." Model answer: U2 (burns cleanly, piped as a gas); U3 (easily liquefied under pressure, so portable in cylinders); R2 (the C=C double bond makes it reactive, so it can be polymerised to poly(ethene)); R7.
3. 6 marks: "Natural gas is odourless. Explain why a sulfur compound is added to it, and name two sources of methane other than natural gas wells." Model answer: U4; L1 (two of landfill, slurry pits, marshes, cattle).
