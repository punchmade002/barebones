## 1. Must cover

Electrochemistry and more sustainable energy (one block)
- [ ] E1 Electrochemical cells convert chemical energy directly into electrical energy, without first burning a fuel to make heat, so less energy is wasted than in an engine or a fossil-fuel power station.
- [ ] E2 Rechargeable (secondary) batteries can be reused hundreds or thousands of times, cutting the waste and raw materials of single-use cells.
- [ ] E3 Rechargeable batteries let electricity from renewable sources (wind, solar) be stored and used later, in devices, in electric vehicles and in large grid batteries; this matters because wind and solar power vary with the weather and time of day.
- [ ] E4 Electrolysis can split water into hydrogen and oxygen (2H₂O → 2H₂ + O₂); if the electricity comes from renewable sources, the hydrogen is made without releasing carbon dioxide ("green hydrogen").
- [ ] E5 Hydrogen stores energy as a fuel: it can be kept, transported and later used in a fuel cell to make electricity again, or used in industry; its only combustion or fuel-cell product is water.
- [ ] E6 Most hydrogen produced today is made from natural gas ("grey hydrogen"), which releases carbon dioxide; "blue hydrogen" is made the same way but with the carbon dioxide captured and stored.

How to carry out the research investigation (one block, one step per bullet)
- [ ] R1 Step 1: Choose one application and write a focused question that can be answered with evidence, e.g. "Are battery electric cars more sustainable than petrol cars in Ireland?"
- [ ] R2 Step 2: Explain the chemistry involved: the redox reactions, the type of cell (galvanic or electrolytic), and the electrode reactions where known.
- [ ] R3 Step 3: Gather evidence from a range of sources: textbooks, peer-reviewed articles, government and public agencies (e.g. the Sustainable Energy Authority of Ireland, the Environmental Protection Agency, the International Energy Agency), manufacturers and news reports.
- [ ] R4 Step 4: Judge each source: who wrote it and their expertise; whether it is peer-reviewed or official; how recent it is (this technology changes quickly); whether the author gains from the conclusion (e.g. a company selling the product); whether it gives data and references rather than opinion; and whether other sources agree.
- [ ] R5 Step 5: Consider the whole life-cycle: raw materials and mining, manufacture, the source of electricity or fuel during use, lifetime, and disposal or recycling.
- [ ] R6 Step 6: Weigh the evidence on each side (environmental, economic, social, safety) and reach a justified conclusion, stating the conditions under which it holds.
- [ ] R7 Step 7: Present the findings with references, and evaluate the investigation: gaps in the evidence, conflicting sources, and what further research would help.

Worked outline 1: rechargeable batteries and electric vehicles (one block)
- [ ] B1 Question: do rechargeable lithium-ion batteries in electric vehicles make transport more sustainable?
- [ ] B2 Chemistry: the lithium-ion cell is a secondary cell, a galvanic cell on discharge and an electrolytic cell on recharging (see "The lithium-ion cell").
- [ ] B3 Evidence for: no exhaust emissions at the point of use (no CO₂, CO, nitrogen oxides or particulates from a tailpipe), improving air quality in towns; electric motors waste less energy than petrol or diesel engines; batteries can be charged from renewable electricity; the same batteries can store surplus renewable electricity.
- [ ] B4 Evidence against: mining lithium, cobalt and nickel damages land and uses large amounts of water; much cobalt is mined in the Democratic Republic of the Congo, where child labour and unsafe working conditions have been reported; making the battery releases a lot of CO₂; the benefit depends on how the electricity is generated; recycling is not yet widespread; damaged cells can catch fire.
- [ ] B5 Justified conclusion (example): electric vehicles reduce emissions over their lifetime where electricity is increasingly renewable, as in Ireland, provided batteries are made responsibly and recycled.

Worked outline 2: green hydrogen from electrolysis (one block)
- [ ] H1 Question: can hydrogen produced by electrolysis using wind power be a sustainable fuel?
- [ ] H2 Chemistry: electrolysis of water; at the cathode 4H⁺ + 4e⁻ → 2H₂, at the anode 2H₂O → O₂ + 4H⁺ + 4e⁻ (see "The electrolytic cell").
- [ ] H3 Evidence for: no CO₂ is released if the electricity is renewable; it can use surplus wind electricity at times when supply is greater than demand, storing it as a fuel; hydrogen can power fuel-cell vehicles and replace fossil fuels in industries that are hard to electrify; the only product when it is used is water.
- [ ] H4 Evidence against: energy is lost at every step, so converting electricity to hydrogen and back to electricity returns well under half of the original energy, much less than a battery; electrolysers need pure water and some need scarce metal catalysts (e.g. platinum and iridium); hydrogen is hard to store (high pressure or very low temperature) and highly flammable; green hydrogen currently costs more than grey hydrogen.
- [ ] H5 Justified conclusion (example): green hydrogen is most useful where batteries are unsuitable (heavy transport, industry, long-term storage), but only if powered by genuinely renewable electricity.

Worked outline 3: fuel cells (one block)
- [ ] F1 Question: should hydrogen fuel cells power public buses?
- [ ] F2 Chemistry: a galvanic cell; anode H₂ → 2H⁺ + 2e⁻; cathode O₂ + 4H⁺ + 4e⁻ → 2H₂O (see "The hydrogen fuel cell").
- [ ] F3 Evidence for: water is the only product at the point of use; quiet; refuelled in minutes; long range suits buses on long, fixed routes that return to one depot for refuelling.
- [ ] F4 Evidence against: few refuelling stations; expensive vehicles and platinum catalyst; high-pressure storage tanks; if the hydrogen is grey, the overall CO₂ saving is small or none.
- [ ] F5 Justified conclusion (example): fuel-cell buses are a good choice only where green hydrogen is available at a competitive price.

Worked outline 4: corrosion protection (one block)
- [ ] C1 Question: how does electrochemistry help steel structures last longer, and why is that sustainable?
- [ ] C2 Chemistry: rusting is the oxidation of iron; a metal higher in the electrochemical series, such as zinc or magnesium, is oxidised in preference to iron. Galvanising (a zinc coating) and sacrificial anodes (blocks of zinc or magnesium attached to ship hulls, pipelines and steel piers) use this (see "Oxidation and reduction" and "The electrochemical series and displacement").
- [ ] C3 Evidence for: steel lasts much longer, so less steel has to be made to replace it, saving iron ore, energy and the CO₂ released in steelmaking; it prevents dangerous failures of bridges, pipelines and ships.
- [ ] C4 Evidence against: sacrificial anodes are used up and must be replaced; zinc released into seawater can harm marine life; galvanised coatings wear away eventually.
- [ ] C5 Justified conclusion (example): corrosion protection is a cheap and effective way of conserving metal resources, though zinc release should be monitored.

## 3. Traps

- An RI answer must contain chemistry (named reactions and cell types), not only opinion.
- "Zero emissions" applies only at the point of use; always consider the source of the electricity or hydrogen and the whole life-cycle.
- Don't repeat the lithium-ion reactions or life-cycle here; refer to "The lithium-ion cell".
- Give both sides before concluding; a conclusion must be justified by the evidence and state its conditions.
- Electroplating, industrial electrolysis (e.g. aluminium extraction) and anodising are not on the specification; don't use them as the research topic.

## 4. What exams ask (topics only)

- How can electrochemistry provide more sustainable uses of energy? Rechargeable batteries; hydrogen by electrolysis using renewable electricity.
- Advantages and disadvantages of electric vehicles or hydrogen fuel cells.
- How sacrificial anodes and galvanising protect steel.
- How to judge the reliability of a source in a research investigation.
