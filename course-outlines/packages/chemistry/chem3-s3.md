# Package: chem3-s3 — Identifying elements

  Learn about: "identifying elements: from flame tests (primary data, limited to salts of: Na,

## 1. Must cover

Why elements give colours (one block)
- [ ] W1 Heating an atom in a flame gives energy to its electrons, which move up to higher energy levels.
- [ ] W2 The electrons fall back to lower levels and give out the energy as light.
- [ ] W3 Each element has its own set of energy levels, so it gives out its own colours of light: a flame colour or line spectrum identifies the element.

Flame test method (one block, investigation)
- [ ] F1 Aim: to identify the metal in a salt by the colour it gives to a flame.
- [ ] F2 Equipment: Bunsen burner, platinum or nichrome wire loop (or wooden splints soaked overnight in deionised water), watch glasses, concentrated hydrochloric acid, samples of the chlorides of the six metals, deionised water.
- [ ] F3 Step 1: Clean the wire by dipping it in concentrated hydrochloric acid and holding it in the hottest part of a non-luminous (blue) Bunsen flame until it gives no colour.
- [ ] F4 Step 2: Dip the wire in the acid again, then into the salt so a little sticks to it (chlorides are used because they are volatile).
- [ ] F5 Step 3: Hold it at the edge of the non-luminous flame and note the colour.
- [ ] F6 Step 4: Clean the wire again before testing the next salt; use a different splint for each salt if splints are used.
- [ ] F7 Viewing potassium through cobalt-blue glass filters out yellow light from any sodium impurity.
- [ ] F8 Safety: wear goggles; concentrated hydrochloric acid is corrosive (gloves, small volume); barium salts are toxic (wash hands; don't ingest); keep the hot wire away from skin.
- [ ] F9 Diagram: a Bunsen burner with a non-luminous flame and a wire loop holding the salt at the edge of the flame.

Flame colours (one block, one bullet per metal)
- [ ] C1 Lithium: crimson red.
- [ ] C2 Sodium: bright yellow-orange (persistent).
- [ ] C3 Potassium: lilac (pale purple).
- [ ] C4 Copper: blue-green.
- [ ] C5 Barium: pale (apple) green.
- [ ] C6 Strontium: red (scarlet).
- [ ] C7 Lithium and strontium are both red and are hard to tell apart by eye; their line spectra distinguish them.

Line emission spectra (one block)
- [ ] L1 A spectroscope splits the light from a flame or discharge into its separate colours.
- [ ] L2 An element gives a line emission spectrum: a pattern of bright lines at specific wavelengths on a dark background, unique to that element like a fingerprint.
- [ ] L3 To identify an element, compare the lines from an unknown (primary data from a spectroscope, or secondary data such as a published spectrum) with reference spectra of known elements; a match of all lines identifies it.
- [ ] L4 Sodium: two very close bright yellow lines at about 589 nm (the "sodium D lines"), which give the yellow flame.
- [ ] L5 Strontium: several lines, strongest in the red; copper: lines mainly in the green, giving the blue-green flame.
- [ ] L6 Line spectra are used to identify elements in samples and in stars; fireworks use these metal salts for their colours (e.g. strontium for red, barium for green, sodium for yellow, copper for blue-green).

## 3. Traps

- Only the six metals named in the specification; don't add calcium or others.
- Use a non-luminous flame (air hole open); a yellow luminous flame hides colours.
- Don't introduce Em − En = hf, ground and excited states (Higher Level, in "Energy levels and the hydrogen emission spectrum").
- Atomic absorption spectroscopy is not on the specification.

## 4. What exams ask (topics only)

- Describe how to carry out a flame test; how to clean the wire.
- State the flame colours of named metals.
- Explain why elements give characteristic colours; why cobalt-blue glass is used.

## 5. Practice questions to write

2. 6 marks: "Explain why different metals give different colours in a flame and how a line emission spectrum can be used to distinguish lithium from strontium." Model answer: W1–W3, L2, L3, C7.
