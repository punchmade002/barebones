## Part: Le Châtelier's principle (formerly `chem18-s4`)

- **loId:** `chem18-s4` · **chapterId:** `chem18` · **syllabusRef:** 3.3.4
- **Syllabus:** 3.3.4 "apply Le Châtelier's principle to a variety of processes to predict
  responses to disturbances to the equilibrium **and to predict conditions for optimising
  yields of product**". Learn about: "changes in temperature, concentration and pressure, but
  not the use of catalyst, which can cause a disturbance to the state of dynamic equilibrium".
  Bold = Higher Level only.
- **Prepared by:** a higher model, 2026-10-30. The principle, one block per disturbance, the
  catalyst note, everyday and environmental applications, then (HL) optimising yield in the
  Haber and Contact processes. The laboratory investigation (iron(III)–thiocyanate, cobalt
  chloride, digital simulations) is in "Investigation: disturbing an equilibrium"; give results
  there, not here. Indicators are in "Titration curves, indicators and vinegar"; mention only as an application.
  Effects on Kc are in "Dynamic equilibrium and Kc"; restate only the one-line rule.

### 1. Must cover

The principle (one block)
- [ ] L1 Le Châtelier's principle: if a system at equilibrium is disturbed (by a change in concentration, pressure or temperature), the system responds so as to oppose the change, and a new equilibrium is set up.
- [ ] L2 "Shifts to the right" means more products form (the forward reaction is favoured until equilibrium is restored); "shifts to the left" means more reactants form.
- [ ] L3 The principle predicts the direction of the shift, not how fast it happens or how big it is; the change is only partly reversed.

Concentration (one block)
- [ ] C1 Adding a reactant (or removing a product) shifts the equilibrium to the right, using up some of the added reactant and forming more product.
- [ ] C2 Adding a product (or removing a reactant) shifts the equilibrium to the left.
- [ ] C3 Kc is unchanged: the concentrations adjust until the ratio in the Kc expression is back at its old value.
- [ ] C4 Removing a product as it forms keeps pulling the equilibrium to the right, e.g. ammonia is liquefied and removed in the Haber process; in esterification, using an excess of ethanol (cheaper reactant) increases the yield of ester.
- [ ] C5 Example: in Fe³⁺(aq) + SCN⁻(aq) ⇌ FeSCN²⁺(aq) (red), adding either reactant deepens the red colour (details in "Investigation: disturbing an equilibrium").

Pressure, for reactions of gases (one block)
- [ ] P1 Increasing the pressure shifts the equilibrium towards the side with fewer moles of gas, since fewer molecules exert a lower pressure; decreasing the pressure shifts it towards the side with more moles of gas.
- [ ] P2 Count only the gas molecules, using the coefficients: N₂(g) + 3H₂(g) ⇌ 2NH₃(g) has 4 mol of gas on the left and 2 on the right, so high pressure shifts it to the right (more ammonia).
- [ ] P3 If both sides have the same number of moles of gas, pressure has no effect on the position of equilibrium, e.g. H₂(g) + I₂(g) ⇌ 2HI(g) (2 mol each side).
- [ ] P4 Compressing H₂ + I₂ ⇌ 2HI makes the purple colour deeper because every concentration (including I₂) rises, but the percentage of each substance is unchanged.
- [ ] P5 2NO₂(g) (brown) ⇌ N₂O₄(g) (colourless): compressing the mixture in a sealed syringe makes it darker at once (higher concentration), then it becomes paler as the equilibrium shifts to the right (2 mol → 1 mol of gas), though not as pale as before.
- [ ] P6 A change of pressure does not change Kc.

Temperature (one block)
- [ ] T1 Raising the temperature shifts the equilibrium in the endothermic direction (which absorbs heat); lowering it shifts the equilibrium in the exothermic direction (which releases heat).
- [ ] T2 If the forward reaction is exothermic (ΔH negative), heating shifts the equilibrium to the left and cooling to the right; the reverse is true if it is endothermic.
- [ ] T3 Example: 2NO₂(g) ⇌ N₂O₄(g), ΔH about −57 kJ mol⁻¹: heating shifts it to the left (darker brown, more NO₂); cooling in ice-water shifts it to the right (paler).
- [ ] T4 Temperature is the only disturbance that changes Kc: for an exothermic forward reaction Kc falls as temperature rises; for an endothermic one it rises.

Catalysts (one block)
- [ ] K1 A catalyst does not disturb an equilibrium or change its position: it speeds up the forward and reverse reactions equally.
- [ ] K2 It lets equilibrium be reached more quickly, so the same yield is obtained in less time, and it does not change Kc.

Applications in everyday life and the environment (one block)
- [ ] E1 Fizzy drinks: CO₂(g) ⇌ CO₂(aq). Opening the bottle lowers the pressure of CO₂ above the drink, so the equilibrium shifts to the left and gas bubbles out; a cold drink holds more dissolved CO₂.
- [ ] E2 Oceans (sustainability): CO₂(g) ⇌ CO₂(aq); CO₂(aq) + H₂O(l) ⇌ H₂CO₃(aq); H₂CO₃(aq) + CO₃²⁻(aq) ⇌ 2HCO₃⁻(aq). More CO₂ in the air increases dissolved CO₂ and carbonic acid; the third equilibrium shifts right, using up carbonate ions, so less carbonate is available to shellfish and corals to build calcium carbonate shells and skeletons.
- [ ] E3 Indicators: the colour of an acid–base indicator changes because adding acid or base shifts HIn ⇌ H⁺ + In⁻ (see "Titration curves, indicators and vinegar").

Optimising yield: the Haber process (Higher Level only; one block)
- [ ] H1 [HL] N₂(g) + 3H₂(g) ⇌ 2NH₃(g), ΔH = −92 kJ mol⁻¹. Ammonia is used mainly to make fertilisers.
- [ ] H2 [HL] Pressure: 4 mol of gas → 2 mol, so high pressure increases the yield (Le Châtelier) and also increases the rate (higher concentration).
- [ ] H3 [HL] Pressure in practice: about 200 atm is used as a compromise; higher pressures would give more ammonia, but plant able to withstand them is very expensive to build and run, and the risk of leaks and explosions increases.
- [ ] H4 [HL] Temperature: the forward reaction is exothermic, so a low temperature gives a higher yield at equilibrium; but at low temperature the reaction is too slow to be economic.
- [ ] H5 [HL] Temperature in practice: a compromise of about 400–500 °C (about 450 °C) gives a reasonable yield at a fast enough rate.
- [ ] H6 [HL] Catalyst: iron, which does not change the yield but lets equilibrium be approached quickly at the compromise temperature.
- [ ] H7 [HL] Concentration: ammonia is removed by cooling and liquefying it, and unreacted nitrogen and hydrogen are recycled through the catalyst, so the overall conversion is high.

Optimising yield: other processes (Higher Level only; one block)
- [ ] O1 [HL] Contact process (sulfuric acid manufacture): 2SO₂(g) + O₂(g) ⇌ 2SO₃(g), ΔH about −197 kJ mol⁻¹. Le Châtelier predicts high pressure (3 mol → 2 mol of gas) and low temperature for the best yield.
- [ ] O2 [HL] In practice about 450 °C (a compromise between yield and rate), a vanadium(V) oxide (V₂O₅) catalyst, and a pressure only slightly above atmospheric (1–2 atm), because the yield is already very high and extra pressure would cost more than it gains.
- [ ] O3 [HL] General method for any given equilibrium: for the best yield choose the temperature that favours the exothermic or endothermic direction as needed, the pressure that favours the side with fewer (or more) gas moles, and an excess of a cheap reactant or removal of product; then weigh this against rate, cost, energy use and safety.
- [ ] O4 [HL] Sustainability link: lower temperatures and pressures save energy and reduce CO₂ emissions; a good catalyst lets a process run at a lower temperature.

### 3. Traps

- The specification does not require the use of a catalyst as a disturbance to equilibrium; a catalyst does not shift an equilibrium at all (K1).
- Pressure only matters when the numbers of moles of gas differ; count gas moles only.
- Only temperature changes Kc.
- Don't say the system "removes the change completely"; it partly opposes it.
- Industrial conditions are compromises; Le Châtelier alone predicts very high pressure and low temperature, which are not used.
- Don't describe the plant or the raw-material production in detail (industrial case studies are off the specification).

### 4. What exams ask (topics only)

- State Le Châtelier's principle.
- Predict and explain the effect of temperature, pressure and concentration changes (and of a catalyst) on a given equilibrium: NO₂/N₂O₄, H₂ + I₂, PCl₅, Haber, SO₂/SO₃, CO + 2H₂ ⇌ CH₃OH, CO + H₂O.
- Colour changes on heating, cooling or compressing a coloured gas equilibrium.
- (HL) Conditions for the best yield of ammonia or sulfur trioxide; why the predicted conditions are not all used in industry.
- Effect of more CO₂ on carbonate in seawater and on shell-making.

## Part: Investigation: disturbing an equilibrium (formerly `chem18-s5`)

- **loId:** `chem18-s5` · **chapterId:** `chem18` · **syllabusRef:** 3.3.5
- **Syllabus:** 3.3.5 "investigate, using primary and/or secondary data, how changes in
  temperature and concentration can affect the state of equilibrium". Learn about: "how data can
  be gathered and analysed, through experimentation including, but not limited to iron(III)
  chloride-potassium thiocyanate and digital simulations, as evidence to show factors affecting
  dynamic equilibrium". Normal type.
- **Prepared by:** a higher model, 2026-10-30. Practical work on coloured equilibria: the
  iron(III)–thiocyanate system for concentration (the named setting), the cobalt(II) chloride
  system for concentration and temperature, and the NO₂/N₂O₄ demonstration for temperature;
  then digital simulations and secondary data. The principle and its reasoning are in
  "Le Châtelier's principle"; state the shift and refer there. Kc effects are in "The
  equilibrium constant".

### 1. Must cover

Aim and approach (one block)
- [ ] A1 Aim: to investigate how changes in concentration and temperature affect the position of an equilibrium, and to test whether the results agree with Le Châtelier's principle.
- [ ] A2 A coloured equilibrium is used, so a shift is seen as a colour change: more of the coloured product (or reactant) means a deeper colour.
- [ ] A3 Set up one equilibrium mixture, divide it into equal portions in identical test tubes, keep one untouched as a control, and change one factor in each of the others.
- [ ] A4 Compare each tube with the control against a white background, and record the colour before and after the change.

Iron(III) chloride and potassium thiocyanate: concentration (one block)
- [ ] F1 Equation: Fe³⁺(aq) + SCN⁻(aq) ⇌ FeSCN²⁺(aq). Fe³⁺ (from iron(III) chloride) is yellow, SCN⁻ (from potassium thiocyanate) is colourless, FeSCN²⁺ is blood-red.
- [ ] F2 Method: add about 1 cm³ of 0.1 M iron(III) chloride and 1 cm³ of 0.1 M potassium thiocyanate to about 100 cm³ of deionised water and stir; a pale orange-red solution forms. (Diluting it makes colour changes easy to see.)
- [ ] F3 Pour equal portions (about 10 cm³) into four test tubes; tube 1 is the control.
- [ ] F4 Tube 2: add a few drops of iron(III) chloride solution: the red deepens. [Fe³⁺] increased, so the equilibrium shifted to the right, forming more FeSCN²⁺.
- [ ] F5 Tube 3: add a few drops of potassium thiocyanate solution: the red deepens. [SCN⁻] increased; the equilibrium shifted to the right.
- [ ] F6 Tube 4: add a few drops of silver nitrate solution: a white precipitate forms and the red colour fades. Ag⁺ removes SCN⁻ as insoluble silver thiocyanate (AgSCN), lowering [SCN⁻], so the equilibrium shifted to the left.
- [ ] F7 Conclusion: adding a reactant shifts the equilibrium to the right; removing a reactant shifts it to the left, as Le Châtelier's principle predicts.
- [ ] F8 Diagram: a row of four test tubes in a rack, labelled control, + FeCl₃, + KSCN, + AgNO₃, with colours pale red, deep red, deep red, pale/colourless with white precipitate.

Cobalt(II) chloride: concentration and temperature (one block)
- [ ] B1 Equation: [Co(H₂O)₆]²⁺(aq) + 4Cl⁻(aq) ⇌ [CoCl₄]²⁻(aq) + 6H₂O(l); [Co(H₂O)₆]²⁺ is pink and [CoCl₄]²⁻ is blue; the forward reaction is endothermic.
- [ ] B2 Dissolve a little cobalt(II) chloride in water (pink); add concentrated hydrochloric acid drop by drop until the solution is violet (a mixture of pink and blue), then divide it into test tubes, keeping one as a control.
- [ ] B3 Concentration: adding more concentrated HCl (more Cl⁻) turns the solution blue: shift to the right. Adding water turns it pink: shift to the left (water is a product; diluting favours the side with more dissolved particles).
- [ ] B4 Temperature: put one tube in a beaker of hot water (about 80 °C): it turns blue, because heating favours the endothermic forward reaction. Put another in ice-water: it turns pink, because cooling favours the exothermic reverse reaction.
- [ ] B5 The changes reverse when the tubes return to room temperature, showing the reaction is reversible and still dynamic.
- [ ] B6 Since heating shifts this equilibrium to the right, Kc for it increases with temperature.
- [ ] B7 Safety: concentrated hydrochloric acid is corrosive and gives off choking fumes (goggles, gloves, fume cupboard); cobalt(II) chloride is toxic, may cause cancer and skin allergy, and is harmful to aquatic life (gloves; dispose of in the residue container, not the sink).
- [ ] B8 Diagram: test tubes of the violet mixture: one in a beaker of hot water (blue), one in a beaker of ice-water (pink), one at room temperature as a control (violet), with a thermometer in each beaker.

Nitrogen dioxide and dinitrogen tetroxide: temperature (teacher demonstration; one block)
- [ ] N1 2NO₂(g) ⇌ N₂O₄(g), forward reaction exothermic; NO₂ is brown, N₂O₄ is colourless.
- [ ] N2 Sealed glass tubes containing the same equilibrium mixture are placed in hot water and in ice-water: the hot tube becomes darker brown (shift to the left, the endothermic direction); the cold tube becomes paler (shift to the right).
- [ ] N3 Safety: NO₂ is very toxic; the tubes are sealed and the demonstration is done by the teacher.

Using digital simulations and secondary data (one block)
- [ ] D1 A digital simulation models the particles of a reversible reaction; the user can add reactant or product, or change the temperature, and watch the numbers of each particle and the forward and reverse rates change until a new equilibrium is set up.
- [ ] D2 Simulations show what can't be seen in the laboratory: both reactions continuing at equal rates at equilibrium (dynamic), and graphs of concentration against time showing the sudden change and the new constant levels.
- [ ] D3 Simulations are safe, fast and repeatable, and avoid toxic chemicals, but they are models: their results depend on the rules programmed into them, so they are checked against experimental data.
- [ ] D4 Secondary data, such as a table or graph of the percentage of ammonia at equilibrium at different temperatures and pressures, can be analysed for trends: the percentage of ammonia rises with pressure and falls with temperature, so the forward reaction reduces the moles of gas and is exothermic.
- [ ] D5 Reading a concentration–time graph: a sudden vertical jump in one line shows a substance being added; the gradual changes that follow show the shift; new horizontal lines show the new equilibrium.

Evaluating the investigation (one block)
- [ ] E1 Judging colour by eye is subjective: compare with the control tube against a white background, use the same volume in identical tubes, and have the same observer.
- [ ] E2 Add the same number of drops to each tube, and allow time for the new equilibrium to set up (the colour to stop changing) before recording.
- [ ] E3 Concentration changes change the position of equilibrium but not Kc; temperature changes both.
- [ ] E4 Adding a solution also dilutes the mixture a little, so add only a few drops (or add the same volume of water to the control).

### 3. Traps

- Don't state whether heating deepens or fades the red of the iron(III)–thiocyanate equilibrium as a fixed fact: its enthalpy change is small and sources differ. If a question tells you the colour change on heating or cooling, deduce the direction of the shift, the sign of ΔH and the change in Kc from it (e.g. "the red fades on cooling" means cooling shifts it to the left, so the forward reaction is endothermic and Kc is smaller at the lower temperature).
- Use temperature-dependent examples (cobalt chloride, NO₂/N₂O₄) to describe a temperature experiment.
- Adding water to the cobalt chloride mixture is a change in concentration, not just "dilution of colour".
- Don't name a particular simulation package; none is checked.
- Catalysts are not part of this investigation.

- Deduce from a given colour change whether the forward reaction is exothermic or endothermic, and how Kc changes.
- Interpret secondary data (percentage yield against temperature and pressure).
