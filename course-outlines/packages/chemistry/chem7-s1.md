## Part: Specific groups (formerly `chem7-s1`)

- **loId:** `chem7-s1` · **chapterId:** `chem7` · **syllabusRef:** 1.3.2
- **Syllabus:** 1.3.2 "identify specific groups of elements and relate the group properties to
  its position in the periodic table". Learn about: "specific groups of elements: Groups 1, 2,
  17 and 18 (state, conductivity and reactivity)". Normal type.
- **Prepared by:** a higher model, 2026-10-30. One block per group: members, state,
  conductivity, reactivity with equations, the trend down the group and its explanation from
  electron arrangement (Ordinary-level wording; ionisation energy is Higher Level in
  "Ionisation energies and atomic radii").

### 1. Must cover

Group 1: the alkali metals (one block)
- [ ] A1 Members: lithium, sodium, potassium, rubidium, caesium (francium); each has one outer electron.
- [ ] A2 State and conductivity: soft, shiny (when freshly cut) solid metals with low densities and low melting points for metals; good conductors of heat and electricity.
- [ ] A3 Reactivity: very reactive; they tarnish quickly in air and are stored under oil.
- [ ] A4 With water they form a hydroxide (an alkaline solution) and hydrogen: 2Na(s) + 2H₂O(l) → 2NaOH(aq) + H₂(g); likewise 2Li + 2H₂O → 2LiOH + H₂ and 2K + 2H₂O → 2KOH + H₂.
- [ ] A5 Observations: lithium floats and fizzes steadily; sodium melts into a silvery ball that darts about on the surface; potassium reacts violently and the hydrogen burns with a lilac flame. The resulting solution turns universal indicator purple (alkaline).
- [ ] A6 Reactivity increases down the group, because the outer electron is further from the nucleus and more shielded, so it is lost more easily.
- [ ] A7 They form 1+ ions (Li⁺, Na⁺, K⁺) and ionic compounds, e.g. sodium chloride.
- [ ] A8 Safety: small pieces only, behind a safety screen, with goggles; never touch with bare hands.

Group 2: the alkaline earth metals (one block)
- [ ] B1 Members: beryllium, magnesium, calcium, strontium, barium (radium); two outer electrons.
- [ ] B2 State and conductivity: solid metals, harder and denser than group 1 metals; good conductors.
- [ ] B3 Reactivity: reactive, but less so than group 1 metals in the same period, because two electrons must be lost.
- [ ] B4 Calcium reacts steadily with cold water: Ca(s) + 2H₂O(l) → Ca(OH)₂(aq) + H₂(g); magnesium reacts only very slowly with cold water but burns with a bright white flame in air: 2Mg + O₂ → 2MgO.
- [ ] B5 Reactivity increases down the group, for the same reason as group 1.
- [ ] B6 They form 2+ ions (Mg²⁺, Ca²⁺).

Group 17: the halogens (one block)
- [ ] H1 Members: fluorine, chlorine, bromine, iodine (astatine); seven outer electrons; they exist as diatomic molecules (F₂, Cl₂, Br₂, I₂).
- [ ] H2 State and appearance at room temperature: fluorine pale yellow gas; chlorine yellow-green gas; bromine red-brown liquid; iodine grey-black solid that forms a purple vapour when heated.
- [ ] H3 Conductivity: non-metals; they don't conduct electricity.
- [ ] H4 Reactivity: very reactive non-metals that gain one electron to form 1− ions (halide ions: F⁻, Cl⁻, Br⁻, I⁻); they react with metals to form salts, e.g. 2Na + Cl₂ → 2NaCl.
- [ ] H5 Reactivity decreases down the group, because the larger atoms attract an extra electron less strongly (it is further from the nucleus and more shielded).
- [ ] H6 Displacement: a more reactive halogen displaces a less reactive one from a solution of its salt, e.g. Cl₂ + 2KBr → 2KCl + Br₂ (colourless solution turns orange-brown); Cl₂ + 2KI → 2KCl + I₂ (turns brown); bromine displaces iodine but not chlorine.
- [ ] H7 Safety: halogens are toxic and corrosive; use a fume cupboard.

Group 18: the noble gases (one block)
- [ ] N1 Members: helium, neon, argon, krypton, xenon, radon.
- [ ] N2 State: colourless, odourless gases made of single atoms (monatomic).
- [ ] N3 Conductivity: non-conductors.
- [ ] N4 Reactivity: very unreactive (inert), because they have full outer energy levels (helium 2, the others 8), a stable arrangement.
- [ ] N5 Uses that depend on their unreactivity or low density: helium in balloons and airships; argon in filament light bulbs and as a shield gas in welding; neon in advertising signs (glows red in an electric discharge).

Position and properties (one block)
- [ ] P1 Same group = same number of outer electrons = similar reactions; going down a group, atoms have more energy levels, which explains the trends in reactivity.
- [ ] P2 Groups 1 and 2 are on the left (metals that lose electrons); 17 and 18 are on the right (non-metals: halogens gain electrons, noble gases neither gain nor lose).

### 2. Checked facts and examples

All observations, colours, equations and uses above are standard.

### 3. Traps

- Hydrogen is in group 1 in some tables but is not an alkali metal.
- Halogen reactivity decreases down the group; metal reactivity increases.
- Don't use ionisation energy in explanations (Higher Level, in "Ionisation energies and atomic radii").

### 4. What exams ask (topics only)

- Describe the reaction of sodium (or potassium) with water, with observations and equation.
- Explain the trend in reactivity of group 1 or group 17.
- Describe a halogen displacement reaction.
- Explain why the noble gases are unreactive; give a use.

### 5. Practice questions to write

1. 12 marks: "Describe what you would observe when a small piece of sodium is added to water containing universal indicator, write a balanced equation, and explain why potassium reacts more vigorously." Model answer: A4, A5, A6.
2. 9 marks: "Chlorine gas is bubbled into a colourless solution of potassium bromide. State what you would observe, write a balanced equation and explain why the reaction occurs." Model answer: H6, H5.
3. 6 marks: "Explain why the noble gases are very unreactive and give one use of argon." Model answer: N4, N5.

## Part: Trends and relationships (formerly `chem7-s2`)

- **loId:** `chem7-s2` · **chapterId:** `chem7` · **syllabusRef:** 1.3.4
- **Syllabus:** 1.3.4 "examine trends and relationships in the periodic table". Learn about:
  trends related to "atomic number, relative atomic mass **and atomic (covalent) radius**;
  electronic structure; electronegativity (using Pauling scale); chemical reactivity
  (including the octet rule); physical properties; **first ionisation energy and successive
  ionisation energies** (limited to the first 20 **(36)** elements)". Bold = Higher Level only.
- **Prepared by:** a higher model, 2026-10-30. A survey of the trends at Ordinary level, each in
  one block. Atomic radius and ionisation energies (Higher Level) are taught in full in
  "Ionisation energies and atomic radii"; here give one Higher Level block that names the two
  trends and points there.

### 1. Must cover

Atomic number and relative atomic mass (one block)
- [ ] Z1 Atomic number increases by one from each element to the next.
- [ ] Z2 Relative atomic mass generally increases with atomic number, but there are a few exceptions where it falls (e.g. argon 39.9 before potassium 39.1; tellurium 127.6 before iodine 126.9), because of the mix of isotopes.

Electronic structure (one block)
- [ ] E1 Across a period, the number of outer electrons increases by one at each step, in the same main energy level.
- [ ] E2 Down a group, the number of outer electrons stays the same but a new main energy level is added each time.
- [ ] E3 The repeating pattern of outer-electron arrangements is the cause of periodicity: elements with the same outer arrangement fall in the same group.

Electronegativity (one block)
- [ ] N1 Electronegativity increases across a period and decreases down a group (Pauling scale; e.g. Na 0.93 → Cl 3.16; F 3.98 → I 2.66). The explanation is in "Bond type and intermolecular forces".

Chemical reactivity and the octet rule (one block)
- [ ] R1 Atoms react to reach a full outer level of eight electrons (the octet rule), by losing, gaining or sharing electrons.
- [ ] R2 Metals react by losing electrons: reactivity increases down a group (group 1 and group 2) and decreases across a period from group 1 to group 2 to group 13.
- [ ] R3 Non-metals react by gaining electrons: reactivity decreases down group 17 and generally increases across a period towards group 17.
- [ ] R4 Noble gases already have a full outer level and are unreactive.

Physical properties (one block)
- [ ] P1 Across period 3, the elements change from metals (sodium, magnesium, aluminium: shiny conductors) through a metalloid (silicon: semiconductor) to non-metals (phosphorus, sulfur, chlorine, argon: non-conductors).
- [ ] P2 Melting points across period 3 rise to a maximum at silicon (giant covalent structure) and then fall sharply for the molecular non-metals and argon.
- [ ] P3 Down group 1, melting points decrease (lithium 181 °C, sodium 98 °C, potassium 63 °C); down group 17, melting and boiling points increase (gases to liquid to solid).

Higher Level trends (Higher Level only; one block)
- [ ] H1 [HL] Atomic (covalent) radius decreases across a period and increases down a group.
- [ ] H2 [HL] First ionisation energy generally increases across a period and decreases down a group; successive ionisation energies of an element always increase. Both are explained, with their exceptions, in "Ionisation energies and atomic radii".
- [ ] H3 [HL] Higher Level trends extend to the first 36 elements (to krypton); Ordinary level uses the first 20 (to calcium).

|---|---|

| P2 | Period 3 melting points peak at silicon (1414 °C). |

### 3. Traps

- Don't explain ionisation energy or radius trends in detail here; that is "Ionisation energies and atomic radii".
- Relative atomic mass anomalies are not explained by "mistakes"; they come from isotopic composition.

### 4. What exams ask (topics only)

- Describe and explain trends in reactivity down group 1 and group 17.
- State the trend in electronegativity across a period.

### 5. Practice questions to write

1. 9 marks: "Describe how electronic structure changes (i) across a period, (ii) down a group, and use this to explain the trend in reactivity down group 1." Model answer: E1, E2, R2.
2. 6 marks: "Describe the change in the physical properties of the elements across period 3 from sodium to argon." Model answer: P1, P2.

## Part: Ionisation energies and atomic radii (formerly `chem7-s3`)

- **loId:** `chem7-s3` · **chapterId:** `chem7` · **syllabusRef:** 1.3.5
- **Syllabus:** 1.3.5 "**explain trends in first ionisation energies, including exceptions, and
  in successive ionisation energies and atomic radii**". All bold: the whole subsection is
  Higher Level only. Mark every block heading " (Higher Level only)". Limited to the first 36
  elements.
- **Prepared by:** a higher model, 2026-10-30. The three factors (nuclear charge, distance,
  screening) first; then radius; then first ionisation energy with exceptions; then successive
  ionisation energies.

### 1. Must cover

The three factors (one block)
- [ ] F1 [HL] Effective nuclear charge: the more protons, the stronger the pull on the outer electrons.
- [ ] F2 [HL] Distance: the further the outer electrons are from the nucleus (more energy levels), the weaker the pull.
- [ ] F3 [HL] Screening (shielding): inner electrons shield the outer electrons from the full nuclear charge; electrons in the same level shield each other very little.

Atomic radius (one block)
- [ ] R1 [HL] Atomic (covalent) radius: half the distance between the nuclei of two atoms of the same element joined by a single covalent bond.
- [ ] R2 [HL] Across a period it decreases: nuclear charge increases while electrons enter the same main energy level, with little extra screening, so the outer electrons are pulled closer.
- [ ] R3 [HL] Down a group it increases: each element has an extra energy level, and the extra inner electrons screen the outer electrons, outweighing the increase in nuclear charge.
- [ ] R4 [HL] Noble gases are not given a covalent radius because they don't form bonds.

First ionisation energy (one block)
- [ ] I1 [HL] First ionisation energy: the minimum energy needed to completely remove the most loosely bound electron from a neutral gaseous atom in its ground state: X(g) → X⁺(g) + e⁻. Unit: kJ mol⁻¹.
- [ ] I2 [HL] Values (kJ mol⁻¹), period 2: Li 520, Be 899, B 801, C 1086, N 1402, O 1314, F 1681, Ne 2081; period 3: Na 496, Mg 738, Al 578, Si 787, P 1012, S 1000, Cl 1251, Ar 1521; group 1: Li 520, Na 496, K 419.
- [ ] I3 [HL] General trend across a period: increases, because nuclear charge increases and atomic radius decreases, so the outer electron is held more tightly. Noble gases have the highest values in each period.
- [ ] I4 [HL] Down a group: decreases, because the outer electron is further from the nucleus and more screened, despite the larger nuclear charge.
- [ ] I5 [HL] Group 1 metals have the lowest values in each period, which is why they lose electrons easily and are so reactive.

Exceptions to the trend across a period (one block)
- [ ] X1 [HL] Beryllium is higher than boron (899 vs 801): beryllium's 2s sublevel is full (1s² 2s²), a stable arrangement; boron's outer electron is alone in a 2p orbital (1s² 2s² 2p¹), slightly higher in energy and more screened, so it is removed more easily.
- [ ] X2 [HL] Nitrogen is higher than oxygen (1402 vs 1314): nitrogen's 2p sublevel is half full with one electron in each orbital (2p³), a stable arrangement; in oxygen (2p⁴) one 2p orbital holds a pair, and repulsion between the paired electrons makes one easier to remove.
- [ ] X3 [HL] The same pattern occurs in period 3: magnesium higher than aluminium; phosphorus higher than sulfur.
- [ ] X4 [HL] Diagram: a graph of first ionisation energy against atomic number for elements 1–20, showing the peaks at the noble gases, the troughs at the alkali metals and the small dips after Be, N, Mg and P.

Successive ionisation energies (one block)
- [ ] S1 [HL] Second ionisation energy: the energy to remove an electron from a 1+ ion: X⁺(g) → X²⁺(g) + e⁻; and so on for the third and later.
- [ ] S2 [HL] Successive ionisation energies always increase, because each electron is removed from an increasingly positive ion, with the same nuclear charge holding fewer electrons more tightly.
- [ ] S3 [HL] A very large jump occurs when an electron is removed from a new, inner energy level, closer to the nucleus and less screened.
- [ ] S4 [HL] Example: magnesium 738, 1451, 7733 kJ mol⁻¹: the big jump between the second and third shows two outer electrons, so magnesium is in group 2; sodium 496 then 4562: one outer electron, group 1.
- [ ] S5 [HL] The pattern of jumps is evidence for main energy levels: plotting the log of successive ionisation energies against electron number for an element such as potassium shows groups of 2, 8, 8, 1 electrons.

| Item | Fact |
|---|---|
| I2 | First ionisation energies (kJ mol⁻¹) as listed (standard data book values, rounded). |
| S4 | Mg: 738, 1451, 7733; Na: 496, 4562 kJ mol⁻¹. |

No atomic radius values.

### 3. Traps

- Ionisation energy refers to gaseous atoms; always include (g).
- The second ionisation energy is from the 1+ ion, not the second electron of the neutral atom.
- The exceptions are about sublevel stability and electron pairing; don't explain them with "full outer shell".

### 4. What exams ask (topics only)

- Define first ionisation energy; explain the trend across a period and down a group.
- Explain why the first ionisation energy of nitrogen is greater than that of oxygen (or Be > B, Mg > Al).
- Explain the trend in atomic radius.
- Deduce the group of an element from its successive ionisation energies.

### 5. Practice questions to write

1. 12 marks: "Define first ionisation energy. Explain why first ionisation energy generally increases across period 2, and why the value for oxygen is lower than for nitrogen." Model answer: I1, I3, X2.
2. 9 marks: "The first three ionisation energies of an element are 738, 1451 and 7733 kJ mol⁻¹. Explain why successive ionisation energies increase, and identify the group of the element, giving your reasoning." Model answer: S2–S4, group 2.
3. 6 marks: "Explain why atomic radius decreases across a period but increases down a group." Model answer: R2, R3.
