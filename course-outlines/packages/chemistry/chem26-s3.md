## 1. Must cover

Definitions (Higher Level only; one block)
- [ ] D1 [HL] Limiting reagent (limiting reactant): the reactant that is completely used up first in a reaction; it decides the maximum amount of product that can form, and the reaction stops when it runs out.
- [ ] D2 [HL] Reagent in excess: a reactant present in more than the amount needed to react with all of the limiting reagent; some of it is left unreacted at the end.
- [ ] D3 [HL] The amount of product depends only on the amount of the limiting reagent, not on the amount of the reagent in excess.
- [ ] D4 [HL] Analogy: a sandwich needs 2 slices of bread and 1 slice of cheese; with 10 slices of bread and 50 slices of cheese, only 5 sandwiches can be made: the bread is limiting and the cheese is in excess.
- [ ] D5 [HL] The limiting reagent is not necessarily the one with the smaller mass, or even the smaller number of moles: the mole ratio in the equation must be taken into account.

Finding the limiting reagent (Higher Level only; one block, skills; one step per bullet)
- [ ] F1 [HL] Step 1: Write the balanced equation.
- [ ] F2 [HL] Step 2: Convert the amount of each reactant to moles.
- [ ] F3 [HL] Step 3 (method A): Use the mole ratio to work out how many moles of the second reactant are needed to react with all of the first; if more than that is present, the second is in excess and the first is limiting; if less is present, the second is limiting.
- [ ] F4 [HL] Step 3 (method B): Divide the moles of each reactant by its coefficient in the equation; the reactant with the smallest result is the limiting reagent.
- [ ] F5 [HL] Step 4: Calculate the amount of product from the moles of the limiting reagent only.
- [ ] F6 [HL] Step 5 (if asked): Moles of excess reagent left = moles present − moles that reacted with the limiting reagent; convert to a mass if needed.

Example 1: iron and sulfur (Higher Level only; one block, one step per bullet; sample data)
- [ ] A1 [HL] 10.0 g of iron is heated with 10.0 g of sulfur: Fe(s) + S(s) → FeS(s). Find the limiting reagent, the mass of iron(II) sulfide formed and the mass of the excess reagent left.
- [ ] A2 [HL] Moles of Fe = 10.0 ÷ 56 = 0.1786 mol; moles of S = 10.0 ÷ 32 = 0.3125 mol.
- [ ] A3 [HL] The ratio is 1 : 1, so 0.1786 mol of Fe needs only 0.1786 mol of S; 0.3125 mol is present, so sulfur is in excess and iron is the limiting reagent.
- [ ] A4 [HL] Moles of FeS = 0.1786 mol; mass = 0.1786 × 88 = 15.7 g.
- [ ] A5 [HL] Sulfur left = 0.3125 − 0.1786 = 0.1339 mol = 0.1339 × 32 = 4.29 g.
- [ ] A6 [HL] Check by conservation of mass: 15.71 g + 4.29 g = 20.0 g, the total mass of reactants.

Example 2: magnesium and hydrochloric acid (Higher Level only; one block, one step per bullet; sample data)
- [ ] B1 [HL] 0.480 g of magnesium is added to 50.0 cm³ of 0.500 mol L⁻¹ hydrochloric acid: Mg(s) + 2HCl(aq) → MgCl₂(aq) + H₂(g).
- [ ] B2 [HL] Moles of Mg = 0.480 ÷ 24 = 0.0200 mol; moles of HCl = 0.500 × 0.0500 = 0.0250 mol.
- [ ] B3 [HL] 0.0200 mol of Mg needs 2 × 0.0200 = 0.0400 mol of HCl, but only 0.0250 mol is present, so hydrochloric acid is the limiting reagent and magnesium is in excess (though there are more moles of HCl than of Mg).
- [ ] B4 [HL] Moles of H₂ = 0.0250 ÷ 2 = 0.0125 mol; volume = 0.0125 × 22.4 = 0.280 L (280 cm³) at STP.
- [ ] B5 [HL] Magnesium that reacted = 0.0125 mol; magnesium left = 0.0200 − 0.0125 = 0.00750 mol = 0.180 g, seen as unreacted ribbon when the fizzing stops.

Example 3: reacting gas volumes (Higher Level only; one block, one step per bullet; sample data)
- [ ] C1 [HL] 50 cm³ of hydrogen is exploded with 40 cm³ of oxygen and cooled to room temperature: 2H₂(g) + O₂(g) → 2H₂O(l). All volumes are at the same temperature and pressure.
- [ ] C2 [HL] By Avogadro's law the volume ratio equals the mole ratio: 50 cm³ of H₂ needs 25 cm³ of O₂.
- [ ] C3 [HL] 40 cm³ of O₂ is present, so hydrogen is limiting and 40 − 25 = 15 cm³ of oxygen is left over.
- [ ] C4 [HL] The water is a liquid at room temperature, so the final gas volume is 15 cm³ (all oxygen).

Example 4: the "divide by the coefficient" method (Higher Level only; one block, one step per bullet; sample data)
- [ ] G1 [HL] 2.70 g of aluminium is heated in 7.10 g of chlorine: 2Al(s) + 3Cl₂(g) → 2AlCl₃(s).
- [ ] G2 [HL] Moles: Al = 2.70 ÷ 27 = 0.100 mol; Cl₂ = 7.10 ÷ 71 = 0.100 mol.
- [ ] G3 [HL] Divide by coefficients: Al 0.100 ÷ 2 = 0.0500; Cl₂ 0.100 ÷ 3 = 0.0333; chlorine has the smaller value, so chlorine is limiting, even though the moles are equal.
- [ ] G4 [HL] Moles of AlCl₃ = 0.100 × (2 ÷ 3) = 0.0667 mol; mass = 0.06667 × 133.5 = 8.90 g.
- [ ] G5 [HL] Aluminium left = 0.100 − 0.0667 = 0.0333 mol = 0.900 g; check: 8.90 + 0.90 = 9.80 g = 2.70 + 7.10 g.

Example 5: limiting reagent and percentage yield (Higher Level only; one block, one step per bullet; sample data)
- [ ] H1 [HL] 2.00 cm³ of phenylmethanol (density 1.04 g cm⁻³) was oxidised with 6.32 g of potassium manganate(VII) under basic conditions; 1.05 g of pure benzoic acid was obtained after acidifying and recrystallising: 3C₆H₅CH₂OH + 4KMnO₄ → 3C₆H₅COOH + 4MnO₂ + H₂O + 4KOH.
- [ ] H2 [HL] Mass of phenylmethanol = 2.00 × 1.04 = 2.08 g; moles = 2.08 ÷ 108 = 0.01926 mol.
- [ ] H3 [HL] Moles of KMnO₄ = 6.32 ÷ 158 = 0.0400 mol.
- [ ] H4 [HL] 0.01926 mol of phenylmethanol needs 0.01926 × (4 ÷ 3) = 0.0257 mol of KMnO₄; 0.0400 mol is present, so KMnO₄ is in excess and phenylmethanol is the limiting reagent.
- [ ] H5 [HL] Theoretical yield of benzoic acid (ratio 3 : 3 = 1 : 1) = 0.01926 mol × 122 = 2.35 g.
- [ ] H6 [HL] Percentage yield = (1.05 ÷ 2.35) × 100 = 44.7% (the practical itself is in "Investigation: synthesising benzoic acid").

Why a reagent is used in excess (Higher Level only; one block)
- [ ] X1 [HL] Using one reagent in excess makes sure that all of the other, usually more expensive or more important, reagent reacts, so none of it is wasted and the yield based on it is as high as possible.
- [ ] X2 [HL] The cheaper or more easily removed reagent is chosen to be in excess, e.g. excess air (oxygen) in combustion so that the fuel burns completely to carbon dioxide rather than toxic carbon monoxide.
- [ ] X3 [HL] For a reversible reaction, an excess of one reactant shifts the equilibrium to the right (Le Châtelier's principle) and increases the yield, e.g. excess ethanol (or ethanoic acid) in making ethyl ethanoate.
- [ ] X4 [HL] In analysis, the reagent is added in excess so the substance being measured reacts completely, e.g. excess silver nitrate in gravimetric analysis, or the excess acid in a back titration (see "Worked acid–base volumetric problems").
- [ ] X5 [HL] Drawback: the excess reagent is left in the product mixture and must be removed (e.g. excess KMnO₄ removed in the benzoic acid preparation; excess NaOH would leave a soap caustic, see "Investigation: making soap"), and it is wasted unless recycled.

## 3. Traps

- The limiting reagent is not simply the one with fewer grams or fewer moles: compare against the mole ratio (Examples 2 and 4).
- Calculate the product from the limiting reagent, never from the reagent in excess.
- When asked for the excess left over, subtract the amount that reacted from the amount present.
- In gas-volume problems, check whether a product is a liquid (water at room temperature) before adding volumes.
- Questions sometimes say "in excess" outright; then the other reactant is limiting and no comparison is needed.

## 4. What exams ask (topics only)

- Define limiting reagent and reagent in excess.
- Identify the limiting reagent from given masses, volumes or concentrations, showing working.
- Calculate the mass or volume of product and the amount of excess reagent remaining.
- Combined problems: limiting reagent, then theoretical and percentage yield of an organic preparation.
- Explain why one reagent is used in excess.
