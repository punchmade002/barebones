## Part: Gas relationships and the ideal gas (formerly `chem9-s5`)

- **loId:** `chem9-s5` · **chapterId:** `chem9` · **syllabusRef:** 2.3.1
- **Syllabus:** 2.3.1 "explain what is meant by the ideal gas, **accounting for deviations of
  real gases from ideal gas behaviour**". Learn about: "relationships between pressure, volume,
  temperature of gases; the model of an ideal gas which was developed to enable analysis and
  predictions of how gases behave". Bold = Higher Level only.
- **Prepared by:** a higher model, 2026-10-30. The three gas laws (qualitative relationship,
  statement, particle explanation, one calculation each), the ideal gas, then [HL] deviations.
  PV = nRT is in "The ideal gas equation".

### 1. Must cover

Pressure, volume and temperature (one block)
- [ ] B1 Boyle's law: at constant temperature, the volume of a fixed mass of gas is inversely proportional to its pressure: P₁V₁ = P₂V₂. Particle explanation: in a smaller volume the particles hit the walls more often, so the pressure rises.
- [ ] B2 Boyle's law example: 200 cm³ of gas at 100 kPa is compressed to 50 cm³ at the same temperature: P₂ = 100 × 200 ÷ 50 = 400 kPa.
- [ ] C1 Charles's law: at constant pressure, the volume of a fixed mass of gas is directly proportional to its temperature in kelvin: V₁/T₁ = V₂/T₂. Particle explanation: hotter particles move faster and hit the walls harder, so the gas expands to keep the pressure the same.
- [ ] C2 Charles's law example: 300 cm³ of gas at 27 °C (300 K) is heated to 127 °C (400 K) at constant pressure: V₂ = 300 × 400 ÷ 300 = 400 cm³.
- [ ] G1 Pressure law (Gay-Lussac's law): at constant volume, the pressure of a fixed mass of gas is directly proportional to its kelvin temperature: P₁/T₁ = P₂/T₂. Particle explanation: faster particles hit the walls more often and harder.
- [ ] G2 Combined gas law: P₁V₁/T₁ = P₂V₂/T₂, for a fixed mass of gas.
- [ ] G3 Temperatures must be in kelvin; absolute zero (0 K = −273 °C) is the temperature at which, in theory, an ideal gas would have zero volume and the particles would stop moving.
- [ ] G4 Diagram: graphs of V against 1/P (straight line, Boyle) and V against T in K (straight line through the origin, Charles).

The ideal gas (one block)
- [ ] I1 An ideal gas is a gas that obeys the gas laws (and PV = nRT) exactly under all conditions.
- [ ] I2 The ideal gas model assumes: the gas particles are in constant random motion; the volume of the particles themselves is negligible compared with the volume of the container; there are no attractive or repulsive forces between the particles; collisions are perfectly elastic; the average kinetic energy is proportional to the kelvin temperature.
- [ ] I3 No real gas is ideal, but most gases behave nearly ideally at room temperature and pressure, so the model allows useful predictions.

Deviations of real gases (Higher Level only; one block)
- [ ] D1 [HL] Real gases deviate from ideal behaviour most at high pressure and low temperature.
- [ ] D2 [HL] At high pressure the particles are squeezed close together, so their own volume is no longer negligible compared with the volume of the gas.
- [ ] D3 [HL] At low temperature the particles move slowly, so the attractive forces between them become significant; this is close to the point where the gas condenses.
- [ ] D4 [HL] Gases behave most ideally at low pressure and high temperature.
- [ ] D5 [HL] Small, non-polar particles with weak intermolecular forces (helium, hydrogen) behave most ideally; polar molecules with strong attractions, especially those that hydrogen bond (ammonia, water vapour), deviate most.

### 3. Traps

- Charles's and the pressure law need kelvin; using °C gives wrong answers.
- "Fixed mass" is part of each law.
- van der Waals' equation is not required.
- Keep deviations in the Higher Level block.

- State the assumptions of the ideal gas model; explain why real gases deviate (Higher Level).

## Part: The ideal gas equation (formerly `chem9-s6`)

- **loId:** `chem9-s6` · **chapterId:** `chem9` · **syllabusRef:** 2.3.2
- **Syllabus:** 2.3.2 "solve and interpret quantitative problems using the ideal gas equation".
  Learn about: "the ideal gas equation PV = nRT (van der Waal's equation is not required)";
  "how to verify and use the ideal gas equation". Normal type.
- **Prepared by:** a higher model, 2026-10-30. Equation, symbols and units, unit conversions,
  worked problems (finding V, n, P, T, Mr), and a verification experiment. The Mr of a volatile
  liquid investigation is "The mole and molar volume"; refer to it.

### 1. Must cover

The equation (one block)
- [ ] E1 PV = nRT, where P is pressure in pascals (Pa), V is volume in cubic metres (m³), n is the number of moles, R is the gas constant 8.314 J K⁻¹ mol⁻¹, and T is temperature in kelvin (K).
- [ ] E2 It combines Boyle's law, Charles's law, the pressure law and Avogadro's law into one equation.
- [ ] E3 It applies exactly only to an ideal gas but gives good results for real gases at ordinary conditions.

Converting units first (one block, skills)
- [ ] U1 Pressure: kPa × 1000 = Pa (101 kPa = 101 000 Pa).
- [ ] U2 Volume: cm³ × 10⁻⁶ = m³ (250 cm³ = 2.50 × 10⁻⁴ m³); L × 10⁻³ = m³.
- [ ] U3 Temperature: °C + 273 = K.

Worked problems (one block, one step per bullet)
- [ ] W1 Volume of a gas: 0.50 mol of nitrogen at 27 °C and 100 kPa: V = nRT ÷ P = 0.50 × 8.314 × 300 ÷ 100 000 = 0.0125 m³ = 12.5 L.
- [ ] W2 Moles of a gas: 600 cm³ of oxygen at 20 °C and 101 kPa: n = PV ÷ RT = (101 000 × 6.00 × 10⁻⁴) ÷ (8.314 × 293) = 60.6 ÷ 2436 = 0.0249 mol.
- [ ] W3 Relative molecular mass from mass: since n = m ÷ M, M = mRT ÷ PV; e.g. 1.60 g of a gas occupies 1.20 L at 27 °C and 101 kPa: n = (101 000 × 1.20 × 10⁻³) ÷ (8.314 × 300) = 121.2 ÷ 2494 = 0.0486 mol; M = 1.60 ÷ 0.0486 = 32.9 g mol⁻¹ (sample data; close to oxygen, 32).
- [ ] W4 Pressure in a sealed container: 0.10 mol of gas in a 2.0 L container at 25 °C: P = nRT ÷ V = 0.10 × 8.314 × 298 ÷ 2.0 × 10⁻³ = 1.24 × 10⁵ Pa = 124 kPa.
- [ ] W5 Interpreting answers: check the units of the answer and whether it is sensible (e.g. a mole of gas at room conditions is about 24 L).

Verifying the equation (one block, investigation)
- [ ] V1 Aim: to verify the ideal gas equation by comparing a measured gas volume with the volume it predicts.
- [ ] V2 Step 1: React a weighed piece of magnesium ribbon (e.g. 0.048 g) with excess dilute hydrochloric acid in a flask connected to a gas syringe: Mg(s) + 2HCl(aq) → MgCl₂(aq) + H₂(g).
- [ ] V3 Step 2: When the reaction stops and the apparatus has cooled to room temperature, record the volume of hydrogen, the room temperature and the atmospheric pressure.
- [ ] V4 Step 3: Moles of hydrogen = moles of magnesium = 0.048 ÷ 24 = 0.0020 mol; predicted V = nRT ÷ P = 0.0020 × 8.314 × 293 ÷ 101 000 = 4.82 × 10⁻⁵ m³ = 48.2 cm³ (sample data).
- [ ] V5 Step 4: Compare the measured volume with the prediction; close agreement verifies the equation (for a gas behaving nearly ideally).
- [ ] V6 Alternatively, use a gas syringe connected to a pressure sensor to show PV is constant at constant temperature, and a sealed flask in a water bath to show P/T is constant.
- [ ] V7 Diagram: a conical flask containing acid, with the magnesium suspended or added, connected by a delivery tube to a gas syringe.

### 3. Traps

- Mixed units (kPa with cm³, or °C) are the commonest error; convert to Pa, m³ and K.
- van der Waals' equation is not required.
- The volatile-liquid method belongs to "The mole and molar volume".

### 4. What exams ask (topics only)

- Calculate the volume, moles, pressure or Mr of a gas using PV = nRT, with unit conversions.

### 5. Practice questions to write

1. 9 marks: "Calculate the volume, in litres, occupied by 4.4 g of carbon dioxide at 27 °C and 100 kPa." Model answer: n = 0.10 mol; V = 0.10 × 8.314 × 300 ÷ 100 000 = 2.49 × 10⁻³ m³ = 2.49 L.
2. 9 marks: "0.29 g of a gas occupies 120 cm³ at 20 °C and 101 kPa. Calculate its relative molecular mass." Model answer: n = (101 000 × 1.20 × 10⁻⁴) ÷ (8.314 × 293) = 12.12 ÷ 2436 = 4.98 × 10⁻³ mol; M = 0.29 ÷ 4.98 × 10⁻³ = 58.
