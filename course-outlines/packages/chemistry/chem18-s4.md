## 1. Must cover

The principle (one block)
- [ ] L1 Le Châtelier's principle: if a system at equilibrium is disturbed (by a change in concentration, pressure or temperature), the system responds so as to oppose the change, and a new equilibrium is set up.
- [ ] L2 "Shifts to the right" means more products form (the forward reaction is favoured until equilibrium is restored); "shifts to the left" means more reactants form.
- [ ] L3 The principle predicts the direction of the shift, not how fast it happens or how big it is; the change is only partly reversed.

Concentration (one block)
- [ ] C1 Adding a reactant (or removing a product) shifts the equilibrium to the right, using up some of the added reactant and forming more product.
- [ ] C2 Adding a product (or removing a reactant) shifts the equilibrium to the left.
- [ ] C3 Kc is unchanged: the concentrations adjust until the ratio in the Kc expression is back at its old value.
- [ ] C4 Removing a product as it forms keeps pulling the equilibrium to the right, e.g. ammonia is liquefied and removed in the Haber process; in esterification, using an excess of ethanol (cheaper reactant) increases the yield of ester.
- [ ] C5 Example: in Fe³⁺(aq) + SCN⁻(aq) ⇌ FeSCN²⁺(aq) (red), adding either reactant deepens the red colour (details in "Investigation: disturbing an equilibrium").

Pressure, for reactions of gases (one block)
- [ ] P1 Increasing the pressure shifts the equilibrium towards the side with fewer moles of gas, since fewer molecules exert a lower pressure; decreasing the pressure shifts it towards the side with more moles of gas.
- [ ] P2 Count only the gas molecules, using the coefficients: N₂(g) + 3H₂(g) ⇌ 2NH₃(g) has 4 mol of gas on the left and 2 on the right, so high pressure shifts it to the right (more ammonia).
- [ ] P3 If both sides have the same number of moles of gas, pressure has no effect on the position of equilibrium, e.g. H₂(g) + I₂(g) ⇌ 2HI(g) (2 mol each side).
- [ ] P4 Compressing H₂ + I₂ ⇌ 2HI makes the purple colour deeper because every concentration (including I₂) rises, but the percentage of each substance is unchanged.
- [ ] P5 2NO₂(g) (brown) ⇌ N₂O₄(g) (colourless): compressing the mixture in a sealed syringe makes it darker at once (higher concentration), then it becomes paler as the equilibrium shifts to the right (2 mol → 1 mol of gas), though not as pale as before.
- [ ] P6 A change of pressure does not change Kc.

Temperature (one block)
- [ ] T1 Raising the temperature shifts the equilibrium in the endothermic direction (which absorbs heat); lowering it shifts the equilibrium in the exothermic direction (which releases heat).
- [ ] T2 If the forward reaction is exothermic (ΔH negative), heating shifts the equilibrium to the left and cooling to the right; the reverse is true if it is endothermic.
- [ ] T3 Example: 2NO₂(g) ⇌ N₂O₄(g), ΔH about −57 kJ mol⁻¹: heating shifts it to the left (darker brown, more NO₂); cooling in ice-water shifts it to the right (paler).
- [ ] T4 Temperature is the only disturbance that changes Kc: for an exothermic forward reaction Kc falls as temperature rises; for an endothermic one it rises.

Catalysts (one block)
- [ ] K1 A catalyst does not disturb an equilibrium or change its position: it speeds up the forward and reverse reactions equally.
- [ ] K2 It lets equilibrium be reached more quickly, so the same yield is obtained in less time, and it does not change Kc.

Applications in everyday life and the environment (one block)
- [ ] E1 Fizzy drinks: CO₂(g) ⇌ CO₂(aq). Opening the bottle lowers the pressure of CO₂ above the drink, so the equilibrium shifts to the left and gas bubbles out; a cold drink holds more dissolved CO₂.
- [ ] E2 Oceans (sustainability): CO₂(g) ⇌ CO₂(aq); CO₂(aq) + H₂O(l) ⇌ H₂CO₃(aq); H₂CO₃(aq) + CO₃²⁻(aq) ⇌ 2HCO₃⁻(aq). More CO₂ in the air increases dissolved CO₂ and carbonic acid; the third equilibrium shifts right, using up carbonate ions, so less carbonate is available to shellfish and corals to build calcium carbonate shells and skeletons.
- [ ] E3 Indicators: the colour of an acid–base indicator changes because adding acid or base shifts HIn ⇌ H⁺ + In⁻ (see "How indicators work").

Optimising yield: the Haber process (Higher Level only; one block)
- [ ] H1 [HL] N₂(g) + 3H₂(g) ⇌ 2NH₃(g), ΔH = −92 kJ mol⁻¹. Ammonia is used mainly to make fertilisers.
- [ ] H2 [HL] Pressure: 4 mol of gas → 2 mol, so high pressure increases the yield (Le Châtelier) and also increases the rate (higher concentration).
- [ ] H3 [HL] Pressure in practice: about 200 atm is used as a compromise; higher pressures would give more ammonia, but plant able to withstand them is very expensive to build and run, and the risk of leaks and explosions increases.
- [ ] H4 [HL] Temperature: the forward reaction is exothermic, so a low temperature gives a higher yield at equilibrium; but at low temperature the reaction is too slow to be economic.
- [ ] H5 [HL] Temperature in practice: a compromise of about 400–500 °C (about 450 °C) gives a reasonable yield at a fast enough rate.
- [ ] H6 [HL] Catalyst: iron, which does not change the yield but lets equilibrium be approached quickly at the compromise temperature.
- [ ] H7 [HL] Concentration: ammonia is removed by cooling and liquefying it, and unreacted nitrogen and hydrogen are recycled through the catalyst, so the overall conversion is high.

Optimising yield: other processes (Higher Level only; one block)
- [ ] O1 [HL] Contact process (sulfuric acid manufacture): 2SO₂(g) + O₂(g) ⇌ 2SO₃(g), ΔH about −197 kJ mol⁻¹. Le Châtelier predicts high pressure (3 mol → 2 mol of gas) and low temperature for the best yield.
- [ ] O2 [HL] In practice about 450 °C (a compromise between yield and rate), a vanadium(V) oxide (V₂O₅) catalyst, and a pressure only slightly above atmospheric (1–2 atm), because the yield is already very high and extra pressure would cost more than it gains.
- [ ] O3 [HL] General method for any given equilibrium: for the best yield choose the temperature that favours the exothermic or endothermic direction as needed, the pressure that favours the side with fewer (or more) gas moles, and an excess of a cheap reactant or removal of product; then weigh this against rate, cost, energy use and safety.
- [ ] O4 [HL] Sustainability link: lower temperatures and pressures save energy and reduce CO₂ emissions; a good catalyst lets a process run at a lower temperature.

## 3. Traps

- The specification does not require the use of a catalyst as a disturbance to equilibrium; a catalyst does not shift an equilibrium at all (K1).
- Pressure only matters when the numbers of moles of gas differ; count gas moles only.
- Only temperature changes Kc.
- Don't say the system "removes the change completely"; it partly opposes it.
- Industrial conditions are compromises; Le Châtelier alone predicts very high pressure and low temperature, which are not used.
- Don't describe the plant or the raw-material production in detail (industrial case studies are off the specification).

## 4. What exams ask (topics only)

- State Le Châtelier's principle.
- Predict and explain the effect of temperature, pressure and concentration changes (and of a catalyst) on a given equilibrium: NO₂/N₂O₄, H₂ + I₂, PCl₅, Haber, SO₂/SO₃, CO + 2H₂ ⇌ CH₃OH, CO + H₂O.
- Colour changes on heating, cooling or compressing a coloured gas equilibrium.
- (HL) Conditions for the best yield of ammonia or sulfur trioxide; why the predicted conditions are not all used in industry.
- Effect of more CO₂ on carbonate in seawater and on shell-making.
