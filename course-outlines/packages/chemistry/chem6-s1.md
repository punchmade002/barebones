## Part: Electronegativity and bond type (formerly `chem6-s1`)

- **loId:** `chem6-s1` · **chapterId:** `chem6` · **syllabusRef:** 2.1.2
- **Syllabus:** 2.1.2 "predict the nature of chemical bonds between atoms, using trends in
  electronegativity values". Learn about: "how the concept of electronegativity, and explaining
  trends in electronegativity values, can be used to predict the nature of the chemical bond
  along the continuum". Normal type. 1.3 also names "electronegativity (using Pauling scale)".
- **Prepared by:** a higher model, 2026-10-30. Definition, Pauling values, trends with
  explanation, and predicting bond type from the difference. The continuum itself is introduced
  in "Bonding, formulae and shapes"; refer to it.

### 1. Must cover

Electronegativity (one block)
- [ ] E1 Electronegativity is the relative attraction that an atom in a molecule has for the shared pair of electrons in a covalent bond.
- [ ] E2 It is measured on the Pauling scale, on which fluorine, the most electronegative element, has the highest value (3.98, about 4); the values have no units.
- [ ] E3 Values to use (Pauling scale): F 3.98; O 3.44; Cl 3.16; N 3.04; Br 2.96; I 2.66; S 2.58; C 2.55; H 2.20; P 2.19; Si 1.90; Al 1.61; Mg 1.31; Ca 1.00; Li 0.98; Na 0.93; K 0.82.
- [ ] E4 Non-metals have high electronegativities; metals have low ones.

Trends and their explanation (one block)
- [ ] T1 Across a period (left to right) electronegativity increases, e.g. Na 0.93 → Cl 3.16.
- [ ] T2 Explanation across a period: the nuclear charge increases while electrons are added to the same main energy level, so the atomic radius decreases and the nucleus attracts bonding electrons more strongly.
- [ ] T3 Down a group electronegativity decreases, e.g. F 3.98 → I 2.66.
- [ ] T4 Explanation down a group: each element has an extra energy level, so the atomic radius increases and the inner electrons screen (shield) the outer electrons from the nucleus; the bonding electrons are further away and less strongly attracted, even though the nuclear charge is greater.
- [ ] T5 Noble gases are usually given no electronegativity value because they rarely form bonds.

Predicting bond type (one block, skills)
- [ ] P1 Find the difference in electronegativity between the two atoms; the larger the difference, the more ionic the bond.
- [ ] P2 Difference greater than 1.7: mainly ionic, e.g. NaCl: 3.16 − 0.93 = 2.23.
- [ ] P3 Difference between about 0.4 and 1.7: polar covalent, e.g. H–Cl: 3.16 − 2.20 = 0.96; O–H: 3.44 − 2.20 = 1.24.
- [ ] P4 Difference less than about 0.4: non-polar (pure) covalent, e.g. C–H: 2.55 − 2.20 = 0.35; Cl–Cl: 0.
- [ ] P5 The more electronegative atom carries the partial negative charge (δ−) in a polar bond.
- [ ] P6 These cut-offs are guides, not sharp boundaries, because bonding is a continuum.

### 2. Checked facts and examples

| Item | Fact |
|---|---|
| E3 | Pauling electronegativities as listed (as in the SEC Formulae and Tables). |
| P2–P4 | Cut-offs ~1.7 (ionic) and ~0.4 (non-polar) as used in Leaving Certificate chemistry. |

### 3. Traps

- Electronegativity is for an atom in a bond; it is not the same as ionisation energy (taught in "Families and trends in the periodic table").
- Don't give older rounded values (e.g. O 3.5, C 2.5) alongside these; use one set.
- Bond-type properties belong to "Bonding and properties".

### 4. What exams ask (topics only)

- Define electronegativity; explain the trends across a period and down a group.
- Use electronegativity values to predict the type of bond in given compounds; identify the δ− atom.

### 5. Practice questions to write

1. 9 marks: "Define electronegativity and explain why electronegativity decreases down group 17." Model answer: E1, T3, T4.
2. 9 marks: "Use electronegativity values to predict the type of bonding in (i) KBr, (ii) NH₃, (iii) Br₂, showing your working." Model answer: 2.96 − 0.82 = 2.14 ionic; 3.04 − 2.20 = 0.84 polar covalent; 0 pure covalent.

## Part: Bonding and properties (formerly `chem6-s2`)

- **loId:** `chem6-s2` · **chapterId:** `chem6` · **syllabusRef:** 2.1.4
- **Syllabus:** 2.1.4 "relate the properties of simple compounds to the nature of bonding
  present". Learn about: "properties of compounds to include: electrical conductivity, melting
  and boiling points, solubility in water and state of matter at room temperature". Normal type.
- **Prepared by:** a higher model, 2026-10-30. Compare ionic and covalent (molecular) compounds
  property by property, each with its explanation. Intermolecular forces are explained in the
  next subsections; here say "weak forces between molecules" and refer to "Intramolecular
  bonding and intermolecular forces".

### 1. Must cover

Ionic compounds (one block)
- [ ] I1 State: solids at room temperature, e.g. sodium chloride, magnesium oxide.
- [ ] I2 High melting and boiling points (NaCl melts at 801 °C), because a lot of energy is needed to overcome the strong electrostatic attractions between oppositely charged ions throughout the lattice.
- [ ] I3 Electrical conductivity: they don't conduct when solid, because the ions are held in fixed positions; they conduct when molten or dissolved in water, because the ions are free to move and carry charge.
- [ ] I4 Solubility: many are soluble in water, because polar water molecules attract and surround the ions; they are insoluble in non-polar solvents such as cyclohexane.

Covalent (molecular) compounds (one block)
- [ ] C1 State: many are gases or liquids at room temperature (e.g. methane, carbon dioxide, water), or solids with low melting points (e.g. iodine, sugar).
- [ ] C2 Low melting and boiling points, because only the weak forces between molecules have to be overcome, not the strong covalent bonds within them.
- [ ] C3 Electrical conductivity: they don't conduct, because there are no ions or free electrons to carry charge (some, like hydrogen chloride, form ions when they dissolve in water and then conduct).
- [ ] C4 Solubility: non-polar molecules (e.g. methane, iodine) are insoluble or only slightly soluble in water but dissolve in non-polar solvents; polar molecules that can form hydrogen bonds (e.g. ethanol, sugar, ammonia) dissolve in water. "Like dissolves like."

Comparison and testing (one block)
- [ ] K1 Summary as paired points: ionic high vs covalent low melting points; ionic conduct when molten or in solution vs covalent don't conduct; ionic usually soluble in water vs covalent usually soluble in non-polar solvents; ionic solids vs covalent often gases or liquids.
- [ ] K2 A simple test: dissolve or melt the substance and test whether it conducts with a battery, bulb (or ammeter) and two carbon electrodes; a lit bulb shows ions are present.
- [ ] K3 Diagram: a conductivity circuit (battery, bulb, two electrodes in a beaker of solution).
- [ ] K4 Giant covalent substances are the exception: diamond and graphite have very high melting points because strong covalent bonds must be broken (taught in the carbon allotropes subsection of the hydrocarbons chapter).

### 2. Checked facts and examples

| Item | Fact |
|---|---|
| I2 | NaCl melting point 801 °C; boiling point 1413 °C. |
| C1 | Methane bp −162 °C; iodine sublimes on gentle heating and melts at 114 °C. |

### 3. Traps

- When a molecular substance melts or boils, the covalent bonds don't break; only the forces between molecules are overcome.
- Solid ionic compounds don't conduct; don't say "ionic compounds conduct" without the condition.
- Don't teach the types of intermolecular force here.

### 4. What exams ask (topics only)

- Give and explain two properties of ionic compounds.
- Explain why sodium chloride has a high melting point and conducts only when molten or in solution.
- Explain why a covalent compound such as methane has a low boiling point.

### 5. Practice questions to write

1. 9 marks: "Explain why sodium chloride (i) has a high melting point, (ii) does not conduct electricity as a solid, (iii) conducts electricity when dissolved in water." Model answer: I2, I3.
2. 6 marks: "Compare the electrical conductivity and boiling points of ionic and covalent compounds, explaining each difference." Model answer: K1 with I2, I3, C2, C3.

## Part: Intramolecular bonding and intermolecular forces (formerly `chem6-s3`)

- **loId:** `chem6-s3` · **chapterId:** `chem6` · **syllabusRef:** 2.2.1
- **Syllabus:** 2.2.1 "distinguish between intramolecular bonding and a range of intermolecular
  forces". Learn about: "a range of intermolecular forces that fall collectively under the
  umbrella of van der Waal's forces: London dispersion forces; permanent dipole-dipole,
  including Hydrogen bonding; **ion-dipole forces** (**dipole moments are not required**)".
  Bold = Higher Level only.
- **Prepared by:** a higher model, 2026-10-30. Define each force, how it arises and an example.
  Effects on boiling point and solubility are in "Intermolecular forces and physical
  properties"; mention them only as one-line consequences.

### 1. Must cover

Intramolecular and intermolecular (one block)
- [ ] D1 Intramolecular bonding: the strong bonds within a molecule (covalent bonds), holding its atoms together.
- [ ] D2 Intermolecular forces: the weaker forces of attraction between separate molecules.
- [ ] D3 Intermolecular forces are much weaker than covalent bonds: boiling water overcomes the forces between H₂O molecules but does not break the O–H bonds.
- [ ] D4 Van der Waals forces is the collective name for the intermolecular forces: London dispersion forces and permanent dipole–dipole forces (including hydrogen bonding).
- [ ] D5 Diagram: two water molecules with the O–H covalent bonds labelled "intramolecular" and the attraction between an H of one molecule and the O of the other labelled "intermolecular (hydrogen bond)".

London dispersion forces (one block)
- [ ] L1 They act between all molecules, and are the only intermolecular forces between non-polar molecules (e.g. H₂, Cl₂, CH₄, noble gas atoms).
- [ ] L2 They arise because electrons are always moving: at any instant the electron cloud may be unevenly spread, giving a temporary (instantaneous) dipole, which induces a dipole in a neighbouring molecule; the two attract.
- [ ] L3 They are the weakest type of intermolecular force for small molecules, but get stronger as molecules get larger and have more electrons.

Permanent dipole–dipole forces (one block)
- [ ] P1 A polar molecule has a permanent dipole: a δ+ end and a δ− end.
- [ ] P2 Dipole–dipole forces are the attractions between the δ+ end of one polar molecule and the δ− end of a neighbouring one, e.g. between HCl molecules (Hδ+–Clδ−).
- [ ] P3 They are stronger than London dispersion forces between molecules of similar size.

Hydrogen bonding (one block)
- [ ] H1 A hydrogen bond is a particularly strong dipole–dipole attraction between a hydrogen atom bonded to a small, very electronegative atom (fluorine, oxygen or nitrogen) in one molecule and a lone pair on an F, O or N atom of a neighbouring molecule.
- [ ] H2 It is strong because the H–F, O–H and N–H bonds are very polar, leaving the small hydrogen atom strongly δ+.
- [ ] H3 Examples: water (H₂O), ammonia (NH₃), hydrogen fluoride (HF), alcohols such as ethanol, carboxylic acids.
- [ ] H4 Hydrogen bonds are the strongest intermolecular forces, but still much weaker than covalent bonds.
- [ ] H5 Diagram: water molecules with δ+ and δ− labelled and hydrogen bonds drawn as dashed lines from H to the lone pair on O of the next molecule.

Ion–dipole forces (Higher Level only; one block)
- [ ] X1 [HL] An ion–dipole force is the attraction between an ion and the oppositely charged end of a polar molecule.
- [ ] X2 [HL] When an ionic compound such as sodium chloride dissolves in water, the δ− oxygen ends of water molecules are attracted to Na⁺ ions and the δ+ hydrogen ends to Cl⁻ ions; the ions become surrounded by water molecules (hydrated).
- [ ] X3 [HL] Ion–dipole forces are stronger than dipole–dipole forces; they explain why ionic compounds dissolve in polar solvents such as water.
- [ ] X4 [HL] Diagram: a Na⁺ ion surrounded by water molecules with their O atoms pointing towards it, and a Cl⁻ ion surrounded by water molecules with their H atoms pointing towards it.

Order of strength (one block)
- [ ] S1 For molecules of similar size: London dispersion < dipole–dipole < hydrogen bonding, all much weaker than covalent and ionic bonds.

### 2. Checked facts and examples

Only the examples above.

### 3. Traps

- Dipole moments are not required: don't define or calculate them.
- A hydrogen bond is not a covalent bond to hydrogen; it is between molecules.
- Hydrogen bonding needs H bonded directly to N, O or F: there is no hydrogen bonding between CH₄ molecules or in HCl.
- The specification writes "van der Waal's forces"; write "van der Waals forces".
- Keep ion–dipole in its own Higher Level block.

### 4. What exams ask (topics only)

- Distinguish intramolecular bonding from intermolecular forces.
- Explain how London dispersion forces arise; explain hydrogen bonding with water as an example.
- Name the strongest intermolecular force in a named substance.

### 5. Practice questions to write

1. 9 marks: "Distinguish between intramolecular bonding and intermolecular forces, and explain how London dispersion forces arise." Model answer: D1–D3, L2.
2. 9 marks: "What is meant by hydrogen bonding? Explain why hydrogen bonding occurs between water molecules but not between methane molecules." Model answer: H1, H2, trap point on CH₄.
3. 6 marks (Higher Level): "Explain, in terms of ion–dipole forces, how sodium chloride dissolves in water." Model answer: X1–X3.
