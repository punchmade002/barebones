## Part: The mole (formerly `chem8-s1`)

- **loId:** `chem8-s1` · **chapterId:** `chem8` · **syllabusRef:** 1.4.1
- **Syllabus:** 1.4.1 "define and explain the mole in terms of the Avogadro constant, and relate
  the mole to how the amount of a substance can be quantified". Learn about: "the significance
  and scale of the mole as a means of quantifying the amount of matter in chemistry; how the
  mole concept allows the number of particles, mass, relative atomic/molecular mass, volume
  (for gases) and moles to be interrelated; definitions of molar mass, relative molecular mass,
  density". Normal type.
- **Prepared by:** a higher model, 2026-10-30. Definitions, then the conversion relationships
  with one worked example each. Gas volumes and molar volume are in "Molar volume and
  Avogadro's law"; give the relationship here and point there. Use Ar values: H 1, C 12, N 14,
  O 16, Na 23, Mg 24, S 32, Cl 35.5, Ca 40.

### 1. Must cover

The mole and the Avogadro constant (one block)
- [ ] M1 The mole (symbol mol) is the SI unit of amount of substance.
- [ ] M2 One mole contains exactly 6.02214076 × 10²³ elementary entities (atoms, molecules, ions or electrons); this number is the Avogadro constant, NA = 6.02 × 10²³ mol⁻¹.
- [ ] M3 This is the number of atoms in 12 g of carbon-12 (the definition used before 2019).
- [ ] M4 Always state the particle: one mole of oxygen atoms (O) contains 6.02 × 10²³ atoms; one mole of oxygen molecules (O₂) contains 6.02 × 10²³ molecules and 1.204 × 10²⁴ atoms.
- [ ] M5 Significance and scale: atoms are far too small to count, so chemists count them by weighing; the mole links a mass you can weigh to a number of particles. One mole of water is only 18 g (about 18 cm³) but contains 6.02 × 10²³ molecules.

Relative molecular mass and molar mass (one block)
- [ ] R1 Relative molecular mass (Mr): the sum of the relative atomic masses of all the atoms in a molecule (or formula unit), e.g. Mr(H₂O) = 2(1) + 16 = 18; Mr(H₂SO₄) = 2(1) + 32 + 4(16) = 98; Mr(CaCO₃) = 40 + 12 + 3(16) = 100. Mr has no units.
- [ ] R2 Molar mass (M): the mass of one mole of a substance, in grams per mole (g mol⁻¹); numerically equal to Ar or Mr, e.g. the molar mass of water is 18 g mol⁻¹.
- [ ] R3 Density: mass per unit volume, ρ = m ÷ V, in g cm⁻³ or kg m⁻³ (e.g. water about 1.00 g cm⁻³).

Interconverting quantities (one block, skills; one bullet per relationship with a worked example)
- [ ] C1 Moles from mass: n = m ÷ M. Example: 8.8 g of CO₂ (M = 44 g mol⁻¹) = 8.8 ÷ 44 = 0.20 mol.
- [ ] C2 Mass from moles: m = n × M. Example: 0.25 mol of NaCl (M = 58.5 g mol⁻¹) = 0.25 × 58.5 = 14.6 g.
- [ ] C3 Number of particles from moles: N = n × NA. Example: 0.20 mol of CO₂ = 0.20 × 6.02 × 10²³ = 1.20 × 10²³ molecules.
- [ ] C4 Moles from number of particles: n = N ÷ NA.
- [ ] C5 Number of atoms in a molecule: multiply by the atoms per molecule, e.g. 0.20 mol CO₂ contains 0.60 mol of atoms = 3.61 × 10²³ atoms.
- [ ] C6 Gas volume from moles: V = n × molar volume (22.4 L mol⁻¹ at STP; see "Molar volume and Avogadro's law").
- [ ] C7 Mass from volume and density: m = ρ × V, then convert to moles, e.g. 10.0 cm³ of ethanol (density 0.789 g cm⁻³, M = 46 g mol⁻¹) = 7.89 g = 0.172 mol.
- [ ] C8 Summary as a route: mass ⇄ moles ⇄ number of particles, and moles ⇄ gas volume; always go through moles.

### 2. Checked facts and examples

| Item | Fact |
|---|---|
| M2 | NA = 6.02214076 × 10²³ mol⁻¹ exactly (SI redefinition, 2019); use 6.02 × 10²³. |
| R3, C7 | Density of water about 1.00 g cm⁻³; ethanol 0.789 g cm⁻³. |

### 3. Traps

- Mr has no units; molar mass has units g mol⁻¹.
- For diatomic gases, the number of atoms is twice the number of molecules.
- Don't teach molar volume or STP in detail here.

### 4. What exams ask (topics only)

- Define the mole and the Avogadro constant.
- Calculate moles, masses and numbers of particles; numbers of atoms in a given mass.

### 5. Practice questions to write

1. 6 marks: "Define the mole and calculate the number of molecules in 4.4 g of carbon dioxide." Model answer: M1, M2; 4.4 ÷ 44 = 0.10 mol; × 6.02 × 10²³ = 6.02 × 10²² molecules.
2. 9 marks: "Calculate (i) the mass of 0.15 mol of calcium carbonate, (ii) the number of moles in 9.8 g of sulfuric acid, (iii) the number of oxygen atoms in 9.8 g of sulfuric acid." Model answer: 15 g; 0.10 mol; 0.40 mol O atoms = 2.41 × 10²³ atoms.

## Part: Relative atomic mass from isotopic abundance (formerly `chem8-s2`)

- **loId:** `chem8-s2` · **chapterId:** `chem8` · **syllabusRef:** 1.4.2
- **Syllabus:** 1.4.2 "solve problems involving relative atomic mass and percentage abundance of
  isotopes". Normal type.
- **Prepared by:** a higher model, 2026-10-30. Definitions of isotope and relative atomic mass
  are in "Atomic structure: the nuclear model"; recap in one bullet
  each, then the two kinds of calculation with worked examples.

### 1. Must cover

Recap (one block)
- [ ] R1 Isotopes are atoms of the same element with different mass numbers.
- [ ] R2 Relative atomic mass is the weighted average of the isotope masses, using their percentage abundances, relative to one-twelfth of the mass of a carbon-12 atom.

Finding Ar from abundances (one block, skills)
- [ ] A1 Method: multiply each isotope's mass number by its percentage abundance, add the products and divide by 100: Ar = Σ(mass number × % abundance) ÷ 100.
- [ ] A2 Chlorine (75% ³⁵Cl, 25% ³⁷Cl): (35 × 75 + 37 × 25) ÷ 100 = (2625 + 925) ÷ 100 = 35.5.
- [ ] A3 Boron (19.9% ¹⁰B, 80.1% ¹¹B): (10 × 19.9 + 11 × 80.1) ÷ 100 = (199 + 881.1) ÷ 100 = 10.8.
- [ ] A4 Magnesium, three isotopes (79.0% ²⁴Mg, 10.0% ²⁵Mg, 11.0% ²⁶Mg): (24 × 79.0 + 25 × 10.0 + 26 × 11.0) ÷ 100 = (1896 + 250 + 286) ÷ 100 = 24.3.
- [ ] A5 The answer must lie between the lightest and heaviest isotope masses, closer to the more abundant one: a quick check.

Finding abundances from Ar (one block, skills)
- [ ] B1 Method for two isotopes: let the abundance of one be x%; the other is (100 − x)%; set up Ar = [mass₁ × x + mass₂ × (100 − x)] ÷ 100 and solve for x.
- [ ] B2 Boron (Ar 10.8; isotopes ¹⁰B and ¹¹B): 10x + 11(100 − x) = 1080 → 1100 − x = 1080 → x = 20; so about 20% boron-10 and 80% boron-11, matching the data in A3.
- [ ] B3 Using exact whole mass numbers is an approximation; data-book isotope masses give more accurate abundances.

### 2. Checked facts and examples

| Item | Fact |
|---|---|
| A2 | Cl: about 75% ³⁵Cl, 25% ³⁷Cl → 35.5. |
| A3 | B: 19.9% ¹⁰B, 80.1% ¹¹B → 10.8. |
| A4 | Mg: 79.0% ²⁴Mg, 10.0% ²⁵Mg, 11.0% ²⁶Mg → 24.3. |

### 3. Traps

- Don't introduce the mass spectrometer (not on the specification); abundances are given as data.
- Divide by 100 when abundances are percentages; by the total if they are given as ratios.

### 4. What exams ask (topics only)

- Calculate the relative atomic mass of an element from given isotopic abundances.
- Calculate abundances from a given relative atomic mass.

### 5. Practice questions to write

1. 6 marks: "Gallium consists of 60.1% gallium-69 and 39.9% gallium-71. Calculate the relative atomic mass of gallium to one decimal place." Model answer: (69 × 60.1 + 71 × 39.9) ÷ 100 = (4146.9 + 2832.9) ÷ 100 = 69.8.
2. 6 marks: "Lithium has isotopes of mass numbers 6 and 7 and a relative atomic mass of 6.94. Calculate the percentage abundance of each isotope (sample data)." Model answer: 6x + 7(100 − x) = 694 → x = 6; 6% lithium-6 and 94% lithium-7.

## Part: Molar volume and Avogadro's law (formerly `chem8-s3`)

- **loId:** `chem8-s3` · **chapterId:** `chem8` · **syllabusRef:** 1.4.3
- **Syllabus:** 1.4.3 "state Avogadro's law and deduce the molar volume of a gas". Learn about:
  "molar volume of a gas and Avogadro's law; the conditions for standard temperature (K) and
  pressure (STP)". Normal type.
- **Prepared by:** a higher model, 2026-10-30. Use the SEC/Leaving Certificate values: STP is
  273 K and 101.3 kPa (1 atm), molar volume 22.4 L mol⁻¹; room temperature and pressure about
  24 L mol⁻¹. The ideal gas equation itself is taught in "Gas laws and the ideal gas equation"; use it once
  to deduce the molar volume.

### 1. Must cover

Avogadro's law (one block)
- [ ] A1 Avogadro's law: equal volumes of all gases, at the same temperature and pressure, contain equal numbers of molecules.
- [ ] A2 So at fixed temperature and pressure, the volume of a gas is directly proportional to the number of moles: V ∝ n.
- [ ] A3 (assumed) Gay-Lussac's law of combining volumes: gases react in volumes that are in simple whole-number ratios to one another and to the volumes of any gaseous products, at the same temperature and pressure, e.g. 2 volumes of hydrogen react with 1 volume of oxygen.
- [ ] A4 Avogadro's law explains Gay-Lussac's law: the volume ratio equals the mole ratio in the balanced equation, e.g. N₂(g) + 3H₂(g) → 2NH₃(g): 1 volume + 3 volumes → 2 volumes.

STP and molar volume (one block)
- [ ] S1 Standard temperature and pressure (STP): 0 °C (273 K) and 101.3 kPa (1 atmosphere, 1.013 × 10⁵ Pa).
- [ ] S2 Molar volume: the volume occupied by one mole of any gas at a stated temperature and pressure; at STP it is 22.4 L (22 400 cm³).
- [ ] S3 At room temperature (about 20 °C) and pressure, the molar volume is about 24 L.
- [ ] S4 IUPAC now uses a standard pressure of 100 kPa, at which the molar volume at 273 K is 22.7 L; Leaving Certificate calculations use 22.4 L at STP unless told otherwise.

Deducing the molar volume (one block, skills)
- [ ] D1 From PV = nRT, V = nRT ÷ P.
- [ ] D2 For 1 mol at 273 K and 101 325 Pa, with R = 8.314 J K⁻¹ mol⁻¹: V = 1 × 8.314 × 273 ÷ 101 325 = 0.0224 m³ = 22.4 L.
- [ ] D3 Experimentally: measure the mass of a known volume of a gas (e.g. oxygen from a cylinder, by weighing the cylinder before and after releasing gas into a gas syringe), find the number of moles from the mass, and scale the volume to one mole, correcting to STP.

Calculations (one block, skills)
- [ ] C1 Moles from gas volume: n = V ÷ 22.4 (V in L at STP), e.g. 5.6 L of CO₂ at STP = 0.25 mol.
- [ ] C2 Volume from moles: V = n × 22.4, e.g. 0.50 mol O₂ occupies 11.2 L at STP.
- [ ] C3 Mass to volume through moles: 8.0 g of methane (M = 16 g mol⁻¹) = 0.50 mol = 11.2 L at STP.
- [ ] C4 Volumes of gases in a reaction: 50 cm³ of methane burns in 100 cm³ of oxygen (CH₄ + 2O₂ → CO₂ + 2H₂O), giving 50 cm³ of CO₂, measured at the same temperature and pressure.

### 2. Checked facts and examples

| Item | Fact |
|---|---|
| S1, S2 | STP 273 K, 101.325 kPa; molar volume 22.4 L mol⁻¹ (SEC Formulae and Tables value). |
| S3 | ~24.0 L mol⁻¹ at 20 °C and 101.3 kPa. |
| S4 | IUPAC STP 273.15 K, 100 kPa → 22.7 L mol⁻¹. |
| D2 | R = 8.314 J K⁻¹ mol⁻¹. |

### 3. Traps

- Molar volume applies to gases only, not liquids or solids.
- Avogadro's law is about numbers of molecules, not atoms or mass.
- Temperature must be in kelvin.
- Leave the full treatment of PV = nRT, its units and its assumptions to "Gas laws and the ideal gas equation" and "Gas laws and the ideal gas equation".

### 4. What exams ask (topics only)

- State Avogadro's law; state the conditions of STP; give the molar volume at STP.
- Calculate the volume of a gas at STP from its mass, or moles from volume.
- Calculate volumes of reacting gases.

### 5. Practice questions to write

1. 6 marks: "State Avogadro's law and calculate the volume, at STP, occupied by 3.2 g of oxygen gas." Model answer: A1; 3.2 ÷ 32 = 0.10 mol; × 22.4 = 2.24 L.
2. 9 marks: "Show, using the ideal gas equation, that one mole of gas occupies 22.4 L at STP, and calculate the mass of 1.12 L of carbon dioxide at STP." Model answer: D2; 1.12 ÷ 22.4 = 0.050 mol × 44 = 2.2 g.

## Part: Relative molecular mass of a volatile liquid (formerly `chem8-s4`)

- **loId:** `chem8-s4` · **chapterId:** `chem8` · **syllabusRef:** 1.4.4
- **Syllabus:** 1.4.4 "conduct an experiment to determine the relative molecular mass of a gas
  derived from a liquid". Normal type.
- **Prepared by:** a higher model, 2026-10-30. Write as one full investigation (NOTES_STANDARD
  "Investigation" block type) using the standard school method: a volatile liquid (propanone)
  vaporised in a conical flask in a boiling water bath, the vapour's mass, volume, temperature
  and pressure measured, and PV = nRT used to find n and then Mr. The ideal gas equation is
  taught in "Gas laws and the ideal gas equation"; use it without re-teaching. Use R = 8.314 J K⁻¹ mol⁻¹.

### 1. Must cover

Principle (one block)
- [ ] P1 A volatile liquid is one that evaporates easily (has a low boiling point), so it can be turned completely into vapour at 100 °C.
- [ ] P2 If the mass (m), volume (V), temperature (T) and pressure (P) of the vapour are measured, the number of moles is n = PV ÷ RT, and Mr = m ÷ n (molar mass in g mol⁻¹).
- [ ] P3 The vapour is assumed to behave as an ideal gas.

Equipment (one block)
- [ ] E1 A clean, dry conical flask (about 250 cm³), a small square of aluminium foil with a rubber band, a pin, a large beaker of boiling water (water bath) on a hotplate, a thermometer, a top-pan balance (0.01 g), a barometer, a graduated (measuring) cylinder, and a dropper of the volatile liquid (propanone).

Method (one block, one step per bullet)
- [ ] M1 Weigh the dry conical flask with the foil cap and rubber band.
- [ ] M2 Add about 2–3 cm³ of propanone, replace the foil cap and pierce one small pinhole in it.
- [ ] M3 Clamp the flask in the boiling water bath so that as much of it as possible is immersed.
- [ ] M4 Leave it until all the liquid has vaporised; the vapour pushes the air out of the flask through the pinhole, and excess vapour escapes, so the flask is left full of propanone vapour at the temperature of the boiling water.
- [ ] M5 Record the temperature of the water bath and the atmospheric pressure from the barometer.
- [ ] M6 Remove the flask, cool it (the vapour condenses), dry the outside thoroughly and reweigh it. The increase in mass is the mass of vapour that filled the flask.
- [ ] M7 Find the volume of the flask by filling it completely with water and measuring the water with a graduated cylinder.
- [ ] M8 Diagram: the conical flask with a pierced foil cap, clamped in a beaker of boiling water on a hotplate, with a thermometer in the water.

Calculation (one block, worked sample data)
- [ ] C1 Sample data: mass of vapour 0.51 g; volume of flask 270 cm³ = 2.70 × 10⁻⁴ m³; temperature 100 °C = 373 K; pressure 101 000 Pa.
- [ ] C2 n = PV ÷ RT = (101 000 × 2.70 × 10⁻⁴) ÷ (8.314 × 373) = 27.27 ÷ 3101 = 8.79 × 10⁻³ mol.
- [ ] C3 Mr = m ÷ n = 0.51 ÷ 8.79 × 10⁻³ = 58.0; the accepted Mr of propanone (CH₃COCH₃) is 58.
- [ ] C4 Units: P in Pa, V in m³, T in K; 1 cm³ = 1 × 10⁻⁶ m³.

Safety (one block)
- [ ] S1 Propanone is highly flammable: no naked flames; heat the water bath with a hotplate or electric heater.
- [ ] S2 Propanone vapour is an irritant: work in a well-ventilated room; take care with boiling water and hot glassware.

Sources of error and improvements (one block)
- [ ] R1 Not all the liquid vaporises, or the flask is removed too early: mass too high, Mr too high; wait until no liquid is visible.
- [ ] R2 Water on the outside of the flask when reweighed: mass too high; dry carefully.
- [ ] R3 The pinhole is too large and air re-enters as the flask cools, or vapour escapes before weighing: affects the measured mass.
- [ ] R4 Part of the flask is above the water, so the vapour there is cooler than 373 K.
- [ ] R5 The vapour is not a perfect ideal gas, especially close to its boiling point.
- [ ] R6 The mass of the vapour is small compared with the flask, so a balance reading to 0.01 g (or better) is needed; repeat and average.

### 2. Checked facts and examples

| Item | Fact |
|---|---|
| E1, S1 | Propanone, CH₃COCH₃, boiling point 56 °C, Mr 58, highly flammable. |
| C2 | R = 8.314 J K⁻¹ mol⁻¹. |

The numbers in C1 are sample data.

### 3. Traps

- The mass measured after cooling is the mass of the vapour that filled the flask at 373 K (it has condensed); the air in the flask at the start doesn't affect it because the vapour displaced it.
- Use the temperature of the boiling water, not room temperature, in the calculation.
- Convert cm³ to m³ (× 10⁻⁶) when P is in pascals.

### 4. What exams ask (topics only)

- Describe how the mass and volume of the vapour were found.
- Why is a pinhole made; why is a volatile liquid used; why must no flame be used?
- Calculate Mr from given data.

### 5. Practice questions to write

1. 12 marks: "Describe how you would determine the relative molecular mass of propanone by vaporising it in a conical flask, stating the measurements that must be made." Model answer: M1–M7.
2. 9 marks: "In an experiment, 0.44 g of the vapour of a volatile liquid filled a flask of volume 250 cm³ at 373 K and 101 000 Pa. Calculate the relative molecular mass of the liquid (sample data)." Model answer: n = (101 000 × 2.50 × 10⁻⁴) ÷ (8.314 × 373) = 25.25 ÷ 3101 = 8.14 × 10⁻³ mol; Mr = 0.44 ÷ 8.14 × 10⁻³ = 54.
