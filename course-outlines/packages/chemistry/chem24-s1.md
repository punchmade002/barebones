### 1. Must cover

Functional groups and homologous series (one block)
- [ ] F1 A functional group is an atom or group of atoms, attached to the hydrocarbon part of a molecule, that is responsible for the characteristic chemical properties of a family of organic compounds, e.g. –OH in alcohols, –COOH in carboxylic acids.
- [ ] F2 The hydrocarbon part (C–C and C–H bonds) is fairly unreactive, so reactions take place mainly at the functional group; compounds with the same functional group react in the same way (e.g. every carboxylic acid fizzes with sodium carbonate).
- [ ] F3 A homologous series is a family of compounds with the same general formula and the same functional group, in which each member differs from the next by CH₂ (a mass of 14).
- [ ] F4 Members of a homologous series have similar chemical properties and a gradual change (trend) in physical properties, such as boiling point rising with chain length.
- [ ] F5 Each family can be written with R for the hydrocarbon part (e.g. R–OH); this notation is explained in "Representing organic molecules".

Classification table (one block; one bullet per family: functional group, general formula, ending, first member)
- [ ] T1 Alkanes: C–C single bonds only; CₙH₂ₙ₊₂; ending -ane; methane CH₄ (naming in "Groups of hydrocarbons and their properties").
- [ ] T2 Cycloalkanes, limited to cyclohexane: a ring of six carbons with single bonds, C₆H₁₂ (general formula CₙH₂ₙ, the same as the alkenes); named with the prefix cyclo-; a saturated, non-polar liquid (bp 81 °C) used as a solvent.
- [ ] T3 Alkenes: C=C double bond; CₙH₂ₙ; ending -ene; ethene C₂H₄.
- [ ] T4 Alkynes: C≡C triple bond; CₙH₂ₙ₋₂; ending -yne; ethyne C₂H₂.
- [ ] T5 Aromatics, limited to benzene: C₆H₆, a planar ring of six carbons with delocalised electrons (see "Groups of hydrocarbons and their properties").
- [ ] T6 Haloalkanes: halogen atom –X (F, Cl, Br, I) in place of H; CₙH₂ₙ₊₁X (one halogen); prefix fluoro-, chloro-, bromo-, iodo-; chloromethane CH₃Cl.
- [ ] T7 Alcohols: hydroxyl group –OH; CₙH₂ₙ₊₁OH; ending -ol; methanol CH₃OH.
- [ ] T8 Aldehydes: carbonyl group at the end of the chain, –CHO; CₙH₂ₙ₊₁CHO (n = 0, 1, 2 …), i.e. CₙH₂ₙO; ending -al; methanal HCHO.
- [ ] T9 Ketones: carbonyl group C=O inside the chain, bonded to two carbons; CₙH₂ₙO (n ≥ 3); ending -one; propanone CH₃COCH₃.
- [ ] T10 Carboxylic acids (monocarboxylic, one –COOH): carboxyl group –COOH; CₙH₂ₙ₊₁COOH, i.e. CₙH₂ₙO₂; ending -oic acid; methanoic acid HCOOH.
- [ ] T11 Esters: ester group –COO– linking two hydrocarbon parts; RCOOR′, CₙH₂ₙO₂ (n ≥ 2); name "alkyl …-oate"; methyl methanoate HCOOCH₃.
- [ ] T12 Aldehydes, ketones, carboxylic acids and esters all contain the carbonyl group C=O.

General IUPAC rules (one block, skills; one step per bullet)
- [ ] N1 Step 1: Find the longest continuous carbon chain that contains the functional group; its number of carbons gives the stem: 1 meth-, 2 eth-, 3 prop-, 4 but-, 5 pent-, 6 hex-.
- [ ] N2 Step 2: Add the ending (suffix) for the functional group (-ol, -al, -one, -oic acid, -oate), or the prefix for a halogen (chloro- etc.).
- [ ] N3 Step 3: Number the chain from the end that gives the functional group the lowest number, and put the number before the ending (e.g. butan-2-ol) or before the prefix (2-chlorobutane).
- [ ] N4 Step 4: Name and number branches (methyl-, ethyl-) and halogen substituents as prefixes, in alphabetical order; use di-, tri-, tetra- for two, three or four of the same group, giving each a number (e.g. 1,2-dichloroethane, 2,2-dimethylpropan-1-ol).
- [ ] N5 Numbers are separated from each other by commas and from words by hyphens.
- [ ] N6 No number is needed when there is only one possible position: the –CHO and –COOH carbons are always carbon 1 (ethanal, propanoic acid), and there is only one propanone and one butanone.

Haloalkanes (one block)
- [ ] H1 A haloalkane is an alkane in which one or more hydrogen atoms have been replaced by halogen atoms; a chloroalkane contains chlorine.
- [ ] H2 Examples: chloromethane CH₃Cl; dichloromethane CH₂Cl₂; trichloromethane CHCl₃; tetrachloromethane CCl₄; chloroethane CH₃CH₂Cl; 1-chloropropane CH₃CH₂CH₂Cl; 2-chloropropane CH₃CHClCH₃; 1-bromobutane CH₃CH₂CH₂CH₂Br; 2-bromo-2-methylpropane (CH₃)₃CBr; 1,2-dichloroethane CH₂ClCH₂Cl.
- [ ] H3 Naming example: CH₃CH₂CHClCH₃ has a four-carbon chain with Cl on carbon 2 (numbered from the nearer end): 2-chlorobutane.
- [ ] H4 Uses: solvents for oils and grease (dichloromethane, e.g. in paint strippers); trichloromethane (chloroform) was one of the first anaesthetics, used from 1847, but is toxic; chloroethene is the monomer of PVC.

Alcohols (one block)
- [ ] A1 Members: methanol CH₃OH; ethanol CH₃CH₂OH; propan-1-ol CH₃CH₂CH₂OH; propan-2-ol CH₃CH(OH)CH₃; butan-1-ol CH₃CH₂CH₂CH₂OH; butan-2-ol CH₃CH₂CH(OH)CH₃; 2-methylpropan-1-ol (CH₃)₂CHCH₂OH; 2-methylpropan-2-ol (CH₃)₃COH; pentan-1-ol; hexan-1-ol.
- [ ] A2 Naming: replace the final -e of the alkane by -ol and give the position of the –OH, e.g. CH₃CH₂CH(OH)CH₃ is butan-2-ol.
- [ ] A3 More than one –OH: keep the -e and add -diol or -triol, e.g. propane-1,2,3-triol (glycerol), CH₂(OH)CH(OH)CH₂OH, which forms the backbone of fats and oils.
- [ ] A4 Primary alcohol: the carbon bearing the –OH is attached to at most one other carbon atom (–CH₂OH), e.g. ethanol, propan-1-ol, butan-1-ol, 2-methylpropan-1-ol (methanol, with no other carbon, is also treated as primary).
- [ ] A5 Secondary alcohol: the carbon bearing the –OH is attached to two other carbon atoms (>CHOH), e.g. propan-2-ol, butan-2-ol.
- [ ] A6 Tertiary alcohol: the carbon bearing the –OH is attached to three other carbon atoms, e.g. 2-methylpropan-2-ol.
- [ ] A7 Occurrence and uses: ethanol is the alcohol in alcoholic drinks (about 40% v/v in spirits such as whiskey), a fuel and a solvent (perfumes, deodorants, hairsprays, varnishes); methanol is toxic; propan-2-ol is used in hand sanitisers and as a cleaning solvent.

Aldehydes and ketones (one block)
- [ ] K1 Aldehydes: methanal HCHO; ethanal CH₃CHO; propanal CH₃CH₂CHO; butanal CH₃CH₂CH₂CHO; 2-methylpropanal (CH₃)₂CHCHO. The –CHO group is always at the end of the chain and its carbon is carbon 1.
- [ ] K2 Ketones: propanone CH₃COCH₃; butanone CH₃COCH₂CH₃; pentan-2-one CH₃COCH₂CH₂CH₃; pentan-3-one CH₃CH₂COCH₂CH₃; 3-methylbutan-2-one CH₃COCH(CH₃)₂. The C=O is inside the chain, so the smallest ketone has three carbons.
- [ ] K3 Naming: replace the -e of the alkane by -al (aldehyde) or -one (ketone); the position of the ketone C=O is numbered from the nearer end when there is a choice (pentan-2-one, not pentan-4-one).
- [ ] K4 Occurrence and uses: methanal in water (formalin) preserves biological specimens; ethanal is the first product formed when the body breaks down ethanol; propanone is a solvent, e.g. in nail varnish remover; benzaldehyde gives the flavour of almonds.

Carboxylic acids (one block)
- [ ] C1 Members: methanoic acid HCOOH; ethanoic acid CH₃COOH; propanoic acid CH₃CH₂COOH; butanoic acid CH₃CH₂CH₂COOH; 2-methylpropanoic acid (CH₃)₂CHCOOH; pentanoic and hexanoic acids.
- [ ] C2 Naming: replace the -e of the alkane by -oic acid; the carbon of the –COOH group is counted in the chain as carbon 1, and substituents are numbered from it, e.g. CH₃CHClCOOH is 2-chloropropanoic acid and CH₂ClCOOH is chloroethanoic acid.
- [ ] C3 The carboxyl group contains a C=O and an –OH on the same carbon, but behaves differently from both an alcohol and a ketone: it is acidic (see "Physical properties and acidity").
- [ ] C4 Occurrence and uses: methanoic acid is in the stings of ants and nettles (its old name, formic acid, is from the Latin for ant); ethanoic acid gives vinegar its sharp taste (vinegar is about 5% ethanoic acid) and is used as a food flavouring and preservative; benzoic acid and sodium benzoate are food preservatives.

Esters (one block)
- [ ] E1 Esters are formed when a carboxylic acid reacts with an alcohol, with loss of water: carboxylic acid + alcohol ⇌ ester + water (the reaction is in "Tests, reaction schemes and preparing an ester").
- [ ] E2 Naming step 1: split the ester at the single-bonded oxygen into the part from the acid (containing C=O) and the part from the alcohol.
- [ ] E3 Naming step 2: name the alcohol part as an alkyl group (methyl, ethyl, propyl …) and write it first.
- [ ] E4 Naming step 3: name the acid part by changing -oic acid to -oate; e.g. CH₃COOCH₂CH₃ (from ethanoic acid and ethanol) is ethyl ethanoate.
- [ ] E5 Examples: methyl methanoate HCOOCH₃; ethyl methanoate HCOOCH₂CH₃; methyl ethanoate CH₃COOCH₃; ethyl ethanoate CH₃COOCH₂CH₃; propyl methanoate HCOOCH₂CH₂CH₃; methyl propanoate CH₃CH₂COOCH₃; ethyl propanoate CH₃CH₂COOCH₂CH₃; propyl ethanoate CH₃COOCH₂CH₂CH₃.
- [ ] E6 The condensed formula is written acid part first (CH₃COOCH₂CH₃) but the name gives the alcohol part first (ethyl ethanoate); HCOOC₂H₅ and C₂H₅OOCH are the same compound, ethyl methanoate.
- [ ] E7 Only esters made from monocarboxylic acids and primary alcohols are required.
- [ ] E8 Occurrence and uses: esters have sweet, fruity smells and give many fruits and flowers their scent; used as food flavourings, in perfumes, and as solvents (ethyl ethanoate in glues and nail varnish remover); fats and oils are esters of propane-1,2,3-triol (see "Redox and acid–base reactions of organic compounds").

### 3. Traps

- The –COOH and –CHO carbon is carbon 1 and counts in the chain: CH₃CH₂COOH is propanoic acid, not ethanoic acid.
- Ester names: the alcohol part comes first. CH₃COOCH₃ is methyl ethanoate; HCOOC₂H₅ is ethyl methanoate (they are isomers, not the same compound).
- Primary/secondary/tertiary depends on the number of carbons bonded to the carbon with the –OH, not on the number of –OH groups or the position number.
- Don't name aromatic compounds other than benzene (benzaldehyde and benzoic acid may be mentioned by name as substances, not as naming exercises).
- Ethers, amines and amides are not on the list; don't name them.
- Don't use the old trivial names as the answer when an IUPAC name is asked (acetone → propanone; acetic acid → ethanoic acid).

### 4. What exams ask (topics only)

- Give IUPAC names for given formulae (alcohols, aldehydes, ketones, acids, esters, chloroalkanes) and draw structures from names.
- Name the family (homologous series) of a compound; give another member.
- Classify an alcohol as primary, secondary or tertiary and justify the classification.
- Name the acid and alcohol from which an ester is made.
- Give a use or occurrence of a named compound.

## Part: Representing organic molecules (formerly `chem24-s3`)

- **loId:** `chem24-s3` · **chapterId:** `chem24` · **syllabusRef:** 4.2.3
- **Syllabus:** 4.2.3 "construct and compare representations of organic molecules". Learn
  about: "representations of the above molecules: molecular formula; condensed structural
  formula; expanded molecular structures; 3D physical models (tetrahedral and planar geometry
  around the carbon atoms); use of R to represent part of an organic molecule; comparison
  involving structural isomerism". Normal type.
- **Prepared by:** a higher model, 2026-10-30. The four representations applied to the
  functional-group families, tetrahedral and planar carbon (with counting examples, a common
  exam task), R notation, and structural isomers between and within families. The same ideas
  for hydrocarbons (model kits, condensed and expanded formulae, isomers of alkanes and
  alkenes) are in "Shapes, bonding and isomerism"; geometric (cis–trans) isomers are in "Structural
  and geometric isomerism"; sigma and pi bonds in "Shapes, bonding and isomerism". Don't repeat
  those. Naming is in "Functional groups and nomenclature".

### 1. Must cover

Four representations (one block; one bullet per representation, using ethanoic acid)
- [ ] R1 Molecular formula: the number of atoms of each element in one molecule, e.g. ethanoic acid C₂H₄O₂; it does not show how the atoms are joined.
- [ ] R2 Condensed structural formula: the atoms bonded to each carbon written in order along the chain, without drawing bonds, e.g. CH₃COOH; branches go in brackets, e.g. (CH₃)₂CHOH for propan-2-ol.
- [ ] R3 Expanded (full or displayed) structural formula: every atom and every bond drawn, e.g. ethanoic acid drawn as H₃C with three C–H bonds, joined to a carbon with a C=O double bond and a C–O–H.
- [ ] R4 3D physical model (ball-and-stick, from a model kit): shows the real spatial arrangement and bond angles; usual colours are carbon black, hydrogen white, oxygen red and chlorine green.
- [ ] R5 Empirical formula (simplest ratio, see "Formulae and balancing equations") may also be asked: ethanoic acid C₂H₄O₂ has empirical formula CH₂O, the same as methanal and glucose.
- [ ] R6 Diagram: ethanol shown four ways: C₂H₆O; CH₃CH₂OH; the expanded structure with all bonds; and a ball-and-stick sketch.

Tetrahedral and planar carbon (one block)
- [ ] G1 A carbon with four single bonds has its four bonds pointing to the corners of a tetrahedron, bond angles 109.5°: tetrahedral geometry (e.g. every carbon in ethanol and in haloalkanes).
- [ ] G2 A carbon with one double bond (C=C or C=O) and two single bonds has its three bonding groups in a flat triangle, bond angles 120°: planar (trigonal planar) geometry (e.g. the C=O carbon in aldehydes, ketones, carboxylic acids and esters; both carbons of ethene; all six carbons of benzene).
- [ ] G3 A carbon with a triple bond (ethyne) is linear, 180°; it is neither tetrahedral nor planar in this classification.
- [ ] G4 An expanded formula drawn on paper shows the tetrahedral angles as 90°; only a 3D model (or a wedge drawing) shows the true shape.
- [ ] G5 The shapes follow from electron-pair repulsion: four bonding groups around carbon spread out tetrahedrally; three groups (the double bond counts as one) spread out in a plane (see "Bonding, formulae and shapes").

Counting tetrahedral and planar carbons (one block; one bullet per molecule)
- [ ] K1 Ethanol CH₃CH₂OH: 2 tetrahedral, 0 planar.
- [ ] K2 Ethanal CH₃CHO and ethanoic acid CH₃COOH: 1 tetrahedral (CH₃), 1 planar (the C=O carbon).
- [ ] K3 Propanone CH₃COCH₃: 2 tetrahedral, 1 planar.
- [ ] K4 Methyl methanoate HCOOCH₃: 1 planar (C=O carbon), 1 tetrahedral (CH₃).
- [ ] K5 Ethyl ethanoate CH₃COOCH₂CH₃: 1 planar (C=O carbon), 3 tetrahedral.
- [ ] K6 Methyl propanoate CH₃CH₂COOCH₃: 1 planar, 3 tetrahedral.
- [ ] K7 Ethene CH₂=CH₂ and benzene C₆H₆: all carbons planar; cyclohexane C₆H₁₂: all six carbons tetrahedral, so its ring is not flat.

Using R (one block)
- [ ] U1 R stands for an alkyl group (the hydrocarbon part of a molecule, e.g. CH₃– or C₂H₅–), so the formula highlights the functional group; R and R′ are used when two hydrocarbon parts may differ.
- [ ] U2 General structures: haloalkane R–X; alcohol R–OH; aldehyde R–CHO; ketone R–CO–R′; carboxylic acid R–COOH; ester R–COO–R′.
- [ ] U3 In an aldehyde, carboxylic acid or the acid part of an ester, R may be a hydrogen atom (methanal HCHO, methanoic acid HCOOH, methyl methanoate HCOOCH₃); in a ketone both R groups must be carbon groups, and in an ester R′ must be a carbon group.
- [ ] U4 Alcohol classes with R: primary R–CH₂OH; secondary R₂CHOH; tertiary R₃COH.
- [ ] U5 R is used to show a reaction that is common to a whole family, e.g. RCOOH + NaOH → RCOONa + H₂O.

Structural isomers (one block)
- [ ] I1 Structural isomers are compounds with the same molecular formula but different structural formulae (atoms joined in a different order).
- [ ] I2 Chain isomers differ in the carbon skeleton (branching): butan-1-ol and 2-methylpropan-1-ol (C₄H₁₀O); butanoic acid and 2-methylpropanoic acid (C₄H₈O₂).
- [ ] I3 Position isomers have the same functional group in different positions: propan-1-ol and propan-2-ol (C₃H₈O); 1-chloropropane and 2-chloropropane (C₃H₇Cl); pentan-2-one and pentan-3-one (C₅H₁₀O).
- [ ] I4 Functional group isomers belong to different families: propanal and propanone (C₃H₆O); butanal and butanone (C₄H₈O); ethanoic acid and methyl methanoate (C₂H₄O₂); propanoic acid, methyl ethanoate and ethyl methanoate (C₃H₆O₂); cyclohexane and hex-1-ene (C₆H₁₂).
- [ ] I5 The four alcohols of formula C₄H₉OH: butan-1-ol and 2-methylpropan-1-ol (primary), butan-2-ol (secondary), 2-methylpropan-2-ol (tertiary).
- [ ] I6 Isomers of C₄H₈O₂ on the course: butanoic acid and 2-methylpropanoic acid (acids); ethyl ethanoate, methyl propanoate and propyl methanoate (esters).
- [ ] I7 Comparing isomers: chain and position isomers have similar chemical properties but slightly different physical properties; functional group isomers have very different chemical and physical properties, e.g. ethanoic acid (acidic, bp 118 °C) and methyl methanoate (not acidic, bp 32 °C); see "Physical properties and acidity".

Comparing representations (one block)
- [ ] C1 The molecular formula is compact but cannot tell isomers apart (C₂H₆O could be ethanol or another compound).
- [ ] C2 The condensed formula shows the order of atoms and the functional group, so it distinguishes isomers, but not bond angles or shape.
- [ ] C3 The expanded formula shows every bond (so double bonds and functional groups are clear) but not the 3D shape.
- [ ] C4 The 3D model shows shape and bond angles but is slower to draw, isn't to scale, and doesn't show electron distribution or polarity.
- [ ] C5 Different representations of the same molecule must contain the same atoms joined in the same way, and every carbon must have four bonds (a double bond counts as two).

### 3. Traps

- Count the C=O carbon of an ester as planar and every CH₃ or CH₂ as tetrahedral; the bridging oxygen isn't a carbon.
- Cyclohexane carbons are tetrahedral (not planar like benzene).
- An expanded formula must show the O–H bond in alcohols and acids, not just "OH" on the carbon, when asked for "all the bonds".
- Isomers must have the same molecular formula: check by counting atoms.
- Skeletal formulae are not on the specification; use condensed and expanded formulae.
- Geometric (cis–trans) isomerism belongs to "Shapes, bonding and isomerism", not here.

### 4. What exams ask (topics only)

- Draw expanded structures showing all atoms and bonds; write condensed formulae.
- Identify carbon atoms in planar or tetrahedral geometry; state how many of each are in a molecule.
- Identify structural isomers in a list or reaction scheme; draw and name an isomer of a given compound.
- Define structural isomers.
