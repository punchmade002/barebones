# Package: chem20-s1 — The natural greenhouse effect

- **loId:** `chem20-s1` · **chapterId:** `chem20` · **syllabusRef:** 4.3.1
- **Syllabus:** 4.3.1 "describe the natural greenhouse effect and explain its significance".
  Learn about: "the significance of the carbon cycle in relation to natural and human-influenced
  climate change and sustainability". Normal type.
- **Prepared by:** a higher model, 2026-10-30. The carbon cycle (stores, sources, sinks, the
  balance and how human activity upsets it) and the mechanism of the natural greenhouse effect,
  with why only some gases absorb infrared. The individual greenhouse gases, global warming
  potentials, the evidence for the enhanced effect, its effects and solutions are in "The
  enhanced greenhouse effect"; name the gases here but don't repeat their sources or GWPs.

## 1. Must cover

The carbon cycle (one block)
- [ ] C1 The carbon cycle is the continuous movement of carbon between the atmosphere (mainly as CO₂), the oceans (dissolved CO₂, hydrogencarbonate and carbonate ions), living things (carbohydrates, proteins, fats), soils and peat, and rocks (limestone, fossil fuels).
- [ ] C2 Photosynthesis removes CO₂ from the air: 6CO₂(g) + 6H₂O(l) → C₆H₁₂O₆(aq) + 6O₂(g) (energy from sunlight, in green plants, algae and phytoplankton).
- [ ] C3 Respiration by plants, animals and microorganisms returns CO₂: C₆H₁₂O₆(aq) + 6O₂(g) → 6CO₂(g) + 6H₂O(l); decay (decomposition of dead matter by microorganisms) also releases CO₂ (and CH₄ where oxygen is absent).
- [ ] C4 CO₂ dissolves in the oceans and waterways: CO₂(g) ⇌ CO₂(aq); CO₂(aq) + H₂O(l) ⇌ H₂CO₃(aq); marine organisms use the carbonate to build calcium carbonate shells and skeletons, which over long periods form limestone.
- [ ] C5 Combustion of fuels releases CO₂, e.g. CH₄(g) + 2O₂(g) → CO₂(g) + 2H₂O(l); natural sources also include forest fires and volcanic eruptions.
- [ ] C6 Fossil fuels (coal, oil, natural gas) and peat formed from dead organisms buried over very long periods (peat over thousands of years, coal, oil and gas over millions) without enough oxygen to decay fully; they are long-term carbon stores.
- [ ] C7 Diagram: the carbon cycle with atmospheric CO₂ at the centre and labelled arrows for photosynthesis, respiration, decay, combustion, dissolving in the oceans, formation of shells and limestone, and formation of fossil fuels.

Sources, sinks and balance (one block)
- [ ] B1 A carbon source adds more CO₂ to the atmosphere than it removes (e.g. burning fossil fuels, respiration, decay, volcanoes, heating limestone in cement-making: CaCO₃(s) → CaO(s) + CO₂(g)).
- [ ] B2 A carbon sink removes more carbon from the atmosphere than it releases and stores it (e.g. growing forests, the oceans, undisturbed peatlands and wetlands, where waterlogged conditions stop dead plant material from decaying).
- [ ] B3 Before industrialisation the natural sources and sinks were roughly in balance, so the CO₂ concentration of the air stayed fairly steady (about 280 ppm).
- [ ] B4 Human activity has upset the balance: burning fossil fuels moves carbon from long-term stores into the air far faster than sinks can absorb it; deforestation and draining peatlands both remove sinks and release stored carbon; so atmospheric CO₂ has risen (to about 420 ppm in the mid-2020s).
- [ ] B5 Significance for climate change: CO₂ is a greenhouse gas, so a carbon cycle out of balance changes the climate (natural changes in the cycle over thousands of years have caused past climate changes; the present change is human-influenced and much faster).
- [ ] B6 Significance for sustainability: a sustainable way of living uses resources so that they are not used up and the environment is not damaged for future generations; for carbon this means keeping emissions in balance with what sinks can absorb, e.g. using renewable energy, protecting forests and peatlands.

The natural greenhouse effect (one block)
- [ ] G1 Energy from the Sun reaches the Earth mainly as visible light (shorter wavelength), with some ultraviolet and near-infrared; the atmosphere is largely transparent to visible light, so it passes through.
- [ ] G2 About 30% of incoming solar radiation is reflected back to space by clouds, ice and the surface; the rest is absorbed and warms the land and oceans.
- [ ] G3 The warmed Earth radiates energy back out as longer-wavelength infrared (IR) radiation (heat).
- [ ] G4 Greenhouse gases in the atmosphere absorb some of this IR and re-emit it in all directions, including back down towards the surface, so heat is kept in the lower atmosphere for longer.
- [ ] G5 This warming of the Earth's surface and lower atmosphere by naturally occurring greenhouse gases is the natural greenhouse effect.
- [ ] G6 The main natural greenhouse gases are water vapour (the largest contributor) and carbon dioxide, with smaller amounts of methane and dinitrogen oxide (N₂O); ozone also contributes.
- [ ] G7 The name is an analogy with a glass greenhouse, which lets sunlight in but keeps heat in; the analogy is not exact, since a real greenhouse works mainly by stopping warm air escaping.
- [ ] G8 Diagram: Sun, incoming short-wavelength radiation passing through the atmosphere, some reflected, IR emitted from the surface, some absorbed by a layer of greenhouse gases and re-emitted back down, some escaping to space.

Why some gases absorb infrared (one block)
- [ ] I1 Nitrogen (N₂, about 78% of dry air) and oxygen (O₂, about 21%) are not greenhouse gases; nor is argon: they do not absorb IR radiation.
- [ ] I2 Molecules absorb IR when the energy makes their bonds vibrate (stretch or bend) in a way that changes how the charge is spread across the molecule; molecules of two identical atoms (N₂, O₂) cannot do this, while H₂O, CO₂, CH₄ and N₂O, with three or more atoms and polar bonds, can.
- [ ] I3 Water vapour contributes most to the natural greenhouse effect because it is by far the most abundant greenhouse gas, but its concentration is set by temperature (evaporation and condensation), not directly by human emissions.

Significance (one block)
- [ ] S1 Without the natural greenhouse effect the Earth's average surface temperature would be about −18 °C instead of about +15 °C: oceans would freeze and life as we know it could not exist.
- [ ] S2 The natural greenhouse effect keeps water liquid over most of the planet and evens out the difference between day and night temperatures.
- [ ] S3 The natural effect is essential and beneficial; the problem is the extra warming from increased greenhouse gas concentrations (see "The enhanced greenhouse effect").
- [ ] S4 John Tyndall (1820–1893), born in Leighlinbridge, Co. Carlow, showed by experiment from 1859, at the Royal Institution in London, that water vapour and carbon dioxide absorb heat radiation while nitrogen and oxygen do not; this laid the experimental foundation for understanding the greenhouse effect. The Tyndall National Institute in Cork is named after him.

## 3. Traps

- The greenhouse effect is not the same as the ozone hole; the ozone layer and CFCs are not on the specification.
- Don't say Tyndall "discovered" or "first described" the greenhouse effect; say his experiments showed which gases absorb heat radiation.
- The gases re-emit IR in all directions; they don't "reflect" heat back.
- N₂ and O₂ are not greenhouse gases even though they make up 99% of dry air.
- The incoming radiation is mainly visible light; the outgoing radiation is infrared. Don't reverse them.
- Don't give global warming potentials or the gases' human sources here; they are in "The enhanced greenhouse effect".

## 4. What exams ask (topics only)

- What is the greenhouse effect; why is it essential for life on Earth?
- Name a greenhouse gas and a gas in the atmosphere that is not a greenhouse gas.
- Compare the contributions of CO₂ and water vapour to the natural greenhouse effect and account for the difference.
- The carbon cycle: how the carbon in fossil fuels reaches the atmosphere; a natural process that removes CO₂; why atmospheric CO₂ has risen over the last 200 years.
