## Part: pH titration curves (formerly `chem12-s3`)

- **loId:** `chem12-s3` · **chapterId:** `chem12` · **syllabusRef:** 4.1.3
- **Syllabus:** 4.1.3 "**investigate pH titration curves, using primary data from acid-base
  reactions, justifying appropriate indicators for each titration**". Learn about: "**issues
  around generation of curves for weak acid-weak base systems (limited to monoprotic and single
  inflection point curves)**". All bold: the whole subsection is Higher Level only. Mark every
  block heading " (Higher Level only)".
- **Prepared by:** a higher model, 2026-10-30. Investigation method, the four curve shapes
  (adding base from the burette to 25 cm³ of acid, all 0.1 M), choosing indicators, and the
  weak–weak issue. How indicators work is "How indicators work".

### 1. Must cover

Investigation: generating a pH curve (one block)
- [ ] I1 [HL] Aim: to plot how the pH changes as an alkali is added to an acid, using primary data, and to use the curve to choose an indicator.
- [ ] I2 [HL] Calibrate a pH meter (or pH sensor and data-logger) with buffer solutions of known pH.
- [ ] I3 [HL] Pipette 25.0 cm³ of the acid into a beaker with a magnetic stirrer (or swirl), put the pH probe in, and record the starting pH.
- [ ] I4 [HL] Add the alkali from a burette in 1 cm³ portions, recording the pH after each; near the equivalence point add 0.1–0.2 cm³ portions, because the pH changes rapidly there; continue well past it.
- [ ] I5 [HL] Plot pH (y-axis) against volume of alkali added (x-axis); the equivalence point is the midpoint of the steep (vertical) section.
- [ ] I6 [HL] Diagram: burette above a beaker on a magnetic stirrer, with a pH probe connected to a meter or data-logger.

The curve shapes (one block; one bullet per pair, all 0.1 M)
- [ ] C1 [HL] Strong acid–strong base (HCl and NaOH): starts at pH 1; rises slowly, then very steeply from about pH 3 to pH 11 at the equivalence point (pH 7); levels off near pH 13.
- [ ] C2 [HL] Weak acid–strong base (ethanoic acid and NaOH): starts higher (about pH 3); a gentler rise; the steep section is from about pH 7 to 11, with the equivalence point above 7 (about 8.7), because the ethanoate ion is a weak base.
- [ ] C3 [HL] Strong acid–weak base (HCl and aqueous ammonia): starts at pH 1; the steep section is from about pH 3 to 7, with the equivalence point below 7 (about 5), because the ammonium ion is a weak acid; levels off around pH 9–10.
- [ ] C4 [HL] Weak acid–weak base (ethanoic acid and ammonia): no steep section; the pH changes gradually through the equivalence point (about pH 7).
- [ ] C5 [HL] Diagram: the four curves sketched on pH–volume axes, with the steep sections and equivalence points marked, and the ranges of phenolphthalein and methyl orange shaded.

Justifying indicators (one block)
- [ ] J1 [HL] An indicator is suitable if its colour-change range lies completely within the steep section of the curve, so that one drop at the equivalence point changes its colour.
- [ ] J2 [HL] Strong acid–strong base: phenolphthalein (8.3–10) or methyl orange (3.1–4.4); both lie in the steep section.
- [ ] J3 [HL] Weak acid–strong base (e.g. vinegar with NaOH): phenolphthalein; methyl orange would change too early.
- [ ] J4 [HL] Strong acid–weak base (e.g. HCl with sodium carbonate or ammonia): methyl orange; phenolphthalein would change too early (in alkaline solution).

Weak acid–weak base: the issues (one block)
- [ ] W1 [HL] With no steep section, no indicator changes colour sharply at the equivalence point, so no indicator is suitable; the end point is unclear.
- [ ] W2 [HL] The curve has only a gentle point of inflection, which is hard to locate from the data; small errors in pH readings move it a lot.
- [ ] W3 [HL] The equivalence point is found instead from the curve itself (the point of steepest slope, located with a pH meter and many small additions), or such titrations are avoided.
- [ ] W4 [HL] The specification limits this to monoprotic acids and bases with a single inflection point.

### 2. Checked facts and examples

| Item | Fact |
|---|---|
| J2 | Phenolphthalein 8.3–10; methyl orange 3.1–4.4. |
| C2, C3 | Equivalence points for 0.1 M: ethanoic acid/NaOH about pH 8.7; HCl/NH₃ about pH 5.1–5.3 (use "about 5"). |

### 3. Traps

- The equivalence point is not always at pH 7.
- The steep section's range depends on concentration; use the approximate values given.
- Litmus is not used in titrations (its colour change is gradual).

### 4. What exams ask (topics only)

- Sketch a pH curve for a named titration; mark the equivalence point.
- Justify a choice of indicator from a pH curve.
- Explain why an indicator can't be used for a weak acid–weak base titration.

### 5. Practice questions to write

1. 12 marks: "Describe how you would obtain a pH curve for the titration of ethanoic acid with sodium hydroxide, sketch the curve, and use it to justify the choice of indicator." Model answer: I2–I5, C2, J1, J3.
2. 6 marks: "Explain why no indicator is suitable for the titration of a weak acid with a weak base." Model answer: C4, W1.

## Part: How indicators work (formerly `chem12-s4`)

- **loId:** `chem12-s4` · **chapterId:** `chem12` · **syllabusRef:** 4.1.4
- **Syllabus:** 4.1.4 "**explain how weak acid and weak base acid-base indicators function,
  using Le Châtelier's principle**". All bold: the whole subsection is Higher Level only. Mark
  every block heading " (Higher Level only)".
- **Prepared by:** a higher model, 2026-10-30. Le Châtelier's principle is taught in the
  chemical equilibrium chapter; state it once here and apply it.

### 1. Must cover

Indicators as weak acids (one block)
- [ ] A1 [HL] Many indicators are weak acids, written HIn, in which the undissociated molecule (HIn) and its conjugate base (In⁻) have different colours.
- [ ] A2 [HL] In water an equilibrium is set up: HIn(aq) ⇌ H⁺(aq) + In⁻(aq).
- [ ] A3 [HL] Le Châtelier's principle: if a system at equilibrium is disturbed, it shifts to oppose the change.
- [ ] A4 [HL] Adding acid increases [H⁺], so the equilibrium shifts to the left: most of the indicator is HIn, and its colour is seen.
- [ ] A5 [HL] Adding base removes H⁺ (H⁺ + OH⁻ → H₂O), so the equilibrium shifts to the right: most is In⁻, and its colour is seen.
- [ ] A6 [HL] Phenolphthalein: HIn is colourless and In⁻ is pink.
- [ ] A7 [HL] Methyl orange: HIn is red and In⁻ is yellow.

Indicators as weak bases (one block)
- [ ] B1 [HL] A weak base indicator can be written InOH, with InOH and In⁺ different colours: InOH(aq) ⇌ In⁺(aq) + OH⁻(aq).
- [ ] B2 [HL] Adding acid removes OH⁻, shifting the equilibrium to the right (In⁺ colour); adding base increases [OH⁻], shifting it to the left (InOH colour).

The colour change range (one block)
- [ ] R1 [HL] The colour change happens over a range of about two pH units, centred on the pH at which [HIn] = [In⁻].
- [ ] R2 [HL] At the midpoint of the range both colours are present (methyl orange looks orange).
- [ ] R3 [HL] Different indicators are weak acids of different strengths, so they change colour at different pH values (phenolphthalein 8.3–10; methyl orange 3.1–4.4); this is why the indicator must be chosen to match the titration.
- [ ] R4 [HL] Only a few drops of indicator are used, because the indicator is itself an acid and would react with some of the titrant.

### 2. Checked facts and examples

| Item | Fact |
|---|---|
| A6, A7, R3 | Phenolphthalein colourless/pink, 8.3–10; methyl orange red/yellow, 3.1–4.4. |

### 3. Traps

- Don't say the indicator "is" acidic or basic in colour; the colour depends on which form is in excess.
- Ka of indicators is not required.

### 4. What exams ask (topics only)

- Explain, using Le Châtelier's principle, why an indicator changes colour.

### 5. Practice questions to write

1. 9 marks: "Phenolphthalein is a weak acid, HIn; the HIn form is colourless and the In⁻ form is pink. Use Le Châtelier's principle to explain its colour in acidic and in basic solution." Model answer: A2–A6.
2. 6 marks: "Explain why only a few drops of indicator are used in a titration and why different indicators change colour at different pH values." Model answer: R4, R3.

## Part: Investigation: ethanoic acid in vinegar (formerly `chem12-s5`)

- **loId:** `chem12-s5` · **chapterId:** `chem12` · **syllabusRef:** 4.1.5
- **Syllabus:** 4.1.5 "investigate, using primary data, how to find the concentration of
  ethanoic acid in vinegar" (EI: Experimental Investigation). Normal type.
- **Prepared by:** a higher model, 2026-10-30. Full investigation: dilution, titration with
  standard sodium hydroxide and phenolphthalein, and a full sample calculation to mol L⁻¹,
  g L⁻¹ and %w/v. The titration technique is in "Standard solutions and acid–base titrations"; refer there rather than
  repeating each rinse.

### 1. Must cover

Aim and chemistry (one block)
- [ ] A1 Aim: to find the concentration of ethanoic acid in a commercial vinegar by titration with a standard solution of sodium hydroxide.
- [ ] A2 Equation: CH₃COOH(aq) + NaOH(aq) → CH₃COONa(aq) + H₂O(l); mole ratio 1 : 1.
- [ ] A3 The sodium hydroxide must first be standardised (it is not a primary standard; see "Standard solutions and acid–base titrations").

Method (one block, one step per bullet)
- [ ] M1 Dilute the vinegar ten times: pipette 25.0 cm³ of vinegar into a 250 cm³ volumetric flask, make up to the mark with deionised water and invert about 20 times.
- [ ] M2 Rinse the burette with deionised water and then with the standard sodium hydroxide solution, and fill it.
- [ ] M3 Rinse a pipette with deionised water and then with the diluted vinegar, and transfer 25.0 cm³ of diluted vinegar to a conical flask.
- [ ] M4 Add two or three drops of phenolphthalein.
- [ ] M5 Titrate with the sodium hydroxide, swirling, until the solution turns a permanent pale pink; do a rough titration, then accurate titrations until two agree within 0.1 cm³.

Why each choice (one block)
- [ ] W1 The vinegar is diluted because it is too concentrated: undiluted, it would need a very large titre of 0.1 M sodium hydroxide (more than a burette holds).
- [ ] W2 Phenolphthalein is used because this is a weak acid–strong base titration, with the equivalence point in the alkaline region (about pH 9); methyl orange would change colour too soon.
- [ ] W3 The sodium hydroxide is placed in the burette, so the colour change is from colourless to pink, which is easy to see.
- [ ] W4 Brown or coloured vinegar makes the end point hard to see; use white (clear) vinegar.

Sample calculation (one block, one step per bullet; sample data)
- [ ] C1 Mean titre: 20.8 cm³ of 0.100 M NaOH for 25.0 cm³ of diluted vinegar.
- [ ] C2 Moles NaOH = 0.100 × 0.0208 = 2.08 × 10⁻³ mol = moles of ethanoic acid in 25.0 cm³ of diluted vinegar.
- [ ] C3 Concentration of diluted vinegar = 2.08 × 10⁻³ ÷ 0.0250 = 0.0832 mol L⁻¹.
- [ ] C4 Concentration of original vinegar = 0.0832 × 10 = 0.832 mol L⁻¹.
- [ ] C5 In g L⁻¹: 0.832 × 60 (M of CH₃COOH) = 49.9 g L⁻¹.
- [ ] C6 As %w/v: 49.9 ÷ 10 = 4.99% w/v, close to the 5% typical of vinegar.

Safety and errors (one block)
- [ ] S1 Safety: sodium hydroxide is corrosive, especially to the eyes: wear goggles; use a pipette filler.
- [ ] S2 Errors: not rinsing the burette with NaOH (dilutes it, titre too high, result too high); overshooting the end point (too high); air bubble below the burette tap (wrong titre); the NaOH absorbing CO₂ from the air over time (changes its concentration; standardise just before use).
- [ ] S3 Diagram: burette of NaOH above a conical flask of diluted vinegar with phenolphthalein on a white tile; and the 250 cm³ volumetric flask used for the dilution.

### 2. Checked facts and examples

| Item | Fact |
|---|---|
| C5 | M(CH₃COOH) = 60 g mol⁻¹. |
| C6 | Commercial vinegar is typically about 4–6% ethanoic acid (often labelled 5%). |
| W2 | Equivalence point of ethanoic acid–NaOH ≈ pH 8.7. |

C1–C6 are sample data; arithmetic checked.

### 3. Traps

- Remember the dilution factor of 10 when finding the original concentration.
- Phenolphthalein, not methyl orange.
- The conical flask is not rinsed with vinegar.

### 4. What exams ask (topics only)

- Why is the vinegar diluted; how is it diluted accurately?
- Which indicator, and why; the colour change.
- Calculate the concentration of ethanoic acid in the original vinegar in mol L⁻¹, g L⁻¹ and %w/v.

### 5. Practice questions to write

1. 12 marks: "Describe how you would find the concentration of ethanoic acid in a sample of vinegar by titration with standard sodium hydroxide, including how the vinegar is diluted and the indicator used." Model answer: M1–M5, W1, W2.
2. 12 marks: "25.0 cm³ of vinegar was diluted to 250 cm³. 25.0 cm³ of the diluted vinegar required 18.5 cm³ of 0.120 M sodium hydroxide. Calculate the concentration of ethanoic acid in the original vinegar in mol L⁻¹ and as %w/v (sample data)." Model answer: moles NaOH = 2.22 × 10⁻³; diluted = 0.0888 M; original = 0.888 M; × 60 = 53.3 g L⁻¹ = 5.33% w/v.
