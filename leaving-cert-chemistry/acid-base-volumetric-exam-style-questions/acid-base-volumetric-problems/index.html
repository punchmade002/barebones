The titration method

Work through the steps in order; each step produces the quantity the next step needs. Units are as defined in "Solutions and concentration".

  • Step 1: Write the balanced equation and note the mole ratio of the two reactants.
  • Step 2: Calculate the moles of the substance whose concentration and volume are both known, using n = c × V with V in litres (cm³ ÷ 1000).
  • Step 3: Use the mole ratio to find the moles of the other substance.
  • Step 4: Divide those moles by the volume of that solution in litres to get its concentration in mol L⁻¹.
  • Step 5: Convert to the units asked for: multiply the concentration in mol L⁻¹ by the molar mass in g mol⁻¹ to get g L⁻¹, then allow for any dilution.
  • To give %w/v, divide the value in g L⁻¹ by 10, because %w/v is grams of solute per 100 cm³ of solution.

The titration formula

  • For the reaction aA + bB → products: (cA × VA) ÷ a = (cB × VB) ÷ b, where a and b are the coefficients in the balanced equation.
  • The volumes can be in cm³ on both sides, because the units cancel.
  • Example: for Na₂CO₃ + 2HCl, (c(Na₂CO₃) × V(Na₂CO₃)) ÷ 1 = (c(HCl) × V(HCl)) ÷ 2.
  • The formula gives the same answer as the step-by-step method, so show the working whichever route is used.

Dilution factors

  • If a sample was diluted before titration, for example 25.0 cm³ made up to 250 cm³, the titration gives the diluted concentration.
  • Multiply the diluted concentration by the dilution factor to get the original concentration: 250 ÷ 25.0 = 10.
  • If a solid was dissolved and made up to a volume, the titration of a portion gives the moles in that portion only.
  • Scale up by (total volume ÷ portion volume) to get the moles in the whole sample, for example × 10 for 25.0 cm³ out of 250 cm³.

Good practice in calculations

  • Use the mean of the concordant titres, not the rough titre.
  • Give answers to three significant figures with units, matching the precision of the titration data.
  • Check the answer is sensible: a dilute laboratory solution is usually 0.01–2 M, and a % purity cannot exceed 100%.

Unfamiliar contexts (Higher Level only)

  • An unfamiliar problem may involve an unusual substance, a back titration, finding a formula or percentage purity, or a substance used in everyday life; the same method applies.
  • Read the question for the balanced equation, or write it yourself, then list the known quantities with units.
  • Plan the route through moles before calculating anything.
  • Back titration: an excess of a known amount of reagent is added to the sample, for example acid added to an insoluble antacid, and the excess is then titrated.
  • Moles that reacted with the sample = moles added − moles in excess.
  • Back titration is used when the sample is insoluble or reacts slowly.

Problem 1: standardising hydrochloric acid

Each problem follows the method above. All data are sample data.

  • Data: 25.0 cm³ of 0.0500 M sodium carbonate was titrated with hydrochloric acid, using methyl orange.
  • Data: the mean titre of hydrochloric acid was 24.2 cm³.
  • Equation: Na₂CO₃ + 2HCl → 2NaCl + H₂O + CO₂.
  • Moles Na₂CO₃ = 0.0500 mol L⁻¹ × 0.0250 L = 1.25 × 10⁻³ mol.
  • Moles HCl = 2 × 1.25 × 10⁻³ mol = 2.50 × 10⁻³ mol, since Na₂CO₃ : HCl = 1 : 2.
  • Concentration of HCl = 2.50 × 10⁻³ mol ÷ 0.0242 L = 0.103 mol L⁻¹.

Problem 2: ammonia in a household cleaner

  • Data: 25.0 cm³ of a cleaner was diluted to 250 cm³.
  • Data: 25.0 cm³ of the diluted solution needed 21.5 cm³ of 0.100 M hydrochloric acid, using methyl orange.
  • Equation: NH₃ + HCl → NH₄Cl, a 1 : 1 reaction.
  • Moles HCl = 0.100 mol L⁻¹ × 0.0215 L = 2.15 × 10⁻³ mol, which equals the moles of NH₃ in 25.0 cm³ of diluted cleaner.
  • Diluted concentration = 2.15 × 10⁻³ mol ÷ 0.0250 L = 0.0860 mol L⁻¹.
  • Original concentration = 0.0860 mol L⁻¹ × 10 = 0.860 mol L⁻¹, because the dilution factor is 250 ÷ 25.0 = 10.
  • Mass concentration of ammonia = 0.860 mol L⁻¹ × 17 g mol⁻¹ = 14.6 g L⁻¹.

Problem 3: water of crystallisation in washing soda (Higher Level only)

  • Data: 3.58 g of washing soda crystals, Na₂CO₃·xH₂O, were dissolved and made up to 250 cm³.
  • Data: 25.0 cm³ portions needed a mean titre of 25.0 cm³ of 0.100 M hydrochloric acid.
  • Moles HCl = 0.100 mol L⁻¹ × 0.0250 L = 2.50 × 10⁻³ mol.
  • Moles Na₂CO₃ in 25.0 cm³ = 2.50 × 10⁻³ mol ÷ 2 = 1.25 × 10⁻³ mol, since Na₂CO₃ : HCl = 1 : 2.
  • Moles Na₂CO₃ in 250 cm³ = 1.25 × 10⁻³ mol × 10 = 1.25 × 10⁻² mol.
  • Mass of Na₂CO₃ = 1.25 × 10⁻² mol × 106 g mol⁻¹ = 1.325 g.
  • Mass of water = 3.58 g − 1.325 g = 2.255 g.
  • Moles of water = 2.255 g ÷ 18 g mol⁻¹ = 0.125 mol.
  • Ratio of water to Na₂CO₃ = 0.125 ÷ 0.0125 = 10, so x = 10 and the formula is Na₂CO₃·10H₂O.

Problem 4: a back titration of an antacid tablet (Higher Level only)

  • Data: an antacid tablet containing calcium carbonate was added to 50.0 cm³ of 1.00 M hydrochloric acid, an excess.
  • Data: when the tablet had reacted, the remaining acid needed 30.0 cm³ of 0.500 M sodium hydroxide.
  • Moles HCl added = 1.00 mol L⁻¹ × 0.0500 L = 0.0500 mol.
  • Moles HCl in excess = moles NaOH, because they react 1 : 1; moles NaOH = 0.500 mol L⁻¹ × 0.0300 L = 0.0150 mol.
  • Moles HCl that reacted with the tablet = 0.0500 mol − 0.0150 mol = 0.0350 mol.
  • Equation: CaCO₃ + 2HCl → CaCl₂ + H₂O + CO₂.
  • Moles CaCO₃ = 0.0350 mol ÷ 2 = 0.0175 mol.
  • Mass of calcium carbonate per tablet = 0.0175 mol × 100 g mol⁻¹ = 1.75 g.
  • A back titration is needed because calcium carbonate is insoluble and reacts too slowly to titrate directly.

Key terms

Titre
The volume of solution delivered from the burette at the end point of a titration.
Concordant titres
Titre readings that agree closely with one another and are averaged to give the final result.
Rough titre
A quick first titration done to find the approximate end point; it is not used in the mean.
Mole ratio
The ratio of the amounts in moles of substances in a balanced equation, given by the coefficients.
Dilution factor
The final volume divided by the volume of the portion taken, e.g. 250 ÷ 25.0 = 10.
Titration formula
For aA + bB → products, (cA × VA) ÷ a = (cB × VB) ÷ b, where a and b are the coefficients.
Back titration
A titration in which an excess of a known reagent reacts with the sample and the leftover reagent is then titrated.
%w/v
The mass of solute in grams per 100 cm³ of solution; its value is one-tenth of the value in g L⁻¹.
Standard solution
A solution of accurately known concentration, prepared by weighing a pure solid and making it up to a known volume.
Standardise
To find the exact concentration of a solution by titrating it against a standard solution.
Water of crystallisation
Water molecules held in the crystal of a hydrated salt and written after a dot, e.g. Na₂CO₃·10H₂O.
Scale factor
The total volume divided by the volume of the portion titrated, used to scale the moles in a portion up to the whole sample.
Excess reagent
Reagent added in more than the amount needed, so that the sample reacts completely.
Mean titre
The average of concordant titres, used in calculations in place of a single reading.
