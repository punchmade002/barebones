Changes of state

  • To melt or boil a molecular substance, energy must be supplied to overcome the intermolecular forces.
  • The stronger the intermolecular forces, the higher the melting and boiling points.
  • Substances with only London dispersion forces between small molecules are gases at room temperature, e.g. methane, which boils at −162 °C.
  • Group 14 hydrides rise steadily in boiling point: CH₄ < SiH₄ < GeH₄ < SnH₄.
  • Hydrogen bonding gives unusually high boiling points: water boils at 100 °C, but hydrogen sulfide, a heavier molecule with no hydrogen bonding, boils at −60 °C.

Solubility

  • A substance dissolves when the attractions between solvent and solute particles are similar to or stronger than the attractions they replace: “like dissolves like”.
  • Polar and hydrogen-bonding substances such as ethanol, ammonia and sugar dissolve in water, because they form hydrogen bonds with water molecules.
  • Non-polar substances such as iodine, hexane and oils do not dissolve in water, but dissolve in non-polar solvents such as cyclohexane, where London forces are similar.
  • Hexane and water do not mix: they form two layers.

Water: properties from hydrogen bonding

  • Water has a high boiling point and a high melting point for such a small molecule.
  • Ice is less dense than liquid water: hydrogen bonds hold the molecules in an open lattice, so ice floats.
  • Water is a good solvent for ionic and polar substances.

Secondary data: boiling points

  • Noble gas boiling points: He −269 °C; Ne −246 °C; Ar −186 °C; Kr −153 °C; Xe −108 °C.
  • Halogens at room temperature: fluorine and chlorine are gases, bromine is a liquid and iodine is a solid.
  • Alkane boiling points: methane −162 °C; ethane −89 °C; propane −42 °C; butane −1 °C; pentane 36 °C; hexane 69 °C.
  • Group 16 hydride boiling points: H₂O 100 °C; H₂S −60 °C; H₂Se −41 °C; H₂Te −2 °C.
  • Group 15 and 17 hydride boiling points: NH₃ −33 °C vs PH₃ −88 °C; HF 20 °C vs HCl −85 °C.
  • Compounds of similar mass: ethanol (bp 78 °C, hydrogen bonding) vs propane (bp −42 °C, London forces only).

Accounting for trends (Higher Level only)

  • Down group 18, down group 17 and along the alkane series, boiling points rise because the molecules have more electrons and larger electron clouds, so London dispersion forces get stronger.
  • In the group 16 hydrides, boiling point rises from H₂S to H₂Te as London forces increase with size; water is far higher than the trend predicts because of hydrogen bonding.
  • The same anomaly appears for NH₃ in group 15 and HF in group 17, which both form hydrogen bonds.
  • Molecules of similar mass have higher boiling points if they are polar (dipole–dipole forces), and much higher ones if they hydrogen bond, e.g. ethanol compared with propane.
  • Diagram a graph of boiling point against period for the hydrides of groups 14, 15, 16 and 17, showing the high values of H₂O, HF and NH₃ and the steady rise from CH₄ to SnH₄.

Polar bonds and polar molecules (Higher Level only)

The shapes named here are given; see “VSEPR theory” for how shapes are predicted.

  • A polar bond has an uneven share of electrons, giving δ+ and δ− ends.
  • A polar molecule has an overall uneven distribution of charge, with a δ+ end and a δ− end.
  • Whether a molecule is polar depends on both its bonds and its shape: polar bonds arranged symmetrically cancel out.

Symmetry and cancelling dipoles (Higher Level only)

  • Carbon dioxide: each C=O bond is polar, but the molecule is linear (O=C=O), so the two bond polarities point in opposite directions and cancel; CO₂ is non-polar.
  • Tetrachloromethane (CCl₄): four polar C–Cl bonds arranged tetrahedrally cancel; CCl₄ is non-polar.
  • Boron trifluoride (BF₃): three polar B–F bonds at 120° in a trigonal plane cancel; BF₃ is non-polar.
  • Water: two polar O–H bonds in a V-shaped (bent) molecule do not cancel; water is polar, with δ− on oxygen.
  • Ammonia: three polar N–H bonds in a pyramidal molecule do not cancel; NH₃ is polar.
  • Trichloromethane (CHCl₃): the C–H bond differs from the three C–Cl bonds, so the tetrahedral molecule is unsymmetrical and polar.
  • Diagram CO₂, CCl₄, H₂O and NH₃, with arrows (or δ+ and δ− labels) on each bond showing whether the polarities cancel; label each molecule polar or non-polar.

Effect on intermolecular forces (Higher Level only)

  • Polar molecules attract each other by permanent dipole–dipole forces (or hydrogen bonds) as well as London dispersion forces, so they have stronger intermolecular forces than non-polar molecules of similar size.
  • Non-polar molecules, including symmetrical ones with polar bonds, have only London dispersion forces between them: carbon dioxide is a gas at room temperature, and tetrachloromethane is immiscible with water.
  • A symmetrical molecule usually has a lower boiling point and lower solubility in water than an unsymmetrical one of similar size.
  • Tetrachloromethane is immiscible with water, while trichloromethane is slightly soluble; both dissolve in non-polar solvents.

Shape decides overall polarity

  • A molecule with polar bonds is polar overall only if its shape is unsymmetrical, so that its charge is unevenly spread.
  • Water is V-shaped (bent), so it is polar.
  • Ammonia is pyramidal, so it is polar.
  • Carbon dioxide is linear and methane is tetrahedral: both are non-polar.

Predicting physical properties

  • Polar molecules have stronger forces between them than non-polar molecules of similar size, so they have higher boiling points.
  • Polar molecules tend to dissolve in polar solvents such as water; non-polar molecules dissolve in non-polar solvents such as cyclohexane.
  • Water is a liquid at room temperature and dissolves salts, because it is polar and forms hydrogen bonds; methane is a gas and is almost insoluble in water.
  • Ammonia, which is polar, is very soluble in water; carbon dioxide, which is non-polar, is only slightly soluble.

Investigation: testing liquids for polarity with a charged rod

  • Aim: to show whether a liquid is made of polar molecules.
  • Apparatus: a burette clamped above a beaker, a polythene (or acetate) rod, a cloth, and liquids such as water and cyclohexane.
  • Diagram a burette clamped above a beaker, releasing a thin stream of liquid; label the burette, the beaker and the stream.
  • Diagram a charged polythene rod held beside the stream, with the stream bending towards it; label the rod, the stream and the direction of deflection.
  • Independent variable: the liquid tested.
  • Dependent variable: whether the stream is deflected.
  • Controlled variables: the same rod and cloth, with the rod recharged before each test.
  • Step 1: Charge the rod by rubbing it with the cloth.
  • Step 2: Let a thin stream of the liquid run from the burette, and hold the rod close to the stream without touching it.
  • Step 3: Repeat with the other liquid, recharging the rod each time.
  • Result: the stream of water is deflected (attracted) towards the rod; the stream of cyclohexane is not deflected.
  • The water stream is attracted to the rod whether the rod is charged positively or negatively, because the molecules rotate.
  • Explanation: polar molecules turn so that their oppositely charged end faces the rod and are attracted to it; non-polar molecules are not attracted.
  • Safety: cyclohexane is flammable and harmful, so use no flames, small volumes and good ventilation, and collect it for disposal.

Key terms

Boiling point
The temperature at which a liquid's vapour pressure equals the atmospheric pressure and it boils.
Solubility
The extent to which a solute dissolves in a solvent at a stated temperature.
Like dissolves like
The principle that substances dissolve readily in solvents with similar types of intermolecular forces.
Solvent
The liquid in which a solute dissolves to form a solution.
Solute
The substance that dissolves in a solvent.
Hydrogen-bonding substance
A substance whose molecules contain H bonded to F, O or N, so that they form hydrogen bonds.
Open lattice
The spaced-out arrangement of molecules in ice, held by hydrogen bonds, that makes ice less dense than liquid water.
Immiscible
Describes two liquids that do not mix and form separate layers, such as hexane and water.
Polar bond
A covalent bond in which the shared electrons are drawn unevenly, giving δ+ and δ− ends.
Polar molecule
A molecule with an overall uneven distribution of charge, so that it has a δ+ end and a δ− end.
Non-polar molecule
A molecule whose charge is evenly distributed overall, either because it has no polar bonds or because its polar bonds cancel.
Cancelling dipoles
Polar bonds whose polarities point in opposite or balancing directions, so that they give no overall polarity.
Symmetrical molecule
A molecule whose shape is arranged so that its bond polarities balance, such as a linear or tetrahedral molecule with identical bonds.
Trigonal planar
A flat shape in which three bonded atoms lie at 120° to one another around a central atom.
Polar solvent
A solvent made of polar molecules, such as water, that dissolves ionic and polar substances.
Non-polar solvent
A solvent made of non-polar molecules, such as cyclohexane, that dissolves non-polar substances.
V-shaped (bent) molecule
A molecule with two bonded atoms at an angle to a central atom, such as water.
Pyramidal molecule
A molecule with three bonded atoms arranged in a pyramid above a central atom, such as ammonia.
Tetrahedral molecule
A molecule with four bonded atoms arranged at the corners of a tetrahedron around a central atom, such as methane.
Linear molecule
A molecule whose atoms lie in a straight line, such as carbon dioxide.
