Electronegativity

Electronegativity measures how strongly an atom attracts the shared pair of electrons in a covalent bond. The continuum from ionic to non-polar covalent bonding is introduced in “Types of chemical bonding”.

  • Electronegativity is the relative attraction that an atom in a molecule has for the shared pair of electrons in a covalent bond.
  • It is measured on the Pauling scale, on which fluorine, the most electronegative element, has the highest value of 3.98 (about 4); Pauling values have no units.
  • Pauling values, highest to lowest: F 3.98; O 3.44; Cl 3.16; N 3.04; Br 2.96; I 2.66; S 2.58; C 2.55; H 2.20; P 2.19; Si 1.90; Al 1.61; Mg 1.31; Ca 1.00; Li 0.98; Na 0.93; K 0.82.
  • Non-metals have high electronegativities and metals have low ones.

Trends across periods and down groups

  • Across a period (left to right) electronegativity increases, e.g. from Na 0.93 to Cl 3.16.
  • Across a period the nuclear charge increases while electrons are added to the same main energy level, so the atomic radius decreases.
  • Because the atomic radius decreases across a period, the nucleus attracts the bonding electrons more strongly.
  • Down a group electronegativity decreases, e.g. from F 3.98 to I 2.66.
  • Down a group each element has an extra energy level, so the atomic radius increases.
  • Inner electrons screen (shield) the outer electrons from the nucleus; the bonding electrons are further away and less strongly attracted, even though the nuclear charge is greater.
  • Noble gases are usually given no electronegativity value because they rarely form bonds.

Predicting bond type: method and worked examples

The difference in electronegativity between two bonded atoms predicts the bond type. The cut-offs below are guides, not sharp boundaries, because bonding is a continuum.

  • Step 1: Look up the Pauling electronegativity of each atom.
  • Step 2: Subtract the smaller value from the larger to find the difference.
  • Step 3: Compare the difference with the cut-offs below.
  • The larger the difference, the more ionic the bond.
  • A difference greater than 1.7 gives a mainly ionic bond, e.g. NaCl: 3.16 − 0.93 = 2.23.
  • A difference between about 0.4 and 1.7 gives a polar covalent bond, e.g. H–Cl: 3.16 − 2.20 = 0.96; O–H: 3.44 − 2.20 = 1.24.
  • A difference less than about 0.4 gives a non-polar (pure) covalent bond, e.g. C–H: 2.55 − 2.20 = 0.35.
  • A bond between identical atoms has a difference of zero and is non-polar, e.g. Cl–Cl: 3.16 − 3.16 = 0.
  • In a polar bond the more electronegative atom carries the partial negative charge (δ−) and the less electronegative atom carries the partial positive charge (δ+).

Ionic compounds

  • Ionic compounds are solids at room temperature, e.g. sodium chloride and magnesium oxide.
  • Sodium chloride melts at 801 °C and boils at 1413 °C.
  • The melting and boiling points are high because a lot of energy is needed to overcome the strong electrostatic attractions between oppositely charged ions throughout the lattice.
  • Solid ionic compounds do not conduct electricity, because the ions are held in fixed positions.
  • Molten ionic compounds, and ionic compounds dissolved in water, conduct electricity, because the ions are free to move and carry charge.
  • Many ionic compounds are soluble in water, because polar water molecules attract and surround the ions.
  • Ionic compounds are insoluble in non-polar solvents such as cyclohexane.

Covalent (molecular) compounds

  • Many covalent compounds are gases or liquids at room temperature, e.g. methane, carbon dioxide and water.
  • Some covalent compounds are solids with low melting points, e.g. iodine and sugar.
  • Iodine sublimes on gentle heating and melts at 114 °C.
  • Covalent compounds have low melting and boiling points, because only the weak forces between molecules have to be overcome.
  • The covalent bonds inside the molecules do not break when a covalent substance melts or boils: methane boils at −162 °C.
  • Covalent compounds do not conduct electricity, because there are no ions or free electrons to carry charge.
  • Some covalent compounds, such as hydrogen chloride, form ions when they dissolve in water and then conduct.
  • Non-polar molecules such as methane and iodine are insoluble or only slightly soluble in water, but dissolve in non-polar solvents.
  • Polar molecules such as ethanol, sugar and ammonia dissolve in water; see “Intermolecular forces and physical properties”.
  • “Like dissolves like” sums up covalent solubility: polar molecules dissolve in water and non-polar molecules dissolve in non-polar solvents.

Comparing ionic and covalent compounds

  • Melting and boiling points: ionic compounds are high; covalent compounds are low.
  • Electrical conductivity: ionic compounds conduct when molten or dissolved; covalent compounds generally do not conduct.
  • Solubility: ionic compounds are usually soluble in water; covalent compounds are usually soluble in non-polar solvents.
  • State at room temperature: ionic compounds are solids; covalent compounds are often gases or liquids.
  • Step 1: Melt the substance, or dissolve it in water.
  • Step 2: Dip two carbon electrodes into it, connected to a battery and a bulb (or an ammeter).
  • Step 3: A lit bulb, or a current on the ammeter, shows that ions are present and free to carry charge.
  • Diagram a conductivity circuit with a battery, a bulb and two carbon electrodes dipping into a beaker of solution; label the battery, bulb, electrodes and beaker.
  • Exception: giant covalent substances such as diamond and graphite have very high melting points, because strong covalent bonds must be broken; see the chapter “Allotropes of Carbon. Hydrocarbons”.

Intramolecular bonding and intermolecular forces

  • Intramolecular bonding is the strong covalent bonding within a molecule, holding its atoms together.
  • Intermolecular forces are the weaker forces of attraction between separate molecules.
  • Intermolecular forces are much weaker than covalent bonds: boiling water overcomes the forces between H₂O molecules but does not break the O–H bonds.
  • Van der Waals forces are the collective name for the intermolecular forces: London dispersion forces and permanent dipole–dipole forces (including hydrogen bonding).
  • Diagram two water molecules, with the O–H covalent bonds labelled “intramolecular” and the attraction between an H of one molecule and the O of the other labelled “intermolecular (hydrogen bond)”.

London dispersion forces

  • London dispersion forces act between all molecules, and are the only intermolecular forces between non-polar molecules, e.g. H₂, Cl₂, CH₄ and noble gas atoms.
  • They arise because electrons are always moving: at any instant the electron cloud may be unevenly spread, giving a temporary (instantaneous) dipole.
  • The temporary dipole induces a dipole in a neighbouring molecule, and the two attract.
  • London dispersion forces are the weakest intermolecular forces for small molecules, but they get stronger as molecules get larger and have more electrons.

Permanent dipole–dipole forces

  • A polar molecule has a permanent dipole: a δ+ end and a δ− end; see “Electronegativity and bond type”.
  • Dipole–dipole forces are attractions between the δ+ end of one polar molecule and the δ− end of a neighbouring one, e.g. between HCl molecules (Hδ+–Clδ−).
  • Dipole–dipole forces are stronger than London dispersion forces between molecules of similar size.

Hydrogen bonding

  • A hydrogen bond is a particularly strong dipole–dipole attraction between a hydrogen atom bonded to a small, very electronegative atom (F, O or N) in one molecule and a lone pair on an F, O or N atom of a neighbouring molecule.
  • It is strong because the H–F, O–H and N–H bonds are very polar, leaving the small hydrogen atom strongly δ+.
  • A hydrogen bond is between molecules, not a covalent bond to hydrogen.
  • Hydrogen bonding needs H bonded directly to N, O or F: there is no hydrogen bonding between CH₄ molecules or in HCl.
  • Examples: water (H₂O), ammonia (NH₃), hydrogen fluoride (HF), alcohols such as ethanol, and carboxylic acids.
  • Hydrogen bonds are the strongest intermolecular forces.
  • Diagram water molecules with δ+ and δ− labelled, and hydrogen bonds drawn as dashed lines from H to the lone pair on O of the next molecule.

Ion–dipole forces (Higher Level only)

  • An ion–dipole force is the attraction between an ion and the oppositely charged end of a polar molecule.
  • When sodium chloride dissolves in water, the δ− oxygen ends of water molecules are attracted to Na⁺ ions and the δ+ hydrogen ends to Cl⁻ ions.
  • The ions become surrounded by water molecules (hydrated).
  • Ion–dipole forces are stronger than dipole–dipole forces; they explain why ionic compounds dissolve in polar solvents such as water.
  • Diagram a Na⁺ ion surrounded by water molecules with their O atoms pointing towards it, and a Cl⁻ ion surrounded by water molecules with their H atoms pointing towards it.

Order of strength

  • For molecules of similar size, London dispersion forces are weaker than dipole–dipole forces, which are weaker than hydrogen bonds.
  • All intermolecular forces are much weaker than covalent and ionic bonds.

Key terms

Electronegativity
The relative attraction that an atom in a molecule has for the shared pair of electrons in a covalent bond.
Pauling scale
The scale of electronegativity values, unitless, on which fluorine has the highest value of 3.98.
Screening (shielding)
The reduction in attraction between the nucleus and outer electrons caused by inner electrons.
Atomic radius
The size of an atom, measured from its nucleus to its outermost electrons.
Ionic bond
A mainly electrostatic attraction between oppositely charged ions, formed when the electronegativity difference is large.
Polar covalent bond
A covalent bond in which the shared electrons are drawn unevenly towards the more electronegative atom.
Non-polar covalent bond
A covalent bond in which the shared electrons are shared equally, because the two atoms have the same or nearly the same electronegativity.
Partial charge (δ+ and δ−)
A small positive (δ+) or negative (δ−) charge on an atom in a polar bond, shown with the symbol δ.
Ionic compound
A compound held together by electrostatic attractions between oppositely charged ions, usually a metal and a non-metal.
Covalent (molecular) compound
A compound made of molecules in which atoms are held together by covalent bonds, with only weak forces between molecules.
Electrostatic attraction
The force of attraction between oppositely charged ions.
Lattice
The regular three-dimensional arrangement of ions in a solid ionic compound.
Electrical conductivity
The ability of a substance to carry an electric current, which needs free ions or free electrons.
Solubility
The mass of a solute that dissolves in a given volume of solvent at a stated temperature.
Sublimation
The change of state from solid directly to gas on heating, without passing through the liquid state.
Non-polar solvent
A solvent made of non-polar molecules, such as cyclohexane, that dissolves non-polar substances.
Intramolecular bonding
The strong covalent bonds that hold the atoms of a molecule together.
Intermolecular forces
The weaker forces of attraction between separate molecules.
Van der Waals forces
The collective name for London dispersion forces and permanent dipole–dipole forces.
London dispersion forces
Weak attractions between all molecules, caused by temporary dipoles that induce dipoles in neighbouring molecules.
Permanent dipole
An uneven distribution of charge in a polar molecule, giving a δ+ end and a δ− end.
Dipole–dipole forces
Attractions between the δ+ end of one polar molecule and the δ− end of a neighbouring polar molecule.
Hydrogen bond
A strong dipole–dipole attraction between a hydrogen atom bonded to F, O or N and a lone pair on an F, O or N atom of a neighbouring molecule.
Ion–dipole force
The attraction between an ion and the oppositely charged end of a polar molecule.
Hydrated ion
An ion surrounded by water molecules, held by ion–dipole forces.
