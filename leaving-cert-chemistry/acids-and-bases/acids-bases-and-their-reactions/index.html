Properties of acids

Acids show a common set of properties that can be observed in the laboratory and at home.

  • Acids taste sour; laboratory chemicals must never be tasted.
  • Acids turn blue litmus red and have a pH below 7.
  • Acids react with reactive metals such as magnesium and zinc to give hydrogen gas.
  • Acids react with carbonates to give carbon dioxide, seen as fizzing.
  • Acids neutralise bases.
  • Examples: hydrochloric acid (HCl), sulfuric acid (H₂SO₄), nitric acid (HNO₃), ethanoic acid (CH₃COOH, in vinegar) and citric acid (in citrus fruits).

Properties of bases

  • Bases taste bitter and feel soapy or slippery, because they react with the oils on the skin.
  • Bases turn red litmus blue and have a pH above 7.
  • Bases neutralise acids.
  • An alkali is a base that dissolves in water, e.g. sodium hydroxide (NaOH), potassium hydroxide (KOH) and aqueous ammonia (NH₃).
  • Many bases are insoluble in water, e.g. copper(II) oxide and magnesium hydroxide, so they are not alkalis.
  • Concentrated alkalis are corrosive: sodium hydroxide is the main ingredient of oven cleaners and drain cleaners.

Acid–base indicators

An indicator is a substance that changes colour depending on whether it is in an acidic or a basic solution.

  • Litmus is red in acid and blue in base.
  • Phenolphthalein is colourless in acid and pink in base; it changes colour between pH 8.3 and pH 10.
  • Methyl orange is red in acid and yellow in base; it changes colour between pH 3.1 and pH 4.4.
  • Universal indicator is a mixture of indicators that gives a range of colours across the pH scale: red in strong acid, through orange, yellow and green at pH 7, to blue and purple in strong base.
  • Natural indicators, such as red cabbage extract, also change colour between acidic and basic solutions.

Everyday acids and bases

Common substances are classed as acids or bases according to the properties they show.

  • Vinegar is an acid: it contains ethanoic acid, tastes sour, turns blue litmus red and fizzes with baking soda.
  • Citric juices are acids: lemon juice contains citric acid, tastes sour and has a pH of about 2–3; orange juice has a pH of about 3–4.
  • Antacids are bases: they contain magnesium hydroxide, aluminium hydroxide, calcium carbonate or sodium hydrogencarbonate, and neutralise excess stomach acid.
  • Toothpaste is mildly basic, so it neutralises the acids that bacteria in plaque produce, which would otherwise attack tooth enamel.
  • Baking soda (sodium hydrogencarbonate) is a weak base.
  • Oven cleaner (sodium hydroxide) is a strong alkali.
  • Lemonade and cola are acids: they contain citric, carbonic or phosphoric acid.

Neutralisation in everyday life

Neutralisation is the reaction of an acid with a base to form a salt and water; the acid and base cancel out each other's properties.

  • Lime in agriculture: crops grow poorly in acidic soil, so farmers spread lime (calcium oxide or calcium hydroxide) or ground limestone (calcium carbonate) to neutralise the acid and raise the soil pH.
  • Ground limestone in soil: CaCO₃ + 2H⁺ → Ca²⁺ + H₂O + CO₂.
  • Acid indigestion: the stomach contains hydrochloric acid, and excess acid causes indigestion and heartburn.
  • Antacid tablets neutralise the excess acid, e.g. Mg(OH)₂ + 2HCl → MgCl₂ + 2H₂O.
  • Sodium hydrogencarbonate in an antacid also neutralises stomach acid: NaHCO₃ + HCl → NaCl + H₂O + CO₂.
  • Lime is added to acidic lakes to neutralise the acid.

Salts

Acid–base reactions produce salts, and the name of a salt comes from the acid and the metal.

  • A salt is a compound formed when the hydrogen of an acid is replaced by a metal ion or an ammonium ion.
  • Hydrochloric acid gives chlorides, e.g. NaCl.
  • Sulfuric acid gives sulfates; nitric acid gives nitrates; ethanoic acid gives ethanoates; carbonic acid gives carbonates.
  • Sulfuric acid is diprotic, with two replaceable hydrogens, so one mole of it reacts with twice as much sodium hydroxide as one mole of hydrochloric acid does.

Acid + base (neutralisation)

General word equation: acid + base → salt + water.

  • HCl(aq) + NaOH(aq) → NaCl(aq) + H₂O(l)
  • H₂SO₄(aq) + 2KOH(aq) → K₂SO₄(aq) + 2H₂O(l)
  • With an insoluble metal oxide: 2HCl(aq) + CuO(s) → CuCl₂(aq) + H₂O(l); the black solid dissolves to give a blue solution.
  • With ammonia: NH₃(aq) + HCl(aq) → NH₄Cl(aq); this forms an ammonium salt and no water.
  • Ionic equation for any strong acid and strong alkali: H⁺(aq) + OH⁻(aq) → H₂O(l).
  • Neutralisation is exothermic, so it releases heat.

Acid + metal

General word equation: acid + metal → salt + hydrogen. This applies to metals above hydrogen in reactivity, such as magnesium, zinc and iron.

  • Mg(s) + 2HCl(aq) → MgCl₂(aq) + H₂(g): the magnesium disappears, the mixture fizzes and the flask gets warm.
  • Zn(s) + H₂SO₄(aq) → ZnSO₄(aq) + H₂(g).
  • Copper does not react with dilute hydrochloric or sulfuric acid, because it is below hydrogen in the electrochemical series.
  • Test for hydrogen: a lighted splint held at the mouth of the tube gives a squeaky pop.

Acid + carbonate or hydrogencarbonate

General word equation: acid + carbonate → salt + water + carbon dioxide.

  • CaCO₃(s) + 2HCl(aq) → CaCl₂(aq) + H₂O(l) + CO₂(g); acids react with carbonates even when the carbonate is insoluble, as calcium carbonate is.
  • Na₂CO₃(aq) + H₂SO₄(aq) → Na₂SO₄(aq) + H₂O(l) + CO₂(g).
  • Hydrogencarbonates react the same way: NaHCO₃(s) + HCl(aq) → NaCl(aq) + H₂O(l) + CO₂(g).
  • Test for carbon dioxide: it turns limewater milky, Ca(OH)₂(aq) + CO₂(g) → CaCO₃(s) + H₂O(l).

Predicting products

Use this method to write the equation for any reaction of an acid.

  • Step 1: Identify the reaction type from the other reactant: a base, a metal, or a carbonate or hydrogencarbonate.
  • Step 2: Name the salt from the metal (or ammonium) ion and the acid.
  • Step 3: Add the other products: water for a base; hydrogen for a metal; water and carbon dioxide for a carbonate or hydrogencarbonate.
  • Step 4: Balance the equation.
  • Example: magnesium oxide and nitric acid give MgO(s) + 2HNO₃(aq) → Mg(NO₃)₂(aq) + H₂O(l), magnesium nitrate.
  • Example: potassium carbonate and ethanoic acid give K₂CO₃ + 2CH₃COOH → 2CH₃COOK + H₂O + CO₂, potassium ethanoate.

Key terms

Acid
A substance with a pH below 7 that tastes sour, turns blue litmus red and neutralises bases.
Base
A substance with a pH above 7 that turns red litmus blue, tastes bitter and neutralises acids.
Alkali
A base that dissolves in water.
Indicator
A substance that changes colour depending on whether it is in an acidic or a basic solution.
Universal indicator
A mixture of indicators that gives a range of colours across the pH scale.
Neutralisation
The reaction of an acid with a base to form a salt and water, in which the acid and base cancel out each other's properties.
Antacid
A base that neutralises excess stomach acid, e.g. magnesium hydroxide.
Corrosive
Able to destroy or burn skin and other materials, as concentrated acids and alkalis are.
Salt
A compound formed when the hydrogen of an acid is replaced by a metal ion or an ammonium ion.
Diprotic acid
An acid with two replaceable hydrogen atoms, such as sulfuric acid.
Ammonium salt
A salt containing the ammonium ion, NH₄⁺, such as ammonium chloride, formed when ammonia reacts with an acid.
Ionic equation
An equation showing only the ions and molecules that take part in the change, e.g. H⁺(aq) + OH⁻(aq) → H₂O(l).
Exothermic
Describes a reaction that releases heat to the surroundings.
Limewater test
A test for carbon dioxide: the gas turns limewater milky.
Splint test
A test for hydrogen: a lighted splint held at the mouth of the tube gives a squeaky pop.
