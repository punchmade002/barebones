The Arrhenius theory

Svante Arrhenius defined acids and bases by the ions they produce in water (1887).

  • An Arrhenius acid is a substance that dissociates in water to produce hydrogen ions (H⁺): HCl(aq) → H⁺(aq) + Cl⁻(aq).
  • An Arrhenius base is a substance that dissociates in water to produce hydroxide ions (OH⁻): NaOH(aq) → Na⁺(aq) + OH⁻(aq).
  • Neutralisation is the combination of hydrogen and hydroxide ions to form water: H⁺ + OH⁻ → H₂O.

Limitations of the Arrhenius theory

  • It applies only to aqueous solutions.
  • It cannot explain substances that act as bases but contain no hydroxide, such as ammonia (NH₃) and sodium carbonate.
  • It cannot explain acid–base reactions that happen without water, e.g. ammonia gas and hydrogen chloride gas reacting to form ammonium chloride: NH₃(g) + HCl(g) → NH₄Cl(s).
  • It ignores the role of water: a bare hydrogen ion does not exist in water; it bonds to a water molecule to form the hydronium ion, H₃O⁺.

The Brønsted–Lowry theory

Johannes Brønsted and Thomas Lowry proposed this theory independently in 1923. It defines acids and bases by the transfer of a proton.

  • A proton is the same particle as a hydrogen ion, H⁺.
  • A Brønsted–Lowry acid is a proton donor; a Brønsted–Lowry base is a proton acceptor.
  • An acid–base reaction is the transfer of a proton from an acid to a base.
  • HCl(g) + H₂O(l) → H₃O⁺(aq) + Cl⁻(aq): HCl donates a proton, so it is the acid, and water accepts it, so it is the base.
  • NH₃(aq) + H₂O(l) ⇌ NH₄⁺(aq) + OH⁻(aq): ammonia accepts a proton, so it is the base, and water donates one, so it is the acid.
  • Water can act as an acid or a base depending on what it reacts with, so it is described as amphoteric.

Why Brønsted–Lowry is more extensive

A theory is more extensive if it explains a wider range of substances and reactions with fewer restrictions.

  • It includes every Arrhenius acid and base, and more: the hydroxide ion is itself a proton acceptor.
  • It explains bases with no OH⁻, such as ammonia, and the carbonate ion, which accepts a proton: CO₃²⁻ + H⁺ → HCO₃⁻.
  • It applies to reactions without water, such as ammonia reacting with hydrogen chloride in the gas phase.
  • It explains the role of water as a proton acceptor when it forms H₃O⁺, which the Arrhenius theory cannot describe.
  • Acid: Arrhenius gives a substance that produces H⁺ in water; Brønsted–Lowry gives a proton donor, so the definition does not depend on water.
  • Base: Arrhenius gives a substance that produces OH⁻ in water; Brønsted–Lowry gives a proton acceptor, so it covers bases such as ammonia.
  • Scope: Arrhenius applies only to aqueous solutions; Brønsted–Lowry applies in any solvent and in the absence of a solvent.

Conjugate acid–base pairs (Higher Level only)

  • When an acid donates a proton, what is left can accept a proton back: it is the conjugate base of that acid.
  • When a base accepts a proton, the species formed is the conjugate acid of that base.
  • A conjugate acid–base pair is two species that differ by one proton (H⁺), e.g. HCl and Cl⁻; H₃O⁺ and H₂O; NH₄⁺ and NH₃.
  • H₃O⁺ and OH⁻ are not a conjugate pair, because they differ by two protons.
  • Every Brønsted–Lowry reaction has two conjugate pairs: acid₁ + base₂ ⇌ acid₂ + base₁.

Identifying the species in an equation (Higher Level only)

  • Step 1: For each reactant, see whether it loses or gains a proton in going to the products.
  • Step 2: The reactant that loses a proton is the acid; the reactant that gains a proton is the base.
  • Step 3: On the product side, the species formed from the base is its conjugate acid, and the species formed from the acid is its conjugate base.
  • Step 4: In a reversible reaction, the products act as an acid and a base in the reverse reaction.

Worked examples (Higher Level only)

  • HCl + H₂O → H₃O⁺ + Cl⁻: HCl is the acid and H₂O is the base; the pairs are HCl/Cl⁻ and H₃O⁺/H₂O.
  • NH₃ + H₂O ⇌ NH₄⁺ + OH⁻: the acids are H₂O and NH₄⁺; the bases are NH₃ and OH⁻; the pairs are NH₄⁺/NH₃ and H₂O/OH⁻.
  • CH₃COOH + H₂O ⇌ CH₃COO⁻ + H₃O⁺: the acids are CH₃COOH and H₃O⁺; the bases are H₂O and CH₃COO⁻; the pairs are CH₃COOH/CH₃COO⁻ and H₃O⁺/H₂O.
  • HCO₃⁻ + H₂O ⇌ CO₃²⁻ + H₃O⁺: here HCO₃⁻ acts as an acid; the pairs are HCO₃⁻/CO₃²⁻ and H₃O⁺/H₂O.
  • HCO₃⁻ + H₃O⁺ ⇌ H₂CO₃ + H₂O: here HCO₃⁻ acts as a base; the pairs are H₂CO₃/HCO₃⁻ and H₃O⁺/H₂O.
  • NH₃ + HCl → NH₄⁺ + Cl⁻ (no water involved): NH₃ is the base and HCl is the acid; the pairs are NH₄⁺/NH₃ and HCl/Cl⁻.

Strength of conjugates and amphoteric species (Higher Level only)

  • The stronger an acid, the weaker its conjugate base: HCl is a strong acid, so Cl⁻ is a very weak base that barely accepts protons.
  • A weak acid has a relatively strong conjugate base: ethanoic acid is a weak acid, and the ethanoate ion (CH₃COO⁻) readily accepts a proton.
  • Water and the hydrogencarbonate ion are amphoteric (also called amphiprotic): each can act as either an acid or a base, depending on the reaction, as shown in the examples above.

Strong and weak acids

  • A strong acid is completely dissociated (ionised) into ions in water, shown with a single arrow: HCl(aq) → H⁺(aq) + Cl⁻(aq).
  • Strong acids include hydrochloric acid (HCl), nitric acid (HNO₃) and sulfuric acid (H₂SO₄).
  • A weak acid is only slightly dissociated in water: most molecules stay undissociated and an equilibrium is set up, e.g. CH₃COOH(aq) ⇌ CH₃COO⁻(aq) + H⁺(aq).
  • Weak acids include ethanoic acid (vinegar), citric acid, carbonic acid (CO₂ in water) and most organic acids.

Strong and weak bases

  • A strong base is completely dissociated in water, e.g. sodium hydroxide and potassium hydroxide: NaOH(aq) → Na⁺(aq) + OH⁻(aq).
  • A weak base reacts only slightly with water to produce hydroxide ions, e.g. ammonia: NH₃(aq) + H₂O(l) ⇌ NH₄⁺(aq) + OH⁻(aq).

Concentrated and dilute

Concentration describes how much acid or base is present; strength describes how far it dissociates. The two are separate properties.

  • Concentration is the amount of acid or base dissolved per litre of solution, measured in mol L⁻¹.
  • A concentrated acid has a large amount of acid per litre, e.g. 10 M HCl; a dilute acid has a small amount per litre, e.g. 0.1 M HCl.
  • A dilute strong acid, e.g. 0.01 M HCl, is still fully dissociated.
  • A concentrated weak acid, e.g. glacial ethanoic acid, is still only slightly dissociated.

Evidence that ethanoic acid is weaker than hydrochloric acid

Compare 0.1 M hydrochloric acid with 0.1 M ethanoic acid, which have the same concentration.

  • pH: 0.1 M hydrochloric acid has a pH of 1.0, while 0.1 M ethanoic acid has a pH of about 2.9, because it produces far fewer H⁺ ions.
  • Ethanoic acid has an acid dissociation constant (Ka) of 1.8 × 10⁻⁵, a small value showing that it is only slightly dissociated.
  • Rate of reaction: magnesium ribbon fizzes much faster in 0.1 M hydrochloric acid than in 0.1 M ethanoic acid, because hydrogen ions are present at a higher concentration.
  • Conductivity: 0.1 M hydrochloric acid conducts electricity much better than 0.1 M ethanoic acid, because it contains more ions.
  • Equal volumes of the two acids at the same concentration neutralise the same amount of sodium hydroxide and give the same total volume of hydrogen with excess magnesium, because the weak acid's equilibrium shifts to release more H⁺ as H⁺ is used up.
  • Safety: concentrated acids and alkalis are corrosive, so wear goggles and gloves; dilute an acid by adding it to water.

Key terms

Arrhenius acid
A substance that dissociates in water to produce hydrogen ions, H⁺.
Arrhenius base
A substance that dissociates in water to produce hydroxide ions, OH⁻.
Brønsted–Lowry acid
A proton donor.
Brønsted–Lowry base
A proton acceptor.
Proton
A hydrogen ion, H⁺.
Hydronium ion
The ion H₃O⁺, formed when a hydrogen ion bonds to a water molecule.
Amphoteric
Describes a substance, such as water, that can act as either an acid or a base.
Hydroxide ion
The ion OH⁻, which an Arrhenius base produces in water.
Conjugate base
The species left after an acid donates a proton.
Conjugate acid
The species formed when a base accepts a proton.
Conjugate acid–base pair
Two species that differ by exactly one proton, H⁺.
Reversible reaction
A reaction that can proceed in both directions, shown by the symbol ⇌.
Strong acid
An acid that is completely dissociated into ions in water.
Weak acid
An acid that is only slightly dissociated in water, setting up an equilibrium with its ions.
Strong base
A base that is completely dissociated in water.
Weak base
A base that reacts only slightly with water to produce hydroxide ions.
Dissociation
The splitting of a compound into ions when it dissolves in water.
Concentration
The amount of acid or base dissolved per litre of solution, measured in mol L⁻¹.
Concentrated acid
An acid with a large amount of acid dissolved per litre, e.g. 10 M HCl.
Dilute acid
An acid with a small amount of acid dissolved per litre, e.g. 0.1 M HCl.
Corrosive
Able to destroy or burn skin and other materials, as concentrated acids and alkalis are.
