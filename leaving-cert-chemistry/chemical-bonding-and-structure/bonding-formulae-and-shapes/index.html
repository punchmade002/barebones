Why atoms bond

  • A chemical bond is a force of attraction that holds atoms or ions together in an element or compound.
  • Atoms bond to reach a more stable arrangement of electrons, usually a full outer energy level like the nearest noble gas (the octet rule); bonding lowers their energy.
  • Only the outer (valence) electrons take part in bonding.

Ionic bonding

  • An ionic bond is the electrostatic attraction between oppositely charged ions.
  • It forms when electrons are transferred from one atom, usually a metal that forms a positive ion, to another atom, usually a non-metal that forms a negative ion.
  • Example: sodium (2,8,1) gives one electron to chlorine (2,8,7): Na → Na⁺ + e⁻ and Cl + e⁻ → Cl⁻.
  • Both ions have the electron arrangement of a noble gas.
  • Ionic compounds form a giant lattice of ions, not molecules.
  • In sodium chloride each Na⁺ ion is surrounded by six Cl⁻ ions, and each Cl⁻ ion by six Na⁺ ions.
  • The formula NaCl gives only the ratio of the ions, one Na⁺ to one Cl⁻.
  • Diagram a section of the sodium chloride lattice, drawn as a cube, showing alternating Na⁺ and Cl⁻ ions.

Covalent bonding

  • A covalent bond is a shared pair of electrons between two atoms, usually two non-metals; both nuclei are attracted to the shared pair.
  • A single bond is one shared pair, as in H–H and Cl–Cl.
  • A double bond is two shared pairs, as in O=O and C=O in CO₂.
  • A triple bond is three shared pairs, as in N≡N.
  • Covalently bonded atoms form molecules, e.g. H₂O, CH₄ and NH₃.
  • A few substances have giant covalent structures, in which atoms are linked by covalent bonds throughout, e.g. diamond.

Pure and polar covalent bonds

  • A pure (non-polar) covalent bond shares electrons equally, because both atoms attract them equally, as in H–H and Cl–Cl.
  • The C–H bond is nearly non-polar.
  • A polar covalent bond shares electrons unequally, because one atom attracts them more strongly, as in H–Cl and O–H.
  • The atom that attracts the shared electrons more strongly gets a partial negative charge (δ−), and the other atom gets a partial positive charge (δ+), e.g. Hδ+–Clδ−.

The bonding continuum

  • Bonds are not simply ionic or covalent; they lie on a continuum from pure covalent, through polar covalent, to ionic.
  • At one end, pure covalent bonds share electrons equally.
  • In the middle, polar covalent bonds share electrons unequally.
  • At the other end, ionic bonds involve complete transfer of electrons.
  • Examples along the continuum: Cl–Cl (pure covalent), H–Cl (polar covalent), Na⁺Cl⁻ (ionic).
  • Most bonds have some ionic and some covalent character.
  • The difference in electronegativity between the atoms decides where a bond lies (see "Bond type and intermolecular forces").
  • Diagram a horizontal line labelled pure covalent – polar covalent – ionic, with the electron pair drawn shared equally at the left end, shifted towards one atom in the middle, and fully transferred at the right end.

Comparing the types

  • Ionic bonding transfers electrons and forms ions; covalent bonding shares electrons and forms molecules (mostly).
  • Ionic bonding occurs between a metal and a non-metal; covalent bonding occurs mostly between two non-metals.
  • Ionic compounds are giant lattices; covalent bonds are polar or non-polar depending on the atoms involved.
  • Metallic bonding (assumed): positive metal ions are held together by a sea of delocalised outer electrons, which explains why metals conduct electricity.

Valency and the octet rule

  • Valency is the number of electrons an atom loses, gains or shares when it bonds; it is the combining power of an element.
  • The octet rule: atoms tend to lose, gain or share electrons to reach eight electrons in their outer energy level.
  • Hydrogen follows a different rule: it needs two electrons in its outer level, matching helium.
  • Elements in group 1 and group 17 have a valency of 1.
  • Elements in group 2 and group 16 have a valency of 2.
  • Elements in group 13 and group 15 have a valency of 3.
  • Elements in group 14 have a valency of 4.

Predicting ionic formulae

  • Step 1: Write the charge on each ion, e.g. Ca²⁺ and Cl⁻.
  • Step 2: Combine the ions so that the total positive charge equals the total negative charge.
  • Calcium ions (Ca²⁺) and chloride ions (Cl⁻) give CaCl₂, as one Ca²⁺ balances two Cl⁻.
  • Aluminium ions (Al³⁺) and oxide ions (O²⁻) give Al₂O₃, as two Al³⁺ balance three O²⁻.
  • Magnesium ions (Mg²⁺) and nitride ions (N³⁻) give Mg₃N₂, as three Mg²⁺ balance two N³⁻.

Compound ions

  • Negative compound ions: hydroxide OH⁻, nitrate NO₃⁻, hydrogencarbonate HCO₃⁻, carbonate CO₃²⁻, sulfate SO₄²⁻, sulfite SO₃²⁻ and phosphate PO₄³⁻.
  • Positive compound ion: ammonium NH₄⁺.
  • Brackets are used round a compound ion when more than one is needed, e.g. Ca(OH)₂ and (NH₄)₂SO₄.

Predicting covalent formulae

  • Covalent formulae: atoms share enough electron pairs to complete each octet.
  • Hydrogen (valency 1) and oxygen (valency 2) form H₂O.
  • Nitrogen (valency 3) and hydrogen form NH₃.
  • Carbon (valency 4) and hydrogen form CH₄.
  • Carbon and oxygen form CO₂, with two double bonds.

Lewis (dot-and-cross) diagrams

  • A Lewis diagram shows the outer electrons of each atom as dots or crosses.
  • Shared pairs of electrons are drawn between the atoms they join.
  • Lone pairs are pairs of outer electrons that are not used in bonding.
  • Ionic compounds: show each ion in square brackets with its charge, e.g. [Na]⁺ [Cl]⁻, with eight electrons around the chloride ion.
  • Magnesium oxide is drawn as [Mg]²⁺ [O]²⁻.
  • Covalent examples to draw: H₂, Cl₂, HCl and H₂O, where water has two lone pairs on oxygen.
  • Ammonia, NH₃, has one lone pair on nitrogen.
  • O₂ has a double bond, N₂ has a triple bond, and CO₂ has two double bonds.
  • Diagram dot-and-cross diagrams of sodium chloride, water and nitrogen, with lone pairs labelled.

Orbital overlap

  • A covalent bond forms when an orbital of one atom, holding one electron, overlaps an orbital of another atom holding one electron.
  • The shared pair occupies the overlap region between the two nuclei.

Sigma and pi bonds (Higher Level only)

  • A sigma (σ) bond is formed by head-on overlap of orbitals along the line joining the two nuclei.
  • Sigma bonds are formed by s–s overlap in H₂, s–p overlap in HCl, and end-on p–p overlap in Cl₂.
  • A pi (π) bond is formed by sideways overlap of two parallel p orbitals, above and below the line joining the nuclei.
  • Every single bond is a σ bond.
  • A double bond is one σ bond and one π bond; a triple bond is one σ bond and two π bonds, as in N₂.
  • π bonds are weaker than σ bonds because sideways overlap is less effective.
  • π bonds prevent rotation about a double bond.
  • Diagram head-on overlap of two s orbitals and of two p orbitals (σ); sideways overlap of two p orbitals, showing electron density above and below the axis (π).

Delocalised bonding (Higher Level only)

  • In delocalised bonding, electrons are spread over several atoms rather than confined between two.
  • In benzene (C₆H₆), each carbon uses one p orbital, and the six p orbitals overlap sideways all round the ring.
  • This forms a ring of delocalised π electrons above and below the plane of the ring.
  • All six carbon–carbon bonds in benzene are identical, with lengths between those of single and double bonds.
  • Benzene's structure is used again in the chapter "Allotropes of Carbon. Hydrocarbons".

Extending the rules to element 36 (Higher Level only)

  • The same rules apply to elements 21–36 in the main groups.
  • Potassium bromide is K⁺Br⁻.
  • Calcium bromide is CaBr₂.
  • Gallium (group 13) forms Ga³⁺ ions.
  • Arsenic (group 15) and hydrogen form AsH₃.
  • Selenium (group 16) and hydrogen form H₂Se.

Limitations in predicting bonding (Higher Level only)

  • Some molecules are stable with fewer than eight outer electrons on the central atom, e.g. BF₃, with six around boron.
  • Elements in period 3 and beyond can have more than eight outer electrons, e.g. PCl₅, with ten around phosphorus.
  • SF₆ has twelve electrons around sulfur.
  • The octet rule and Lewis diagrams do not predict the shape of a molecule, how polar a bond is, or bond strength.
  • They cannot show delocalised bonding, as in benzene.
  • Predictions from valency assume purely ionic or purely covalent bonding, but real bonds lie on a continuum.

The VSEPR theory (Higher Level only)

  • VSEPR stands for valence shell electron pair repulsion.
  • The theory applies to molecules of the form ABn, with up to four pairs of electrons around a central atom and single bonds only.
  • Electron pairs in the outer (valence) shell of the central atom repel each other and arrange themselves as far apart as possible, which fixes the shape of the molecule.
  • Both bonding pairs and lone pairs count.
  • Lone pairs repel more strongly than bonding pairs: lone pair–lone pair repulsion is greater than lone pair–bond pair repulsion, which is greater than bond pair–bond pair repulsion.
  • Lone pairs therefore squeeze the bond angles smaller.
  • The shape is described by the positions of the atoms only, not the lone pairs.

Method for predicting shape (Higher Level only)

  • Step 1: Draw the Lewis (dot-and-cross) diagram of the molecule.
  • Step 2: Count the electron pairs around the central atom, and how many of them are bonding pairs and how many are lone pairs.
  • Step 3: Arrange the pairs as far apart as possible, and name the shape from the positions of the atoms, adjusting the angle for lone pairs.

The five shapes (Higher Level only)

  • Two bonding pairs and no lone pairs: linear, 180°, e.g. BeCl₂ (gas).
  • Three bonding pairs and no lone pairs: trigonal planar, 120°, e.g. BF₃.
  • Four bonding pairs and no lone pairs: tetrahedral, 109.5°, e.g. CH₄, CCl₄ and SiH₄.
  • Three bonding pairs and one lone pair: pyramidal (trigonal pyramidal), about 107°, e.g. NH₃. PH₃ is also pyramidal, but its measured angle is much smaller, about 93.5°.
  • Two bonding pairs and two lone pairs: V-shaped (bent), about 104.5°, e.g. H₂O.
  • H₂S is also V-shaped, but its measured bond angle is much smaller than water's, about 92°.

Bond angles and lone pairs (Higher Level only)

  • NH₃ and H₂O have four electron pairs arranged roughly tetrahedrally, as in CH₄.
  • The lone pairs push the bonds closer together, so the bond angle falls as the number of lone pairs rises from 0 to 2: from CH₄ to NH₃ to H₂O.

Visualising shapes (Higher Level only)

  • Diagram 3D shapes drawn with wedges for bonds towards the viewer and dashed lines for bonds away from the viewer, with any lone pairs shown on the central atom.
  • 3D models: ball-and-stick kits show bond angles.
  • Digital models, from molecular-modelling software or apps, can be rotated on screen.
  • Diagram the five shapes, each with its example, bond angle and any lone pairs labelled.

Key terms

Chemical bond
A force of attraction that holds atoms or ions together in an element or compound.
Valence electrons
The outer electrons of an atom, the only ones that take part in bonding.
Ionic bond
The electrostatic attraction between oppositely charged ions.
Covalent bond
A shared pair of electrons between two atoms, with both nuclei attracted to the shared pair.
Giant lattice
A regular, repeating three-dimensional arrangement of ions, as in sodium chloride.
Pure covalent bond
A covalent bond in which the shared electrons are attracted equally by both atoms, as in Cl–Cl.
Polar covalent bond
A covalent bond in which the shared electrons are attracted unequally, giving the atoms partial charges.
Partial charge (δ+ and δ−)
A small charge on an atom in a polar bond: δ− on the atom that attracts the electrons more strongly, and δ+ on the other.
Bonding continuum
The range of bonds from pure covalent, through polar covalent, to ionic.
Metallic bond
The attraction between positive metal ions and a sea of delocalised outer electrons.
Valency
The number of electrons an atom loses, gains or shares when it bonds.
Octet rule
The tendency of atoms to lose, gain or share electrons to reach eight electrons in their outer energy level.
Compound ion
A group of atoms that carries an overall charge, such as SO₄²⁻ or NH₄⁺.
Lewis diagram
A diagram that shows the outer electrons of each atom as dots or crosses to represent bonding.
Lone pair
A pair of outer electrons on an atom that is not used in bonding.
Orbital overlap
The overlap of an orbital of one atom with an orbital of another, each holding one electron, to form a covalent bond.
Sigma bond
A covalent bond formed by head-on overlap of orbitals along the line joining the two nuclei.
Pi bond
A covalent bond formed by sideways overlap of two parallel p orbitals, above and below the line joining the nuclei.
Delocalised electrons
Electrons spread over several atoms rather than confined between two atoms, as in benzene.
VSEPR theory
Valence shell electron pair repulsion theory, which predicts molecular shape from the repulsion between electron pairs around the central atom.
Central atom
The atom in a molecule to which the other atoms are bonded.
Bonding pair
A pair of electrons shared between two atoms in a covalent bond.
Bond angle
The angle between two bonds that share the same central atom.
Linear
A shape in which two bonded atoms lie on opposite sides of the central atom, giving a bond angle of 180°.
Trigonal planar
A shape in which three bonded atoms lie in one plane around the central atom, giving bond angles of 120°.
Tetrahedral
A shape in which four bonded atoms are arranged around the central atom, giving bond angles of 109.5°.
Pyramidal
A shape with three bonded atoms and one lone pair on the central atom, giving bond angles of about 107°.
V-shaped (bent)
A shape with two bonded atoms and two lone pairs on the central atom, giving a bond angle of about 104.5° in water.
