General points

  • A salt contains a cation (positive ion, usually a metal) and an anion (negative ion).
  • The anion tests are carried out on a solution of the salt in deionised water.
  • Tests work only above a certain minimum concentration of the ion; very dilute solutions may give no visible result.
  • Use deionised water and clean test tubes, so that ions from tap water or other samples don't give false results.
  • Test a blank of deionised water as a control.
  • A precipitate is an insoluble solid that forms when two solutions are mixed.
  • Effervescence is fizzing as a gas is given off.

Cation: flame test

  • Flame tests identify six cations: sodium, potassium, copper, lithium, barium and strontium, each giving its own flame colour (see "The orbital model and electronic structure").

Chloride

  • Add a few drops of silver nitrate solution, acidified with dilute nitric acid.
  • A white precipitate of silver chloride forms: Ag⁺(aq) + Cl⁻(aq) → AgCl(s).
  • Confirm the precipitate by showing that it dissolves in dilute ammonia solution.
  • Use nitric acid, not hydrochloric acid, which would add chloride ions.
  • Nitric acid is added first to remove carbonate ions, which would also give a precipitate with silver nitrate.

Sulfate and sulfite

  • Add barium chloride solution: sulfate and sulfite both give a white precipitate.
  • Sulfate: Ba²⁺(aq) + SO₄²⁻(aq) → BaSO₄(s).
  • Sulfite: Ba²⁺(aq) + SO₃²⁻(aq) → BaSO₃(s).
  • Then add dilute hydrochloric acid: barium sulfate does not dissolve, but barium sulfite dissolves.
  • Barium sulfite reacts with the acid: BaSO₃(s) + 2HCl(aq) → BaCl₂(aq) + SO₂(g) + H₂O(l).
  • Safety: barium compounds are toxic.
  • Safety: sulfur dioxide is a toxic, choking gas, so use small amounts in good ventilation.

Carbonate and hydrogencarbonate

  • Add dilute hydrochloric acid to the solid: both give effervescence of carbon dioxide.
  • Carbonate: CO₃²⁻ + 2H⁺ → CO₂ + H₂O.
  • Hydrogencarbonate: HCO₃⁻ + H⁺ → CO₂ + H₂O.
  • Confirm the gas as carbon dioxide: it turns limewater milky, Ca(OH)₂(aq) + CO₂(g) → CaCO₃(s) + H₂O(l).
  • To tell them apart, add magnesium sulfate solution: a carbonate gives a white precipitate immediately in the cold, Mg²⁺ + CO₃²⁻ → MgCO₃(s).
  • A hydrogencarbonate gives no precipitate with magnesium sulfate solution until the mixture is heated.
  • On heating: Mg²⁺ + 2HCO₃⁻ → MgCO₃(s) + CO₂(g) + H₂O(l).

Nitrate: the brown ring test

  • Add freshly made iron(II) sulfate solution to the test solution in a test tube.
  • Hold the tube at an angle and carefully run concentrated sulfuric acid down the side, so that it forms a dense layer at the bottom; don't shake.
  • A brown ring forms where the two layers meet, showing nitrate.
  • Iron(II) reduces nitrate to nitrogen monoxide: 3Fe²⁺ + NO₃⁻ + 4H⁺ → 3Fe³⁺ + NO + 2H₂O.
  • Nitrogen monoxide forms a brown complex with more iron(II): Fe²⁺ + NO → [FeNO]²⁺.
  • Safety: concentrated sulfuric acid is very corrosive; wear goggles and gloves, and add it slowly.

Phosphate

  • Add a few drops of concentrated nitric acid, then ammonium molybdate solution, and warm gently.
  • A yellow precipitate of ammonium phosphomolybdate shows phosphate.
  • Equation: PO₄³⁻ + 3NH₄⁺ + 12MoO₄²⁻ + 24H⁺ → (NH₄)₃PO₄·12MoO₃(s) + 12H₂O.
  • Safety: concentrated nitric acid is corrosive and an oxidising agent; use a fume cupboard.

Links to water supplies

  • Chlorides come from sea water and road salt; chlorination of water also gives chloride.
  • Nitrates and phosphates come from fertilisers, animal slurry, sewage and detergents.
  • Nitrates and phosphates contaminate water and cause eutrophication (taught in the environmental chemistry chapter).
  • Hard water contains dissolved calcium and magnesium ions.
  • Calcium and magnesium ions with hydrogencarbonate ions cause temporary hardness, which is removed by boiling.
  • Calcium and magnesium ions with sulfate or chloride ions cause permanent hardness.
  • Hard water forms scum with soap.
  • Carbonates and hydrogencarbonates come from water flowing over limestone, which is calcium carbonate.

Investigation: identifying an unknown salt

  • Aim: to identify the cation and the anion in an unknown salt, using primary data.
  • Step 1: Carry out a flame test on the solid to identify the cation.
  • Step 2: Add dilute hydrochloric acid to a little of the solid to test for carbonate and hydrogencarbonate, then dissolve a little of the salt in deionised water.
  • Step 3: Carry out the other anion tests on separate portions of the solution in a logical order, recording the reagent, observation and conclusion in a table.
  • Step 4: Run each test on a known sample of the ion alongside, as a positive control, and on deionised water as a negative control.
  • Step 5: Conclude the identity of the salt and write its formula, e.g. a lilac flame and a white precipitate with silver nitrate that dissolves in ammonia indicate potassium chloride, KCl.
  • Diagram the brown ring test tube, showing the lower layer of concentrated sulfuric acid, the upper aqueous layer and the brown ring at the boundary.

Key terms

Cation
A positively charged ion, usually a metal ion.
Anion
A negatively charged ion.
Deionised water
Water from which dissolved ions have been removed, used so that ions in tap water don't give false results.
Precipitate
An insoluble solid that forms when two solutions are mixed.
Effervescence
Fizzing caused by a gas being given off.
Positive control
A test on a known sample of the ion, run alongside the unknown to check that the test works.
Negative control
A test on deionised water, to check that the result is not a false positive.
Temporary hardness
Hardness caused by calcium or magnesium hydrogencarbonate in water, which boiling removes.
Permanent hardness
Hardness caused by dissolved calcium or magnesium sulfate or chloride, which boiling does not remove.
