Early attempts to find patterns among the elements

By the mid-1800s over 60 elements were known, and chemists looked for relationships among their properties.

  • An element is a substance that cannot be split into simpler substances by chemical means.
  • Johann Döbereiner (1829) noticed triads: groups of three elements with similar properties in which the atomic weight of the middle element is about the average of the other two.
  • Döbereiner's triads included lithium, sodium and potassium; calcium, strontium and barium; and chlorine, bromine and iodine.
  • John Newlands (1864–1865) proposed the law of octaves: when the elements were arranged by atomic weight, every eighth element had properties similar to the first.
  • Newlands' law worked for the lighter elements but broke down for the heavier ones, and he left no gaps in his table.

Mendeleev's periodic table (1869)

  • Dmitri Mendeleev (1869) arranged the elements in order of increasing atomic weight (relative atomic mass), placing elements with similar properties in the same vertical column, called a group.
  • Mendeleev's periodic law: the properties of the elements vary periodically with their atomic weights.
  • He left gaps for elements not yet discovered and predicted their properties.
  • His predicted elements eka-aluminium and eka-silicon were matched by gallium (discovered 1875) and germanium (discovered 1886); scandium (discovered 1879) matched eka-boron.
  • The discovery of elements with the predicted properties convinced chemists that the table was correct.
  • Where atomic weight order placed an element in the wrong group, he reversed the order of the pair: tellurium (relative atomic mass 127.6) is placed before iodine (relative atomic mass 126.9) so that iodine sits with the other halogens.
  • The table organised all the known elements, showed relationships between them, made predictions that were confirmed, and became the basis of the modern table.

Refinements leading to the modern table

  • The noble gases helium, neon, argon, krypton and xenon were discovered in the 1890s (William Ramsay and others) and were added as a new group, now group 18.
  • Henry Moseley (1913) showed by experiment that each element has a whole-number atomic number, equal to the number of protons in its nucleus.
  • The modern periodic table is arranged in order of increasing atomic number, so tellurium (atomic number 52) naturally comes before iodine (atomic number 53) and Mendeleev's reversal is unnecessary.
  • Modern periodic law: the properties of the elements are a periodic function of their atomic numbers.
  • The repeating pattern is explained by electron arrangement: elements in the same group have the same outer-electron arrangement.

Mendeleev's table compared with the modern table

  • Order: Mendeleev ordered the elements by atomic weight; the modern table orders them by atomic number.
  • Gaps: Mendeleev's table had gaps for undiscovered elements; the modern table has none.
  • Noble gases: absent from Mendeleev's table, which was drawn up before they were discovered.
  • Number of elements: Mendeleev's table contained fewer elements, while the modern table contains 118.
  • Blocks: Mendeleev's table had no separate blocks of elements; the modern table is divided into blocks.

Groups: vertical columns

The table is arranged so that elements with the same outer electrons fall in the same column.

  • A group is a vertical column of elements; using IUPAC numbering there are 18 groups, numbered 1 to 18.
  • Elements in the same group have the same number of electrons in their outer energy level, so they have similar chemical properties.
  • For groups 1 and 2, the group number equals the number of outer electrons.
  • For groups 13 to 18, the number of outer electrons is the group number minus 10, so group 17 has 7 outer electrons; helium, in group 18, is the exception with 2 outer electrons.
  • Group 1 elements are the alkali metals and group 2 elements are the alkaline earth metals.
  • Group 17 elements are the halogens and group 18 elements are the noble gases.

Periods: horizontal rows

  • A period is a horizontal row of elements; there are 7 periods.
  • The period number equals the number of occupied main energy levels; sodium, with electron arrangement 2,8,1, is in period 3.
  • Across a period, one proton and one electron are added at each step.
  • Period 1 has 2 elements (hydrogen and helium); periods 2 and 3 have 8 each; period 4 has 18.

Metals, non-metals and metalloids

  • About three-quarters of the elements are metals, found on the left and in the centre of the table.
  • Non-metals are found on the right of the table; hydrogen is also a non-metal.
  • A staircase line running from boron to astatine separates metals from non-metals.
  • Metalloids (semi-metals) lie next to the staircase line, including boron, silicon, germanium, arsenic, antimony and tellurium; they have some properties of metals and some of non-metals.
  • Metals are shiny, good conductors of heat and electricity, malleable and ductile, and form positive ions.
  • Non-metals are dull, poor conductors of electricity (graphite is an exception), brittle as solids, and often form negative ions or share electrons.

Blocks of the periodic table (Higher Level only)

A block is the set of elements whose highest-energy electron enters the same type of sublevel.

  • The s-block is groups 1 and 2, together with helium; its highest-energy electron is in an s sublevel.
  • The p-block is groups 13 to 18, except helium; its highest-energy electron is in a p sublevel.
  • The d-block is groups 3 to 12, in the middle of periods 4 to 7; its electrons fill a d sublevel.
  • The f-block is the lanthanides and actinides, usually shown below the main table; its electrons fill an f sublevel.
  • The shape of the table follows from sublevel capacities: 2 columns for s, 6 for p, 10 for d and 14 for f.

Reading the table

Each element's box and position give information about its atoms and its behaviour.

  • Each box gives the symbol, atomic number and relative atomic mass of an element.
  • The atomic number gives the number of protons and, in a neutral atom, the number of electrons.
  • The group gives the element's outer-electron arrangement.
  • The period gives the number of occupied energy levels.
  • The element's position relative to the staircase line shows whether it is a metal, non-metal or metalloid.

Predicting from groups

Elements in the same group share an outer-electron arrangement, so they behave in similar ways.

  • Reactions can be predicted from a group: sodium reacts with water to give sodium hydroxide and hydrogen, so potassium, in the same group, will react with water in the same way, giving potassium hydroxide and hydrogen.
  • Group 1 elements form 1⁺ ions, group 2 elements form 2⁺ ions and group 13 elements form 3⁺ ions.
  • Group 15 elements form 3⁻ ions, group 16 elements form 2⁻ ions and group 17 elements form 1⁻ ions.
  • Noble gases (group 18) have full outer energy levels and are very unreactive.

Predicting formulae

The formula of a compound follows from the charges of its ions, which must balance to give no overall charge.

  • Magnesium (group 2) forms Mg²⁺ and chlorine (group 17) forms Cl⁻, so two chloride ions balance one magnesium ion and the formula is MgCl₂.
  • Aluminium (group 13) forms Al³⁺ and oxygen (group 16) forms O²⁻, so two aluminium ions balance three oxide ions and the formula is Al₂O₃.

Predicting from trends

Trends in the table allow properties of unfamiliar elements to be estimated from their neighbours.

  • Properties change gradually and predictably down a group and across a period, so an unknown property can be estimated from neighbouring elements.
  • Astatine (At, atomic number 85) lies at the bottom of group 17; from the trend in the halogens it is predicted to be a dark solid that is less reactive than iodine.
  • Mendeleev used the same approach to predict the properties of undiscovered elements.
  • Every trend is explained by electron arrangement: the number of outer electrons, the number of occupied energy levels and the nuclear charge.

Key terms

Element
A substance that cannot be split into simpler substances by chemical means.
Triad
A group of three elements with similar properties in which the middle element's atomic weight is about the average of the other two.
Law of octaves
Newlands' observation that, arranged by atomic weight, every eighth element has properties similar to the first.
Atomic weight
The mass of an element's atoms relative to other atoms, also called relative atomic mass.
Mendeleev's periodic law
The properties of the elements vary periodically with their atomic weights.
Eka-element
An element that Mendeleev predicted, by its position in his table, to exist but not yet have been discovered.
Atomic number
The number of protons in the nucleus of an atom of an element.
Modern periodic law
The properties of the elements are a periodic function of their atomic numbers.
Noble gases
The unreactive elements of group 18, such as helium, neon, argon, krypton and xenon.
Group
A vertical column of elements in the periodic table whose members have the same number of outer electrons.
Period
A horizontal row of elements in the periodic table; the period number equals the number of occupied main energy levels.
Outer electrons
The electrons in the highest occupied energy level of an atom, which determine its chemical properties.
Alkali metals
The group 1 elements, which have one outer electron each.
Alkaline earth metals
The group 2 elements, which have two outer electrons each.
Halogens
The group 17 non-metals, which have seven outer electrons each.
Metalloid
An element lying along the staircase line, with some properties of metals and some of non-metals.
Block
A set of elements whose highest-energy electron enters the same type of sublevel: s, p, d or f.
Relative atomic mass
The mass of an element's atoms relative to other atoms, shown in the box for that element.
Ion
A charged particle formed when an atom gains or loses electrons.
Valency
The charge on an ion of an element, predicted from its group, which determines the formulae of its compounds.
Trend
A gradual and predictable change in a property down a group or across a period of the periodic table.
Full outer energy level
The arrangement of noble gases, which makes them very unreactive.
