Position and properties

  • Elements in the same group have the same number of outer electrons, so they undergo similar reactions.
  • Going down a group, atoms have more energy levels, which explains the trends in reactivity within each group.
  • Groups 1 and 2 are on the left of the periodic table; they are metals that lose electrons to form positive ions.
  • Groups 17 and 18 are on the right; the halogens (group 17) are non-metals that gain electrons, and the noble gases (group 18) neither gain nor lose electrons.
  • Hydrogen appears in group 1 in some tables but is not an alkali metal.

Group 1: the alkali metals

  • Members: lithium, sodium, potassium, rubidium, caesium and francium; each atom has one outer electron.
  • State and conductivity: soft, shiny solids when freshly cut, with low densities and low melting points for metals; good conductors of heat and electricity.
  • Reactivity: very reactive; they tarnish quickly in air and are stored under oil.
  • With water they form an alkaline hydroxide and hydrogen: 2Na(s) + 2H₂O(l) → 2NaOH(aq) + H₂(g).
  • Lithium: 2Li(s) + 2H₂O(l) → 2LiOH(aq) + H₂(g). Potassium: 2K(s) + 2H₂O(l) → 2KOH(aq) + H₂(g).
  • Lithium floats and fizzes steadily on the water.
  • Sodium melts into a silvery ball that darts about on the surface.
  • Potassium reacts violently, and the hydrogen it gives off burns with a lilac flame.
  • The solution formed turns universal indicator purple, showing that it is alkaline.
  • Reactivity increases down the group because the outer electron is further from the nucleus and more shielded, so it is lost more easily.
  • They form 1+ ions (Li⁺, Na⁺, K⁺) and ionic compounds such as sodium chloride.
  • Safety: use only small pieces, behind a safety screen and wearing goggles; never touch them with bare hands.

Group 2: the alkaline earth metals

  • Members: beryllium, magnesium, calcium, strontium, barium and radium; each atom has two outer electrons.
  • State and conductivity: solid metals, harder and denser than group 1 metals; good conductors.
  • Reactivity: reactive, but less so than group 1 metals in the same period, because two electrons must be lost.
  • Calcium reacts steadily with cold water: Ca(s) + 2H₂O(l) → Ca(OH)₂(aq) + H₂(g).
  • Magnesium reacts only very slowly with cold water but burns in air with a bright white flame: 2Mg(s) + O₂(g) → 2MgO(s).
  • Reactivity increases down the group, for the same reason as group 1.
  • They form 2+ ions (Mg²⁺, Ca²⁺).

Group 17: the halogens

  • Members: fluorine, chlorine, bromine and iodine (astatine); each atom has seven outer electrons.
  • The halogens exist as diatomic molecules: F₂, Cl₂, Br₂ and I₂.
  • Fluorine is a pale yellow gas at room temperature.
  • Chlorine is a yellow-green gas at room temperature.
  • Bromine is a red-brown liquid at room temperature.
  • Iodine is a grey-black solid that forms a purple vapour when heated.
  • Conductivity: halogens are non-metals and do not conduct electricity.
  • Reactivity: very reactive non-metals that gain one electron to form 1− halide ions: F⁻, Cl⁻, Br⁻ and I⁻.
  • They react with metals to form salts, e.g. 2Na(s) + Cl₂(g) → 2NaCl(s).
  • Reactivity decreases down the group because larger atoms attract an extra electron less strongly, as it is further from the nucleus and more shielded.
  • Displacement: a more reactive halogen displaces a less reactive one from a solution of its salt.
  • Chlorine and potassium bromide: Cl₂(aq) + 2KBr(aq) → 2KCl(aq) + Br₂(aq); the colourless solution turns orange-brown.
  • Chlorine and potassium iodide: Cl₂(aq) + 2KI(aq) → 2KCl(aq) + I₂(aq); the solution turns brown.
  • Bromine displaces iodine from a solution of its salt but does not displace chlorine.
  • Safety: halogens are toxic and corrosive, so they are used in a fume cupboard.

Group 18: the noble gases

  • Members: helium, neon, argon, krypton, xenon and radon.
  • State: colourless, odourless gases made of single atoms (monatomic).
  • Conductivity: non-conductors.
  • Reactivity: very unreactive (inert) because they have full outer energy levels, a stable arrangement: helium has 2 outer electrons and the others have 8.
  • Helium is used in balloons and airships because it is unreactive and has a low density.
  • Argon is used in filament light bulbs and as a shield gas in welding.
  • Neon is used in advertising signs, where it glows red in an electric discharge.

Atomic number and relative atomic mass

  • Atomic number increases by one from each element to the next.
  • Relative atomic mass generally increases with atomic number.
  • Relative atomic mass falls in a few places: argon (39.95) comes before potassium (39.10), and tellurium (127.6) comes before iodine (126.9).
  • These exceptions come from the mix of isotopes of each element, not from mistakes in the table.

Electronic structure

  • Across a period, the number of outer electrons increases by one at each step, all in the same main energy level.
  • Down a group, the number of outer electrons stays the same, but a new main energy level is added each time.
  • The repeating pattern of outer-electron arrangements causes periodicity: elements with the same outer arrangement fall in the same group.

Electronegativity

Electronegativity is the ability of an atom to attract bonding electrons towards itself. Values are on the Pauling scale.

  • Electronegativity increases across a period, e.g. sodium 0.93 to chlorine 3.16 in period 3.
  • Electronegativity decreases down a group, e.g. fluorine 3.98 to iodine 2.66 in group 17.
  • The explanation for these values is in "Bond type and intermolecular forces".

Chemical reactivity and the octet rule

  • Atoms react to reach a full outer level of eight electrons (the octet rule), by losing, gaining or sharing electrons.
  • Metals react by losing electrons: reactivity increases down group 1 and group 2.
  • Across a period, metal reactivity decreases from group 1 to group 2 to group 13.
  • Non-metals react by gaining electrons: reactivity decreases down group 17.
  • Across a period, non-metal reactivity generally increases towards group 17.
  • Noble gases already have a full outer level and are unreactive.

Physical properties across period 3

  • Sodium, magnesium and aluminium are shiny metals and good conductors.
  • Silicon is a metalloid and a semiconductor.
  • Phosphorus, sulfur, chlorine and argon are non-metals and non-conductors.
  • Melting points rise across period 3 to a maximum at silicon (1414 °C), which has a giant covalent structure.
  • Beyond silicon, melting points fall sharply for the molecular non-metals and for argon.
  • Down group 1, melting points decrease: lithium 181 °C, sodium 98 °C, potassium 63 °C.
  • Down group 17, melting and boiling points increase, so the halogens change from gases to a liquid to a solid.

Atomic radius and ionisation energy trends (Higher Level only)

These trends are Higher Level only and are explained in full below.

  • Atomic (covalent) radius decreases across a period and increases down a group.
  • First ionisation energy generally increases across a period and decreases down a group.
  • Successive ionisation energies of an element always increase.
  • Higher Level trends extend to the first 36 elements, to krypton; Ordinary level uses the first 20, to calcium.

The three factors controlling outer electrons (Higher Level only)

  • Effective nuclear charge: the more protons in the nucleus, the stronger the pull on the outer electrons.
  • Distance: the further the outer electrons are from the nucleus (more energy levels), the weaker the pull on them.
  • Screening (shielding): inner electrons shield the outer electrons from the full nuclear charge; electrons in the same energy level shield each other very little.

Atomic (covalent) radius (Higher Level only)

  • Atomic (covalent) radius: half the distance between the nuclei of two atoms of the same element joined by a single covalent bond.
  • Across a period it decreases: nuclear charge increases while electrons enter the same main energy level, with little extra screening, so the outer electrons are pulled closer.
  • Down a group it increases: each element has an extra energy level, and the extra inner electrons screen the outer electrons, outweighing the increase in nuclear charge.
  • Noble gases are not given a covalent radius because they don't form bonds.

First ionisation energy (Higher Level only)

  • First ionisation energy: the minimum energy needed to completely remove the most loosely bound electron from a neutral gaseous atom in its ground state: X(g) → X⁺(g) + e⁻.
  • Period 2 first ionisation energies (kJ mol⁻¹): Li 520, Be 899, B 801, C 1086, N 1402, O 1314, F 1681, Ne 2081.
  • Period 3 first ionisation energies (kJ mol⁻¹): Na 496, Mg 738, Al 578, Si 787, P 1012, S 1000, Cl 1251, Ar 1521.
  • Values are standard data book figures, rounded.
  • Across a period the value generally increases, because nuclear charge increases and atomic radius decreases, so the outer electron is held more tightly.
  • Noble gases have the highest first ionisation energy in each period.
  • Down a group the value decreases, because the outer electron is further from the nucleus and more screened, despite the larger nuclear charge.
  • Group 1 metals have the lowest values in each period, which is why they lose electrons easily and are so reactive; the value falls to potassium at 419 kJ mol⁻¹.

Exceptions to the trend across a period (Higher Level only)

  • Beryllium is higher than boron (899 against 801 kJ mol⁻¹) because beryllium's 2s sublevel is full (1s² 2s²), a stable arrangement.
  • Boron's outer electron is alone in a 2p orbital (1s² 2s² 2p¹), which is slightly higher in energy and more screened, so it is removed more easily.
  • Nitrogen is higher than oxygen (1402 against 1314 kJ mol⁻¹) because nitrogen's 2p sublevel is half full, with one electron in each orbital (2p³), a stable arrangement.
  • In oxygen (2p⁴) one 2p orbital holds a pair of electrons, and repulsion between the paired electrons makes one of them easier to remove.
  • The same pattern occurs in period 3: magnesium (738) is higher than aluminium (578), and phosphorus (1012) is higher than sulfur (1000).
  • Diagram a graph of first ionisation energy against atomic number for elements 1 to 20, showing peaks at the noble gases, troughs at the alkali metals and small dips after Be, N, Mg and P.

Successive ionisation energies (Higher Level only)

  • Second ionisation energy: the energy needed to remove an electron from a 1+ ion, not a second electron from the neutral atom: X⁺(g) → X²⁺(g) + e⁻; the third and later follow the same pattern.
  • Successive ionisation energies always increase, because each electron is removed from an increasingly positive ion, so the same nuclear charge holds fewer electrons more tightly.
  • A very large jump occurs when an electron is removed from a new, inner energy level, which is closer to the nucleus and less screened.
  • Magnesium: 738, 1451 and 7733 kJ mol⁻¹; the big jump between the second and third shows two outer electrons, so magnesium is in group 2.
  • Sodium: 496 then 4562 kJ mol⁻¹; the big jump after the first shows one outer electron, so sodium is in group 1.
  • A graph of the log of successive ionisation energies against electron number for potassium shows jumps that group its electrons into 2, 8, 8 and 1, which is evidence for main energy levels.

Key terms

Alkali metal
A group 1 metal, with one outer electron, that is soft, very reactive and forms 1+ ions.
Alkaline earth metal
A group 2 metal, with two outer electrons, that forms 2+ ions.
Halogen
A group 17 non-metal, with seven outer electrons, that gains one electron to form a 1− halide ion.
Noble gas
A group 18 element that exists as single atoms and is unreactive because its outer energy level is full.
Monatomic
Describes a substance whose particles are single atoms, as in the noble gases.
Displacement reaction
A reaction in which a more reactive element takes the place of a less reactive element in a compound.
Inert
Describes a substance that is very unreactive and does not take part in chemical reactions under normal conditions.
Diatomic molecule
A molecule made of two atoms of the same element, such as Cl₂.
Atomic number
The number of protons in an atom, which increases by one from each element to the next.
Relative atomic mass
The average mass of an atom of an element compared with one-twelfth of the mass of a carbon-12 atom.
Isotopes
Atoms of the same element with different numbers of neutrons, which affect the relative atomic mass.
Periodicity
The repeating pattern of properties across the periodic table, caused by the repeating outer-electron arrangements.
Octet rule
The tendency of atoms to react to reach a full outer level of eight electrons.
Electronegativity
The ability of an atom to attract bonding electrons towards itself in a bond.
Pauling scale
The scale on which electronegativity values are measured, with fluorine the highest at 3.98.
Metalloid
An element with properties between those of metals and non-metals, such as silicon.
Semiconductor
A substance whose ability to conduct electricity lies between that of a conductor and an insulator, such as silicon.
Giant covalent structure
A structure in which atoms are linked by covalent bonds in a continuous network, as in silicon.
First ionisation energy
The minimum energy needed to remove the most loosely bound electron from a neutral gaseous atom in its ground state, in kJ mol⁻¹.
Second ionisation energy
The energy needed to remove an electron from a 1+ gaseous ion, X⁺(g) → X²⁺(g) + e⁻.
Successive ionisation energies
The energies needed to remove each electron in turn from an atom or ion, each one higher than the last.
Atomic (covalent) radius
Half the distance between the nuclei of two atoms of the same element joined by a single covalent bond.
Effective nuclear charge
The net positive pull of the nucleus on the outer electrons, which increases with the number of protons.
Screening
The shielding of outer electrons from the full nuclear charge by inner electrons.
Ground state
The lowest-energy arrangement of the electrons in an atom.
Sublevel
A subdivision of a main energy level (s, p or d) that holds a fixed number of electrons.
Orbital
A region of space within a sublevel that holds at most two electrons of opposite spin.
