Boyle's law (constant temperature)

For a fixed mass of gas at constant temperature, the pressure and volume are linked by Boyle's law.

  • Boyle's law: at constant temperature, the volume of a fixed mass of gas is inversely proportional to its pressure: P₁V₁ = P₂V₂.
  • The law was stated by Robert Boyle in 1662, and Charles's law is named after Jacques Charles.
  • Particle explanation: in a smaller volume the particles hit the walls more often, so the pressure rises.
  • Example: 200 cm³ of gas at 100 kPa is compressed to 50 cm³ at the same temperature.
  • Step 1: Rearrange P₁V₁ = P₂V₂ to P₂ = P₁V₁ ÷ V₂.
  • Step 2: P₂ = 100 kPa × 200 cm³ ÷ 50 cm³ = 400 kPa.

Charles's law (constant pressure)

For a fixed mass of gas at constant pressure, the volume and temperature are linked by Charles's law.

  • Charles's law: at constant pressure, the volume of a fixed mass of gas is directly proportional to its temperature in kelvin: V₁/T₁ = V₂/T₂.
  • Particle explanation: hotter particles move faster and hit the walls harder, so the gas expands to keep the pressure the same.
  • Example: 300 cm³ of gas at 27 °C (300 K) is heated to 127 °C (400 K) at constant pressure.
  • Step 1: Rearrange V₁/T₁ = V₂/T₂ to V₂ = V₁ × T₂ ÷ T₁.
  • Step 2: V₂ = 300 cm³ × 400 K ÷ 300 K = 400 cm³.

The pressure law and the combined gas law

  • Pressure law (Gay-Lussac's law): at constant volume, the pressure of a fixed mass of gas is directly proportional to its temperature in kelvin: P₁/T₁ = P₂/T₂.
  • Particle explanation: faster particles hit the walls more often and harder, so the pressure rises.
  • Combined gas law: P₁V₁/T₁ = P₂V₂/T₂, for a fixed mass of gas.

Kelvin and absolute zero

  • Temperatures in the gas laws must be in kelvin (K), not in °C; using °C gives wrong answers.
  • Absolute zero (0 K = −273 °C) is the temperature at which, in theory, an ideal gas would have zero volume.
  • At absolute zero the particles would stop moving.

The ideal gas

The ideal gas is a model that allows analysis and prediction of how gases behave.

  • An ideal gas is a gas that obeys the gas laws, and PV = nRT, exactly under all conditions.
  • The ideal gas model assumes that the gas particles are in constant random motion.
  • The ideal gas model assumes that the volume of the particles themselves is negligible compared with the volume of the container.
  • The ideal gas model assumes that there are no attractive or repulsive forces between the particles.
  • The ideal gas model assumes that collisions are perfectly elastic.
  • The ideal gas model assumes that the average kinetic energy is proportional to the kelvin temperature.
  • No real gas is ideal, but most gases behave nearly ideally at room temperature and pressure, so the model allows useful predictions.

Deviations of real gases (Higher Level only)

  • Real gases deviate most from ideal behaviour at high pressure and low temperature, and behave most ideally at low pressure and high temperature.
  • At high pressure the particles are squeezed close together, so their own volume is no longer negligible compared with the volume of the gas.
  • At low temperature the particles move slowly, so the attractive forces between them become significant; this is close to the point where the gas condenses.
  • Small, non-polar particles with weak intermolecular forces, such as helium and hydrogen, behave most ideally.
  • Polar molecules with strong attractions, especially those that hydrogen bond, such as ammonia and water vapour, deviate most.

The ideal gas equation

The ideal gas equation combines the gas laws into one relationship between pressure, volume, amount and temperature.

  • PV = nRT, where P is pressure in pascals (Pa), V is volume in cubic metres (m³), n is the number of moles, and T is temperature in kelvin (K).
  • R is the gas constant, 8.314 J K⁻¹ mol⁻¹.
  • The equation combines Boyle's law, Charles's law, the pressure law and Avogadro's law into one equation.
  • The equation applies exactly only to an ideal gas, but it gives good results for real gases at ordinary conditions.

Converting units first

Mixed units are the commonest error, so convert every quantity before substituting into PV = nRT.

  • Pressure: kPa × 1000 = Pa, so 101 kPa = 101 000 Pa.
  • Volume: cm³ × 10⁻⁶ = m³, so 250 cm³ = 2.50 × 10⁻⁴ m³.
  • Volume: L × 10⁻³ = m³.
  • Temperature: °C + 273 = K.

Finding the volume of a gas

  • Given: 0.50 mol of nitrogen at 27 °C and 100 kPa.
  • Step 1: Convert the temperature: 27 °C + 273 = 300 K.
  • Step 2: Convert the pressure: 100 kPa = 100 000 Pa.
  • Step 3: Rearrange the equation to V = nRT ÷ P.
  • Step 4: V = 0.50 × 8.314 × 300 ÷ 100 000 = 0.0125 m³ (to 3 significant figures).
  • Step 5: Convert to litres: 0.0125 m³ × 1000 = 12.5 L.

Finding the moles of a gas

  • Given: 600 cm³ of oxygen at 20 °C and 101 kPa.
  • Step 1: Convert the volume: 600 cm³ = 6.00 × 10⁻⁴ m³; the temperature is 20 °C + 273 = 293 K.
  • Step 2: Rearrange the equation to n = PV ÷ RT.
  • Step 3: n = (101 000 × 6.00 × 10⁻⁴) ÷ (8.314 × 293) = 60.6 ÷ 2436 = 0.0249 mol.

Finding relative molecular mass from mass

  • Since n = m ÷ M, the molar mass is M = mRT ÷ PV.
  • Given: 1.60 g of a gas occupies 1.20 L at 27 °C and 101 kPa (sample data).
  • Step 1: Convert the volume to m³: 1.20 L = 1.20 × 10⁻³ m³; the temperature is 27 °C + 273 = 300 K.
  • Step 2: n = PV ÷ RT = (101 000 × 1.20 × 10⁻³) ÷ (8.314 × 300) = 121.2 ÷ 2494 = 0.0486 mol.
  • Step 3: M = m ÷ n = 1.60 g ÷ 0.0486 mol = 32.9 g mol⁻¹.
  • The answer is close to 32, the Mr of oxygen, so the gas could be oxygen (sample data).

Finding the pressure in a sealed container

  • Given: 0.10 mol of gas in a 2.0 L container at 25 °C.
  • Step 1: Convert the volume to m³: 2.0 L = 2.0 × 10⁻³ m³; the temperature is 25 °C + 273 = 298 K.
  • Step 2: Rearrange the equation to P = nRT ÷ V.
  • Step 3: P = 0.10 × 8.314 × 298 ÷ 2.0 × 10⁻³ = 1.24 × 10⁵ Pa = 124 kPa.

Checking answers

  • Check the units of the answer, and check whether the answer is sensible.
  • A mole of gas at room conditions occupies about 24 L, so a volume far from this for one mole signals an error.

Verifying the ideal gas equation (investigation)

The equation can be verified by comparing a measured gas volume with the volume it predicts.

  • Aim: to verify the ideal gas equation by comparing a measured gas volume with the volume it predicts.
  • Step 1: React a weighed piece of magnesium ribbon, e.g. 0.048 g, with excess dilute hydrochloric acid in a flask connected to a gas syringe: Mg(s) + 2HCl(aq) → MgCl₂(aq) + H₂(g).
  • Step 2: When the reaction stops and the apparatus has cooled to room temperature, record the volume of hydrogen, the room temperature and the atmospheric pressure.
  • Step 3: Moles of hydrogen = moles of magnesium = 0.048 g ÷ 24 g mol⁻¹ = 0.0020 mol, using Ar(Mg) = 24.
  • Step 4: Predicted volume V = nRT ÷ P = 0.0020 × 8.314 × 293 ÷ 101 000 = 4.82 × 10⁻⁵ m³ = 48.2 cm³ (sample data).
  • Step 5: Compare the measured volume with the predicted volume; close agreement verifies the equation for a gas behaving nearly ideally.
  • Diagram a conical flask containing acid, with the magnesium added, connected by a delivery tube to a gas syringe.

Other ways to verify the equation

  • A gas syringe connected to a pressure sensor shows that PV is constant at constant temperature.
  • A sealed flask in a water bath shows that P/T is constant.

Key terms

Boyle's law
At constant temperature, the volume of a fixed mass of gas is inversely proportional to its pressure: P₁V₁ = P₂V₂.
Charles's law
At constant pressure, the volume of a fixed mass of gas is directly proportional to its temperature in kelvin.
Pressure law
At constant volume, the pressure of a fixed mass of gas is directly proportional to its temperature in kelvin.
Combined gas law
The relationship P₁V₁/T₁ = P₂V₂/T₂ for a fixed mass of gas.
Absolute zero
The lowest possible temperature, 0 K or −273 °C, at which an ideal gas would have zero volume.
Kelvin
The SI unit of temperature used in the gas laws, where 0 K is absolute zero.
Ideal gas
A gas that obeys the gas laws and PV = nRT exactly under all conditions.
Intermolecular forces
The attractive forces between particles that become significant for real gases at high pressure and low temperature.
Ideal gas equation
The relationship PV = nRT, which links the pressure, volume, amount in moles and kelvin temperature of an ideal gas.
Gas constant (R)
The constant 8.314 J K⁻¹ mol⁻¹ used in the ideal gas equation.
Pascal (Pa)
The SI unit of pressure; 1 kPa is 1000 Pa.
Kelvin (K)
The SI unit of temperature, found from degrees Celsius by adding 273.
Mole (mol)
The SI unit of amount of substance, represented by n in the ideal gas equation.
Molar mass (M)
The mass of one mole of a substance, in grams per mole (g mol⁻¹).
