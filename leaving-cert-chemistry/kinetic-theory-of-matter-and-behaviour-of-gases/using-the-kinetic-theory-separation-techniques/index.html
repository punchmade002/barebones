Explaining the properties of the states

  • Solids have a fixed shape and volume and cannot be compressed: the particles are held tightly in fixed positions by strong forces and only vibrate.
  • Liquids have a fixed volume but take the shape of their container and flow: the particles are close together but can slide past one another.
  • Gases fill their container and are easily compressed: the particles are far apart, with weak forces between them, and move rapidly and randomly.
  • Gas pressure is caused by gas particles colliding with the walls of the container.

Changes of state

Each change of state is explained by the energy of the particles and the forces between them.

  • Melting (solid to liquid): heating makes the particles vibrate more until they have enough energy to overcome some of the forces holding them in place and begin to move past one another; this happens at the melting point.
  • Freezing (liquid to solid): cooling removes energy, the particles slow down, and the forces pull them back into fixed positions.
  • Evaporation (liquid to gas, below the boiling point): the fastest-moving particles at the surface escape, and the remaining liquid cools.
  • Boiling (liquid to gas at the boiling point): particles throughout the liquid have enough energy to overcome the forces between them, so bubbles of vapour form within the liquid.
  • Condensation (gas to liquid): cooling slows the particles so that the attractive forces pull them close together.
  • Sublimation (solid to gas directly, without melting), e.g. iodine, and solid carbon dioxide (“dry ice”), which sublimes at atmospheric pressure at −78 °C.
  • Deposition (gas to solid directly), the reverse of sublimation, e.g. iodine vapour forming crystals on a cold surface.
  • Diagram a triangle of the three states with arrows labelled melting, freezing, evaporation and boiling, condensation, sublimation and deposition.

Heating curve

  • When a pure solid is heated at a steady rate, its temperature rises until it reaches the melting point, then stays constant until it has all melted.
  • During a change of state the energy supplied is used to overcome the forces between particles, not to increase their kinetic energy, so the temperature does not rise.
  • The temperature then rises again until the boiling point, where it stays constant until all the liquid has boiled.
  • The boiling plateau is longer than the melting plateau because far more energy is needed to separate the particles completely than to loosen them.
  • Diagram a heating curve for water (temperature against time) from ice below 0 °C to steam above 100 °C, with the two flat sections labelled melting at 0 °C and boiling at 100 °C.

Assumptions of the model (Higher Level only)

The kinetic theory is a model. Its assumptions and limitations show when it explains behaviour well and when it does not.

  • Assumption: the particles are tiny spheres in constant, random motion.
  • Assumption: the higher the temperature, the greater the average kinetic energy of the particles.
  • Assumption: collisions between particles are perfectly elastic, so no kinetic energy is lost.
  • Assumption: in a gas the volume of the particles themselves is negligible, and there are no forces between the particles except during collisions.
  • Limitation: real particles are not hard spheres, and they do attract one another; the forces between particles are what make liquids and solids exist and decide melting and boiling points, which the simple gas model ignores.
  • Limitation: the model treats all particles as identical, so it does not explain why different substances have different melting and boiling points; that needs the types of bonding and intermolecular forces (see “Intermolecular forces and physical properties”).
  • Limitation: the model does not describe the structure inside particles or what happens to bonds, and it is qualitative, giving no values.
  • Limitation: in real gases at high pressure or low temperature the particles' volume and attractions matter, so gases deviate from the model (see “Gas relationships and the ideal gas”).

Choosing a technique

Choose the technique from the physical property in which the components differ.

  • Particle size and solubility separate by filtration; solubility and volatility by evaporation and crystallisation; boiling point by distillation; solubility in a solvent and attraction to paper by chromatography; immiscibility and density by a separating funnel.
  • Heterogeneous mixtures, such as an insoluble solid in a liquid or two immiscible liquids, are separated by filtration, decanting or a separating funnel.
  • Homogeneous mixtures, such as solutions and miscible liquids, need evaporation, crystallisation, distillation or chromatography.

Filtration

Filtration separates an insoluble solid from a liquid.

  • Example: sand is separated from salt solution by filtration.
  • Step 1: Fold filter paper into a cone and place it in a funnel.
  • Step 2: Pour the mixture through the filter paper; the solid (residue) stays on the paper and the liquid (filtrate) passes through.
  • Diagram filter funnel with filter paper over a conical flask, labelled residue and filtrate.

Evaporation and crystallisation

  • Evaporation recovers a dissolved solid by heating the solution until all the solvent has gone, e.g. salt from salt water; it is used when the solid does not decompose on heating.
  • Step 1 of crystallisation: Heat the solution to evaporate some solvent until it is saturated; a drop on a cold glass rod forms crystals when it is saturated.
  • Step 2 of crystallisation: Leave the solution to cool slowly so that crystals form, e.g. copper(II) sulfate crystals.
  • Step 3 of crystallisation: Filter off the crystals, wash them with a little cold solvent and dry them.
  • Crystallisation gives purer, larger crystals than evaporating to dryness, because soluble impurities stay in the solution.

Distillation

Distillation separates a liquid from a solution, or two liquids with different boiling points, by boiling and condensing the vapour.

  • Simple distillation separates a solvent from a solution, or two liquids with very different boiling points, e.g. pure water from salt water.
  • Step 1: Heat the mixture in a flask containing anti-bumping granules.
  • Step 2: The liquid with the lower boiling point boils, and its vapour passes into a water-cooled condenser.
  • Step 3: The vapour condenses in the condenser, which has water in at the bottom and out at the top.
  • Step 4: The distillate is collected in a receiving flask.
  • Step 5: A thermometer at the side arm reads the boiling point of the liquid that is distilling.
  • Fractional distillation separates miscible liquids with close boiling points, using a fractionating column, e.g. ethanol (boiling point 78 °C) from water (boiling point 100 °C).
  • Crude oil is separated into fractions by fractional distillation.
  • Safety: flammable liquids are heated with a heating mantle or water bath, not a naked flame, and a closed system is never heated.
  • Diagram a distillation set-up with a round-bottomed flask and granules, the thermometer bulb at the side arm, a Liebig condenser with water flow, and a receiving flask.

Paper chromatography

Paper chromatography separates and identifies small amounts of soluble substances, such as the dyes in an ink or food colouring, or a mixture of indicators.

  • Step 1: Draw a pencil line near the bottom of the chromatography paper; use pencil, because ink would run.
  • Step 2: Put small spots of the mixture and of known substances on the line.
  • Step 3: Stand the paper in a little solvent in a covered container, with the solvent level below the spots.
  • Step 4: Let the solvent rise up the paper, then remove the paper and mark the solvent front.
  • Principle: components that are more soluble in the solvent (the mobile phase) and less attracted to the paper (the stationary phase) travel further.
  • Retention factor (Rf) is the distance moved by the spot divided by the distance moved by the solvent.
  • Components are identified by matching their spots and Rf values with those of known substances.
  • Diagram a chromatogram with the pencil start line, the spots, the solvent front and the separated spots.

Separating funnel

A separating funnel separates two immiscible liquids of different density.

  • Examples: oil and water, or an organic layer from an aqueous layer, which is used to purify an ester.
  • Step 1: Pour the mixture into the separating funnel and let the layers settle.
  • Step 2: Remove the stopper and run off the lower, denser layer through the tap.
  • Step 3: Collect the upper layer separately.
  • Diagram a separating funnel with two layers, the stopper and the tap labelled.

Key terms

Melting
The change of state from solid to liquid when the particles gain enough energy to move past one another.
Freezing
The change of state from liquid to solid as the particles lose energy and are pulled into fixed positions.
Evaporation
The escape of the fastest-moving particles from the surface of a liquid at any temperature below the boiling point.
Boiling
The change of state from liquid to gas throughout the liquid, at the boiling point, with bubbles of vapour forming within it.
Condensation
The change of state from gas to liquid as the particles are cooled and pulled close together.
Sublimation
The change of state from solid directly to gas, without melting.
Deposition
The change of state from gas directly to solid, without becoming a liquid.
Heating curve
A graph of temperature against time for a substance heated at a steady rate, with flat sections at its melting and boiling points.
Filtration
A technique that separates an insoluble solid from a liquid by passing the mixture through filter paper.
Residue
The solid that remains on the filter paper after filtration.
Filtrate
The liquid that passes through the filter paper during filtration.
Crystallisation
A technique that forms pure crystals by evaporating some solvent from a solution, then cooling it slowly.
Distillation
A technique that separates liquids by boiling the mixture and condensing the vapour that is given off.
Fractional distillation
Distillation using a fractionating column to separate miscible liquids with close boiling points.
Immiscible
Describes liquids that do not mix, forming two separate layers.
Paper chromatography
A technique that separates soluble substances by the different rates at which they travel up paper in a solvent.
Mobile phase
The solvent that carries the components up the chromatography paper.
Stationary phase
The paper, which holds back the components according to how strongly they are attracted to it.
Retention factor (Rf)
The distance moved by a spot divided by the distance moved by the solvent front.
