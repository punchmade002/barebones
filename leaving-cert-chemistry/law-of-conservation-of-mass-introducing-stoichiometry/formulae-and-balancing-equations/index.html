Definitions

  • Empirical formula: the simplest whole-number ratio of the atoms of each element in a compound, e.g. CH₂O.
  • Molecular formula: the actual number of atoms of each element in one molecule, e.g. C₆H₁₂O₆ for glucose.
  • The molecular formula is a whole-number multiple of the empirical formula.
  • For ionic compounds the formula is always the empirical formula, e.g. NaCl and MgO.

Percentage composition by mass

  • Relative atomic masses used in this section: H 1, C 12, N 14, O 16, Mg 24, S 32, Cu 63.5.
  • Percentage by mass of an element = (number of atoms of the element × Ar ÷ Mr) × 100.
  • Example: nitrogen in ammonium nitrate, NH₄NO₃ (Mr = 80): (2 × 14 ÷ 80) × 100 = 35.0%.
  • Example: water in hydrated copper(II) sulfate, CuSO₄·5H₂O (Mr = 249.5): (5 × 18 ÷ 249.5) × 100 = 36.1%.

Finding an empirical formula

  • Step 1: Write the mass (or percentage) of each element.
  • Step 2: Divide each by its relative atomic mass to get the number of moles.
  • Step 3: Divide each by the smallest number of moles to get the ratio.
  • Step 4: If the ratio is not whole (e.g. 1.5 or 1.33), multiply all values by 2 or 3 to get whole numbers.
  • Do not round a ratio such as 1.5 to 2; multiply all the values up instead.
  • Example from percentages (40.0% C, 6.7% H, 53.3% O): C 40.0 ÷ 12 = 3.33; H 6.7 ÷ 1 = 6.7; O 53.3 ÷ 16 = 3.33.
  • Dividing by the smallest value, 3.33, gives H 6.7 ÷ 3.33 = 2.0, so the ratio is 1 : 2 : 1 and the empirical formula is CH₂O.
  • Example from masses (sample data): 2.40 g of magnesium burns to give 4.00 g of magnesium oxide.
  • The mass of oxygen is the mass of oxide minus the mass of metal: 4.00 − 2.40 = 1.60 g.
  • Mg 2.40 ÷ 24 = 0.100 mol and O 1.60 ÷ 16 = 0.100 mol.
  • The ratio Mg : O is 1 : 1, so the formula is MgO.

Finding a molecular formula

  • Step 1: Divide the relative molecular mass (Mr) by the mass of the empirical formula unit.
  • Step 2: Multiply every number in the empirical formula by that result.
  • Example: empirical formula CH₂O (mass 30) and Mr = 180: 180 ÷ 30 = 6, so the molecular formula is C₆H₁₂O₆.
  • Example: a hydrocarbon of 85.7% C and 14.3% H, with Mr 56: C 85.7 ÷ 12 = 7.14 and H 14.3 ÷ 1 = 14.3.
  • Dividing by 7.14 gives the ratio 1 : 2, so the empirical formula is CH₂ (mass 14).
  • 56 ÷ 14 = 4, so the molecular formula is C₄H₈.

Investigation: formula of magnesium oxide

  • Step 1: Weigh a clean crucible and lid, add a coiled length of clean magnesium ribbon and reweigh.
  • Step 2: Heat strongly, lifting the lid slightly from time to time to let air in while keeping white smoke (magnesium oxide) from escaping.
  • Step 3: When the magnesium no longer glows, remove the lid and heat strongly for a few more minutes.
  • Step 4: Cool and reweigh, then heat, cool and reweigh again until the mass is constant.
  • Step 5: Calculate the masses of magnesium and of oxygen that combined, then their moles and ratio, as in the mass example for empirical formulae.
  • Safety: do not look directly at burning magnesium, which is very bright; the crucible is very hot, so use tongs and a heat-proof mat.
  • Diagram a crucible with its lid on a pipeclay triangle, on a tripod over a Bunsen burner; label the crucible, lid, pipeclay triangle, tripod and Bunsen burner.

Water of crystallisation

  • Water of crystallisation: water chemically held in a fixed ratio within the crystals of some salts, shown after a dot in the formula, e.g. CuSO₄·5H₂O and Na₂CO₃·10H₂O.
  • Heating to constant mass drives off the water of crystallisation; for example, blue hydrated copper(II) sulfate turns white, becoming anhydrous.
  • Example (sample data): 2.50 g of CuSO₄·xH₂O gives 1.60 g of anhydrous CuSO₄ (Mr 159.5) when heated to constant mass.
  • Mass of water lost: 2.50 − 1.60 = 0.90 g.
  • Moles of water: 0.90 ÷ 18 = 0.050 mol (Mr of H₂O = 18).
  • Moles of CuSO₄: 1.60 ÷ 159.5 = 0.0100 mol.
  • Ratio of water to CuSO₄ = 0.050 : 0.0100 = 5 : 1, so x = 5 and the formula is CuSO₄·5H₂O.

What an equation shows

  • A chemical equation shows the reactants (left) and products (right), written as formulae.
  • A balanced equation has the same number of atoms of each element on both sides, because atoms are conserved (law of conservation of mass).
  • State symbols: (s) solid, (l) liquid, (g) gas, (aq) aqueous, meaning dissolved in water.
  • The coefficients, the large numbers in front of formulae, give the ratio of moles, or molecules, that react.
  • For example, 2H₂ + O₂ → 2H₂O means 2 mol of hydrogen react with 1 mol of oxygen to give 2 mol of water.

Balancing method

  • Step 1: Write the correct formula of every reactant and product.
  • Never change a formula to balance an equation; change only the coefficients.
  • Step 2: Count the atoms of each element on each side.
  • Step 3: Balance by putting coefficients in front of formulae, starting with an element that appears in only one substance on each side.
  • Leave hydrogen, oxygen and any element on its own until last when balancing.
  • Step 4: Check every element again and make sure the coefficients are the smallest whole numbers.
  • Add state symbols to every formula.
  • Hydrogen, nitrogen, oxygen, fluorine, chlorine, bromine and iodine are diatomic and are written as H₂, N₂, O₂, F₂, Cl₂, Br₂ and I₂.

Worked examples

  • Magnesium burning: 2Mg(s) + O₂(g) → 2MgO(s).
  • Methane burning: CH₄(g) + 2O₂(g) → CO₂(g) + 2H₂O(l).
  • Propane burning: C₃H₈(g) + 5O₂(g) → 3CO₂(g) + 4H₂O(l).
  • Ethane burning, with the fractional coefficient 3½ for O₂ doubled to give whole numbers: 2C₂H₆(g) + 7O₂(g) → 4CO₂(g) + 6H₂O(l).
  • Aluminium and hydrochloric acid: 2Al(s) + 6HCl(aq) → 2AlCl₃(aq) + 3H₂(g).
  • Neutralisation: 2NaOH(aq) + H₂SO₄(aq) → Na₂SO₄(aq) + 2H₂O(l).
  • Decomposition of hydrogen peroxide: 2H₂O₂(aq) → 2H₂O(l) + O₂(g).
  • Calcium carbonate and hydrochloric acid: CaCO₃(s) + 2HCl(aq) → CaCl₂(aq) + H₂O(l) + CO₂(g).
  • Sodium and water: 2Na(s) + 2H₂O(l) → 2NaOH(aq) + H₂(g).

Ionic equations

  • An ionic equation shows only the ions that take part in the reaction, leaving out spectator ions (ions that are unchanged in solution).
  • Example: AgNO₃(aq) + NaCl(aq) → AgCl(s) + NaNO₃(aq) becomes Ag⁺(aq) + Cl⁻(aq) → AgCl(s).
  • In that reaction Na⁺ and NO₃⁻ are spectator ions.
  • Neutralisation of any strong acid by a strong base is H⁺(aq) + OH⁻(aq) → H₂O(l).
  • An ionic equation must balance in charge as well as in atoms.

Mole ratios from equations

  • The balanced equation gives the mole ratio of any two substances in the reaction.
  • In propane burning, 1 mol of propane needs 5 mol of oxygen and produces 3 mol of carbon dioxide.
  • This mole ratio is the starting point for calculating masses of reactants and products, taught in the chapter “Stoichiometry: Limiting Reactants and Percentage Yield”.

Key terms

Empirical formula
The simplest whole-number ratio of the atoms of each element in a compound.
Molecular formula
The actual number of atoms of each element in one molecule of a compound.
Relative atomic mass (Ar)
The average mass of an atom of an element compared with one twelfth of the mass of a carbon-12 atom.
Relative molecular mass (Mr)
The sum of the relative atomic masses of all the atoms in a formula.
Mole
The amount of a substance whose mass in grams equals its relative atomic or molecular mass.
Percentage composition
The percentage by mass of each element in a compound.
Water of crystallisation
Water held in a fixed ratio within the crystals of some salts, shown after a dot in the formula.
Anhydrous
Describes a salt with no water of crystallisation.
Balanced equation
An equation with the same number of atoms of each element on both sides.
Coefficient
The large number written in front of a formula that gives the number of moles or molecules in the equation.
State symbol
A symbol in brackets after a formula, (s), (l), (g) or (aq), showing its physical state.
Aqueous
Dissolved in water.
Diatomic element
An element whose molecules contain two atoms, such as O₂.
Ionic equation
An equation showing only the ions that take part in a reaction.
Spectator ion
An ion that is unchanged in solution and is left out of an ionic equation.
Mole ratio
The ratio of the moles of two substances, given by the coefficients in a balanced equation.
