Physical and chemical properties

  • A physical property can be observed or measured without changing the substance into a new substance.
  • Physical properties include colour, state, density, melting point, boiling point, solubility, electrical conductivity and hardness.
  • A chemical property describes how a substance reacts to form new substances.
  • Chemical properties include flammability, reactivity with water, oxygen or acids, whether a substance is acidic or basic, and how it decomposes on heating.

Physical change

  • A physical change is one in which no new substance is formed; the particles are not changed, only their arrangement or spacing.
  • A physical change is usually easily reversed and involves only a small energy change.
  • A change of state is always a physical change.
  • Examples of physical change: melting ice, boiling water, dissolving sugar or salt in water, sublimation of iodine, crushing a solid and magnetising iron.
  • The mass is unchanged in a physical change.

Chemical change

  • A chemical change, or chemical reaction, is one in which one or more new substances are formed, with different properties.
  • In a chemical change bonds are broken and new bonds are made, so the atoms are rearranged into new particles.
  • A chemical change is usually difficult to reverse.
  • A chemical change usually involves a noticeable energy change.
  • Total mass is conserved in a chemical change too, if nothing escapes (see “Investigation: conservation of mass”).

Signs of a chemical change

  • A gas is given off (effervescence).
  • A precipitate forms.
  • A permanent colour change occurs.
  • The temperature changes.
  • Light is produced.
  • A smell is produced.
  • A colour change alone is not proof of a chemical change: dissolving copper sulfate in water gives a blue solution, which is a physical change.

Examples of chemical change

  • Burning magnesium forms white magnesium oxide.
  • Rusting of iron.
  • Cooking an egg.
  • Magnesium fizzing in hydrochloric acid.
  • Heating copper(II) carbonate decomposes it: the green solid forms black copper(II) oxide and carbon dioxide.
  • Neutralisation of an acid by a base, with the equations given in “Balancing equations”.

Comparing physical and chemical change

  • No new substance is formed in a physical change, but a new substance is formed in a chemical change.
  • A physical change is easily reversed; a chemical change is usually difficult to reverse.
  • A physical change involves a small energy change; a chemical change usually involves a large energy change.
  • Particles are unchanged in a physical change, but atoms are rearranged into new particles in a chemical change.
  • Borderline case: dissolving salt in water is a physical change, because evaporating the water recovers the salt unchanged, even though its ions are separated.

Law of conservation of mass

  • Law of conservation of mass: in a chemical reaction or physical change, matter is neither created nor destroyed, so the total mass of the products equals the total mass of the reactants.
  • Antoine Lavoisier established the law in the late 1700s (published 1789) by careful weighing of reactions in sealed vessels.
  • The number of moles or molecules may change in a reaction, but the mass does not.

Law of conservation of energy

  • Law of conservation of energy: energy cannot be created or destroyed, only changed from one form to another.
  • When a fuel burns, chemical energy is changed to heat and light.
  • The total energy of a reaction and its surroundings stays the same.

Why the laws matter

  • Both laws underpin all physical and chemical changes.
  • Conservation of atoms, and so of mass, is why chemical equations must be balanced.
  • Conservation of energy is why energy changes in reactions can be measured.

Investigation: aim and hypothesis

  • Aim: to verify, using primary data, that mass is conserved in a chemical reaction.
  • Hypothesis: the total mass before and after the reaction will be the same, because atoms are only rearranged.
  • Measured variable: the total mass of the stoppered apparatus before and after the reaction; no variable is changed on purpose.

Investigation: apparatus and method

  • Apparatus: conical flask, small test tube, stopper, electronic balance (0.01 g), sodium chloride solution and silver nitrate solution.
  • Diagram a stoppered conical flask on a balance, containing sodium chloride solution, with a small test tube of silver nitrate solution standing upright inside; label the flask, stopper, test tube and both solutions.
  • Step 1: Pour some sodium chloride solution into the conical flask.
  • Step 2: Half-fill the small test tube with silver nitrate solution and lower it carefully, upright, into the flask so the solutions don't mix.
  • Step 3: Stopper the flask and weigh the whole apparatus.
  • Step 4: Tilt the flask so the solutions mix; a white precipitate of silver chloride forms: AgNO₃(aq) + NaCl(aq) → AgCl(s) + NaNO₃(aq).
  • Step 5: Reweigh the stoppered flask, and repeat the investigation three times.

Investigation: results and safety

  • Results: the mass before and after the reaction is the same, within the precision of the balance, so mass is conserved.
  • Safety: silver nitrate stains the skin and is an irritant, so wear gloves and goggles.
  • Lead nitrate is not used in this method because it is toxic.

Open and closed systems

  • In an open flask, marble chips (calcium carbonate) reacting with hydrochloric acid make the mass on the balance decrease: CaCO₃(s) + 2HCl(aq) → CaCl₂(aq) + H₂O(l) + CO₂(g).
  • The carbon dioxide escapes, so the apparent loss of mass is gas leaving the flask, not mass being destroyed.
  • If the same reaction is done in a sealed flask, or with a balloon over the neck to trap the gas, the mass stays the same.
  • When magnesium burns in air, the product is heavier than the magnesium, because oxygen from the air has combined with it: 2Mg + O₂ → 2MgO.

Explaining with models

  • Particle model: in a reaction the atoms in the reactants are rearranged to form the products; no atoms are created or destroyed, so the total mass stays the same.
  • A balanced equation is a symbolic model of conservation: the silver nitrate equation has 1 Ag, 1 N, 3 O, 1 Na and 1 Cl on each side.
  • A physical model, such as molecular model kits, shows the same idea: building the reactant molecules, taking them apart and rebuilding the product molecules uses exactly the same pieces.
  • Diagram particle diagrams of 2H₂ + O₂ → 2H₂O, showing four H atoms and two O atoms before the reaction and after it.

Key terms

Physical property
A property that can be observed or measured without changing the substance into a new substance.
Chemical property
A property that describes how a substance reacts to form new substances.
Physical change
A change in which no new substance is formed and only the arrangement or spacing of the particles alters.
Chemical change
A change in which one or more new substances, with different properties, are formed as bonds are broken and made.
Effervescence
Fizzing caused by a gas being given off during a reaction.
Precipitate
A solid that forms and separates out from a solution during a reaction.
Sublimation
A change of state directly from solid to gas without passing through the liquid state.
Neutralisation
A reaction between an acid and a base that forms a salt and water.
Law of conservation of mass
In a chemical reaction matter is neither created nor destroyed, so the products have the same total mass as the reactants.
Law of conservation of energy
Energy cannot be created or destroyed, only changed from one form to another.
Closed system
A sealed apparatus through which matter cannot enter or leave, so any mass change is measured within it.
Open system
An apparatus through which gas can escape or enter, so the mass on a balance can appear to change.
Hypothesis
A testable prediction of what an investigation will show and why.
Balanced equation
An equation with the same number of atoms of each element on both sides.
Particle model
The idea that matter is made of particles that are rearranged, not created or destroyed, in a reaction.
