Investigation: generating a pH curve (Higher Level only)

A pH curve uses primary data to show how pH changes as one solution is added to another.

  • Aim: to plot how the pH changes as an alkali is added to an acid, using primary data, and to use the curve to choose an indicator.
  • Calibrate the pH meter, or the pH sensor and data-logger, with buffer solutions of known pH.
  • Pipette 25.0 cm³ of the acid into a beaker on a magnetic stirrer, or into a beaker that is swirled; put the pH probe in and record the starting pH.
  • Add the alkali from a burette in 1 cm³ portions, recording the pH after each.
  • Near the equivalence point, add 0.1–0.2 cm³ portions, because the pH changes rapidly there; continue well past it.
  • Plot pH on the y-axis against the volume of alkali added on the x-axis.
  • The equivalence point is the midpoint of the steep, vertical section of the curve.
  • Diagram a burette above a beaker on a magnetic stirrer, with a pH probe connected to a pH meter or data-logger; label burette, beaker, stirrer, probe and meter.

Shapes of pH curves (Higher Level only)

Each curve is for 25 cm³ of acid with base added from a burette, using 0.1 mol L⁻¹ solutions throughout.

  • Strong acid–strong base (HCl and NaOH): starts at about pH 1; rises slowly, then very steeply from about pH 3 to pH 11, with the equivalence point at pH 7; levels off near pH 13.
  • Weak acid–strong base (ethanoic acid and NaOH): starts higher, at about pH 3, with a gentler rise; the steep section is from about pH 7 to 11, with the equivalence point above pH 7 (about 8.7), because the ethanoate ion is a weak base.
  • Strong acid–weak base (HCl and aqueous ammonia): starts at about pH 1; the steep section is from about pH 3 to 7, with the equivalence point below pH 7 (about 5), because the ammonium ion is a weak acid; levels off around pH 9–10.
  • Weak acid–weak base (ethanoic acid and ammonia): has no steep section; the pH changes gradually through the equivalence point, at about pH 7.
  • Diagram the four curves sketched on pH–volume axes, with the steep sections and equivalence points marked, and the colour-change ranges of phenolphthalein and methyl orange shaded.

Choosing an indicator from the curve (Higher Level only)

  • An indicator is suitable if its colour-change range lies completely within the steep section of the curve, so that one drop at the equivalence point changes its colour.
  • Strong acid–strong base: phenolphthalein (pH 8.3–10) or methyl orange (pH 3.1–4.4); both ranges lie within the steep section.
  • Weak acid–strong base (e.g. vinegar with NaOH): phenolphthalein; methyl orange would change colour too early.
  • Strong acid–weak base (e.g. HCl with sodium carbonate or ammonia): methyl orange; phenolphthalein would change colour too early, while the solution is still alkaline and before the equivalence point.

Weak acid–weak base: the issues (Higher Level only)

  • With no steep section, no indicator changes colour sharply at the equivalence point, so no indicator is suitable and the end point is unclear.
  • The curve has only a gentle point of inflection, which is hard to locate from the data, and small errors in pH readings move it a lot.
  • The equivalence point must instead be found from the curve itself, at the point of steepest slope, located with a pH meter and many small additions; or such titrations are avoided.
  • The specification limits this topic to monoprotic acids and bases with a single inflection point.

Indicators as weak acids (Higher Level only)

Many acid–base indicators are weak acids, so their colour depends on the position of an equilibrium.

  • Many indicators are weak acids, written HIn, in which the undissociated molecule HIn and its conjugate base In⁻ have different colours.
  • In water an equilibrium is set up: HIn(aq) ⇌ H⁺(aq) + In⁻(aq).
  • Le Châtelier's principle: if a system at equilibrium is disturbed, it shifts to oppose the change.
  • Adding acid increases [H⁺], so the equilibrium shifts to the left: most of the indicator is HIn, and its colour is seen.
  • Adding base removes H⁺ (H⁺ + OH⁻ → H₂O), so the equilibrium shifts to the right: most of the indicator is In⁻, and its colour is seen.
  • Phenolphthalein: HIn is colourless and In⁻ is pink.
  • Methyl orange: HIn is red and In⁻ is yellow.

Indicators as weak bases (Higher Level only)

  • A weak base indicator can be written InOH, with InOH and In⁺ having different colours: InOH(aq) ⇌ In⁺(aq) + OH⁻(aq).
  • Adding acid removes OH⁻, so the equilibrium shifts to the right and the In⁺ colour is seen.
  • Adding base increases [OH⁻], so the equilibrium shifts to the left and the InOH colour is seen.

The colour-change range (Higher Level only)

  • The colour change happens over a range of about two pH units, centred on the pH at which [HIn] = [In⁻].
  • At the midpoint of the range both colours are present; methyl orange looks orange.
  • Different indicators are weak acids of different strengths, so they change colour at different pH values: phenolphthalein at pH 8.3–10 and methyl orange at pH 3.1–4.4.
  • This is why the indicator must be chosen to match the titration; see “pH titration curves”.
  • Only a few drops of indicator are used, because the indicator is itself an acid and would react with some of the titrant.

Aim and chemistry

  • Aim: to find the concentration of ethanoic acid in a commercial vinegar by titration with a standard solution of sodium hydroxide.
  • Equation: CH₃COOH(aq) + NaOH(aq) → CH₃COONa(aq) + H₂O(l), with a mole ratio of 1 : 1.
  • The sodium hydroxide must first be standardised, because it is not a primary standard; see “Primary standards and standard solutions”.
  • Commercial vinegar is typically about 4–6% ethanoic acid, often labelled 5%.

Method

The rinsing and titration technique is as described in “Acid–base titrations”.

  • Dilute the vinegar ten times: pipette 25.0 cm³ of vinegar into a 250 cm³ volumetric flask, make up to the mark with deionised water and invert about 20 times.
  • Rinse the burette with deionised water, then with the standard sodium hydroxide solution, and fill it.
  • Rinse a pipette with deionised water, then with the diluted vinegar, and transfer 25.0 cm³ of diluted vinegar to a conical flask.
  • Add two or three drops of phenolphthalein.
  • Titrate with the sodium hydroxide, swirling, until the solution turns a permanent pale pink; do a rough titration, then accurate titrations until two agree within 0.1 cm³.
  • Diagram a burette of sodium hydroxide above a conical flask of diluted vinegar with phenolphthalein on a white tile; and the 250 cm³ volumetric flask used for the dilution, labelled with the 25.0 cm³ pipette.

Why each choice is made

  • The vinegar is diluted because, undiluted, it would need a very large titre of 0.1 mol L⁻¹ sodium hydroxide, more than a burette holds.
  • Phenolphthalein is used because this is a weak acid–strong base titration, with the equivalence point in the alkaline region (about pH 8.7); methyl orange would change colour too soon.
  • The sodium hydroxide is placed in the burette, so the colour change is from colourless to pink, which is easy to see.
  • Brown or coloured vinegar makes the end point hard to see, so white (clear) vinegar is used.

Sample calculation (sample data)

The steps convert a titre into a concentration, then into mass concentration and %w/v.

  • Mean titre: 20.8 cm³ of 0.100 mol L⁻¹ NaOH is needed for 25.0 cm³ of diluted vinegar.
  • Moles NaOH = 0.100 × 0.0208 = 2.08 × 10⁻³ mol, which equals the moles of ethanoic acid in 25.0 cm³ of diluted vinegar.
  • Concentration of diluted vinegar = 2.08 × 10⁻³ mol ÷ 0.0250 L = 0.0832 mol L⁻¹.
  • Concentration of original vinegar = 0.0832 × 10 = 0.832 mol L⁻¹, because the vinegar was diluted ten times.
  • Mass concentration = 0.832 mol L⁻¹ × 60 g mol⁻¹ = 49.9 g L⁻¹, using M(CH₃COOH) = 60 g mol⁻¹.
  • As %w/v: 49.9 g L⁻¹ ÷ 10 = 4.99% w/v, close to the 5% typical of vinegar.

Safety and errors

  • Safety: sodium hydroxide is corrosive, especially to the eyes, so wear goggles and use a pipette filler.
  • Not rinsing the burette with sodium hydroxide dilutes it, so the titre is too high and the result is too high.
  • Overshooting the end point gives too large a titre, so the result is too high.
  • An air bubble below the burette tap gives a wrong titre.
  • Sodium hydroxide absorbs carbon dioxide from the air over time, changing its concentration, so it should be standardised just before use.

Key terms

pH curve
A graph of pH, on the y-axis, against the volume of titrant added, on the x-axis.
Steep section
The vertical part of a pH curve around the equivalence point, where a small addition of titrant causes a large change in pH.
Equivalence point
The point at which the reactants have reacted in exactly the mole ratio of the balanced equation, found at the midpoint of the steep section.
Point of inflection
The point on a curve where its slope changes direction, which for a weak acid–weak base titration is gentle and hard to locate.
Monoprotic
Describes an acid or base that gives or accepts one proton per molecule.
Buffer solution
A solution of known pH, used to calibrate a pH meter.
Colour-change range
The range of pH over which an indicator changes colour, about two pH units wide.
Weak acid indicator
A weak acid, written HIn, whose undissociated form and conjugate base have different colours.
Conjugate base
The species formed when an acid loses a proton; for an indicator, In⁻ is the conjugate base of HIn.
Undissociated form
The molecule of a weak acid or base that has not split into ions, such as HIn.
Equilibrium (⇌)
A state in which the forward and reverse reactions occur at equal rates, so the concentrations stay constant.
Le Châtelier's principle
If a system at equilibrium is disturbed, it shifts to oppose the change.
Weak base indicator
A weak base, written InOH, whose undissociated form and ion In⁺ have different colours.
Volumetric flask
A flask that holds one fixed volume, such as 250 cm³, when filled to its mark, used to make accurate dilutions.
Dilution factor
The number of times a solution is diluted, found from the ratio of the final volume to the volume of the original solution taken.
Mass concentration
The mass of solute in grams per litre of solution, found as concentration in mol L⁻¹ multiplied by molar mass in g mol⁻¹.
%w/v
The mass of solute in grams per 100 cm³ of solution, equal to the mass concentration in g L⁻¹ divided by 10.
Molar mass
The mass of one mole of a substance in g mol⁻¹, such as 60 g mol⁻¹ for CH₃COOH.
Titre
The volume of solution added from the burette in a titration.
Phenolphthalein
An indicator that is colourless in acid and pink in alkaline solution, used for the titration of vinegar with sodium hydroxide.
Mole ratio
The ratio of the amounts, in moles, of reactants given by the coefficients in a balanced equation.
