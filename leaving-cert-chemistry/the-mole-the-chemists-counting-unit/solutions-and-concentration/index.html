Solutions, solutes and solvents

  • A solution is a uniform (homogeneous) mixture of a solute dissolved in a solvent.
  • The solute is the substance that dissolves; the solvent is the substance it dissolves in, which is water in an aqueous solution.

Unsaturated, saturated and supersaturated solutions

  • An unsaturated solution can still dissolve more solute at that temperature.
  • A saturated solution contains the maximum amount of solute that can dissolve at that temperature, with undissolved solid present.
  • A supersaturated solution contains more dissolved solute than a saturated solution holds at that temperature, and is unstable.
  • A supersaturated solution is made by cooling a hot saturated solution carefully.
  • Adding a small seed crystal to a supersaturated solution makes the excess solute crystallise suddenly.
  • Examples of solutes that form supersaturated solutions: sodium thiosulfate and sodium ethanoate.

Concentration in mol L⁻¹

  • Concentration is the amount of solute dissolved in a given volume of solution, usually in mol L⁻¹ (molarity, M) or g L⁻¹.
  • Concentration (mol L⁻¹) = moles of solute ÷ volume of solution in litres, so c = n ÷ V and n = c × V.
  • Example: 5.30 g of anhydrous sodium carbonate (Na₂CO₃, M = 106 g mol⁻¹): n = 5.30 ÷ 106 = 0.0500 mol.
  • The 0.0500 mol is made up to 500 cm³ (0.500 L): c = 0.0500 ÷ 0.500 = 0.100 mol L⁻¹.
  • Example of a mass needed: 250 cm³ of 0.100 M sodium hydroxide (M = 40 g mol⁻¹): n = 0.100 × 0.250 = 0.0250 mol.
  • The mass needed is m = 0.0250 × 40 = 1.00 g.
  • Model: the same number of solute particles in a smaller volume gives a higher concentration; diluting spreads the same particles through a larger volume.
  • A standard solution is a solution whose concentration is accurately known.

Primary standards

  • A primary standard is a substance that can be weighed out accurately to make a standard solution directly.
  • A primary standard is available in a very pure state.
  • A primary standard is stable in air: it does not absorb water or carbon dioxide, or react with oxygen.
  • A primary standard has a known formula and dissolves readily in water.
  • A primary standard preferably has a high molar mass, which reduces weighing errors.
  • Examples: anhydrous sodium carbonate, used in acid–base titrations.
  • Ammonium iron(II) sulfate, (NH₄)₂Fe(SO₄)₂·6H₂O, dissolved in dilute sulfuric acid, is a primary standard for redox titrations.
  • Sodium hydroxide is not a primary standard because it absorbs water and carbon dioxide from the air.
  • Hydrochloric acid is not a primary standard because its concentration changes as HCl evaporates.
  • Solutions of sodium hydroxide and hydrochloric acid are standardised by titration against a primary standard.

Preparing a standard solution

The method below makes up 250 cm³ of a standard solution from a weighed primary standard.

  • Weigh accurately (to 0.01 g or better) the calculated mass of the primary standard on a clock glass or weighing boat.
  • Transfer the solid to a beaker, and rinse the clock glass into the beaker with deionised water from a wash bottle.
  • Dissolve the solid in a small volume of deionised water, stirring with a glass rod.
  • Pour the solution through a funnel into a volumetric flask of the required volume, e.g. 250 cm³.
  • Rinse the beaker, glass rod and funnel into the flask with deionised water so that no solute is lost.
  • Add deionised water until the flask is near the mark, then add the last drops with a dropper until the bottom of the meniscus sits on the graduation mark, viewed at eye level.
  • Stopper the flask and invert it about 20 times so the solution is uniform.
  • Precision and accuracy: the volumetric flask holds an accurately known volume, whereas a beaker or measuring cylinder is not accurate enough.
  • Diagram a volumetric flask with its graduation mark, and the meniscus shown at eye level.
  • Diagram a funnel in the neck of the volumetric flask during the transfer.
  • Diagram a beaker with a glass rod stirring the dissolving solid.

Dilution and serial dilution

  • Diluting adds solvent, so the number of moles of solute stays the same: c₁V₁ = c₂V₂.
  • Example: to make 250 cm³ of 0.10 M HCl from 1.0 M HCl: V₁ = (0.10 × 250) ÷ 1.0 = 25 cm³.
  • Pipette 25 cm³ of the 1.0 M acid into a 250 cm³ volumetric flask and make up to the mark with water.
  • Use a pipette, with a pipette filler, for the volume of the original solution, and a volumetric flask for the final volume.
  • Diagram a pipette with a pipette filler, filled to the graduation mark.
  • Serial dilution is a series of dilutions, each using the previous solution, usually by the same factor.
  • Example: starting from 1.0 M, take 10 cm³ and make it up to 100 cm³ to give 0.10 M; repeating from that solution gives 0.010 M and then 0.0010 M.
  • Serial dilution is used to make a range of concentrations accurately.

Units of concentration

  • mol L⁻¹ (molarity, M): moles of solute per litre of solution.
  • g L⁻¹: grams of solute per litre of solution.
  • %w/v (weight per volume): grams of solute per 100 cm³ of solution; e.g. 5% w/v glucose contains 5 g per 100 cm³.
  • %v/v (volume per volume): cm³ of solute per 100 cm³ of solution, used for a liquid dissolved in a liquid; e.g. wine labelled 12% v/v contains 12 cm³ of ethanol in every 100 cm³.
  • %w/w (weight per weight): grams of solute per 100 g of the whole mixture, used for solids and creams; e.g. 1% w/w hydrocortisone cream has 1 g in every 100 g.

Converting between units

  • mol L⁻¹ to g L⁻¹: multiply by the molar mass.
  • Example: 0.10 M NaOH × 40 g mol⁻¹ = 4.0 g L⁻¹.
  • g L⁻¹ to mol L⁻¹: divide by the molar mass.
  • Example: 9.8 g L⁻¹ of H₂SO₄ ÷ 98 g mol⁻¹ = 0.10 mol L⁻¹.
  • g L⁻¹ to %w/v: divide by 10, since 100 cm³ is one-tenth of a litre.
  • Example: 4.0 g L⁻¹ = 0.40% w/v; to reverse the step, multiply %w/v by 10 to get g L⁻¹.
  • %w/v to mol L⁻¹: multiply by 10 to get g L⁻¹, then divide by the molar mass.
  • Example: 0.9% w/v physiological saline (NaCl, M = 58.5 g mol⁻¹): 0.9 × 10 = 9.0 g L⁻¹.
  • Then 9.0 g L⁻¹ ÷ 58.5 g mol⁻¹ = 0.15 mol L⁻¹.
  • %v/v to volume: volume of solute = volume of solution × %v/v ÷ 100.
  • Example: ethanol in 750 cm³ of 12% v/v wine = 750 × 12 ÷ 100 = 90 cm³.
  • %v/v to mass, using density: mass = volume × density.
  • Example: 90 cm³ of ethanol × 0.789 g cm⁻³ = 71 g.
  • %w/w to mass: mass of solute = mass of mixture × %w/w ÷ 100.
  • Example: 30 g of a 1% w/w cream contains 30 × 1 ÷ 100 = 0.30 g of the active ingredient.

Parts per million (Higher Level only)

  • p.p.m. (parts per million) is the number of parts of solute in one million parts of solution or mixture; it is used for very dilute solutions, such as pollutants and dissolved substances in water.
  • For dilute aqueous solutions, 1 p.p.m. = 1 mg of solute per litre of solution (1 mg L⁻¹).
  • This holds because 1 L of water has a mass of about 1 kg, which is 10⁶ mg.
  • For solids, 1 p.p.m. = 1 mg per kg.
  • Example: 0.0050 g of a substance in 2.5 L of water: 0.0050 g = 5.0 mg.
  • Then 5.0 mg ÷ 2.5 L = 2.0 p.p.m.
  • 1 p.p.m. = 0.001 g L⁻¹ = 0.0001% w/v.
  • Example: 1.0 mg L⁻¹ of fluoride is 1.0 p.p.m.; fluoridated water in Ireland is about 0.6–0.8 p.p.m. (mg L⁻¹).
  • p.p.m. to mol L⁻¹: convert to g L⁻¹ first, e.g. 2.0 p.p.m. = 2.0 mg L⁻¹ = 0.0020 g L⁻¹, then divide by the molar mass.

Key terms

Solution
A uniform mixture of a solute dissolved in a solvent.
Solute
The substance that dissolves in a solvent to form a solution.
Solvent
The substance in which a solute dissolves; water in an aqueous solution.
Concentration
The amount of solute dissolved in a given volume of solution, usually in mol L⁻¹ or g L⁻¹.
Saturated solution
A solution that contains the maximum amount of solute that can dissolve at that temperature, with undissolved solid present.
Supersaturated solution
A solution containing more dissolved solute than a saturated solution holds at that temperature; it is unstable.
Standard solution
A solution whose concentration is accurately known.
Primary standard
A pure, stable substance of known formula that can be weighed out accurately to make a standard solution directly.
Volumetric flask
A flask that holds an accurately known volume when filled to its graduation mark.
Dilution
Reducing a solution's concentration by adding solvent, which keeps the number of moles of solute the same.
Serial dilution
A series of dilutions, each made from the previous solution, usually by the same factor.
Molarity (mol L⁻¹)
Moles of solute per litre of solution.
g L⁻¹
Grams of solute per litre of solution.
%w/v
Grams of solute per 100 cm³ of solution.
%v/v
Volume of solute in cm³ per 100 cm³ of solution, used for a liquid in a liquid.
%w/w
Grams of solute per 100 g of the whole mixture, used for solids and creams.
Physiological saline
A solution of 0.9% w/v sodium chloride, NaCl.
