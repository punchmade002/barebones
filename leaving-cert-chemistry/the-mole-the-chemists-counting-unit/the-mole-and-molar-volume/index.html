The mole and the Avogadro constant

Atoms are far too small to count, so chemists count them by weighing. The mole links a mass that can be weighed to a number of particles.

  • The mole (symbol mol) is the SI unit of amount of substance.
  • One mole contains exactly 6.02214076 × 10²³ elementary entities (atoms, molecules, ions or electrons); this number is the Avogadro constant, NA = 6.02 × 10²³ mol⁻¹.
  • Before 2019 the mole was defined through carbon-12: the Avogadro constant was the number of atoms in 12 g of carbon-12.
  • Since the 2019 SI redefinition the value of the Avogadro constant is exact by definition.
  • Always state the particle: one mole of oxygen atoms (O) contains 6.02 × 10²³ atoms.
  • One mole of oxygen molecules (O₂) contains 6.02 × 10²³ molecules and 1.204 × 10²⁴ atoms.
  • One mole of water is only 18 g (about 18 cm³, since water has a density of about 1.00 g cm⁻³) but contains 6.02 × 10²³ molecules.

Relative molecular mass, molar mass and density

  • Relative molecular mass (Mr) is the sum of the relative atomic masses of all the atoms in a molecule (or formula unit); it has no units.
  • Relative atomic masses used in this section: H 1, C 12, N 14, O 16, Na 23, Mg 24, S 32, Cl 35.5, Ca 40.
  • Mr(H₂O) = 2(1) + 16 = 18.
  • Mr(H₂SO₄) = 2(1) + 32 + 4(16) = 98.
  • Mr(CaCO₃) = 40 + 12 + 3(16) = 100.
  • Molar mass (M) is the mass of one mole of a substance, in grams per mole (g mol⁻¹); it is numerically equal to Ar or Mr, so the molar mass of water is 18 g mol⁻¹.
  • Density is mass per unit volume: ρ = m ÷ V, in g cm⁻³ or kg m⁻³.

Interconverting mass, moles, particles and volume

  • Moles from mass: n = m ÷ M, with m in g and M in g mol⁻¹.
  • Example: 8.8 g of CO₂ (M = 44 g mol⁻¹): n = 8.8 ÷ 44 = 0.20 mol.
  • Mass from moles: m = n × M.
  • Example: 0.25 mol of NaCl (M = 58.5 g mol⁻¹): m = 0.25 × 58.5 = 14.6 g.
  • Number of particles from moles: N = n × NA.
  • Example: 0.20 mol of CO₂: N = 0.20 × 6.02 × 10²³ = 1.20 × 10²³ molecules.
  • Moles from number of particles: n = N ÷ NA.
  • Number of atoms: multiply the number of molecules by the number of atoms in each molecule.
  • Example: 0.20 mol of CO₂ contains 0.20 × 3 = 0.60 mol of atoms, so 0.60 × 6.02 × 10²³ = 3.61 × 10²³ atoms.
  • Gas volume from moles: V = n × molar volume, using 22.4 L mol⁻¹ at STP.
  • Mass from volume and density: m = ρ × V, then convert the mass to moles with n = m ÷ M.
  • Example: 10.0 cm³ of ethanol (density 0.789 g cm⁻³): m = 10.0 × 0.789 = 7.89 g.
  • Continuing the ethanol example (M = 46 g mol⁻¹): n = 7.89 ÷ 46 = 0.172 mol.
  • Conversion route: mass ⇄ moles ⇄ number of particles, and moles ⇄ gas volume; always go through moles.

Isotopes and relative atomic mass

  • Isotopes are atoms of the same element with different mass numbers.
  • The mass number of an isotope is the total number of protons and neutrons in its nucleus.
  • Relative atomic mass (Ar) is the weighted average of the isotope masses, using their percentage abundances, relative to one-twelfth of the mass of a carbon-12 atom.

Calculating Ar from percentage abundances

Each isotope contributes to the average in proportion to how abundant it is.

  • Step 1: multiply each isotope's mass number by its percentage abundance.
  • Step 2: add the products together.
  • Step 3: divide the total by 100, so Ar = Σ(mass number × % abundance) ÷ 100.
  • Chlorine example (75% ³⁵Cl, 25% ³⁷Cl): the products are 35 × 75 = 2625 and 37 × 25 = 925.
  • Adding the products gives 2625 + 925 = 3550, and 3550 ÷ 100 = 35.5, so Ar(Cl) = 35.5.
  • Boron example (19.9% ¹⁰B, 80.1% ¹¹B): the products are 10 × 19.9 = 199 and 11 × 80.1 = 881.1.
  • Adding the products gives 199 + 881.1 = 1080.1, and 1080.1 ÷ 100 = 10.8, so Ar(B) = 10.8.
  • Magnesium example, three isotopes (79.0% ²⁴Mg, 10.0% ²⁵Mg, 11.0% ²⁶Mg): the products are 24 × 79.0 = 1896, 25 × 10.0 = 250 and 26 × 11.0 = 286.
  • Adding the products gives 1896 + 250 + 286 = 2432, and 2432 ÷ 100 = 24.3, so Ar(Mg) = 24.3.
  • Quick check: the answer must lie between the lightest and heaviest isotope mass numbers, nearer to the more abundant isotope.

Calculating percentage abundances from Ar

  • For two isotopes, let the abundance of one isotope be x%; the other is then (100 − x)%.
  • Set up Ar = [mass₁ × x + mass₂ × (100 − x)] ÷ 100 and solve for x.
  • Boron example (Ar = 10.8; isotopes ¹⁰B and ¹¹B): 10x + 11(100 − x) = 1080.
  • Expanding gives 10x + 1100 − 11x = 1080, so 1100 − x = 1080 and x = 20.
  • The abundances are therefore about 20% boron-10 and 80% boron-11, matching the 19.9% and 80.1% used in the Ar calculation.
  • Using whole mass numbers is an approximation; data-book isotope masses give more accurate abundances.

Avogadro's law and combining volumes

  • Avogadro's law: equal volumes of all gases, at the same temperature and pressure, contain equal numbers of molecules.
  • So at fixed temperature and pressure, the volume of a gas is directly proportional to the number of moles: V ∝ n.
  • Gay-Lussac's law of combining volumes: gases react in volumes that are in simple whole-number ratios to one another and to the volumes of any gaseous products, at the same temperature and pressure.
  • Example of combining volumes: 2 volumes of hydrogen react with 1 volume of oxygen.
  • Avogadro's law explains Gay-Lussac's law: the volume ratio equals the mole ratio in the balanced equation.
  • Example: N₂(g) + 3H₂(g) → 2NH₃(g) reads as 1 volume of nitrogen + 3 volumes of hydrogen → 2 volumes of ammonia.

STP and molar volume

  • Standard temperature and pressure (STP) is 0 °C (273 K) and 101.3 kPa (1 atmosphere, 1.013 × 10⁵ Pa).
  • Temperature in gas calculations must be in kelvin.
  • Molar volume is the volume occupied by one mole of any gas at a stated temperature and pressure.
  • At STP the molar volume is 22.4 L mol⁻¹ (22 400 cm³).
  • At room temperature (about 20 °C) and pressure, the molar volume is about 24.0 L mol⁻¹.
  • IUPAC now uses a standard pressure of 100 kPa, at which the molar volume at 273.15 K is 22.7 L mol⁻¹.
  • Leaving Certificate calculations use 22.4 L mol⁻¹ at STP unless told otherwise.
  • Molar volume applies to gases only, not to liquids or solids.

Deducing the molar volume

  • From the ideal gas equation PV = nRT (see "Gas laws and the ideal gas equation"), the volume of n moles is V = nRT ÷ P.
  • For 1 mol at 273 K and 101 325 Pa, with R = 8.314 J K⁻¹ mol⁻¹: V = 1 × 8.314 × 273 ÷ 101 325.
  • This gives V = 0.0224 m³ = 22.4 L, the molar volume at STP.
  • Experimentally, weigh a gas cylinder (for example, of oxygen) before and after releasing a known volume of gas into a gas syringe.
  • Find the number of moles of gas released from the mass lost, using n = m ÷ M.
  • Scale the volume to one mole and correct it to STP to obtain the molar volume.
  • Diagram a gas cylinder on a balance, connected by tubing to a gas syringe; label the cylinder, the balance, the tubing and the scale of the gas syringe.

Calculations with molar volume

  • Moles from gas volume at STP: n = V ÷ 22.4, with V in L.
  • Example: 5.6 L of CO₂ at STP: n = 5.6 ÷ 22.4 = 0.25 mol.
  • Volume from moles at STP: V = n × 22.4 L.
  • Example: 0.50 mol of O₂ occupies 0.50 × 22.4 = 11.2 L at STP.
  • Mass to volume goes through moles: first n = m ÷ M.
  • Example: 8.0 g of methane (M = 16 g mol⁻¹): n = 8.0 ÷ 16 = 0.50 mol.
  • Then V = 0.50 × 22.4 = 11.2 L at STP.
  • Example of reacting gases: CH₄ + 2O₂ → CO₂ + 2H₂O.
  • The volumes of methane and oxygen are in the ratio 50 cm³ : 100 cm³, which is 1 : 2 as in the equation, so all the reactants are used up.
  • So 50 cm³ of CO₂ is formed, measured at the same temperature and pressure.

Principle

Aim: to determine the relative molecular mass of a volatile liquid by turning a measured mass of it into vapour.

  • A volatile liquid evaporates easily, i.e. it has a low boiling point, so it can be turned completely into vapour at 100 °C.
  • If the mass (m), volume (V), temperature (T) and pressure (P) of the vapour are measured, the number of moles is n = PV ÷ RT.
  • Relative molecular mass is then Mr = m ÷ n, giving the molar mass in g mol⁻¹.
  • The vapour is assumed to behave as an ideal gas.

Apparatus

  • A clean, dry conical flask (about 250 cm³), a small square of aluminium foil held by a rubber band, and a pin.
  • A large beaker of boiling water (the water bath) on a hotplate, with a thermometer in the water.
  • A top-pan balance reading to 0.01 g, a barometer, a graduated (measuring) cylinder, and a dropper of the volatile liquid (propanone).

Method

  • Weigh the dry conical flask with the foil cap and rubber band.
  • Add about 2–3 cm³ of propanone, replace the foil cap and pierce one small pinhole in it.
  • Clamp the flask in the boiling water bath so that as much of it as possible is immersed.
  • Leave it until all the liquid has vaporised; the vapour pushes the air out of the flask through the pinhole and excess vapour escapes.
  • The flask is then left full of propanone vapour at the temperature of the boiling water.
  • Record the temperature of the water bath and the atmospheric pressure from the barometer.
  • Remove the flask and cool it, so the vapour condenses.
  • Dry the outside of the flask thoroughly and reweigh it; the increase in mass is the mass of vapour that filled the flask.
  • Find the volume of the flask by filling it completely with water and measuring the water with a graduated cylinder.
  • Diagram the conical flask with its foil cap, rubber band and pinhole, clamped in a beaker of boiling water on a hotplate; label the water bath and the thermometer.
  • Diagram a graduated (measuring) cylinder, read at eye level with the bottom of the meniscus on the scale.

Calculation (sample data)

The numbers below are sample data for illustration.

  • Sample data: mass of vapour 0.51 g; volume of flask 270 cm³ = 2.70 × 10⁻⁴ m³; temperature 100 °C = 373 K; pressure 101 000 Pa.
  • Units: P in Pa, V in m³ and T in K; 1 cm³ = 1 × 10⁻⁶ m³.
  • Moles: n = PV ÷ RT, with R = 8.314 J K⁻¹ mol⁻¹.
  • Numerator: PV = 101 000 × 2.70 × 10⁻⁴ = 27.27.
  • Denominator: RT = 8.314 × 373 = 3101.
  • Moles: n = 27.27 ÷ 3101 = 8.79 × 10⁻³ mol.
  • Relative molecular mass: Mr = m ÷ n = 0.51 ÷ (8.79 × 10⁻³) = 58.0.
  • The accepted Mr of propanone (CH₃COCH₃) is 58, so the result agrees with it.

Safety

  • Propanone is highly flammable: use no naked flames, and heat the water bath with a hotplate or electric heater.
  • Propanone vapour is an irritant: work in a well-ventilated room.
  • Take care with boiling water and hot glassware.

Sources of error and improvements

  • If not all the liquid vaporises, or the flask is removed too early, the liquid left in it makes the mass too high and so Mr too high.
  • Wait until no liquid is visible in the flask before removing it from the bath.
  • Water left on the outside of the flask when it is reweighed makes the mass too high; dry it carefully.
  • A pinhole that is too large lets air re-enter as the flask cools, or lets vapour escape before weighing, which affects the measured mass.
  • Part of the flask is above the water, so the vapour there is cooler than 373 K.
  • The vapour is not a perfect ideal gas, especially close to its boiling point.
  • The mass of vapour is small compared with the flask, so a balance reading to 0.01 g (or better) is needed.
  • Repeat the experiment and average the results.

Key terms

Mole
The SI unit of amount of substance; one mole contains 6.02 × 10²³ elementary entities.
Avogadro constant
The number of elementary entities in one mole, NA = 6.02 × 10²³ mol⁻¹.
Elementary entity
A single atom, molecule, ion or electron, counted when stating an amount in moles.
Relative molecular mass (Mr)
The sum of the relative atomic masses of all the atoms in a molecule or formula unit; it has no units.
Molar mass (M)
The mass of one mole of a substance in g mol⁻¹, numerically equal to Ar or Mr.
Density
Mass per unit volume, ρ = m ÷ V.
Formula unit
The smallest repeating unit of an ionic compound as written in its formula, such as NaCl.
Molar volume
The volume occupied by one mole of a gas at a stated temperature and pressure.
Isotopes
Atoms of the same element that have different mass numbers.
Mass number
The total number of protons and neutrons in the nucleus of an atom.
Relative atomic mass (Ar)
The weighted average mass of an element's isotopes, relative to one-twelfth of the mass of a carbon-12 atom.
Percentage abundance
The percentage of atoms of an element that are a particular isotope.
Weighted average
An average in which each value is multiplied by how much it contributes before the total is divided by the sum of the weights.
Carbon-12 scale
The standard against which relative atomic masses are measured: one-twelfth of the mass of a carbon-12 atom.
Avogadro's law
Equal volumes of all gases, at the same temperature and pressure, contain equal numbers of molecules.
Gay-Lussac's law of combining volumes
Gases react in simple whole-number ratios by volume, at the same temperature and pressure.
Standard temperature and pressure (STP)
0 °C (273 K) and 101.3 kPa, the conditions for the molar volume of 22.4 L mol⁻¹.
Kelvin
The unit of temperature used in gas calculations; 0 °C is 273 K.
Ideal gas equation
PV = nRT, where P is pressure in Pa, V is volume in m³, n is moles, R = 8.314 J K⁻¹ mol⁻¹ and T is temperature in K.
Volatile liquid
A liquid that evaporates easily, with a low boiling point.
Water bath
A beaker of boiling water that heats the flask to a known temperature of 373 K.
Pinhole
A small hole in the foil cap that lets air and excess vapour escape, so the flask fills with vapour at the bath temperature.
Condense
To change from vapour to liquid on cooling, so the vapour's mass can be found by weighing.
Propanone
A volatile, highly flammable liquid with Mr 58 and a boiling point of 56 °C, used in this experiment.
