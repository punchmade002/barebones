Questions suitable for investigation

  • A scientific question can be answered by collecting evidence through observation or experiment.
  • A good question is specific and focused and names measurable quantities, e.g. “How does the concentration of hydrochloric acid affect the rate of its reaction with calcium carbonate?”
  • Questions of value, opinion or ethics, such as “Are plastics bad?”, cannot be answered by scientific investigation.
  • A vague question, or one that cannot be tested with the available methods, is unsuitable, but a vague question can often be refined into a testable one.

Variables

  • The independent variable is the one the investigator deliberately changes.
  • The dependent variable is the one that is measured, and it may change as a result.
  • Controlled variables are all the other factors that could affect the result; they are kept the same so that the test is fair.

Testable hypotheses

A hypothesis is based on existing scientific theory rather than on a guess.

  • A hypothesis is a proposed explanation that leads to a testable prediction.
  • A good hypothesis states the expected relationship between the independent and dependent variables and gives the reason, e.g. “Increasing the concentration of acid increases the rate of reaction, because there are more acid particles per unit volume and so more frequent effective collisions”, drawn from collision theory.
  • A hypothesis must be falsifiable: an experiment could show it to be wrong.
  • A prediction is the specific result expected if the hypothesis is correct, e.g. “doubling the concentration will roughly halve the time taken”.

Comparing strategies for testing a hypothesis

  • A hypothesis can often be tested in more than one way, e.g. by measuring the volume of gas collected in a gas syringe, the mass lost on a balance, or the time taken for a solid to disappear.
  • Strategies are compared by validity, the accuracy and precision of the measurements, how easily other variables can be controlled, safety, cost and time, and the range and number of data points.
  • The strategy chosen should give the most reliable and valid evidence with acceptable safety, and the choice must be justified.

Planning an investigation

  • A plan states the aim or hypothesis, the variables, the equipment and chemicals, a step-by-step method, the measurements to be taken and how often, and the number of repeats.
  • A plan also includes a risk assessment and states how the data will be recorded and analysed.
  • Choose a suitable range and number of values for the independent variable; at least five values across a sensible range is a suggested minimum.
  • Include a control where appropriate: a set-up in which the factor being tested is absent, for comparison, e.g. hydrogen peroxide with no catalyst.

Design terms: fairness, validity, reliability, error and integrity

  • Fairness (a fair test): only the independent variable is changed, and all other variables are controlled.
  • Validity: the investigation actually tests the hypothesis and measures what it claims to measure; it requires a fair test and suitable measurements.
  • Reliability: results can be trusted because they are consistent when the investigation is repeated.
  • Reliability is improved by repeating each measurement at least three times, calculating a mean, and comparing the results with those of other groups.
  • Accuracy and precision are improved by choosing suitable equipment and good technique; they are defined in “Measurement and error”.
  • Error: likely sources of error are identified in advance and the design is changed to reduce them, e.g. a burette rather than a measuring cylinder is used for accurate volumes.
  • Integrity: all results are recorded honestly, including unexpected ones; data are not invented, altered or selectively deleted; others' work is acknowledged.

Selecting suitable equipment

  • A pipette or burette is used for accurate volumes of solution.
  • A volumetric flask is used to make up a solution to an exact volume.
  • A measuring cylinder is used only where an approximate volume is enough.
  • A balance with a suitable resolution, e.g. 0.01 g, is used for small masses.
  • A thermometer or temperature probe with an appropriate range is used to measure temperature.
  • A gas syringe is used to measure gas volumes.
  • Data-loggers and sensors for pH, temperature and pressure give continuous readings.
  • Each choice of equipment is justified in terms of accuracy, precision and safety.

Safe laboratory practice

  • Wear eye protection (safety glasses or goggles) and a lab coat, and tie back long hair.
  • Do not eat or drink in the laboratory, and wash hands after practical work.
  • Use a fume cupboard for toxic or harmful gases and for volatile, flammable or harmful liquids.
  • Heat flammable liquids such as ethanol with a water bath or electric heater, never with a naked flame.
  • When diluting concentrated acids, add the acid to water, never water to acid.
  • Use a pipette filler; never pipette by mouth.
  • Know the location of the eyewash, fire blanket, extinguisher and first aid; report spills and breakages; dispose of chemicals as instructed.

Risk assessment

  • A hazard is anything that could cause harm, such as a corrosive acid.
  • A risk is the likelihood that harm will happen and how serious it would be.
  • 1. Identify each hazard from labels, hazard pictograms and safety data sheets.
  • 2. Assess the risk of each hazard.
  • 3. Decide on control measures that reduce the risk.
  • 4. Know what to do in an emergency.
  • The GHS (Globally Harmonized System) uses nine hazard pictograms, each with a red diamond border: corrosive, flammable, toxic, harmful or irritant (exclamation mark), oxidising, health hazard, environmental hazard, explosive and compressed gas.
  • Example: concentrated sodium hydroxide is corrosive, so there is a risk of skin and eye burns; controls are goggles, gloves, small volumes and dilute solutions where possible; if it touches the skin, wash it with plenty of water.

Key terms

Independent variable
The variable that the investigator deliberately changes.
Dependent variable
The variable that is measured, and which may change as a result of changing the independent variable.
Controlled variable
A factor, other than the independent variable, that is kept the same so that the test is fair.
Hypothesis
A proposed explanation, based on scientific theory, that leads to a testable prediction.
Prediction
The specific result expected if the hypothesis is correct.
Falsifiable
Able to be shown to be wrong by an experiment.
Testable question
A specific, focused question that can be answered by collecting evidence through observation or experiment.
Fair test
An investigation in which only the independent variable is changed and all other variables are controlled.
Validity
The extent to which an investigation actually tests the hypothesis and measures what it claims to measure.
Reliability
The extent to which results are consistent when an investigation is repeated, so that they can be trusted.
Control
A set-up in which the factor being tested is absent, used for comparison.
Integrity
Recording all results honestly, including unexpected ones, and not inventing, altering or selectively deleting data.
Hazard
Anything that could cause harm.
Risk
The likelihood that harm will happen, and how serious it would be.
Risk assessment
The process of identifying hazards, assessing their risks and deciding on control measures.
Fume cupboard
A ventilated enclosure that removes toxic or harmful fumes from the work area.
