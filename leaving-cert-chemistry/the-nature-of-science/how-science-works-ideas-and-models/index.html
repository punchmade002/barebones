Scientific vocabulary

Scientific knowledge is built from observations and evidence, and each of these words has a precise meaning.

  • Observation: information gathered with the senses or with instruments; it is qualitative (a description such as a colour change) or quantitative (a measurement with a number and a unit).
  • Hypothesis: a testable, tentative explanation or prediction that evidence from an experiment can support or contradict.
  • Theory: a well-tested explanation of a wide range of observations, supported by a large body of evidence from many investigations, e.g. the kinetic theory of matter and collision theory.
  • Law: a statement, often mathematical, that describes what always happens under stated conditions, e.g. the law of conservation of mass and Boyle's law.
  • A law describes what happens; a theory explains why it happens.
  • Evidence: data from observation and experiment that are used to support or reject a hypothesis or theory; scientific knowledge rests on evidence, not on authority or opinion.

How scientists work

Scientific work follows a cycle, but the cycle is rarely a straight line.

  • 1. Make observations and ask a question.
  • 2. Research what is already known.
  • 3. Form a hypothesis and use it to make a prediction.
  • 4. Test the prediction with a fair, controlled experiment.
  • 5. Analyse the data and draw a conclusion.
  • 6. Accept, modify or reject the hypothesis.
  • 7. Communicate the results so that others can check them.
  • Scientists repeat, redesign and troubleshoot experiments, so the cycle is iterative.
  • Unexpected results often lead to new questions and new hypotheses.
  • Creativity and imagination, as well as logic, are used to propose hypotheses and to design experiments and models.
  • One reliable, reproducible result that contradicts a hypothesis can be enough to reject it, whereas agreeing results never prove a hypothesis absolutely.

Why scientific ideas change

  • Scientific knowledge is tentative: it is the best explanation of the evidence available now, and it can change when new evidence appears.
  • Ideas change when new observations do not fit the existing explanation.
  • Ideas change when new technology or instruments allow measurements that were not possible before.
  • Ideas change when a better explanation is proposed and tested against the evidence.
  • A theory is refined or replaced only when the new idea explains all the old evidence as well as the new evidence; this is how science progresses.
  • Long-standing, well-tested theories are rarely discarded completely; they are modified or have their limits identified.

Example: how the model of the atom changed

Each model of the atom was replaced or refined when new evidence did not fit the earlier one.

  • Dalton (early 1800s): matter is made of tiny indivisible atoms, and atoms of the same element are identical; this was supported by the laws of chemical combination.
  • Thomson (1897) discovered the electron, showing that atoms are not indivisible; he proposed the plum pudding model, with negative electrons embedded in a sphere of positive charge.
  • Rutherford (1911): his gold-foil experiment, carried out by Geiger and Marsden, showed that most of the atom is empty space with a tiny, dense, positive nucleus; this is the nuclear model.
  • Bohr (1913) proposed that electrons move in fixed energy levels around the nucleus, which explained line emission spectra.
  • The current orbital model describes electrons in orbitals, which are regions with a high probability of finding an electron; the models themselves are covered in “Models of the atom” and “The current orbital model”.

Bias

  • Bias is a tendency, deliberate or not, to collect, interpret or present information so as to favour one outcome.
  • Confirmation bias: expecting a particular result and then seeing it in the data.
  • Selecting only the data that support a hypothesis, or using small or unrepresentative samples, introduces bias.
  • Funding or commercial interests, and personal or political beliefs, can introduce bias.
  • Scientists reduce bias with fair tests that use controls, repeats and random sampling, and with blind trials where possible.
  • Scientists also publish all results, including negative ones, declare funding and conflicts of interest, and invite independent checks by other scientists (see “Science as a global enterprise”).

What a model is

  • A model is a representation of an object, system, process or idea that helps us understand, explain or predict something we cannot see or handle directly, such as atoms and molecules.
  • Physical models include ball-and-stick and space-filling molecular models.
  • Diagrams used as models include dot-and-cross diagrams, energy-level diagrams and energy profile diagrams.
  • Mathematical models are equations, such as PV = nRT.
  • Symbolic models are chemical formulae and equations.
  • Computer or digital simulations show particles and processes moving and changing.
  • A theory can also act as a model: the kinetic theory models matter as moving particles, and collision theory models how reactions happen.

The power of models

  • Models make the very small, the very large and the invisible understandable; a molecular model shows the shape of a molecule that is far too small to see.
  • Models explain observations: the particle model explains why gases can be compressed but liquids cannot be compressed much.
  • Models allow predictions that can be tested: the ideal gas equation predicts the volume of a gas at a new temperature and pressure, and VSEPR theory predicts molecular shapes.
  • Models help scientists communicate ideas to each other and to the public.

Limitations of models

  • Every model is a simplification: it leaves out detail and rests on assumptions, so it is never a perfect copy of reality.
  • A model works only within a range of conditions; the ideal gas model fails at high pressure and low temperature, where real gases deviate from it.
  • Ball-and-stick models show atoms as hard coloured balls and bonds as rigid sticks, and they show the wrong relative sizes and distances.
  • Different models of the same thing may each explain some observations but not others, so chemists choose the model that suits the question.
  • The Bohr model explains flame colours and line spectra but not the shapes of orbitals.
  • Models are tested against evidence and are modified or replaced when they fail to explain new observations.

Key terms

Observation
Information gathered with the senses or with instruments, either qualitative (descriptive) or quantitative (measured with numbers and units).
Hypothesis
A testable, tentative explanation or prediction that evidence from an experiment can support or contradict.
Theory
A well-tested explanation of a wide range of observations, supported by a large body of evidence from many investigations.
Law
A statement, often mathematical, that describes what always happens under stated conditions, without explaining why.
Evidence
Data from observation and experiment used to support or reject a hypothesis or theory.
Tentative knowledge
Scientific knowledge that is the best explanation of the available evidence and can change when new evidence appears.
Bias
A tendency to collect, interpret or present information so as to favour one outcome.
Confirmation bias
The tendency to expect a particular result and to notice only the data that appear to support it.
Model
A representation of an object, system, process or idea that helps us understand, explain or predict something we cannot see or handle directly.
Physical model
A three-dimensional object, such as a molecular model, that represents the arrangement of atoms in a substance.
Mathematical model
An equation that describes how quantities are related, such as PV = nRT for a gas.
Symbolic model
A representation using chemical formulae, equations or symbols to describe particles and changes.
Ball-and-stick model
A physical model in which atoms are shown as coloured balls and bonds as sticks.
Space-filling model
A physical model in which atoms are shown as overlapping spheres that give a sense of the molecule's overall size.
Dot-and-cross diagram
A diagram showing how the outer electrons of atoms are shared or transferred in bonding.
Energy profile diagram
A graph showing how the energy of a chemical system changes as a reaction proceeds.
