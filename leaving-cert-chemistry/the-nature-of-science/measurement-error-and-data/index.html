SI units and conversions

  • The SI base units used in chemistry are the metre (m), kilogram (kg), second (s), kelvin (K) and mole (mol); derived units include m³ for volume and the pascal (Pa) for pressure.
  • Volume: 1 dm³ = 1 L = 1000 cm³ = 1000 mL, and 1 m³ = 1000 dm³ = 1 000 000 cm³.
  • Mass: 1 kg = 1000 g, and 1 g = 1000 mg.
  • Temperature: T (K) = θ (°C) + 273.15, often rounded to 273; for example 25 °C = 298 K.
  • Pressure: 1 kPa = 1000 Pa; the IUPAC standard pressure is 100 kPa (1 × 10⁵ Pa), and 1 atm = 101 325 Pa.
  • Convert by multiplying or dividing by the conversion factor and writing the unit at each step, e.g. 250 cm³ = 250 ÷ 1000 = 0.250 L.

Accuracy and precision

  • Accuracy is how close a measurement, or a mean, is to the true or accepted value.
  • Precision is how close repeated measurements are to each other, meaning their spread.
  • Precision is also used for the smallest division an instrument can read, which is its resolution.
  • Results can be precise but not accurate, e.g. closely grouped readings that are all off the true value because a balance has not been zeroed.
  • Results can be accurate on average but not precise, with readings scattered on either side of the true value.
  • Every instrument has an uncertainty; typical school values are ±0.06 cm³ for a 25 cm³ pipette, ±0.05 cm³ per reading for a 50 cm³ burette, ±0.3 cm³ for a 250 cm³ volumetric flask and about ±1 cm³ for a 100 cm³ measuring cylinder.
  • Volumetric glassware is far more accurate than a measuring cylinder, which is why a pipette is used for accurate volumes.
  • Significant figures (assumed knowledge): answers are reported to the same number of significant figures as the least precise measurement used, usually three in titration calculations.

Errors

  • Random error is unpredictable variation that scatters readings above and below the true value, e.g. misjudging the end point of a titration or reading a meniscus; it is reduced by repeating and averaging.
  • Systematic error is a consistent error that shifts all readings in the same direction, e.g. a balance that has not been zeroed, a thermometer that reads 1 °C high, or heat lost to the surroundings in a calorimetry experiment.
  • Repeating does not reduce systematic error; it is reduced by calibrating the equipment or improving the method.
  • Parallax error occurs when a scale is read from an angle; the bottom of the meniscus should be read at eye level.
  • Anomalous results caused by mistakes are identified and either repeated or excluded with a reason (see “Producing and analysing data”).
  • Percentage error = (experimental value − accepted value) ÷ accepted value × 100.

Qualitative and quantitative data

  • Qualitative data are descriptions without numbers, such as a colour change, effervescence or a precipitate forming.
  • Quantitative data are measurements with numbers and units.
  • Select the data that answer the question; record the raw data first, then the processed data, such as means and rates.

Results tables

  • The independent variable goes in the first column, and the dependent variable and its repeats go in the following columns, with a mean column.
  • Each column heading gives the quantity and its unit, e.g. “Time / s”, so units are not written in the body of the table.
  • All readings are recorded to the same number of decimal places, matching the resolution of the instrument.

Drawing graphs

  • The independent variable is plotted on the x-axis and the dependent variable on the y-axis, and both axes are labelled with the quantity and its unit.
  • Scales are chosen so that the points fill more than half of the grid, with even and sensible intervals.
  • Points are plotted accurately as small crosses.
  • A line or smooth curve of best fit is drawn through the general trend; the points are not joined dot to dot.
  • The line is not forced through the origin unless the origin is a real data point.
  • A line graph is used when both variables are continuous; a bar chart is used for categories.

Analysing data

  • Describe the pattern: the values increase, decrease, level off, are directly proportional (a straight line through the origin) or are inversely proportional.
  • The gradient of a straight line is the change in y divided by the change in x.
  • For a curve, the gradient at a point is found from a tangent drawn at that point; this gives a rate.
  • Interpolation reads a value within the range of the data; extrapolation extends the line beyond it and is less reliable.
  • Both interpolation and extrapolation are predictions.
  • Trends in a table or a series of compounds allow predictions, e.g. the boiling point of the next alkane can be predicted from the trend in the series.
  • Mathematical representations are rearranged and used, e.g. PV = nRT, and means, percentages and rates are calculated.

Scientific notation (assumed knowledge)

  • Scientific notation (standard form) writes a number as A × 10ⁿ, where A is between 1 and 10.
  • The Avogadro constant is 6.02 × 10²³ mol⁻¹.
  • 0.00050 = 5.0 × 10⁻⁴: the decimal point moves four places to the right, so the power of ten is −4.

Anomalous results and conclusions

  • An anomalous result does not fit the pattern of the other results; it is identified on the graph or in the table and a reason is suggested, such as a misreading.
  • An anomalous result is left out of the mean and of the line of best fit, and this is stated.
  • A conclusion states the relationship shown by the data and says whether it supports the hypothesis.
  • A conclusion explains the relationship using scientific theory or a model, e.g. collision theory.
  • The conclusion is justified by reference to the data and states its limits: the range tested, the amount of data and the size of the errors.
  • Correlation does not prove causation: a link between two variables may be due to a third factor.

Key terms

SI units
The internationally agreed system of units, with base units including the metre, kilogram, second, kelvin and mole.
Accuracy
How close a measurement, or a mean, is to the true or accepted value.
Precision
How close repeated measurements are to each other.
Resolution
The smallest division that an instrument can read.
Random error
Unpredictable variation that scatters readings above and below the true value, reduced by repeating and averaging.
Systematic error
A consistent error that shifts all readings in the same direction, which is not reduced by repeating.
Parallax error
An error caused by reading a scale from an angle rather than at eye level.
Percentage error
The difference between the experimental and accepted values, divided by the accepted value and multiplied by 100.
Significant figures
The digits in a number that are meaningful, used to match the precision of an answer to the measurements used.
Anomalous result
A result that does not fit the pattern of the other results.
Qualitative data
Descriptions of observations that do not involve numbers.
Quantitative data
Measurements recorded as numbers with units.
Gradient
For a straight line, the change in y divided by the change in x; for a curve, the gradient of the tangent at a point.
Line of best fit
A straight line or smooth curve drawn through the general trend of plotted points, not joining them point to point.
Interpolation
Estimating a value within the range of the data from a graph.
Extrapolation
Estimating a value beyond the range of the data by extending a graph, which is less reliable.
Directly proportional
Describes two variables whose graph is a straight line through the origin, so that one doubles when the other doubles.
Scientific notation
A way of writing a number as A × 10ⁿ, where A is between 1 and 10.
Correlation
A relationship between two variables in which one tends to change with the other.
