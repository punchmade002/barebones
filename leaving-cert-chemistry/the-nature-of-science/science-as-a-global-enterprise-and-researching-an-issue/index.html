Science as a global enterprise

  • Scientists work in universities, industry and government laboratories around the world, sharing results and building on each other's work.
  • Large problems such as climate change, new medicines and clean energy are tackled by international collaborations that share data, equipment and funding.
  • Scientists publish in journals and present at conferences so that results are available to everyone and can be checked.
  • Scientific claims must be supported by evidence that others can examine, so data, methods and conclusions are published together.

Clear communication

  • A report must describe the method in enough detail for another scientist to repeat it exactly.
  • Clear communication uses precise scientific terms, SI units, correct significant figures, labelled tables and graphs, and cited sources.

International conventions

Conventions are agreed rules that all scientists use, so that results mean the same everywhere.

  • IUPAC (the International Union of Pure and Applied Chemistry) sets the rules for naming compounds, e.g. the systematic name but-1-ene, and agrees chemical symbols and atomic masses.
  • The SI (Système International) system of units has base units, including the metre (m), kilogram (kg), second (s), kelvin (K) and mole (mol).
  • The SI base units also include the ampere (A) and the candela (cd).
  • Other shared conventions are chemical symbols and formulae, standard conditions for measurements, and international hazard symbols (pictograms) on chemicals.

Peer review

  • Peer review is the evaluation of a scientist's work by other independent experts in the same field before it is published.
  • 1. The scientist submits a paper to a journal.
  • 2. The editor sends the paper to reviewers.
  • 3. The reviewers check the method, data, analysis and conclusions for errors and originality, and whether the conclusions are justified.
  • 4. The paper is accepted, returned for changes, or rejected.
  • Peer review filters out poor or flawed work, reduces bias and gives readers confidence in published results.
  • Peer review does not guarantee that a result is correct; results are checked by others repeating the work.

Repeatability and reproducibility

  • Repeatable: the same experimenter, using the same method and equipment, gets the same or very similar results when the experiment is repeated.
  • Reproducible: a different experimenter, using different equipment or a different method, gets the same results.
  • A claim is accepted only when its results are reproducible; results that no one else can reproduce are not accepted.

Planning the research

  • Start with a focused research question about the issue, e.g. “What are the environmental effects of lithium mining?”
  • Identify keywords and search systematically rather than reading whatever appears first.
  • Record every source used: author, title, date, publisher or website, and the date accessed.
  • Take notes in your own words and keep a research log.

Primary and secondary data

  • Primary data are collected first-hand by the investigator, e.g. by experiment or measurement.
  • Secondary data are collected by someone else, e.g. published results, government statistics, databases or the results of other class groups.
  • Secondary data are used to compare with primary data, to check or support conclusions, or where an experiment cannot be done in school.

Kinds of source

  • Peer-reviewed journal articles are the most reliable sources but are often technical.
  • Books and textbooks, and reports from government agencies and scientific bodies, are further kinds of source.
  • The Environmental Protection Agency (EPA) is Ireland's environmental regulator.
  • The EPA publishes reports and data on air and water quality.
  • News media, websites, blogs and social media are accessible but of variable reliability; they may simplify, sensationalise or omit detail.
  • Company or campaign-group material may be written to sell a product or promote a view.

Evaluating a source

  • Authority: who wrote the source, and are they qualified and named?
  • Accuracy and evidence: are the claims supported by data and references, and can they be checked against other sources?
  • Currency: is the source up to date?
  • Purpose and bias: is it trying to inform, persuade or sell; who funded it; does it present only one side?
  • Detail: does it explain methods, sample sizes and uncertainties, or does it give only conclusions? A source may lack the detail needed to judge it.
  • Corroboration: a claim supported by several independent, reliable sources is more trustworthy than a claim from one source.

Key terms

Peer review
The evaluation of a scientist's work by other independent experts in the same field before it is published.
Repeatable
Giving the same results when the same experimenter repeats the experiment using the same method and equipment.
Reproducible
Giving the same results when a different experimenter repeats the experiment, using different equipment or a different method.
IUPAC
The International Union of Pure and Applied Chemistry, which sets the rules for naming compounds and agrees chemical symbols and atomic masses.
SI units
The Système International system of units, in which the base units include the metre, kilogram, second, kelvin and mole.
Convention
An agreed rule that all scientists follow so that results mean the same everywhere.
Hazard pictogram
A standard symbol on a chemical label that shows a hazard, such as corrosive or flammable.
Research question
A focused question about an issue that guides the search for information.
Primary data
Data collected first-hand by the investigator, for example by experiment or measurement.
Secondary data
Data collected by someone else, such as published results, government statistics or databases.
Peer-reviewed article
A journal article that has been checked by independent experts before publication.
Bias
A tendency to collect, interpret or present information so as to favour one outcome.
Corroboration
Support for a claim from several independent and reliable sources.
Currency
How up to date a source is, which affects how far its information can be trusted.
