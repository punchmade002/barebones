The investigative log

The log is a portfolio of experimental and research data, kept while the work is done rather than written up afterwards.

  • A log (laboratory notebook or portfolio) is a dated, ongoing record of all experimental and research work.
  • The log records the aim and plan, any changes made to the plan and why, the raw data and observations including unexpected ones, calculations, sources consulted, problems met and reflections.
  • The log provides evidence of what was done and supports honesty and integrity.
  • The log allows the work to be repeated or written up later; the Chemistry in Practice Investigation brief asks students to keep one.

Evaluating an investigation

An evaluation judges the strengths and weaknesses of the method and the data; it is not a summary of what was done.

  • Evaluate the method: was the test fair and valid, and which variables were hard to control?
  • Evaluate the data: was there enough of it, over a wide enough range; how much did the repeats vary; were there anomalous results?
  • Identify the main sources of error and their likely effect, stating whether each makes the result larger or smaller than the true value.
  • Human error on its own is not an acceptable source of error; name the specific mistake, such as misreading a meniscus.
  • Suggest specific improvements, e.g. collecting a gas in a gas syringe instead of over water, using a data-logger, or insulating a calorimeter.
  • Suggest further investigations that would test the conclusion more fully.
  • Judge how confident you can be in the conclusion.

Reflecting on skills and thinking

  • Reflection asks what went well, what did not, what would be done differently, and what was learned about investigating.
  • Reflection is honest about difficulties.
  • An investigation is valuable for the process it teaches, not only for getting a right answer.

Transferring skills to unfamiliar contexts

  • Skills transfer: the same method of planning, controlling variables and analysing data applies to new problems.
  • In an unfamiliar problem, identify what is being asked, the chemistry it depends on, and the data given.
  • Set out a step-by-step approach to the problem.
  • Check that the answer is sensible.

Structure of a scientific report

  • A report has a title, followed by an introduction giving the background research and the research question, and then the hypothesis.
  • The method gives the equipment, a step-by-step procedure and the safety precautions.
  • The results are presented as tables and graphs, and the analysis gives the calculations and the patterns in the data.
  • The report ends with a conclusion, an evaluation and references.
  • The report is written in the past tense and in an impersonal style.

Scientific terminology and representations

  • Precise terms are used, e.g. “dissolves”, not “melts”, for a solid going into solution, and “effervescence” for bubbles of gas.
  • Correct SI units and IUPAC names are used throughout.
  • Representations include balanced chemical equations with state symbols, structural formulae, labelled diagrams of apparatus, tables, graphs and energy profile diagrams.

Communicating with different audiences

  • Scientists need full technical detail, the data and references, as in a journal paper or a conference poster.
  • The public, policy-makers or a class need plain language, the key findings stated first, simple graphics, and technical terms explained.
  • The same findings may be communicated as a report, poster, presentation, infographic or article.
  • The format is chosen for the purpose and the audience.

Justifying opinions and citing sources

  • An argument states a claim, supports it with evidence and reasoning, and considers counter-arguments.
  • Each source is cited in the text and listed in full in the references, so that readers can check it.
  • Copying someone else's work without acknowledgement is plagiarism.
  • When evaluating someone else's argument, check its evidence, reasoning and sources before agreeing or disagreeing.

Key terms

Investigative log
A dated, ongoing record of all experimental and research work, kept as the work happens.
Portfolio
A collection of records of experimental and research work, used as evidence of what was done.
Evaluation of an investigation
A judgement of the strengths and weaknesses of the method and data, with suggested improvements.
Limitation
A restriction in the method or data that reduces how far a conclusion can be trusted.
Reflection
An honest review of what went well, what did not, and what was learned from carrying out an investigation.
Transferable skill
A method, such as planning, controlling variables or analysing data, that can be applied to new problems.
Scientific report
A written account of an investigation giving its introduction, method, results, analysis, conclusion, evaluation and references.
Counter-argument
A point that opposes a claim, which a well-justified argument considers and answers.
Citation
A reference in the text to a source, matched by a full entry in the list of references.
Plagiarism
Presenting another person's work as your own without acknowledging it.
State symbol
A symbol in brackets, such as (s), (l), (g) or (aq), showing the physical state of a substance in an equation.
Structural formula
A formula that shows how the atoms in a molecule are arranged and bonded.
IUPAC name
A systematic name for a compound, set by the International Union of Pure and Applied Chemistry.
Energy profile diagram
A graph showing how the energy of a chemical system changes as a reaction proceeds.
